## Problem

A disc of radius $R$ is first at rest in an inertial frame.
It is then brought into rotation about its axis with constant angular speed $\omega$, where $\omega R < c$.
Each element of the rim moves along its own length with speed $\omega R$.
So it should be contracted by the factor $1/\gamma$, where $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - \omega^2 R^2/c^2}$ is the [[Lorentz factor|2E9AJVZS6E]].
Each radius moves perpendicular to its own length, so it should not be contracted.
How can a rigid disc, whose radius stays $R$ while its circumference shrinks, be set into rotation?
What is the geometry of the rotating disc?

## Remarks

A [[Born rigid|J7FVATX80A]] body at rest cannot be brought into rotation; this follows from the [[Herglotz–Noether theorem|DPKZYYBCCN]].
So the disc must deform while it is spun up; a real disc develops stresses.
Once it rotates uniformly, it can move in a Born rigid way.
Then rulers at rest on the rim measure the circumference $2\pi R \gamma$, while rulers along a radius measure $R$.
The ratio of measured circumference to measured diameter is $\pi \gamma > \pi$, so the space of the rotating observers is not Euclidean.
Einstein took this observation as a hint that gravity, viewed through acceleration, goes with non-Euclidean geometry.
Paul Ehrenfest posed the problem in 1909.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Spin a wheel very fast.
The rim moves along itself and should shrink by length contraction, but the spokes move sideways and should keep their length.
A circle that shrinks with spokes of fixed length cannot be a flat circle.
The answer is that no wheel can stay perfectly rigid while it speeds up, and observers riding on a spinning wheel find that their space is not Euclidean.
