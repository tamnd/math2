## Summary

Let $V$ be an [[inner product space|0QVNCJXRY3]] over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.
Two vectors $x, y \in V$ are orthogonal, written $x \perp y$, when $\ip{x}{y} = 0$.
A family $(v_1, \dots, v_k)$ of vectors of $V$ is an orthogonal family when $\ip{v_j}{v_l} = 0$ for all $j \neq l$.
The [[formal definition|0TS7VZJBWD]] states both notions.
Orthogonality matters because it is the abstract form of a right angle, and orthogonal vectors make computations of lengths, coordinates and projections simple.

## Motivation

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$, two nonzero vectors are [[perpendicular|03NK6RSRQ1]] exactly when their dot product is $0$.
The dot product is one inner product, and the condition $\ip{x}{y} = 0$ makes sense for every inner product.
So we use it to define right angles in any inner product space, also where we cannot draw a picture.
With this definition the [[Pythagorean theorem|0BVTDM872V]] holds in every inner product space.

## Notation

- We write $x \perp y$ for $\ip{x}{y} = 0$.
- For a set $S$, we write $x \perp S$ when $x \perp s$ for every $s \in S$.
- The [[orthogonal complement|0XJXTBCB2T]] $S^\perp$ is the set of all $x$ with $x \perp S$. A later sub-area treats it.
- Some books say "perpendicular" or "normal" in place of "orthogonal".

## Remarks

### Examples

- In $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product, $(1, 2) \perp (-2, 1)$, because $-2 + 2 = 0$.
- In $\mathbb{C}^2$ with the standard inner product, $(1, i) \perp (1, -i)$, because $1 \cdot 1 + i \cdot \overline{-i} = 1 + i \cdot i = 0$. The [[orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^2$|V2B3V8T8TX]] uses this pair.
- In $\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R})$ with $\int_{-1}^{1} pq$, the polynomials $1$ and $t$ are orthogonal, because $\int_{-1}^{1} t \, dt = 0$. The [[Legendre polynomials|JPDVZM20EX]] form an orthogonal family for this inner product.
- In $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ with the trace inner product, the matrix units $E_{11}$ and $E_{12}$ are orthogonal, because their entries never overlap.
- The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector.

### Non-examples

- Orthogonality depends on the inner product. For the [[weighted inner product $2x_1y_1 + x_2y_2$|H8ZMNX035D]], the vectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ give $1$, so they are not orthogonal, although their dot product is $0$.
- Over $\mathbb{C}$, the [[numbers $1$ and $i$|VT90B84J5V]] satisfy $|1 + i|^2 = |1|^2 + |i|^2$, but they are not orthogonal, since $\ip{1}{i} = -i$.
- In $\mathbb{R}^2$, the vectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$ are not orthogonal, since their dot product is $1$.

### Basic properties

For all $x, y, z \in V$ and scalars $a, b$:

- Symmetry: $x \perp y$ implies $y \perp x$, because $\ip{y}{x} = \overline{\ip{x}{y}} = 0$.
- Linearity: if $x \perp z$ and $y \perp z$, then $(ax + by) \perp z$. So the vectors orthogonal to $z$ form a subspace.
- Only $0$ is orthogonal to itself, by positive definiteness. Only $0$ is orthogonal to every vector of $V$.
- The [[Pythagorean theorem|0BVTDM872V]]: if $x \perp y$, then $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + \norm{y}^2$.
- An orthogonal family of nonzero vectors is linearly independent, as the theorem on [[orthonormal families|0JWM6GJNQW]] shows after normalization.
- For $y \neq 0$, the vector $x - \frac{\ip{x}{y}}{\norm{y}^2}y$ is orthogonal to $y$. This is the step of the [[Gram-Schmidt process|0VT6P0GNQR]].

### History

In $\mathbb{R}^n$ the notion is the perpendicularity of the sub-area on [[vectors in Euclidean space|VKVAJ94SKZ]].
The general notion appears together with the inner product, so its history is the history of the [[inner product|0NB0AWGJBW]].

### Common errors

- Students treat orthogonality as a property of two vectors alone. It depends on the inner product, as the weighted example shows.
- Students use the Pythagorean identity as a test for orthogonality over $\mathbb{C}$. It shows only that $\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} = 0$.
- Students think that an orthogonal family is always independent. The family may contain $0$, and then it is dependent.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Orthogonal is the general word for perpendicular.
Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is zero.
In the plane, $(2, 1)$ and $(-1, 2)$ give $2 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 2 = 0$, so they meet at a right angle.
For other inner products, "right angle" can mean something different from the picture on paper.

### L2 human

Worked example: we find all vectors of $\mathbb{R}^3$ orthogonal to $u = (1, 2, -1)$ for the dot product.

1. A vector $x$ is orthogonal to $u$ when $x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 = 0$.
2. This is one linear equation, so its solutions form a plane through $0$.
3. Solve for $x_3 = x_1 + 2x_2$. A basis of the plane is $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 2)$.
4. Check: $(1, 0, 1) \cdot u = 1 + 0 - 1 = 0$ and $(0, 1, 2) \cdot u = 0 + 2 - 2 = 0$.

The typical use is to split a vector into a part along a direction and a part orthogonal to it.
A trap: the two basis vectors of step 3 are orthogonal to $u$, but not to each other, since their dot product is $2$.

### L3 human

Orthogonality is a symmetric relation that the inner product defines, and for a subset $S$ it gives the subspace $S^\perp$.
The map $S \mapsto S^\perp$ is the base of the theory of [[orthogonal complements|0XJXTBCB2T]] and [[orthogonal projections|0B8KZRT28M]] in the next sub-area.
For a bilinear form that is not positive definite, a nonzero vector can be orthogonal to itself, as for light-like vectors of the Minkowski form.
In functional analysis, orthogonal families of functions, such as the trigonometric functions or the [[orthogonal polynomials|8TXASARQN1]], are the base of series expansions.
