## Summary

Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is zero.
This is the right angle of an arbitrary inner product space, and it is what makes lengths, coordinates and projections easy to compute.
Let $V$ be an [[inner product space|0QVNCJXRY3]] over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.
Vectors $x, y \in V$ are *orthogonal*, written $x \perp y$, when
$$\ip{x}{y} = 0.$$
A family $(v_1, \dots, v_k)$ in $V$ is an *orthogonal family* when $\ip{v_j}{v_l} = 0$ for all $j \neq l$.
The [[formal definition|0TS7VZJBWD]] states both notions.

## Motivation

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$, two nonzero vectors are [[perpendicular|03NK6RSRQ1]] exactly when their dot product is $0$.
The dot product is one inner product among many, and the condition $\ip{x}{y} = 0$ makes sense for each of them.
So we take it as the definition of a right angle in every inner product space, also in spaces of polynomials or matrices where no picture is at hand.
The definition earns its place at once: with it, the [[Pythagorean theorem|0BVTDM872V]] holds in every inner product space.

Orthogonality is the thread of the rest of the sub-area.
[[Orthonormal families|0RW9TNNT87]] are orthogonal families of unit vectors, the [[Gram-Schmidt process|0VT6P0GNQR]] makes a family orthogonal, and the next sub-area builds [[orthogonal complements|0XJXTBCB2T]] and [[orthogonal projections|0B8KZRT28M]] on it.
In signal processing and statistics the same idea appears as uncorrelated signals and as the residual of a least squares fit, which is orthogonal to the fitted values.

## Notation

We write $x \perp y$ for $\ip{x}{y} = 0$, and $x \perp S$ when $x \perp s$ for every $s$ in a set $S$.
The set of all $x$ with $x \perp S$ is the orthogonal complement $S^\perp$.
Some books say "perpendicular" or "normal" in place of "orthogonal".
When two inner products live on one space, say which one you mean, as in "orthogonal for $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}_w$", since the answer can change.

## Remarks

### Right angles in four spaces

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product, $(1, 2) \perp (-2, 1)$, because
$$1 \cdot (-2) + 2 \cdot 1 = 0.$$
In $\mathbb{C}^2$ with the standard inner product $\ip{x}{y} = x_1\overline{y_1} + x_2\overline{y_2}$, the vectors $(1, i)$ and $(1, -i)$ are orthogonal, because
$$1 \cdot \overline{1} + i \cdot \overline{-i} = 1 + i \cdot i = 0.$$
The [[orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^2$|V2B3V8T8TX]] is built from this pair.
On polynomials with $\ip{p}{q} = \int_{-1}^{1} p(t)q(t)\,dt$, the polynomials $1$ and $t$ are orthogonal, since $\int_{-1}^{1} t\,dt = 0$, and the [[Legendre polynomials|JPDVZM20EX]] continue this family.
In $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ with the trace inner product $\ip{A}{B} = \tr(B^*A) = \sum_{j,k} a_{jk}\overline{b_{jk}}$, the matrix units $E_{11}$ and $E_{12}$ are orthogonal, because no position carries a nonzero entry in both.

### What it is not

Orthogonality is not a property of two vectors alone.
For the [[weighted inner product $2x_1y_1 + x_2y_2$|H8ZMNX035D]] on $\mathbb{R}^2$, the vectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ give $2 - 1 = 1$, so they are not orthogonal, although their dot product is $0$.

Over $\mathbb{C}$, the Pythagorean identity is not a test.
The [[numbers $1$ and $i$|VT90B84J5V]] satisfy $\abs{1 + i}^2 = 2 = \abs{1}^2 + \abs{i}^2$, but $\ip{1}{i} = 1 \cdot \overline{i} = -i \neq 0$.
And in $\mathbb{R}^2$ the vectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$ are not orthogonal, since their dot product is $1$.

### The rules you can use

The relation is symmetric: if $\ip{x}{y} = 0$ then $\ip{y}{x} = \overline{\ip{x}{y}} = 0$.
It is linear in one slot: if $x \perp z$ and $y \perp z$, then $(ax + by) \perp z$, so the vectors orthogonal to $z$ form a subspace.
The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector, and positive definiteness makes it the only vector orthogonal to itself, and so the only vector orthogonal to all of $V$.
An orthogonal family of nonzero vectors is linearly independent, which the page on [[orthonormal families|0JWM6GJNQW]] proves after normalization.

### Making a vector orthogonal

For $y \neq 0$, subtract from $x$ its component along $y$:
$$x' = x - \frac{\ip{x}{y}}{\norm{y}^2}\,y.$$
Then $\ip{x'}{y} = \ip{x}{y} - \frac{\ip{x}{y}}{\norm{y}^2}\ip{y}{y} = 0$.
For $x = (3, 1)$ and $y = (1, 1)$ this gives $x' = (3, 1) - 2(1, 1) = (1, -1)$.
Check: $(1, -1) \cdot (1, 1) = 0$.
This single step, repeated, is the [[Gram-Schmidt process|0VT6P0GNQR]].

### Watch out

Three errors come up often.
The first treats orthogonality as a property of the vectors alone, while it depends on the inner product, as the weighted example shows.
The second uses $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + \norm{y}^2$ as a test over $\mathbb{C}$, while it shows only $\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} = 0$, as the pair $1$ and $i$ shows.
The third assumes that every orthogonal family is independent, while $(e_1, 0)$ is orthogonal and dependent.

### Try it

First, for which $t$ is $(1, t, 2) \perp (3, -1, t)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$?
The dot product is $3 - t + 2t = 3 + t$, so $t = -3$.

Second, are $(1, i)$ and $(i, 1)$ orthogonal in $\mathbb{C}^2$?
The inner product is $1 \cdot \overline{i} + i \cdot \overline{1} = -i + i = 0$, so yes.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Orthogonal is the general word for perpendicular.
Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is zero.
In the plane, $(2, 1)$ and $(-1, 2)$ give $2 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 2 = 0$, so the two arrows meet at a right angle.
For other inner products, a right angle can look different from the picture on paper, because the rule that measures angles has changed.

### L2 human

Here is a typical problem: find all vectors of $\mathbb{R}^3$ orthogonal to $u = (1, 2, -1)$ for the dot product.
A vector $x$ is orthogonal to $u$ exactly when
$$x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 = 0.$$
This is one linear equation in three unknowns, so its solutions form a plane through $0$.
Solving for $x_3 = x_1 + 2x_2$ gives the basis $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 2)$ of that plane.
Check: $(1, 0, 1) \cdot u = 1 - 1 = 0$ and $(0, 1, 2) \cdot u = 2 - 2 = 0$.
The problem shows the usual use: split space into a direction and the part orthogonal to it.
Watch out: the two basis vectors are orthogonal to $u$, but not to each other, since their dot product is $2$.

### L3 human

Orthogonality is a symmetric relation defined by the inner product, and for a subset $S$ it gives the subspace $S^\perp$.
The map $S \mapsto S^\perp$ is the base of the theory of orthogonal complements and orthogonal projections in the next sub-area.
For a symmetric bilinear form that is not positive definite, a nonzero vector can be orthogonal to itself, as the light-like vectors of the Minkowski form are, and the sub-area on bilinear forms studies that case.
In analysis, orthogonal families of functions, such as the trigonometric functions or the [[orthogonal polynomials|8TXASARQN1]], are the base of series expansions.
