## Statement

Swapping the two factors of a cross product reverses its direction and keeps its length.
Let $a$ and $b$ be vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and let $\times$ be the [[cross product|0N5N15HWZ7]].
Then
$$b \times a = -(a \times b).$$
In particular, $a \times a = 0$ for each $a \in \mathbb{R}^3$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The statement has no hypothesis beyond $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
It rests on the coordinate formula of the cross product, which exists only for vectors with three components.

### What it gives you

The special case comes first.
With $b = a$ the statement reads $a \times a = -(a \times a)$, so $2(a \times a) = 0$ and $a \times a = 0$.
Combined with bilinearity, this kills every product of parallel vectors: if $b = sa$, then $a \times b = s(a \times a) = 0$.

The statement also shows that the cross product is not [[commutative|099E72KTF8]].
For example, $\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}$ but $\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{i} = -\mathbf{k}$.
Geometrically, $a \times b$ and $b \times a$ have the same length and opposite directions, so they sit on the same line through the origin.

### The converse

For a bilinear product over $\mathbb{R}$, the two properties "$b \times a = -(a \times b)$ for all $a, b$" and "$a \times a = 0$ for all $a$" are equivalent.
One direction is the special case above.
For the other, expand $(a + b) \times (a + b) = 0$ by bilinearity: the terms $a \times a$ and $b \times b$ vanish, which leaves $a \times b + b \times a = 0$.

### Beyond the cross product

Every Lie bracket is anticommutative, and the commutator $AB - BA$ of square matrices is a familiar example.
The exterior product of vectors satisfies $b \wedge a = -(a \wedge b)$ in every dimension.

## History

Anticommutativity appears in the multiplication rules $ij = k$ and $ji = -k$ that Hamilton found for quaternions in 1843.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If you swap the two factors of a cross product, the result points the other way.
Its length stays the same.
The right-hand rule shows this: swapping the factors turns the hand over.

### L2 human

Here is a check with $a = (1, 2, 3)$ and $b = (4, 5, 6)$.
In one order,
$$a \times b = (2 \cdot 6 - 3 \cdot 5, \; 3 \cdot 4 - 1 \cdot 6, \; 1 \cdot 5 - 2 \cdot 4) = (-3, 6, -3),$$
and in the other order,
$$b \times a = (5 \cdot 3 - 6 \cdot 2, \; 6 \cdot 1 - 4 \cdot 3, \; 4 \cdot 2 - 5 \cdot 1) = (3, -6, 3).$$
So $b \times a = -(a \times b)$, as the statement says.
The typical use is to simplify expressions, for instance $a \times b + b \times a = 0$.
Watch out: in a longer product, each swap of two neighboring factors changes the sign, and regrouping is not allowed at all.

### L3 human

Anticommutativity says that the cross product is an alternating bilinear map $\mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$.
Alternating bilinear maps on $V$ correspond to linear maps on $\Lambda^2 V$, which links the cross product to the [[exterior algebra|0MDMZ965VE]] and to the alternating forms of the sub-area on [[bilinear forms|DYAK7YMK7B]].
