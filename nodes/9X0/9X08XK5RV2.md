## Statement

In the system L, to derive "if $\varphi$ then $\psi$" it is enough to assume $\varphi$ and derive $\psi$.

Let L be the [[Hilbert system|1MGMQX43B8]] for formulas built from propositional variables with $\neg$ and $\to$.
Its axiom schemas are (A1) $\varphi \to (\psi \to \varphi)$, (A2) $(\varphi \to (\psi \to \chi)) \to ((\varphi \to \psi) \to (\varphi \to \chi))$ and (A3) $(\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi) \to ((\neg \psi \to \varphi) \to \psi)$.
Its only rule is [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] (MP).
For a set of formulas $\Gamma$, $\Gamma \vdash \psi$ means that some derivation in L from $\Gamma$ ends with $\psi$.

Let $\Gamma$ be a set of formulas, and let $\varphi$ and $\psi$ be formulas.
Then
$$\Gamma \cup \{ \varphi \} \vdash \psi \quad \text{if and only if} \quad \Gamma \vdash \varphi \to \psi.$$

## Remarks

### A first use

From $\{ p \to q, \ q \to r, \ p \}$ two steps of MP give $r$.
The theorem then gives $\{ p \to q, \ q \to r \} \vdash p \to r$.
Two more uses give $\vdash (p \to q) \to ((q \to r) \to (p \to r))$, as in [[the derivation of the transitivity law in L|M6ZAJFQ7E2]].
The proof is in [[the proof of the deduction theorem|6K76NTAEVF]].
The direction from right to left is one step of MP, and the direction from left to right is an induction on the lines of a derivation that uses only A1, A2 and MP.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Without A1 the theorem fails.
Take the three-valued matrix with values $0$, $1$, $u$, in which $a \to b = 1$ when $a = 0$ or $b = 1$, and $a \to b = 0$ otherwise, with $\neg 0 = 1$, $\neg 1 = 0$, $\neg u = u$.
In it A2 and A3 always take the value $1$, MP preserves the value $1$, but $u \to u = 0$.
So $p \to p$ is not derivable from A2, A3 and MP, although $\{ p \} \vdash p$ holds.

Without A2 the theorem fails too.
The same argument works for the matrix with $u \to 0 = 1$, $u \to 1 = 1$, $u \to u = 0$, $1 \to u = 0$, $\neg u = 1$, and the other values as before.
In that matrix A1 and A3 always take the value $1$, and again $u \to u = 0$.
These matrices are the ones used in [[independence of axioms|729RCBPAM3]].

The restriction to MP as the only rule is also needed.
In [[predicate logic|086QKPF0A6]] with the rule of generalization, $\{ P(x) \} \vdash \forall x \, P(x)$ holds.
But $P(x) \to \forall x \, P(x)$ is not valid, so it is not derivable in a sound system.

### What it gives you

Repeated use gives $\Gamma \cup \{ \varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n \} \vdash \psi$ if and only if $\Gamma \vdash \varphi_1 \to (\varphi_2 \to \cdots \to (\varphi_n \to \psi))$.
With $\Gamma$ empty, each derivation from finitely many hypotheses gives a theorem of L.
The theorem is the main tool in the proof of the [[basic theorems of L|Y060MF2NZ7]], T1 to T8 and the rule HS.
Through that lemma it is used in both proofs of the [[completeness theorem for propositional logic|81ZNY1XKYJ]].

### The converse and the semantic version

The statement is an equivalence, so its converse is part of it: from $\Gamma \vdash \varphi \to \psi$ we get $\Gamma \cup \{ \varphi \} \vdash \psi$ by adding the line $\varphi$ and one step of MP.
The semantic analogue also holds: $\Gamma \cup \{ \varphi \} \models \psi$ if and only if $\Gamma \models \varphi \to \psi$.
Here $\vdash$ is derivability, and it differs from the relation $\models$ of [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]], although the two agree for L by soundness and completeness.

### Generalizations

The same proof works for every Hilbert system whose only rule is MP and in which all instances of A1 and A2 are derivable.
Examples are the [[implicational propositional calculus|ZMVBAY7ZZD]] and the usual Hilbert systems for [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]].
For first-order logic the theorem holds when the derivation never applies generalization to a variable free in $\varphi$, in particular when $\varphi$ is a sentence; see [[the deduction theorem in predicate logic|8M9ZFDR4DH]].

## History

The deduction theorem is usually credited to Herbrand, who proved it in his thesis of 1930.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The theorem says that "assume $\varphi$ and prove $\psi$" is a correct way to prove "if $\varphi$ then $\psi$".
Mathematicians use this way of proving all the time.
A Hilbert system has no rule for making an assumption and later dropping it.
The theorem shows that the system can still do this: any proof that uses the assumption can be rewritten into a proof of "if $\varphi$ then $\psi$" without it.
For example, from "$p$ gives $q$", "$q$ gives $r$" and "$p$" we get $r$, so from the first two alone we get "$p$ gives $r$".

### L2 human

We use the theorem to show $\vdash \neg \varphi \to (\varphi \to \psi)$ in L.

1. From the hypotheses $\neg \varphi$ and $\varphi$, A1 and MP give $\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi$ and $\neg \psi \to \varphi$.
2. A3 and MP twice then give $\psi$, so $\{ \neg \varphi, \ \varphi \} \vdash \psi$.
3. The theorem with $\Gamma = \{ \neg \varphi \}$ gives $\{ \neg \varphi \} \vdash \varphi \to \psi$.
4. The theorem with $\Gamma = \emptyset$ gives $\vdash \neg \varphi \to (\varphi \to \psi)$.

The typical use is this pattern: put the antecedents among the hypotheses, derive the consequent, and then remove the hypotheses one at a time, the last one first.
Watch out for the order: the hypothesis removed first becomes the innermost antecedent.
From $\{ \neg \varphi, \ \varphi \} \vdash \psi$, removing $\varphi$ first gives $\neg \varphi \to (\varphi \to \psi)$, and removing $\neg \varphi$ first gives $\varphi \to (\neg \varphi \to \psi)$.
Also, do not use the theorem in a system with more rules, such as generalization, without checking its proof again.

### L3 human

The deduction theorem connects the turnstile $\vdash$, a relation between sets of formulas and formulas, with the connective $\to$ inside the [[object language|D9G8M42GKC]].
In [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]] the same principle is a primitive rule, implication introduction, and in the [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] it is the right rule for $\to$.
In a Hilbert system it is a metatheorem, and its proof is an algorithm that turns a derivation from $\Gamma \cup \{ \varphi \}$ into a derivation of $\varphi \to \psi$ from $\Gamma$.

Under the Curry-Howard correspondence, A1 and A2 are the types of the combinators K and S, and MP is application.
The proof of the deduction theorem is then the bracket abstraction algorithm, which removes a variable from a combinator term.
The derivation of $\varphi \to \varphi$ in five lines corresponds to the identity $I = S K K$.
