## Proof

We assume $p \land \neg p$, take it apart into $p$ and $\neg p$, and apply the second to the first to reach $\bot$.
We work in [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]] and read $\neg \varphi$ as "$\varphi$ implies the false statement $\bot$", as in Lean, where `Not p` is `p → False`.
So the rule for proving $\neg\varphi$ is: assume $\varphi$, derive $\bot$, and discharge the assumption.

1. Assume $p \land \neg p$.
   We want to prove the negation of this formula, so we assume the formula itself.
2. From step 1, infer $p$, by the elimination rule of [[conjunction|025YZN3VJW]] (`And.left` in Lean).
3. From step 1, infer $\neg p$, by the same rule (`And.right`).
4. From steps 2 and 3, infer $\bot$.
   The formula $\neg p$ means $p \to \bot$, and modus ponens with $p$ gives $\bot$.
5. Discharge the assumption of step 1 and conclude $\neg(p \land \neg p)$, by the introduction rule of [[negation|029JBBF693]].
6. The steps use only conjunction elimination, modus ponens and negation introduction.
   None of them is the law of excluded middle or double negation elimination, so the derivation is valid in [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]] as well as in classical logic. $\blacksquare$

## Idea

To refute a statement, assume it and derive the false statement.
The assumption $p \land \neg p$ hands you both $p$ and a way to turn $p$ into $\bot$.
Applying the second to the first gives $\bot$ at once, and that is the whole proof.

## Variants

In Lean the whole derivation is one term, `fun h => (And.right h) (And.left h)`: given a proof `h` of `p ∧ ¬p`, apply its second part to its first part.
The derivation never uses the rule "from $\bot$ infer anything", so it is also valid in minimal logic.
The [[proof by a truth table|82TG5066FF]] is shorter to check, but it gives the classical result only.
