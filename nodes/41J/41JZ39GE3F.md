## Summary

A defined word is a short name for a condition, and a proof has to work with the condition, not with the name.
Unpacking a definition means replacing each defined term in the hypotheses and in the goal by its defining condition, so that you can see what you are given and what you must show.

Here is a claim that gives you nothing to do until you unpack it: if $f \colon A \to B$ and $g \colon B \to C$ are [[injective|09NWETVTNY]], then $g \circ f$ is injective.
The definition of "injective" turns the goal into a statement with a form:

> for all $x, y \in A$, if $g(f(x)) = g(f(y))$, then $x = y$.

The givens unpack in the same way: $f(p) = f(q)$ implies $p = q$, and $g(u) = g(v)$ implies $u = v$.
Now the proof writes itself.

> Let $x, y \in A$ with $g(f(x)) = g(f(y))$.
> Since $g$ is injective, applied to $u = f(x)$ and $v = f(y)$, we get $f(x) = f(y)$.
> Since $f$ is injective, we get $x = y$.
> So $g \circ f$ is injective.
> $\square$

In general, you list the defined terms, write out each definition with fresh letters, and apply the [[moves for each logical form|872K5XB580]] to the result.
You repeat this for the new terms that appear, and you stop at terms whose needed properties are already known results.

## Motivation

A statement such as "a composition of injective functions is injective" names no equation, no inequality and no quantifier, and a student who stares at it sees nothing to do.
The [[definition|8ZCZ3HW91Z]] holds the hidden content.
Once the condition replaces the word, the goal has a form, and the form gives the first sentence of the proof.

This is why the first proofs of every new subject are short once the definitions are written out, and impossible to start before.
The same move opens the first proofs of group theory, of topology and of analysis, where "continuous" unpacks to the [[epsilon-delta definition of a limit|0GBZDAFPX8]].

## Notation

There is no special notation.
On a scratch sheet, many writers put the unpacked form next to the term, for example "$a \mid b$: $b = as$ for some integer $s$".
In the final proof the unpacking usually shrinks to a reason, such as "by the definition of divisibility, $b = as$ for an integer $s$".

## Remarks

### How to unpack

1. List the defined terms of the hypotheses and of the goal.
2. Write the definition of each term with new letters for its bound variables, keeping its [[quantifiers|EGTZPA6QH3]] and connectives.
3. Replace each term by its definition, and rename letters so that no letter has two meanings.
4. Apply the move for the outermost form of the goal, and use the givens by their forms.
5. When a new defined term appears, unpack it if the proof needs its condition, and stop if a known result about it is enough.

### Two worked unpackings

[[Divisibility|X1P97T4T04]] is the cleanest case.
The claim "if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$" has one defined term used three times.
Unpacked, the givens are $b = as$ and $c = bu$, and the goal is "$c = at$ for some integer $t$".
Substitution gives $c = (as)u = a(su)$, so $t = su$ works.

[["Strictly increasing" and "injective"|9R232GGWSB]] is a case with two different terms.
The given says that $x < y$ implies $f(x) < f(y)$, and the goal says that $f(x) = f(y)$ implies $x = y$.
After unpacking, the proof needs only one fact about the real numbers, trichotomy: two different numbers are in one order or the other.

The same move turns a [[set equality|MKHVRNW68H]] into "$x \in A$ if and only if $x \in B$", and it gives the templates for [[proving injectivity and surjectivity|DD2P85Y8E9]].

### When to stop

The stopping rule is the half of the method that students miss.
In [[a parity proof that unpacks too far|TB9PSFGFKZ]], the student unpacks "integer" into classes of pairs of natural numbers and proves the distributive law again.
Nothing in that text is false, but the three-line argument disappears under a page of foundations that the course has already proved.

The opposite error is to unpack too little.
Replacing "$n$ is even" by "$n$ is divisible by $2$" changes the words but still hides the condition "$n = 2k$ for an integer $k$".
And a "proof" that composed continuous functions are continuous "because both preserve closeness" unpacks nothing: it gives no $\varepsilon$ and no $\delta$.

### Facts about unpacking

Unpacking does not change the meaning of a statement, because a term and its definition are interchangeable.
An existential condition, such as "$m$ is even", unpacks to a witness, and two such givens need two letters, as [[one letter for two witnesses|FDT6332G8D]] shows.
A term with [[equivalent definitions|NZ42PS2P1A]] may be unpacked by whichever definition fits, once the equivalence is a known result.
A negated term needs the [[negation of its definition|K930V33BSC]]: "$f$ is not injective" unpacks to "there are $x \ne y$ with $f(x) = f(y)$".

### What unpacking cannot do

Unpacking gives the form of a proof, not its idea.
Full unpacking of "every bounded monotone sequence converges" gives a goal with $\varepsilon$ and $N$, but the proof also needs the completeness of the real numbers, through the [[supremum|0R3H5PANRQ]].
In advanced subjects the method can fail by excess.
A term such as "compact" has many known theorems, and a proof from the bare definition is often much longer than a proof from a theorem.

### Watch out

The most common slip is to unpack the givens with the letters of the goal.
In the proof above, a student who writes the given for $g$ as "$g(x) = g(y)$ implies $x = y$" now has two meanings for $x$, one in $A$ and one in $B$.
The repair is fresh letters for each definition, $u$ and $v$ for $g$, and then a deliberate choice $u = f(x)$, $v = f(y)$.

### History

"Go back to definitions" is one of the questions in the list of Pólya's "How to Solve It" (1945), under the stage of making a plan.
Transition textbooks such as Velleman's "How to Prove It" and Solow's "How to Read and Do Proofs" teach the method from their first chapter on proofs.

### Try it

Unpack "$A \subseteq B$" and write the first sentence of a proof of $A \cap C \subseteq B \cap C$, given $A \subseteq B$.
The goal unpacks to "for all $x$, if $x \in A \cap C$ then $x \in B \cap C$", so the proof begins "Let $x \in A \cap C$".

What does "$f$ is not surjective" unpack to, for $f \colon A \to B$?
There is $b \in B$ such that $f(a) \ne b$ for every $a \in A$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A defined word in mathematics is a short name for a longer condition.
"Even" is a short name for "two times a whole number".
To prove a claim with such words, we first write out the long conditions.
For example, to show that $6 + 10$ is even, we write $6 = 2 \times 3$ and $10 = 2 \times 5$, and then $6 + 10 = 2 \times 8$.
Without the long condition there is nothing to compute.
We stop writing out meanings when we reach facts we already know, such as the rules for adding whole numbers.

### L2 human

We prove by unpacking: if $f \colon A \to B$ and $g \colon B \to C$ are functions and $g \circ f$ is injective, then $f$ is injective.
The goal unpacks to "for all $x, y \in A$, if $f(x) = f(y)$ then $x = y$", so we let $x, y \in A$ with $f(x) = f(y)$.
The given unpacks to "for all $x, y \in A$, if $g(f(x)) = g(f(y))$ then $x = y$".
Applying $g$ to both sides of $f(x) = f(y)$ gives
$$g(f(x)) = g(f(y)),$$
and the given then yields $x = y$, so $f$ is injective.

We did not unpack "function" or "$\in$", since the proof needed only the conditions of injectivity.
Watch out: the claim "then $g$ is injective" is false, and unpacking shows why.
With $A = \{1\}$, $B = \{1, 2\}$, $C = \{1\}$ and $f(1) = 1$, the map $g \circ f$ is injective, but $g(1) = g(2)$.

### L3 human

In logic, unpacking replaces a defined symbol by its definiens, which an extension by definitions allows at every place.
In a proof assistant the same step is the unfolding of a definition, for example with `unfold` or `simp only` in Lean, and the system decides which unfoldings are definitional and which need a lemma.

The stopping rule has a structural meaning.
A subject builds an interface of results about each notion, and good proofs use the interface, not the construction.
This is why mathematics proves that two [[equivalent definitions|NZ42PS2P1A]] agree and then forgets which one came first.
In category theory the interface is often a universal property, and a proof unpacks the universal property of a product or a quotient instead of its construction.
A recursive definition, one of the [[kinds of definitions|B60DBNF77S]], unpacks to a base case and a rule, which leads to a proof by [[induction|0ASJVEFA9J]].
