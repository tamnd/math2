## Summary

Unpacking a definition is the method of replacing each defined term in a statement by its defining condition.
Then the logical form of what is given and what must be proved becomes visible.
The writer unpacks the hypotheses and the goal, and applies the [[strategies for each logical form|872K5XB580]].
The writer repeats the unpacking on the new terms that appear.
The process stops when the remaining terms have known properties that settle the goal.
The rule for stopping is: do not unpack a term whose needed properties are already proved results.
The method matters because most first proofs in a subject are short once the definitions are written out, and impossible to start before.

## Motivation

A statement such as "a composition of injective functions is injective" names no equation, no inequality and no quantifier.
A student who stares at it sees nothing to do.
The [[definition|8ZCZ3HW91Z]] of "injective" contains the hidden content: "for all $x$ and $y$, if $f(x) = f(y)$, then $x = y$".
Once this condition replaces the word, the goal has a form, and the form gives the first sentence of the proof.
Each defined term is an abbreviation of a condition, and a proof must work with the condition, not with the name.

## Remarks

### When it applies

1. The statement contains a defined term whose definition the reader knows, but no theorem about the term fits.
2. The proof is at the start of a subject, where only the definitions are available.
3. A proof is stuck, and the writer does not see the form of the goal.

### The steps

1. List the defined terms of the hypotheses and of the goal.
2. Write the definition of each term, with new letters for its bound variables. Use the [[quantifiers|EGTZPA6QH3]] and connectives of the definition.
3. Replace each term in the statement by its definition. Rename the letters so that no letter has two meanings.
4. Apply the strategy for the outermost form of the goal, and use the givens by their forms.
5. When a new defined term appears, decide whether to unpack it. Unpack it if the proof needs its condition. Stop if a known result about the term is enough.
6. When the goal is settled, write the final proof. It can name the definitions without writing them out, for example "by the definition of divisibility, $b = as$ for an integer $s$".

### Examples

1. [[Unpacking divisibility to prove that it is transitive|X1P97T4T04]]: three uses of "divides" become three equations, and the proof is one substitution.
2. [[Unpacking "strictly increasing" and "injective"|9R232GGWSB]]: two definitions with quantifiers give the frame of the proof, and trichotomy of the real numbers closes it.
3. [[Set equality by double inclusion|MKHVRNW68H]]: the goal $A = B$ unpacks, by the definition of equal sets, to $x \in A \iff x \in B$ for all $x$.
4. [[Proving injectivity and surjectivity|DD2P85Y8E9]]: each of the two words unpacks to a template with quantifiers, and the proof follows the template.
5. The limit $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ unpacks to the [[epsilon-delta definition of a limit|0GBZDAFPX8]], which gives the skeleton of an [[epsilon-delta proof|RXG5JTZ2PZ]].

### Non-examples

1. [[Unpacking too far in a parity proof|TB9PSFGFKZ]]: the writer unpacks "integer" into classes of pairs of natural numbers, and proves the distributive law again. The stopping rule fails.
2. A proof that the composition of continuous functions is continuous by "continuous functions preserve closeness, so the composition does too" does not unpack "continuous". It states an intuition, and it gives no $\varepsilon$ and no $\delta$.
3. Replacing "$n$ is even" by "$n$ is divisible by $2$" is a change of words, not an unpacking. The condition "there is an integer $k$ with $n = 2k$" is still hidden.

### Basic properties

1. Unpacking does not change the meaning of a statement. A term and its definition are interchangeable, by the nature of a [[mathematical definition|8ZCZ3HW91Z]].
2. A defined term in a hypothesis unpacks to a given, and in the goal to a goal. Both are needed: the given shows what to use, and the goal shows the form of the proof.
3. Unpacking an existential condition, such as "$m$ is even", introduces a witness. Two such givens need two new letters, as the page [[one letter for two existential witnesses|FDT6332G8D]] shows.
4. A term can have [[equivalent definitions|NZ42PS2P1A]]. The writer may unpack it by the definition that fits the goal, if the equivalence is a known result.
5. Unpacking a negated term needs the negation of its definition, as on the page [[negating mathematical statements|K930V33BSC]]. For example, "$f$ is not injective" unpacks to "there are $x \ne y$ with $f(x) = f(y)$".

### When it fails

Unpacking gives the form of a proof, not its idea.
Consider the claim "every bounded monotone sequence of real numbers converges".
Full unpacking gives a goal with $\varepsilon$ and $N$, but the proof needs the completeness of the real numbers.
It uses completeness in the form of the [[supremum|0R3H5PANRQ]].
In advanced subjects the method also fails by excess.
A term such as "compact" or "Noetherian" has many known theorems.
A proof from the bare definition is often much longer than a proof from a theorem.

### Related methods

1. [[Proof strategies by logical form|872K5XB580]]: the strategies act on the forms that unpacking shows.
2. [[Scratch work and the final proof|E97XXFW9MV]]: unpacking is a part of the scratch work, and the final proof often only names the definitions.
3. [[Writing proofs|8JQHJK94RZ]]: the final proof gives the definition as the reason of the step that uses it.
4. [[Kinds of definitions|B60DBNF77S]]: a recursive definition unpacks to a base case and a rule, which leads to a proof by [[mathematical induction|0ASJVEFA9J]].

### History

The advice to go back to the definitions is in the list of questions of Pólya's "How to Solve It".
It belongs to the stage of making a plan.
Transition-to-proof textbooks, such as those of Velleman and Solow, teach the method from the first chapter on proofs.
The word "unpacking" for the method is common in the teaching of proof, but the method itself is as old as definitions in mathematics.

### Common errors

1. The student unpacks the hypotheses and not the goal, and so does not see how the proof must start.
2. The student unpacks two existential conditions with one letter, which adds an assumption that the hypotheses do not give.
3. The student unpacks too far, into the foundations of the number system, and loses the argument under details that the course has already proved.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A defined word in mathematics is a short name for a longer condition.
"Even" is a short name for "two times a whole number".
To prove a claim with such words, we first write out the long conditions.
For example, to show that $6 + 10$ is even, we write $6 = 2 \times 3$ and $10 = 2 \times 5$, and then $6 + 10 = 2 \times 8$.
Without the long condition there is nothing to compute.
We stop writing out meanings when we reach facts we already know, such as the rules of adding whole numbers.

### L2 human

We prove by unpacking: if $f \colon A \to B$ and $g \colon B \to C$ are [[injective|09NWETVTNY]], then $g \circ f$ is injective.

1. Unpack the goal: for all $x, y \in A$, if $g(f(x)) = g(f(y))$, then $x = y$.
2. Unpack the givens. For $f$: for all $p, q \in A$, $f(p) = f(q)$ implies $p = q$. For $g$: for all $u, v \in B$, $g(u) = g(v)$ implies $u = v$.
3. Let $x, y \in A$ with $g(f(x)) = g(f(y))$.
4. Apply the given for $g$ with $u = f(x)$ and $v = f(y)$. It gives $f(x) = f(y)$.
5. Apply the given for $f$ with $p = x$ and $q = y$. It gives $x = y$. So $g \circ f$ is injective.
6. We did not unpack "function" or "$\in$". The proof needs only the conditions of injectivity.

The typical use of the method is the first proofs of every new subject, from set theory to group theory.
A trap is to unpack the givens in the variables of the goal, for example writing the given for $g$ with $x$ and $y$.
The letters then clash, and the writer applies the given to the wrong elements.

### L3 human

In logic, unpacking is the replacement of a defined symbol by its definiens, which a conservative extension by definitions allows at every place.
In a proof assistant the same step is the unfolding of a definition, for example with the tactics `unfold` or `simp only` in Lean.
The system decides what is "definitional" and what needs a lemma.
The stopping rule has a structural meaning.
A subject builds an interface of results about each notion, and good proofs use the interface, not the construction.
This is the reason why mathematics proves that two [[equivalent definitions|NZ42PS2P1A]] agree and then forgets which one was primary.
In category theory the interface is often a universal property.
A proof then unpacks the universal property instead of a construction, as in the treatment of products and quotients.
