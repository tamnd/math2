## Summary

Two teachers can follow the same syllabus and still teach different subjects.
One writes the proof that the angles of a triangle add to $180^\circ$ on the board and asks for it back on the test.
The other asks the class to measure triangles, guess the sum, and then find a reason that works for every triangle.
The first teacher treats mathematics as a finished body of results, and the second treats it as a human activity of inquiry.
Students learn that view together with the content.

Philosophy and mathematics in education studies the views of mathematics that teachers and students hold, and how those views shape teaching and learning.
A view of mathematics answers questions such as what mathematics is, where its certainty comes from, and what a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] is for.
Paul Ernest (1989) described three such views among teachers: the instrumentalist view, the Platonist view and the problem-solving view.

## Motivation

What a student counts as a proof depends on what the student thinks mathematics is.
A student who has only seen proofs to copy may decide that a proof is a ritual that the teacher wants, not a reason that settles a question.
Alan Schoenfeld reported a sharp case in 1988.
He observed a high school geometry class that was well taught by the usual standards, and its students did well on the state examination.
Yet when they faced construction problems, the same students did not use the theorems that they had proved, and they tested drawings by eye instead.
Schoenfeld concluded that the students saw proof and construction as two separate activities, and that the teaching itself had taught them this.

So research on teaching proof must ask which views of mathematics and of proof the teachers and the students hold, since those views decide what a lesson actually teaches.

## Notation

The three views carry Ernest's names.
Other authors speak of "beliefs about mathematics" or "conceptions of mathematics" for the same thing, and of a "static" or "dynamic" view for roughly the first two and the third.
These views are not the same as attitudes such as enjoyment or anxiety, which research treats under [[affect in mathematics learning|SAPATZCM0C]].

## Remarks

### Ernest's three views

The instrumentalist view sees mathematics as a set of facts and rules that are useful for other purposes.
A teacher with this view stresses procedures and their correct use, so a proof is one more procedure to learn.
The Platonist view sees mathematics as a fixed and unified body of knowledge that is discovered, not invented.
A teacher with this view stresses the structure of the subject, so a proof shows how a fact fits into that structure.
The problem-solving view sees mathematics as a growing field of human inquiry whose results stay open to revision.
A teacher with this view stresses investigation, conjecture and argument, so a proof is the end of a conversation that the class has had.

Ernest proposed the three views as a theoretical model of teachers' beliefs, and later studies used it to classify teachers.
The model describes; it does not rank the views.

### Proofs that explain

Gila Hanna (1990) separated proofs that only show that a result is true from proofs that also show why it is true.
She argued that in the classroom the explaining function should come first.
In a later review (2000) she argued that explanation and exploration matter in class as much as verification does, building on Michael de Villiers's list of the [[functions of proof|ENY0GKRJS8]].
Both papers are arguments from the literature and from classroom experience, not experiments.

Eric Knuth (2002) interviewed a small group of secondary school mathematics teachers in the United States about proof.
The teachers saw proof mainly as a way to establish that a result is true.
Few of them named explanation as a role of proof in their own classrooms.

### Lakatos in the classroom

Imre Lakatos wrote *Proofs and Refutations* as a classroom dialogue about Euler's formula for polyhedra.
The articles appeared in 1963 and 1964, and the book in 1976, after his death.
In the dialogue, a teacher and students propose a proof, meet counterexamples, and change both the proof and the definitions.
Teachers have used the book as a model for a class that works by conjecture and refutation, and it gave the problem-solving view a philosophical source.

### What belongs elsewhere

Whether mathematical objects exist independently of the mind is a question of the [[philosophy of mathematics|NTJ5XW0PVD]] itself.
It belongs here only when the question is how such a view acts in teaching.
In the same way, a survey of how much students enjoy mathematics studies affect, not views of what mathematics is.

### History

Philosophers of mathematics wrote little about teaching before the 1970s.
The work of Lakatos, and later the writing of Reuben Hersh, described mathematics as a human practice that grows by argument.
Mathematics educators used that description to argue for classrooms of conjecture and refutation.
Ernest published his model of teachers' beliefs in 1989, and his book *The Philosophy of Mathematics Education* appeared in 1991.

### Watch out

Do not assume that a teacher's stated view and the teacher's practice agree.
Studies of teachers' beliefs often report gaps between what a teacher says in an interview and what happens in that teacher's lessons.
Do not read Ernest's views as levels of quality either.
A clear lesson can come from the Platonist view, and a confused lesson can come from the problem-solving view.
Finally, do not assume that students share the teacher's view of proof.
A student may accept an argument only because the teacher gave it, which is an authority view of proof that the teacher never meant to teach.

### Try it

First, a teacher says: "Students must know the quadratic formula; the derivation is for the strong ones."
Which view does this suggest?
The formula is treated as a tool and its proof as optional, which fits the instrumentalist view.

Second, name one question that Knuth's interviews could not answer.
They recorded what teachers said about proof, so they cannot show how often those teachers used proofs to explain in class.

## Explanations

### L1 human

People have different ideas about what mathematics is.
Some think it is a list of rules, such as "to divide by a fraction, turn it upside down and multiply".
Others think it is a set of truths that exist and wait to be found.
Others think it is something people make by asking questions and checking ideas.
A teacher with the first idea asks students to learn the rule.
A teacher with the third idea asks them why it works, for example why $6 \div \frac{1}{2} = 12$: there are $12$ halves in $6$.
These ideas change what happens in a classroom.

### L2 human

Read one task through Ernest's three views: prove that the angles of a triangle add to $180^\circ$.

An instrumentalist lesson treats the task as a result to memorize.
Students copy the standard proof with the line through one corner parallel to the opposite side, and they reproduce it on the test.

A Platonist lesson treats the task as a window onto the structure of Euclidean geometry.
The teacher shows that the proof uses the [[parallel postulate|2PC3XY0PYZ]] and explains why the result depends on it.

A problem-solving lesson treats the task as a question.
Students measure triangles, conjecture the sum, look for a reason, and then ask what changes for a triangle drawn on a sphere, where the angles add to more than $180^\circ$.

All three lessons end with the same [[theorem|05MDKQBEAF]], but only the third makes students meet the functions of discovery and explanation as well as verification.
The typical use of the model is to describe a teacher or a curriculum and to explain why a reform succeeds or fails in practice.

### L3 human

Research on views of mathematics joins the philosophy of mathematics, the psychology of beliefs and the study of teaching practice.
Views of proof are a special case.
The [[proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]] of Harel and Sowder describe what convinces a student, and the teacher's view decides which proof schemes a classroom rewards.
The social view of proof in philosophy, in which a proof is an argument that convinces a qualified community, matches the classroom research on norms in [[reasoning and proof in the classroom|E77B813MT4]].
[[Rigor and the acceptance of proofs|1BTHSHST4M]] treats the same question for research mathematics.
