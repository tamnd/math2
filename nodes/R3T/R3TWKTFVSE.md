## Summary

A tipping point is a threshold beyond which a small further push causes a large change of state of a part of the climate.
The change is often abrupt and self-sustaining, and it may be hard or impossible to reverse.
Proposed examples include the collapse of the Atlantic overturning circulation and the loss of the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets.
Another is the dieback of the Amazon rainforest.
Mathematically, the simplest mechanism is a [[saddle-node bifurcation|MAP5J6HK0T]] at which a stable state disappears, as in the [[Stommel box model|JF9HFV4E4J]] and the [[Budyko–Sellers model|GEQJK8XJ4C]].
Theory predicts warning signs before such a bifurcation, such as slower recovery from small disturbances.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Push a glass slowly toward the edge of a table: for a long time nothing much happens, and then a tiny extra push sends it over.
Some parts of the climate may behave like that glass.
An ice sheet that melts low enough sits in warmer air and melts faster still; a weakened ocean current can switch off.
Once the change has happened, undoing the push does not bring the system back.
Scientists look for signs that a system is near such an edge, for example that it takes longer and longer to recover from small shocks.
