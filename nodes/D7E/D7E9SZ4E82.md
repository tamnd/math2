## Statement

A square matrix with a basis of eigenvectors factors as a change of basis, a diagonal stretch and the change back, and only such matrices factor this way.
Let $F$ be a field and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ [[square matrix|0JXZKX526R]] over $F$.

1. Existence. Suppose that $A$ has $n$ [[linearly independent|0CRMGPAH83]] [[eigenvectors|0N6960W0BV]] $q_1, \dots, q_n$ in $F^n$, with $A q_i = \lambda_i q_i$.
   Let $Q$ be the matrix with the columns $q_1, \dots, q_n$, and let $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ be the [[diagonal matrix|044JNC7ZAA]] of the [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]].
   Then $Q$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] and
$$A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}.$$
2. Converse. Let $A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}$ with $Q$ invertible and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$.
   Then the columns of $Q$ form a basis of $F^n$, and column $i$ is an eigenvector of $A$ for the eigenvalue $\lambda_i$.
3. Spectral sum. In the situation of part 1 or part 2, let $r_i$ be row $i$ of $Q^{-1}$ and let $E_i = q_i r_i$, an $n \times n$ matrix of rank one.
   Then
$$A = \lambda_1 E_1 + \dots + \lambda_n E_n, \qquad E_1 + \dots + E_n = I_n, \qquad E_i E_j = \begin{cases} E_i & \text{if } i = j, \\ 0 & \text{if } i \ne j. \end{cases}$$
4. Uniqueness. The diagonal entries of $\Lambda$ are the roots of the characteristic polynomial of $A$, each repeated as often as its algebraic multiplicity, so $\Lambda$ is unique up to the order of its entries.
   If the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ are distinct, then each $E_i$ is also independent of the choice of $Q$: it is the projection onto the line $F q_i$ along the span of the other eigenvectors.

## Remarks

### A worked decomposition

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.
The vector $q_1 = (1, 0)$ gives $A q_1 = (2, 0) = 2 q_1$, and the vector $q_2 = (1, 1)$ gives $A q_2 = (3, 3) = 3 q_2$.
So part 1 applies with
$$Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $Q \Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, and $Q \Lambda Q^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 + 3 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = A$.
The [[concept of eigendecomposition|D0AP2R7272]] and the [[diagonalization of a 2 × 2 matrix|306FBDECMB]] carry out the same steps for other matrices.

### Why it holds

The proof compares the two sides column by column.
Column $i$ of $AQ$ is $A q_i$, and column $i$ of $Q \Lambda$ is $\lambda_i q_i$, because $\Lambda$ scales column $i$ of $Q$ by $\lambda_i$.
So $A q_i = \lambda_i q_i$ for every $i$ is the same as the single matrix equation $AQ = Q\Lambda$.
For part 1, the columns of $Q$ are $n$ independent vectors in $F^n$, so $Q$ is invertible, and we multiply $AQ = Q \Lambda$ on the right by $Q^{-1}$.
For part 2, we multiply $A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}$ on the right by $Q$, and the columns of an invertible matrix are a basis, so each $q_i$ is nonzero.
For part 3, $Q^{-1} Q = I_n$ says $r_i q_j = 1$ for $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise, which gives $E_i E_j = q_i (r_i q_j) r_j$, and $Q Q^{-1} = I_n$ is the sum $\sum_i q_i r_i = I_n$.

### The spectral sum in the example

For the matrix above, $r_1 = (1, -1)$ and $r_2 = (0, 1)$, so
$$E_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $E_1 + E_2 = I_2$, and $2 E_1 + 3 E_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = A$.
The matrix $E_1$ sends $(x, y)$ to $(x - y, 0)$, so it keeps $q_1$ and kills $q_2$, as a projection onto $F q_1$ along $F q_2$ must do.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The $n$ independent eigenvectors cannot be dropped.
The [[shear|SBDF8APDF7]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the single eigenvalue $1$, and its eigenvectors are the nonzero multiples of $(1, 0)$, so no invertible $Q$ exists.
If it had a decomposition, part 4 would force $\Lambda = I_2$, and then $A = Q I_2 Q^{-1} = I_2$, which is false.

The field matters too.
The rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has no real eigenvalue, so it has no decomposition over $\mathbb{R}$.
Over $\mathbb{C}$ it has the eigenvector $(1, -i)$ for $i$ and $(1, i)$ for $-i$, and these give a decomposition with $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(i, -i)$.
Distinct eigenvalues are enough for part 1, by the theorem that [[n distinct eigenvalues imply diagonalizable|0Y95Z7MT02]], but they are not needed: the identity has one eigenvalue and the decomposition $I_n = I_n I_n I_n^{-1}$.

### What it gives you

Powers become powers of numbers: $A^k = Q \Lambda^k Q^{-1} = \lambda_1^k E_1 + \dots + \lambda_n^k E_n$, because $E_i E_j = 0$ for $i \ne j$ and $E_i^2 = E_i$.
For the example,
$$A^5 = 32 E_1 + 243 E_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 32 & 211 \\ 0 & 243 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check with $k = 2$: $4 E_1 + 9 E_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 5 \\ 0 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$, and direct multiplication gives $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 + 3 \\ 0 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$.
In the same way $p(A) = Q\, p(\Lambda)\, Q^{-1}$ for every polynomial $p$, and the [[powers of a diagonalizable matrix|0TNR9BQ871]] develop this.
The decomposition also reads off two invariants: $\det A = \lambda_1 \cdots \lambda_n$ and $\operatorname{tr} A = \lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_n$, here $6 = 2 \cdot 3$ and $5 = 2 + 3$.

### What is not unique

The matrix $Q$ is far from unique.
You may scale each column by a nonzero number, and you may reorder the columns if you reorder $\Lambda$ in the same way.
For a repeated eigenvalue there is more freedom: for $A = I_n$ every invertible $Q$ works, and the rank-one matrices $E_i$ change with $Q$.
What stays fixed is the sum of the $E_i$ over all $i$ with the same eigenvalue $\mu$: it is the projection onto the eigenspace of $\mu$ along the other eigenspaces.

### Watch out

The order of the factors matters.
The formula is $A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}$ when the eigenvectors are the columns of $Q$, and writing $Q^{-1} \Lambda Q$ instead gives a different matrix in general.
For the example, $Q^{-1} \Lambda Q = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \ne A$.
A second trap is to count eigenvalues instead of eigenvectors: the shear has the eigenvalue $1$ twice, but only one independent eigenvector.

### Generalizations

When $A$ is real symmetric, the [[real spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]] gives an orthogonal $Q$, so $Q^{-1} = Q^{\mathsf{T}}$ and the $E_i$ are orthogonal projections.
When $A$ is complex [[normal|0MR0BYF0K2]], the same holds with a unitary $Q$.
When eigenvectors are missing, the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]] replaces $\Lambda$ by a matrix with some entries $1$ above the diagonal, and the [[Schur decomposition|V1JG4C7R7B]] gives a triangular matrix with a unitary change of basis.

### Try it

First, decompose $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
The vectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ are eigenvectors for $3$ and $-1$, so $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(3, -1)$.

Second, does $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ have an eigendecomposition?
No: its only eigenvalue is $0$, so $\Lambda$ would be the zero matrix and $A = Q \cdot 0 \cdot Q^{-1} = 0$, which is false.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some matrices act in a very simple way once you look along the right directions.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ doubles every arrow along the $x$-axis and triples every arrow along the diagonal direction $(1, 1)$.
Every other arrow is a mix of these two, so the matrix doubles one part and triples the other part.
The eigendecomposition writes this down: switch to the two special directions, stretch each one, and switch back.

### L2 human

Here is a typical problem: solve the recursion $x_{k+1} = A x_k$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $x_0 = (0, 1)$.
First write the start in the eigenvectors: $x_0 = -q_1 + q_2$, since $-(1, 0) + (1, 1) = (0, 1)$.
Each eigenvector is only scaled by $A$, so after $k$ steps
$$x_k = -2^k q_1 + 3^k q_2 = (3^k - 2^k, \ 3^k).$$
Check with $k = 1$: $A x_0 = (1, 3)$, and the formula gives $(3 - 2, 3)$.
Check with $k = 2$: $A (1, 3) = (5, 9)$, and the formula gives $(9 - 4, 9)$.
For large $k$ the term $3^k q_2$ wins, so $x_k$ turns toward the direction of $(1, 1)$.
This is the typical use: the eigendecomposition turns a coupled recursion into separate geometric sequences, one for each eigenvalue.

### L3 human

The theorem is the matrix form of the statement that [[a matrix is diagonalizable if and only if it has a basis of eigenvectors|056SW1E0AC]], and the decomposition into the $E_i$ is the start of the functional calculus $f(A) = \sum_i f(\lambda_i) E_i$.
Over $\mathbb{C}$ the diagonalizable matrices are dense, since matrices with distinct eigenvalues are dense, but the defective ones cannot be ignored, because near them the eigenvectors become almost parallel and $Q$ becomes almost singular.
For normal operators the [[spectral decomposition|D5KVBA1K9R]] makes the $E_i$ orthogonal projections, and in infinite dimension this becomes the spectral theorem with a projection-valued measure.
For a matrix that is not square, or not diagonalizable, the [[singular value decomposition|000NPTMX6J]] gives a factorization with two orthogonal or unitary factors instead of one.
