## Proof

This is a sketch: the facts of cardinal arithmetic are cited, not proved.
We use the axiom of choice throughout.
We cite three facts about [[cardinals|0QKPNZKNEA]], from the Wikipedia section on cardinal arithmetic.

- (C1) If $\kappa$ and $\mu$ are cardinals, $\kappa$ is infinite and $\mu \ne 0$, then $\kappa \cdot \mu = \max(\kappa, \mu)$. A union of $\kappa$ sets with at most $\mu$ elements each has at most $\kappa \cdot \mu$ elements.
- (C2) An infinite set $C$ has exactly $\abs{C}$ finite subsets.
- (C3) $2^\kappa > \kappa$ for every cardinal $\kappa$. This is [[Cantor's theorem|0WTEMGYV2C]] with the [[cardinality of the power set|0GPFKS96T7]].

We first prove the general formula $\dim F^I = \abs{F^I}$ for an infinite set $I$, in four steps.

*Step A: the size of a space.*

1. Let $W$ be a vector space over $F$ with an infinite basis $C$, which exists by the theorem that [[every vector space has a basis|00MS9VMQ9Z]]. We show $\abs{W} = \max(\abs{F}, \abs{C})$.
2. Each $w \in W$ is $\sum_{c \in S} a_c c$ for one finite set $S \subseteq C$ and one function $S \to F$. So $W$ is the image of the union of the sets $F^S$ over finite $S \subseteq C$.
3. Each $F^S$ has at most $\max(\abs{F}, \aleph_0)$ elements, since $S$ is finite. By (C2), there are $\abs{C}$ sets $S$.
4. By (C1), $\abs{W} \le \abs{C} \cdot \max(\abs{F}, \aleph_0) = \max(\abs{C}, \abs{F})$, since $\abs{C} \ge \aleph_0$.
5. Conversely, $W$ contains $C$ and the line $Fc$ for one $c \in C$. So $\abs{W} \ge \max(\abs{C}, \abs{F})$.

*Step B: a lower bound by geometric sequences.*

6. Since $I$ is infinite, it contains a sequence $i_0, i_1, i_2, \dots$ of distinct elements.
7. For $a \in F$, let $g_a \in F^I$ be the function with $g_a(i_k) = a^k$ and $g_a = 0$ outside $\set{i_0, i_1, \dots}$.
8. Let $a_1, \dots, a_m \in F$ be distinct, and let $\sum_j \lambda_j g_{a_j} = 0$. The values at $i_0, \dots, i_{m-1}$ give $M \lambda = 0$, where $M = (a_j^k)_{k, j}$ is an $m \times m$ Vandermonde matrix.
9. By the [[Vandermonde determinant|0P84B554AS]], $\det M = \pm \prod_{j < l} (a_l - a_j) \ne 0$. So $\lambda = 0$.
10. Hence the family $(g_a)_{a \in F}$ is [[linearly independent|0CRMGPAH83]], and $\dim F^I \ge \abs{F}$.

*Step C: the formula for $F^I$.*

11. $F^I$ is infinite-dimensional, since it contains the independent functions $\delta_i$, $i \in I$, with $\delta_i(i) = 1$ and $\delta_i = 0$ elsewhere.
12. By step A, applied to $W = F^I$, $\abs{F^I} = \max(\abs{F}, \dim F^I)$.
13. By step B, $\dim F^I \ge \abs{F}$. So $\max(\abs{F}, \dim F^I) = \dim F^I$, and $\dim F^I = \abs{F^I}$.

*Step D: the dual space.*

14. Let $B$ be a basis of $V$. The map $f \mapsto f|_B$ is a linear isomorphism $V^* \to F^B$, by the [[theorem on maps determined by a basis|0R8X9XA83A]].
15. $B$ is infinite, so step 13 with $I = B$ gives $\dim V^* = \dim F^B = \abs{F^B} = \abs{F}^{\abs{B}}$.
16. Since $\abs{F} \ge 2$, we get $\abs{F}^{\abs{B}} \ge 2^{\abs{B}}$. By (C3), $2^{\abs{B}} > \abs{B} = \dim V$.
17. So $\dim V^* > \dim V$. Isomorphic spaces have equal dimension, so $V^* \not\cong V$.

## Idea

In infinite dimension, a space is not much larger as a set than its dimension: $\abs{W} = \max(\abs{F}, \dim W)$.
The dual $V^* \cong F^B$ is huge as a set, and it has at least $\abs{F}$ independent geometric sequences.
So its dimension must equal its size, which is $\abs{F}^{\abs{B}}$, and Cantor's theorem makes this larger than $\abs{B}$.

## Variants

- If $\abs{F} \le \dim V$, the inequality $\dim V^* > \dim V$ needs no step B. Suppose $\dim V^* \le \dim V$. Then step A gives $\abs{V^*} \le \max(\abs{F}, \dim V) = \dim V$, but $\abs{V^*} = \abs{F}^{\dim V} > \dim V$ by (C3).
- For a countable field and $\dim V = \aleph_0$, the proof is short: $V$ is countable by step A, $V^* \cong F^{\N}$ is uncountable, and a space of countable dimension over a countable field is countable.
