## Proof

We count the elements of $F^I$ in two ways, once as a set of functions and once through a basis, and compare.
This is a sketch: we cite three facts of cardinal arithmetic without proof, and we use the axiom of choice throughout.

- (C1) If $\kappa$ is an infinite [[cardinal|0QKPNZKNEA]] and $\mu \ne 0$, then $\kappa \cdot \mu = \max(\kappa, \mu)$, and a union of $\kappa$ sets with at most $\mu$ elements each has at most $\kappa \cdot \mu$ elements.
- (C2) An infinite set $C$ has exactly $\abs{C}$ finite subsets.
- (C3) $2^\kappa > \kappa$ for every cardinal $\kappa$, which is [[Cantor's theorem|0WTEMGYV2C]] with the [[cardinality of the power set|0GPFKS96T7]].

We first prove the general formula $\dim F^I = \abs{F^I}$ for an infinite set $I$, in steps A to C, and then apply it to $V^*$ in step D.

*Step A: the size of a space.*

1. Let $W$ be a vector space over $F$ with an infinite basis $C$, which exists by the theorem that [[every vector space has a basis|00MS9VMQ9Z]]; we show that $\abs{W} = \max(\abs{F}, \abs{C})$.
2. Each $w \in W$ is $\sum_{c \in S} a_c c$ for a finite set $S \subseteq C$ and a function $S \to F$, so $W$ is the image of the union of the sets $F^S$ over all finite $S \subseteq C$.
3. Each $F^S$ has $\abs{F}^{\abs{S}}$ elements, which is at most $\max(\abs{F}, \aleph_0)$ because $S$ is finite.
4. By (C2) there are $\abs{C}$ finite sets $S$, so (C1) gives $\abs{W} \le \abs{C} \cdot \max(\abs{F}, \aleph_0) = \max(\abs{C}, \abs{F})$, using $\abs{C} \ge \aleph_0$.
5. Conversely, $W$ contains the set $C$ and the line $Fc$ for any $c \in C$, so $\abs{W} \ge \max(\abs{C}, \abs{F})$.

*Step B: a lower bound by geometric sequences.*

6. Since $I$ is infinite, it contains a sequence $i_0, i_1, i_2, \dots$ of distinct elements.
7. For $a \in F$, let $g_a \in F^I$ be the function with $g_a(i_k) = a^k$ for each $k$ (with $a^0 = 1$) and $g_a = 0$ outside $\{i_0, i_1, \dots\}$.
8. Let $a_1, \dots, a_m \in F$ be distinct with $\sum_j \lambda_j g_{a_j} = 0$; the values at $i_0, \dots, i_{m-1}$ give $M \lambda = 0$ for the $m \times m$ Vandermonde matrix $M = (a_j^k)$.
9. By the [[Vandermonde determinant|0P84B554AS]], $\det M = \pm \prod_{j < l} (a_l - a_j)$, which is nonzero because the $a_j$ are distinct, so $\lambda = 0$.
10. Hence the family $(g_a)_{a \in F}$ is [[linearly independent|0CRMGPAH83]], and $\dim F^I \ge \abs{F}$.

*Step C: the formula for $F^I$.*

11. The space $F^I$ is infinite-dimensional, since the functions $\delta_i$ with $\delta_i(i) = 1$ and $\delta_i = 0$ elsewhere are independent for $i \in I$.
12. So step A applies to $W = F^I$ and gives $\abs{F^I} = \max(\abs{F}, \dim F^I)$.
13. By step B, $\dim F^I \ge \abs{F}$, so the maximum is $\dim F^I$, and $\dim F^I = \abs{F^I}$.

*Step D: the dual space.*

14. Let $B$ be a basis of $V$; the restriction $f \mapsto f|_B$ is a linear isomorphism $V^* \to F^B$ by the theorem that [[linear maps are determined by a basis|0R8X9XA83A]].
15. The set $B$ is infinite, so step 13 with $I = B$ gives $\dim V^* = \dim F^B = \abs{F^B} = \abs{F}^{\abs{B}}$.
16. Since $\abs{F} \ge 2$, we have $\abs{F}^{\abs{B}} \ge 2^{\abs{B}}$, and (C3) gives $2^{\abs{B}} > \abs{B} = \dim V$.
17. So $\dim V^* > \dim V$, and since isomorphic spaces have equal dimension, $V^* \not\cong V$.

## Idea

In infinite dimension, a space is not much larger as a set than its basis: step A gives $\abs{W} = \max(\abs{F}, \dim W)$.
Turn this around for $W = F^I$.
As a set of functions, $F^I$ is huge, so its dimension must be huge too, unless the field itself accounts for the size.
The geometric sequences rule out that escape, because they give $\abs{F}$ independent vectors.
So the dimension equals the size of the set, and Cantor's theorem makes that size larger than $\abs{B}$.

## Variants

When $\abs{F} \le \dim V$, the strict inequality $\dim V^* > \dim V$ needs no step B.
Suppose that $\dim V^* \le \dim V$.
Then step A gives $\abs{V^*} \le \max(\abs{F}, \dim V) = \dim V$.
But $\abs{V^*} = \abs{F}^{\dim V} \ge 2^{\dim V} > \dim V$ by (C3), which is a contradiction.
This argument gives the inequality but not the exact value of $\dim V^*$.

For a countable field and $\dim V = \aleph_0$, the argument is shorter still.
By step A, the space $V$ is countable, while $V^* \cong F^{\mathbb{N}}$ is uncountable by Cantor's diagonal argument.
A space of countable dimension over a countable field is countable, again by step A, so $V^*$ cannot have countable dimension.
