## Summary

A *projection* on a vector space $V$ is an [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]] $P \colon V \to V$ with $P^2 = P$.
Each projection splits $V$ as the [[direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] $V = \operatorname{im} P \oplus \ker P$ of its [[image|0WSFKKT6FF]] and its [[kernel|0S23E6YE83]], and it acts as the identity on the image.
Conversely, each decomposition $V = U \oplus W$ gives exactly one projection with image $U$ and kernel $W$.
So projections are the algebraic form of direct sum decompositions.

## Motivation

A decomposition $V = U \oplus W$ lets us split each vector as $v = u + w$.
The rule $v \mapsto u$ that keeps the $U$-part is a linear map, and applying it twice gives the same result as applying it once.
Projections are this rule seen as a linear map.
They turn statements about subspaces into statements about operators, which we can compose, add and represent by matrices.
For example, a shadow cast by parallel light rays on a plane is a projection of space onto the plane along the direction of the rays.

## Notation

The projection onto $U$ along $W$ is written $P_{U, W}$ or $P_U$ when $W$ is clear.
In Mathlib, a projection is an idempotent element of the ring of endomorphisms, `IsIdempotentElem P`, and `LinearMap.IsProj U P` says that $P$ maps into $U$ and fixes $U$.
Some books reserve "projection" for [[orthogonal projections|0B8KZRT28M]] and call the general notion an *oblique projection* or an *idempotent*.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[An oblique projection of the plane onto a line|DZXTTHGFQD]]: $P(x, y) = (x + y, 0)$ projects onto the $x$-axis along $\R(1, -1)$.
- [[The orthogonal projection onto the line spanned by (1, 1)|MAP4F54GJW]], with matrix $\tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
- The identity $I$ and the zero map $0$ are projections, onto $V$ and onto $\{0\}$.
- On functions $\R \to \R$, the map $f \mapsto \tfrac12 (f(x) + f(-x))$ is the projection onto the even functions along the odd functions, as in [[even and odd functions|YK0X90WB6H]].
- If $P$ is a projection, then so is $I - P$.

### Non-examples

- [[A nilpotent map is not a projection|1MX3PFN4BB]]: $N(x, y) = (y, 0)$ has $N^2 = 0 \ne N$.
- A rotation of the plane by $90°$ is not a projection: its square is the rotation by $180°$.
- The map $(x, y, z) \mapsto (x, y)$ from $\R^3$ to $\R^2$ is often called a projection, but it [[is not an endomorphism|VPN91Y1K18]], so it is not a projection in the sense of this page.

### Basic properties

- The [[theorem on projections and decompositions|2ZX5Q7V4VN]]: $V = \operatorname{im} P \oplus \ker P$, $P$ is the identity on $\operatorname{im} P$, and $P \mapsto (\operatorname{im} P, \ker P)$ is a bijection from projections onto pairs $(U, W)$ with $V = U \oplus W$.
- $I - P$ is the projection with image $\ker P$ and kernel $\operatorname{im} P$.
- The only possible eigenvalues of a projection are $0$ and $1$. A projection is diagonalizable, with the $1$-eigenspace $\operatorname{im} P$ and the $0$-eigenspace $\ker P$.
- In a basis adapted to $V = \operatorname{im} P \oplus \ker P$, the matrix of $P$ is $\begin{pmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ with $r = \operatorname{rank} P$. So $\operatorname{tr} P = r \cdot 1$ in $F$.
- Projections $P_1, \dots, P_k$ with $P_i P_j = 0$ for $i \ne j$ and $P_1 + \cdots + P_k = I$ correspond to decompositions $V = U_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_k$.

### History

Projections in this algebraic sense were studied with the spectral theory of operators in the early twentieth century, in the work of Hilbert, von Neumann and Riesz.
The word comes from projective and descriptive geometry, where a figure is projected onto a plane along fixed lines.

### Common errors

- Assuming that a projection is orthogonal. The kernel of a projection need not be perpendicular to its image, as for $P(x, y) = (x + y, 0)$.
- Assuming that the image determines the projection. The projections of $\R^2$ onto the $x$-axis correspond to the choices of a kernel, one for each complement.
- Thinking that a projection is injective or invertible. The only invertible projection is $I$, since $P^2 = P$ and $P$ invertible give $P = I$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A projection pushes every vector onto a subspace along a fixed set of directions.
A vector already in the subspace does not move.
So projecting twice gives the same result as projecting once.

### L2 human

To find the projection of $\R^2$ onto $U = \R(1, 0)$ along $W = \R(1, 2)$, decompose a vector.

1. Write $(x, y) = a(1, 0) + b(1, 2)$. Then $b = y / 2$ and $a = x - y / 2$.
2. Keep the $U$-part: $P(x, y) = (x - y/2, 0)$.
3. The matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1/2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and its square is itself.
4. The image is $U$, and the kernel is $\{(x, y) : x = y/2\} = W$.

The trap is to use the formula of the orthogonal projection, $(x, y) \mapsto (x, 0)$, which has kernel $\R(0, 1)$ instead of $W$.

### L3 human

In the ring $\operatorname{End}(V)$, projections are the idempotents, and decompositions of $V$ correspond to complete families of orthogonal idempotents.
For modules over a ring, a submodule is a direct summand exactly when it is the image of an idempotent endomorphism.
In Hilbert spaces the orthogonal projections are the self-adjoint idempotents, and they are the building blocks of the spectral theorem.
In functional analysis, the Riesz projector $\frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint (z - T)^{-1} \, dz$ gives the projection onto a spectral subspace.
