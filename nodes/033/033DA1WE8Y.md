## Summary

A projection is a linear map that pushes every vector onto a subspace along a fixed set of directions, and then leaves it there.
Doing it twice is the same as doing it once.
Precisely, a *projection* on a vector space $V$ is an [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]] $P \colon V \to V$ with
$$P^2 = P.$$
Each projection splits $V$ as the [[direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] $V = \operatorname{im} P \oplus \ker P$ of its [[image|0WSFKKT6FF]] and its [[kernel|0S23E6YE83]], and it acts as the identity on its image.
Conversely, each decomposition $V = U \oplus W$ gives exactly one projection with image $U$ and kernel $W$, so projections and direct sum decompositions are the same thing.

## Motivation

A decomposition $V = U \oplus W$ splits each vector as $v = u + w$ in one way.
The rule $v \mapsto u$ that keeps the $U$-part is linear, and applying it a second time changes nothing, since $u$ already lies in $U$.
A projection is this rule, seen as a linear map.
The point of the change of view is that maps can be composed, added and written as matrices, while subspaces cannot.
So questions about decompositions turn into algebra with the equation $P^2 = P$.

Projections appear wherever we keep one part of a vector and forget another.
A shadow cast by sunlight on flat ground is a projection of space onto the ground plane along the direction of the rays.
In statistics and numerical work, a least squares fit is the [[orthogonal projection|0B8KZRT28M]] of the data vector onto the space of fitted vectors.
Later in the area, projections return in the trace (over $\R$ the trace of a projection is its rank), in diagonalization, and in the spectral theorem, which writes a symmetric matrix as a combination of orthogonal projections.

## Notation

The projection onto $U$ along $W$ is written $P_{U, W}$, or $P_U$ when the second subspace is clear.
Some books reserve the word "projection" for [[orthogonal projections|0B8KZRT28M]], and they call the general notion an *oblique projection* or an *idempotent*.
In Mathlib a projection is an idempotent element of the ring of endomorphisms, `IsIdempotentElem P`, and `LinearMap.IsProj U P` says that $P$ maps into $U$ and fixes $U$.
The word is also used for maps such as $(x, y, z) \mapsto (x, y)$ from $\R^3$ to $\R^2$, but those are not endomorphisms; see [[the projection from R³ to R²|VPN91Y1K18]].

## Remarks

### A first projection

Let $P(x, y) = (x + y, 0)$ on $\R^2$.
Apply it twice:
$$P(P(x, y)) = P(x + y, 0) = (x + y + 0, 0) = P(x, y).$$
So $P^2 = P$, and $P$ is a projection.
Its image is the $x$-axis, and its kernel is the line $x + y = 0$, which is $\R(1, -1)$.
The vector $(3, 1)$ splits as
$$(3, 1) = (4, 0) + (-1, 1),$$
with $(4, 0) = P(3, 1)$ on the $x$-axis and $(-1, 1)$ in the kernel.
The kernel is not perpendicular to the image, so $P$ is an *oblique* projection; [[an oblique projection of the plane onto a line|DZXTTHGFQD]] studies it.

### Image, kernel and the other projection

Why does every projection split the space?
Write each vector as
$$v = P v + (v - P v).$$
The first term lies in the image, and the second lies in the kernel, because $P(v - Pv) = Pv - P^2 v = 0$.
A vector in both the image and the kernel satisfies $v = Pv = 0$, so the sum is direct.
This is the first half of the [[theorem on projections and decompositions|2ZX5Q7V4VN]].

The second term of the splitting is itself a projection.
Since $(I - P)^2 = I - 2P + P^2 = I - P$, the map $I - P$ is the projection with image $\ker P$ and kernel $\operatorname{im} P$.
For $P(x, y) = (x + y, 0)$ above, $(I - P)(x, y) = (-y, y)$.

### Oblique and orthogonal

On a space with an inner product, a projection is *orthogonal* when its kernel is perpendicular to its image.
The [[orthogonal projection onto the line spanned by (1, 1)|MAP4F54GJW]] has matrix
$$\frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
and its kernel $\R(1, -1)$ is perpendicular to its image $\R(1, 1)$.
Every subspace has exactly one orthogonal projection onto it, but it has one projection for each complement.
The projections of $\R^2$ onto the $x$-axis are the maps $(x, y) \mapsto (x - m y, 0)$, with kernel $\R(m, 1)$, and only $m = 0$ gives the orthogonal one.

### Eigenvalues and matrices

A projection is the simplest diagonalizable map.
If $Pv = \lambda v$ with $v \ne 0$, then $\lambda v = P^2 v = \lambda^2 v$, so $\lambda^2 = \lambda$ and $\lambda$ is $0$ or $1$.
The image is the eigenspace for $1$ and the kernel is the eigenspace for $0$.
In a basis that lists a basis of the image first and then a basis of the kernel, the matrix of $P$ is
$$\begin{pmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad r = \operatorname{rank} P,$$
so the [[trace|0KZDNGA561]] of $P$ is $r \cdot 1$ in $F$.

More generally, projections $P_1, \dots, P_k$ with $P_i P_j = 0$ for $i \ne j$ and $P_1 + \cdots + P_k = I$ correspond to decompositions $V = U_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_k$, with $U_i = \operatorname{im} P_i$.

### What is not a projection

The [[nilpotent map|1MX3PFN4BB]] $N(x, y) = (y, 0)$ has $N^2 = 0 \ne N$, and its image and kernel are the same line.
A rotation of the plane by $90$ degrees is not a projection either, since its square is the rotation by $180$ degrees.
Neither has the eigenvalue pattern of a projection: $N$ has only the eigenvalue $0$ but is not $0$, and the rotation has no real eigenvalue at all.

### Watch out

Do not assume that a projection is orthogonal.
The map $(x, y) \mapsto (x + y, 0)$ is a projection, but its kernel $\R(1, -1)$ is not perpendicular to its image.

Do not assume that the image determines the projection.
The maps $(x, y) \mapsto (x, 0)$ and $(x, y) \mapsto (x + y, 0)$ both have image the $x$-axis, but they send $(0, 1)$ to $(0, 0)$ and to $(1, 0)$.

A projection other than $I$ is never invertible.
If $P$ is invertible, then multiplying $P^2 = P$ by $P^{-1}$ gives $P = I$.

### History

The name comes from geometry.
A parallel projection of space onto a plane, such as a shadow in sunlight, moves each point along a fixed direction until it meets the plane, and this is a projection in the sense above.
In ring theory the elements with $e^2 = e$ are called idempotents, a word introduced by Benjamin Peirce.

### Try it

First, is the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ a projection, and what are its image and kernel?
Its square is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ again, so yes; the image is $\R(1, 1)$ and the kernel is the $y$-axis.

Second, find the projection of $\R^2$ onto the $y$-axis along $\R(1, 1)$.
Write $(x, y) = x(1, 1) + (0, y - x)$, so the projection is $(x, y) \mapsto (0, y - x)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of the sun shining at a slant onto flat ground.
Each point of an object casts a shadow on the ground, and a point that is already on the ground is its own shadow.
So casting the shadow of a shadow gives the same shadow.
A projection does this in any vector space: it moves each vector along a fixed direction onto a subspace, and it leaves the subspace in place.

### L2 human

Here is how to find the projection of $\R^2$ onto $U = \R(1, 0)$ along $W = \R(1, 2)$.
Write $(x, y) = a(1, 0) + b(1, 2)$ and solve: the second entry gives $b = y/2$, and the first gives $a = x - y/2$.
Keep the $U$-part:
$$P(x, y) = \left(x - \tfrac{y}{2}, 0\right), \qquad [P] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\tfrac12 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: the square of the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + (-\tfrac12) \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot (-\tfrac12) + (-\tfrac12) \cdot 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = [P]$.
The kernel is the line $x = y/2$, which is $W$, as it should be.
The typical use is exactly this: a decomposition is given, and we need a formula for one of its parts.
The trap is to use the orthogonal formula $(x, y) \mapsto (x, 0)$, which has kernel $\R(0, 1)$ instead of $W$.

### L3 human

In the ring $\operatorname{End}(V)$, projections are the [[idempotents|0N5NJZKKN3]], and decompositions $V = U_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_k$ correspond to complete families of orthogonal idempotents.
For modules over a ring, a submodule is a direct summand exactly when it is the image of an idempotent endomorphism, and this is the usual way to study direct summands.
In a Hilbert space the orthogonal projections are the self-adjoint idempotents, and they are the building blocks of the spectral theorem.
In operator theory, the Riesz projection $\frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma (z - T)^{-1} \, dz$ is the idempotent onto the part of the spectrum of $T$ inside the curve $\gamma$.
