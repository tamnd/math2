## Summary

The history of mathematical logic is the story of how the study of valid reasoning became a branch of mathematics.
It starts with Aristotle's [[syllogistic|MZBNX2T6Q6]] and the propositional logic of the Stoics in ancient Greece.
In the nineteenth century Boole, De Morgan, Frege and Peirce turned logic into a calculus with symbols and exact rules.
In the early twentieth century logic became the tool of the [[foundations of mathematics|EFFRJFW67Y]], and in the 1930s Gödel, Tarski, Gentzen, Church and Turing proved its main theorems.
Since then the subject has grown into model theory, set theory, computability theory and proof theory, with many links to computer science.
The history matters because it explains why the field has its present shape and its present notation.

## Motivation

A student of logic meets four branches, two kinds of consequence, several proof systems and many logics.
The reasons for this variety are historical.
Each branch began as an answer to a definite problem: the paradoxes of set theory, the consistency of arithmetic, the meaning of "true", and the meaning of "computable".
Knowing these problems makes the definitions of the later sub-areas easier to understand.

## Notation

Much of the notation of logic has a known origin.
The turnstile $\vdash$ comes from Frege's *Begriffsschrift* of 1879.
The membership sign $\in$ and the reversed letter $\exists$ come from Giuseppe Peano.
Gerhard Gentzen introduced the turned letter $\forall$ for the universal quantifier in his work of 1934 and 1935.
Older books, such as *Principia Mathematica*, write $\supset$ for implication and use dots instead of brackets.
The page [[propositions, connectives and quantifiers|PM1AYWABA7]] lists the symbols that are in use today.

## Remarks

### Ancient logic

Aristotle (384 to 322 BC) wrote the first systematic study of valid inference, in the work now called the *Prior Analytics*.
His [[syllogistic|MZBNX2T6Q6]] studies arguments with two premises and a conclusion, each of the form "all $S$ are $P$", "no $S$ is $P$", "some $S$ is $P$" or "some $S$ is not $P$".
He used letters for the terms, so the theory is about forms of arguments and not about their content.
The Stoics, above all Chrysippus in the third century BC, studied a logic of whole statements joined by "if", "and" and "or".
Their five basic argument forms include the forms now called [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] and modus tollens.
Philo of Megara gave a truth condition for "if $p$ then $q$" that is the same as the modern [[material implication|0NMG7W7Q11]].

### From Leibniz to Boole

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz proposed a universal symbolic language and a calculus of reasoning, so that disputes could be settled by calculation.
He did not complete this project, and most of his logical papers were published long after his death.
In 1847 George Boole published *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic*, and in 1854 *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought*.
Boole treated classes and propositions with algebraic equations, and this work led to [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]].
In the same year 1847 Augustus De Morgan published *Formal Logic*, and he studied the logic of relations, which syllogistic cannot express.
His name is attached to [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]].

### Frege, Peirce and Peano

Gottlob Frege's *Begriffsschrift* of 1879 contains the first formal language with quantifiers and variables, and a formal system of proof for it.
With it Frege could express statements such as "every number has a successor", which mix relations and quantifiers.
Charles Sanders Peirce and his students developed a notation for quantifiers independently in the 1880s, and Peirce later invented the diagrammatic [[existential graphs|D3ZS6WHPM1]].
Giuseppe Peano gave his [[axioms for arithmetic|0QFT7Z76N5]] in 1889, in a symbolic notation that Russell later adopted.
Frege tried to derive arithmetic from logic in his *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* (1893 and 1903).
In 1902 Bertrand Russell wrote to him that his system implies a contradiction, now known as [[Russell's paradox|0HESMMSYEG]].

### Principia Mathematica and the Hilbert school

Alfred North Whitehead and Bertrand Russell published *Principia Mathematica* in three volumes from 1910 to 1913.
It derived a large part of mathematics in a formal system based on a theory of types, in the spirit of [[logicism|5PGSQYB5Y1]].
David Hilbert and his school, with Paul Bernays and Wilhelm Ackermann, made the formal systems themselves an object of mathematical study.
[[Hilbert's program|P1RW6JWX73]] asked for finitary proofs that these systems are consistent.
The book of Hilbert and Ackermann of 1928 stated two open problems: the completeness of first-order logic and the decision problem for first-order logic.
In the same period Leopold Löwenheim (1915) and Thoralf Skolem (1920) proved the first theorems about the models of first-order formulas.
Emil Post proved in 1921 that the propositional calculus of *Principia* is complete for the truth-table semantics.

### The 1930s

Kurt Gödel proved the [[completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]] for first-order logic in 1929 and published it in 1930.
In 1931 he proved the [[incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]], which ended the original form of Hilbert's program.
Alfred Tarski gave a mathematical definition of truth for formal languages in 1933, and showed that truth in arithmetic is not definable in arithmetic itself, see [[Tarski's undefinability theorem|YRV6SYK2BY]].
Gerhard Gentzen introduced [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]] and the [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] in 1934 and 1935, and in 1936 he proved the consistency of arithmetic, see [[Gentzen's consistency proof|QPV0HNP22S]].
In 1936 Alonzo Church and Alan Turing showed, independently, that the decision problem for first-order logic has no algorithmic solution, see [[Church's theorem|VA6BNNY2YC]].
For this purpose Church used the [[lambda calculus|BD756KR8FM]] and Turing defined the [[Turing machine|758EYCN27B]].
In 1938 Gödel proved that the axiom of choice and the continuum hypothesis are consistent with the other axioms of set theory.

### Later growth

After 1940 the field divided into four main branches: [[model theory|F3AXVABC35]], [[set theory|G9W9J6KPAR]], [[computability theory|ZNC3817RA6]] and [[proof theory|N4EDJNMTEF]].
Paul Cohen invented [[forcing|F2DTWW9HP7]] in 1963 and proved that the continuum hypothesis is independent of ZFC.
In the late 1950s and the 1960s Saul Kripke and others gave possible-world semantics for [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]] and [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]].
After this work, non-classical logics became a large part of the field.
Abraham Robinson created [[nonstandard analysis|G00EDC2ET4]] in the 1960s with the tools of model theory.
In 1970 Yuri Matiyasevich completed the negative solution of [[Hilbert's tenth problem|X8TVJSVTD4]].
In 1977 Jeff Paris and Leo Harrington found a [[natural statement of combinatorics|509ZWA0R26]] that is true but not provable in Peano arithmetic.
Logic also became a basic science of computing: the [[P versus NP problem|MFN5GYZH5Z]], [[automated theorem proving|A51M1KK9DJ]], type systems of programming languages and [[proof assistants|YM6PRDE88V]] all come from it.

### Examples

- The argument "all men are mortal, Socrates is a man, so Socrates is mortal" is the standard example of Aristotelian logic, although the second premise is about an individual and not a class.
- The argument "if it is day, it is light; it is day; so it is light" is the standard Stoic example of the first indemonstrable form, modus ponens.
- The statement "every number has a larger number" is a typical example of Frege's logic: it has two quantifiers and a relation, and syllogistic cannot express its logical form.
- The Gödel sentence of a theory is the typical example of the 1930s: a statement that the theory can express but cannot decide.

### Non-examples

- The study of persuasion and of informal fallacies, in rhetoric and law, is part of the history of logic in the wide sense, but not of mathematical logic. The [[fallacies in proofs|T6QQ749DM5]] of mathematics are treated in reasoning and proof.
- Euclid's *Elements* is a model of deductive proof, but it does not study the rules of inference themselves, so it belongs to the history of geometry.

### Basic properties

- Each major result of the 1930s answered a question that was stated precisely in the 1920s, mostly by the Hilbert school.
- The negative results of the period (incompleteness, undefinability of truth, undecidability) all use a form of self-reference, which goes back to the liar paradox of antiquity.
- The positive results (completeness, compactness, cut elimination) all concern first-order logic, which became the standard logic of mathematics for this reason.

### History

The history of logic itself became a field of study in the twentieth century.
Jean van Heijenoort's anthology *From Frege to Gödel* (1967) collected the main papers from 1879 to 1931 in English.
The [[history of mathematics|EJZNJJ6M7S]] treats the history of logic in more detail than this page.

### Common errors

- Boole did not use the symbols of modern logic. He wrote logic with the ordinary symbols of algebra, such as $x y$ and $x + y$, and his $+$ was defined only for disjoint classes.
- Russell did not destroy logic with his paradox. He showed that one axiom of Frege's system, unrestricted comprehension, leads to a contradiction, and the type theory of *Principia* and Zermelo's axioms both avoid it.
- Gödel's incompleteness theorems do not contradict his completeness theorem. Completeness says that first-order proof captures truth in all models, and incompleteness says that no consistent effective theory of arithmetic decides every sentence.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Logic is the study of correct reasoning, and it is more than two thousand years old.
Aristotle studied arguments such as "all dogs are animals, all animals breathe, so all dogs breathe".
He noticed that the argument stays correct when the words "dogs", "animals" and "breathe" are replaced by any other words of the same kind.
In the 1800s Boole and Frege wrote logic with symbols, as in algebra.
In the 1930s Gödel, Turing and others used this symbolic logic to prove exact facts about what proofs and computers can do and cannot do.
For example, Turing showed that no computer program can decide, for every logical statement, whether it is a law of logic.

### L2 human

We compare the logic of Aristotle with the logic of Frege on one statement.
The statement is: "if every number has a larger number, then there is no largest number".

1. Syllogistic can only say "all $S$ are $P$" and its three relatives, where $S$ and $P$ are classes. The phrase "has a larger number" relates two numbers, so it is not a class term with a fixed meaning.
2. In Frege's logic we use a relation symbol $L(x, y)$ for "$y$ is larger than $x$", and the quantifiers $\forall$ and $\exists$.
3. "Every number has a larger number" becomes $\forall x \, \exists y \, L(x, y)$.
4. "There is a largest number" becomes $\exists x \, \forall y \, \neg L(x, y)$, that is, some $x$ has no number larger than it.
5. To prove the statement, assume $\forall x \, \exists y \, L(x, y)$ and also $\exists x \, \forall y \, \neg L(x, y)$.
6. Take an $x_0$ with $\forall y \, \neg L(x_0, y)$, by the second assumption.
7. The first assumption with $x = x_0$ gives some $y_0$ with $L(x_0, y_0)$.
8. Step 6 with $y = y_0$ gives $\neg L(x_0, y_0)$, which contradicts step 7.
9. So the assumption of step 5 is false, and the implication holds.

The typical use of this comparison is to see why quantifiers and relations were the main advance of the nineteenth century.
The proof works for every relation $L$, so it is a law of logic, and its form can be checked by a machine.
A trap is to think that Frege's logic is just syllogistic with symbols: it can express the nesting $\forall x \, \exists y$, which syllogistic cannot.

### L3 human

The history shows a steady move from logic as a theory of correct thought to logic as the mathematics of formal systems.
Boole made logic algebraic, Frege made it a formal language with a proof system, Hilbert made the formal system a mathematical object, and Gödel, Tarski, Church and Turing proved theorems about such objects.
The distinction between a formal system and its [[metalanguage|D9G8M42GKC]], which the Hilbert school made precise, is what made these theorems possible.

The later history follows the four classical branches, each of which has its own area in math2: [[model theory|F3AXVABC35]], [[set theory|G9W9J6KPAR]], [[computability theory|ZNC3817RA6]] and the proof theory in [[this area|DYJ0KGTPMR]].
Since the 1970s two further lines have grown: the many [[non-classical logics|NJB94B39QK]] and the links with computer science, from the Curry-Howard correspondence to [[proof assistants|YM6PRDE88V]] that check large parts of mathematics.
A reader who wants the original papers can start with van Heijenoort's anthology for the period up to 1931.
