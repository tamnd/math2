## Statement

Let a deformable body swim in an unbounded fluid at rest at infinity.
Assume that at each instant the fluid motion is a [[Stokes flow|13CB7V2YM4]] with the [[no-slip condition|DF0M8YNQ3D]] on the body.
Assume also that no external force or torque acts on the body.
Let $\gamma$ be a smooth curve in the space of shapes, and let $\theta \colon [0, T] \to \R$ be piecewise continuously differentiable with $\theta(0) = \theta(T)$.
Let the shape of the body at time $t \in [0, T]$ be $\gamma(\theta(t))$.
Then the position and orientation of the body at time $T$ are the same as at time $0$.

Such a stroke is called *reciprocal*: the body goes through a sequence of shapes and then back through the same sequence in reverse.
A stroke that uses only one degree of freedom, such as the opening and closing of a hinge, is always reciprocal.

## Remarks

The Stokes equations have no time derivative and are linear.
So the rigid velocity of the body at an instant is a linear function of the rate of change of its shape.
The coefficients depend only on the current shape.
For a stroke $\gamma(\theta(t))$, the equation for the rigid motion does not change under reparametrisation of time.
So the position at time $t$ is a function of $\theta(t)$ alone.
Since $\theta(T) = \theta(0)$, the net motion is zero, no matter how fast the two halves of the stroke are.
A scallop that opens slowly and shuts quickly would move at high Reynolds number, but not at zero Reynolds number.
Purcell's three-link swimmer, with two hinges moved out of phase, traces a loop in a two-dimensional shape space and does move.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A tiny swimmer, like a bacterium, lives in a world where water feels as thick as honey and there is no coasting.
If it just opens and closes like a clam, it moves forward and then exactly back, getting nowhere, however it times the motion.
To swim, it must change shape in a way that is not the same backwards and forwards, like a rotating corkscrew or a beating flexible tail.

### L2 human

The theorem was stated by E. M. Purcell in his 1977 lecture "Life at low Reynolds number".
In the language of geometry, the net motion of a cyclic stroke is the holonomy of a connection on shape space, and a reciprocal stroke encloses no area.
For small strokes the net displacement is to leading order given by the curvature of this connection times the enclosed area.
The conclusion can fail when inertia matters, or in a fluid that is not Newtonian, such as one with memory of its past deformation.
