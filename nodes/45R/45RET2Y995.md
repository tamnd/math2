## Statement

Multiplying two upper triangular matrices keeps the zeros below the diagonal, and the diagonal of the product is the product of the diagonals.
Let $R$ be a ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A = (a_{ij})$ and $B = (b_{ij})$ be [[upper triangular|0MKABWDRNC]] $n \times n$ matrices over $R$.
Then $AB$ is upper triangular, and
$$(AB)_{ii} = a_{ii} b_{ii} \quad \text{for } 1 \le i \le n.$$
If $A$ and $B$ are strictly upper triangular, then $AB$ is strictly upper triangular.

## Remarks

### A check with numbers

In [[the product of two 3 × 3 upper triangular matrices|NFJP659QFK]], the factors have diagonals $(1, 4, 6)$ and $(2, 1, 3)$, and the product
$$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 & 8 \\ 0 & 4 & 11 \\ 0 & 0 & 18 \end{pmatrix}$$
has diagonal $(2, 4, 18)$ and zeros below it.
The entries above the diagonal follow no such simple rule.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Both factors must be upper triangular.
With $U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and the lower triangular $L = U^{\mathsf{T}}$, [[the product UL|8GDWHV3EPB]] is $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which is not triangular, and its diagonal $(2, 1)$ is not $(1, 1)$.

The ring need not be commutative, because the proof never swaps two factors.
The diagonal entry of $AB$ is $a_{ii} b_{ii}$ in this order, and over a noncommutative ring it can differ from $b_{ii} a_{ii}$.

### What it gives you

Sums and scalar multiples of upper triangular matrices are upper triangular, so with this theorem they form a subring of $M_n(R)$ that contains $I$.
By induction on the number of factors,
$$(A_1 A_2 \cdots A_k)_{ii} = (A_1)_{ii} (A_2)_{ii} \cdots (A_k)_{ii}, \qquad (A^k)_{ii} = a_{ii}^k.$$
For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}^{4}$ has diagonal $(16, 81)$ without any further work.
So the map $A \mapsto (a_{11}, \dots, a_{nn})$ is a ring homomorphism onto the product ring $R^n$.
Over a commutative ring, for a polynomial $p$ the matrix $p(A)$ is upper triangular with diagonal $(p(a_{11}), \dots, p(a_{nn}))$.

The lower triangular case follows from the [[transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]].
If $A$ and $B$ are lower triangular, then $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $B^{\mathsf{T}}$ are upper triangular, so $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$ is upper triangular, and $AB$ is lower triangular.

### The converse

The converse is false: a product can be upper triangular when the factors are not.
For example,
$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = I,$$
and $I$ is upper triangular, but the factor is not.

### Generalizations

The same argument works for block upper triangular matrices whose diagonal blocks have matching sizes, with the diagonal blocks multiplied in place.
In the language of maps, a product of maps that preserve a flag of subspaces preserves the same flag.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When you multiply two upper triangular matrices, the zeros below the diagonal stay zero.
Each diagonal entry of the product is just the product of the two diagonal entries in the same place.
So the product of $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 0 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$ has diagonal $(8, 21)$.

### L2 human

In the sum $(AB)_{ij} = \sum_k a_{ik} b_{kj}$, the factor $a_{ik}$ can be nonzero only for $k \ge i$, and $b_{kj}$ only for $k \le j$.
For $i > j$ no index $k$ satisfies both, so the sum is $0$.
For $i = j$ only $k = i$ is left, so the sum is $a_{ii} b_{ii}$.
The [[proof|YDY12RN2YJ]] writes this out in steps.
The typical use is to know the diagonal of a long product, and so its determinant and its eigenvalues, without computing the product.
Watch out: the rule says nothing about a product of an upper and a lower triangular matrix.

### L3 human

The upper triangular matrices are the matrices that preserve the standard flag $V_1 \subset \cdots \subset V_n$, where $V_j$ is the span of $e_1, \dots, e_j$.
Maps that preserve a flag compose to maps that preserve it, and on each quotient $V_j / V_{j-1}$ they act by the scalar $a_{jj}$, which explains the diagonal rule.
The kernel of the diagonal homomorphism is the set of strictly upper triangular matrices, so they form a two-sided ideal, and each of them is [[nilpotent|07BSPZPSMF]] by the [[nilpotency theorem|AW5RBMW7RK]].
