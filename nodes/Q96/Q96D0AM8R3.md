## Definition

Let $\varepsilon_0, \mu_0 > 0$ and $c = 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_0 \mu_0}$.
Let $P \colon \R^3 \times \R \to \R^3$ be a [[polarization density|ASQAH710WN]] that is the only source, so that the charge and current densities are $\rho = -\nabla \cdot P$ and $J = \partial_t P$.
An *electric Hertz vector* for $P$ is a twice differentiable vector field $\Pi_e \colon \R^3 \times \R \to \R^3$ such that
$$\Delta \Pi_e - \frac{1}{c^2} \partial_t^2 \Pi_e = -P.$$
The associated potentials are
$$\varphi = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0} \nabla \cdot \Pi_e, \qquad A = \mu_0 \, \partial_t \Pi_e,$$
and the fields are $E = -\nabla \varphi - \partial_t A$ and $B = \nabla \times A$.

## Remarks

The potentials satisfy the [[Lorenz gauge condition|YBHAVTWF82]]: $\nabla \cdot A + \frac{1}{c^2} \partial_t \varphi = \mu_0 \partial_t \nabla \cdot \Pi_e - \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 c^2} \partial_t \nabla \cdot \Pi_e = 0$, since $\varepsilon_0 c^2 = 1 / \mu_0$.
Applying $\square = \frac{1}{c^2} \partial_t^2 - \Delta$ gives $\square \varphi = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0} \nabla \cdot P = \rho / \varepsilon_0$ and $\square A = \mu_0 \partial_t P = \mu_0 J$, the [[inhomogeneous electromagnetic wave equations|1AE7YGNX2R]].
In terms of $\Pi_e$ the fields are $E = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0} \left( \nabla (\nabla \cdot \Pi_e) - \frac{1}{c^2} \partial_t^2 \Pi_e \right)$ and $B = \mu_0 \, \partial_t \nabla \times \Pi_e$.
A magnetic Hertz vector is defined in the same way with the magnetization as source.

Example: for a point dipole $P = p(t) \, \delta(r)$ with a smooth moment $p \colon \R \to \R^3$, the retarded solution is $\Pi_e(r, t) = \frac{p(t - \abs{r}/c)}{4 \pi \abs{r}}$.
Away from the origin each component has the form $f(t - \abs{r}/c) / \abs{r}$, an outgoing spherical wave, which solves the homogeneous wave equation.

Non-example: $\Pi_e(r, t) = (t^2, 0, 0)$ with $P = 0$ is not a Hertz vector for $P$, since $\Delta \Pi_e - \frac{1}{c^2} \partial_t^2 \Pi_e = (-2/c^2, 0, 0) \neq 0$.
The defining equation, not the Lorenz condition, is what ties a Hertz vector to its source.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Hertz vector solves a wave equation whose source is the polarization of the medium.
From it, one divergence gives the scalar potential and one time derivative gives the vector potential.
This packs Maxwell's equations with polarization sources into a single vector wave equation.
