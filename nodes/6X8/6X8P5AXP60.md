## Statement

Let $U \subseteq \R^n$ be open and let $f, g_1, \dots, g_m, h_1, \dots, h_p \colon U \to \R$ be [[continuously differentiable|0VW90BBSJ4]].
Let $S = \set{x \in U : g_i(x) \le 0 \text{ for all } i,\ h_j(x) = 0 \text{ for all } j}$.
If $x^*$ is a local minimizer of $f$ on $S$, then there are $\mu_0 \in \R$, $\mu \in \R^m$ and $\lambda \in \R^p$, not all zero, such that:

1. $\mu_0 \nabla f(x^*) + \sum_{i=1}^m \mu_i \nabla g_i(x^*) + \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j \nabla h_j(x^*) = 0$.
2. $\mu_0 \ge 0$ and $\mu_i \ge 0$ for all $i$.
3. $\mu_i g_i(x^*) = 0$ for all $i$.

## Remarks

Here $\nabla$ is the [[gradient|0ABK9V9Y64]].
No constraint qualification is assumed; the price is that $\mu_0$ may be zero.
If $\mu_0 > 0$, dividing by $\mu_0$ gives the [[Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions|HDWEPH4YG4]].
A constraint qualification such as linear independence of the active gradients rules out $\mu_0 = 0$.
The Mangasarian–Fromovitz condition is exactly the condition that no multipliers with $\mu_0 = 0$ exist.
One proof applies a theorem of the alternative, such as Gordan's, to the linearized problem; another penalizes the constraints and passes to a limit.

Example: minimize $x$ subject to $g(x) = x^2 \le 0$.
The minimizer is $x^* = 0$, with $\nabla f = 1$ and $\nabla g = 0$.
The conditions hold with $\mu_0 = 0$ and $\mu_1 = 1$, while no multipliers with $\mu_0 = 1$ exist.

The result is due to Fritz John (1948).

## Explanations

### L1 human

At a constrained minimum, some nonnegative combination of the gradients of the objective and of the active constraints must vanish.
This is always true, but it can be empty of content when the weight on the objective is zero.
That happens only at degenerate points of the constraints.

### L2 human

The Fritz John conditions are the most general first-order necessary conditions for smooth problems.
They are the starting point for proving the KKT theorem: one adds a [[constraint qualification|2WGB8R9SAD]] to force $\mu_0 > 0$.
They are also used directly in problems where constraint qualifications fail, such as some problems with equilibrium constraints.
