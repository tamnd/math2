## Summary

The Taylor–Couette instability is the instability of the steady circular flow of a viscous fluid between two coaxial rotating cylinders.
When the inner cylinder turns fast enough compared with the outer one, the circular flow gives way to a new flow.
The new flow is a stack of steady ring-shaped vortices, the Taylor vortices.
G. I. Taylor computed the onset of this instability and confirmed it by experiment in 1923, an early success of linear [[hydrodynamic stability|H028A17Z55]] theory.
It is driven by centrifugal forces and is a model problem for pattern formation and the route to turbulence.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Fill the gap between two upright cylinders with liquid and spin the inner cylinder slowly: the liquid just goes round in circles.
Spin faster and, at a sharp threshold, the liquid organises into doughnut-shaped rolls stacked along the axis, like a pile of rings.
The reason is that fluid near the fast inner wall is flung outwards, and above the threshold viscosity can no longer hold it back.
Spinning faster still makes the rings wavy, and eventually the flow becomes turbulent.
