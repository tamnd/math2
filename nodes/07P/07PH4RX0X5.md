## Summary

Let $F$ be a field, $\lambda \in F$ and $k \ge 1$.
The [[Jordan block|0PBFS92AJP]] $J_k(\lambda)$ is the $k \times k$ [[upper triangular matrix|0N4XY7Z3NN]] with $\lambda$ on the diagonal, $1$ on the diagonal just above it, and $0$ elsewhere.
So $J_k(\lambda) = \lambda I + N$, where $N = J_k(0)$ is the [[nilpotent|07BSPZPSMF]] shift with $N e_1 = 0$ and $N e_i = e_{i-1}$ for $i \ge 2$.
Its [[characteristic polynomial|039HF58KQ5]] and its [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] are both $(x - \lambda)^k$.
Jordan blocks are the building blocks of the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].

## Motivation

Diagonal matrices are the simplest matrices, but many matrices are not similar to a diagonal matrix.
The smallest example is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is $J_2(0)$.
A Jordan block is the next simplest kind of matrix: one [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]], one line of eigenvectors, and a chain of vectors $e_k \mapsto e_{k-1} \mapsto \cdots \mapsto e_1 \mapsto 0$ under $J_k(\lambda) - \lambda I$.
Over an algebraically closed field, every matrix is similar to a block diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks.
So to understand powers, polynomials and exponentials of matrices, it is enough to understand them for one Jordan block.

## Notation

This page uses the upper version, with the ones above the diagonal.
Some books use the lower version $J_k(\lambda)^{\mathsf T}$, with the ones below the diagonal.
The two versions are similar by the reversal matrix $P$ with $P e_i = e_{k+1-i}$, and $P = P^{-1}$.
In the lower version the chain goes the other way: $e_1 \mapsto e_2 \mapsto \cdots \mapsto e_k \mapsto 0$.
Common symbols are $J_k(\lambda)$, $J_{\lambda, k}$ and $J_k$ when $\lambda$ is fixed.

## Remarks

### Examples

- $J_1(\lambda) = (\lambda)$, a $1 \times 1$ matrix.
- $J_3(\lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \lambda \end{pmatrix}$.
- The [[cube of $J_3(2)$|KRBV0E0TPV]] is $\begin{pmatrix} 8 & 12 & 6 \\ 0 & 8 & 12 \\ 0 & 0 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$, by the binomial formula.
- The [[nilpotent block $J_4(0)$|7SYF048QE6]] has powers of rank $3, 2, 1, 0$.
- The derivative $D$ on polynomials of degree at most $k - 1$ has matrix $J_k(0)$ in the basis $1, x, x^2/2!, \dots, x^{k-1}/(k-1)!$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

### Non-examples

- The [[matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$|7YPGG6CK8N]] is not a Jordan block, because its diagonal entries differ. It is diagonalisable.
- $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not a Jordan block, because the entry above the diagonal is $2$. It is similar to $J_2(1)$, by the change of basis $\operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$.
- The identity matrix $I_2$ is not a Jordan block. It is the direct sum $J_1(1) \oplus J_1(1)$, and [[it is not similar to $J_2(1)$|62XEH0NYE1]].

### Basic properties

- [[Powers and polynomials of a Jordan block|B9CHNGJV5K]]: $J_k(\lambda)^m = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \binom{m}{j} \lambda^{m-j} N^j$, so the entry $(i, i + j)$ of $J_k(\lambda)^m$ is $\binom{m}{j} \lambda^{m-j}$.
- The characteristic and the minimal polynomial are both $(x - \lambda)^k$.
- The only eigenvalue is $\lambda$, with algebraic multiplicity $k$ and geometric multiplicity $1$. The eigenspace is spanned by $e_1$.
- $\dim \ker (J_k(\lambda) - \lambda I)^j = \min(j, k)$ for $j \ge 0$.
- $J_k(\lambda)$ is cyclic, with cyclic vector $e_k$. It is similar to the [[companion matrix|0ZCWPNZTQ3]] of $(x - \lambda)^k$.
- $J_k(\lambda)$ is invertible if and only if $\lambda \ne 0$.
- $J_k(\lambda)$ is diagonalisable if and only if $k = 1$.

### Common errors

- Mixing the upper and the lower versions, for example in the order of the vectors of a Jordan chain.
- Writing $J_k(\lambda)^m$ with $\lambda^m$ above the diagonal; the binomial terms $m \lambda^{m-1}, \dots$ are missing.
- Thinking that a Jordan block has $k$ independent eigenvectors; it has only one, up to scalars.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A Jordan block is a square matrix with one number repeated on the diagonal and ones just above it.
It has only one eigenvalue and only one direction of eigenvectors.
When the size is at least $2$, it is the simplest matrix that you cannot make diagonal.

### L2 human

Take $J = J_2(3) = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = 3I + N$ with $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $N^2 = 0$, and $3I$ commutes with $N$.
2. So $J^m = 3^m I + m \, 3^{m-1} N = \begin{pmatrix} 3^m & m 3^{m-1} \\ 0 & 3^m \end{pmatrix}$.
3. For $m = 2$ this gives $\begin{pmatrix} 9 & 6 \\ 0 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$, and a direct product gives the same matrix.
4. $J - 3I = N$ has rank $1$, so the eigenspace of $3$ has dimension $1$, but $3$ is a double root of $(x - 3)^2$.

The trap is to treat $J^m$ as the matrix with $3^m$ on the diagonal and $1$ above it.

### L3 human

The block $J_k(\lambda)$ is the matrix of multiplication by $x$ on $F[x]/((x - \lambda)^k)$ in the basis $(x - \lambda)^{k-1}, \dots, (x - \lambda), 1$.
So it is the matrix of a cyclic module whose annihilator is a power of a linear polynomial.
For a polynomial $f$, $f(J_k(\lambda)) = \sum_{j < k} b_j N^j$, where the $b_j$ are the coefficients of $f$ in powers of $x - \lambda$; in characteristic $0$, $b_j = f^{(j)}(\lambda) / j!$.
The same formula with power series gives $\exp(t J_k(\lambda)) = e^{\lambda t} \sum_{j < k} t^j N^j / j!$, which is the source of the terms $t^j e^{\lambda t}$ in linear differential equations.
