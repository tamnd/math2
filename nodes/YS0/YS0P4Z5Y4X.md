## Summary

A transmission line is a pair of conductors, such as a coaxial cable or two parallel wires.
It carries electrical signals over distances comparable to or larger than the wavelength.
On such a line, voltage and current travel as waves described by the telegrapher's equations.
The line is characterized by its characteristic impedance and its propagation constant.
Mismatches between the line and its load cause reflections, which matter in radio, high-speed digital circuits and power grids.

## Explanations

### L1 human

At low frequency, a wire just connects two points and the voltage is the same along it.
At high frequency, the signal needs time to travel, and different parts of the cable hold different voltages at the same moment.
The cable then behaves like a road for waves: a pulse sent in runs along it and can bounce back from the far end.
A coaxial cable for television has a characteristic impedance of $75\ \Omega$; if the end is terminated with a $75\ \Omega$ resistor, nothing bounces back.
