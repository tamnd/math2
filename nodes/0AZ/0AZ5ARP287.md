## Summary

A linear map carries vectors forward, and its dual map carries measurements backward.
If you can measure vectors of $W$ with a linear form, you can measure vectors of $V$ too: first apply the map, then measure.
Let $f \colon V \to W$ be a [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] between vector spaces over a field $F$.
The *dual map* of $f$ is the map between the [[dual spaces|07JBSZE0V4]] given by
$$f^* \colon W^* \to V^*, \qquad f^*(\varphi) = \varphi \circ f.$$
The [[formal definition|0C2TQMW5A1]] states it.
In dual bases the matrix of $f^*$ is the transpose of the matrix of $f$, so the dual map is what the transpose of a matrix means when no coordinates are chosen.

## Motivation

Why does the transpose of a matrix keep appearing in linear algebra?
Suppose $f \colon V \to W$ is linear and $\varphi = 0$ is a linear equation on $W$, such as the equation of a plane.
The vectors $v$ with $f(v)$ on that plane are the solutions of $\varphi(f(v)) = 0$, which is the linear equation $f^*(\varphi) = 0$ on $V$.
So the dual map transports equations backward along $f$, and in coordinates this transport is multiplication by the transposed matrix.

The same backward step occurs in calculus.
By the [[chain rule|0RXX2PT1MQ]], the gradient of $\varphi \circ g$ at a point is the transposed [[Jacobian matrix|047NDTEPCV]] of $g$ times the gradient of $\varphi$.
Reverse mode [[automatic differentiation|BW7FEZ8WM1]] computes gradients by applying such transposed Jacobians one step at a time, from the output back to the input.

Later pages use the dual map to study a linear map through its forms.
Its matrix is the [[transpose|0ZPB3180AY]], its kernel and image are [[annihilators|7FA499AW3T]] of the image and the kernel of $f$, and from this follows a proof that row rank equals column rank.

## Notation

Books write $f^*$, $f^{\mathsf{T}}$, $f^t$, ${}^t f$ or $f'$, and they call the map the *dual map*, the *transpose* or the *adjoint* of $f$.
Watch out for the last name.
Later in linear algebra, the *adjoint* means the [[adjoint for an inner product|0R9NMSM1ZP]], a map $W \to V$ that needs inner products on $V$ and $W$.
The dual map $W^* \to V^*$ needs no inner product, and the two maps differ in general, although many books write $f^*$ for both.
Mathlib writes `Module.Dual.transpose`.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Let $f \colon \R^2 \to \R^3$ be $f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1, \, x_1 + x_2, \, 2x_2)$, and let $\varphi(y_1, y_2, y_3) = y_1 - y_3$, a form on $\R^3$.
To find $f^*(\varphi)$, substitute the entries of $f(x)$ into $\varphi$:
$$f^*(\varphi)(x_1, x_2) = \varphi(x_1, \, x_1 + x_2, \, 2x_2) = x_1 - 2x_2.$$
Now do the same with matrices.
The matrix of $f$ and the row of $\varphi$ are
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad y = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Multiply the row by $A$ from the right:
$$yA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 0 + 0 & 0 + 0 - 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
This is the row of the form $x_1 - 2x_2$, as before.
If you write forms as columns instead, the same numbers come from $A^{\mathsf{T}} y^{\mathsf{T}}$, and this is the first sign of the transpose.

### More examples

The [[dual of a matrix map|QM8XZDS9TK]] works the row picture out in general: for $f(x) = Ax$, the dual map sends a row $y$ to the row $yA$.
The [[dual of differentiation on polynomials|BPM4Q1KZ49]] shows the dual map on a space of functions, where $D^*$ turns "value at $1$" into "slope at $1$".

Two more examples come from subspaces.
For the inclusion $\iota \colon U \to V$ of a subspace, $\iota^*(\varphi) = \varphi \circ \iota$ is the restriction $\varphi|_U$, so $\iota^*$ is the restriction map $V^* \to U^*$.
For the [[quotient map|0N4F5FMQZW]] $\pi \colon V \to V/U$, the dual map $\pi^* \colon (V/U)^* \to V^*$ is injective, and its image is the annihilator $U^0$; the [[dimension of the annihilator|06Q1EFVH0Q]] uses this.

### What it is not

The [[inner product adjoint is not the dual map|16WRFVDYB0]].
For the inner product on $\R^2$ with Gram matrix $G = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$, the adjoint of $x \mapsto Ax$ has matrix $G^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}} G$, and for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ this is not $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
The condition that fails is that the adjoint is a map between the spaces themselves, built from an inner product, while the dual map acts on forms.

There is also no natural map $V^* \to W^*$ in the same direction as $f$.
Take $f \colon \R \to \R^2$, $f(t) = (t, 0)$, and the form $\psi(t) = t$ on $\R$.
Both $(a, b) \mapsto a$ and $(a, b) \mapsto a + b$ give back $\psi$ when you compose them with $f$, so nothing selects one form on $\R^2$ from $\psi$.

### The rules you can use

The dual map is linear, since the operations on $V^*$ act value by value.
It reverses composition, $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$, and it keeps identities, $(\id_V)^* = \id_{V^*}$, by the [[functoriality lemma|XBKREPBM9D]].
So when $f$ is an isomorphism, $f^*$ is one too, with inverse $(f^{-1})^*$.

In dual bases the matrix of $f^*$ is the transpose of the matrix of $f$, by the theorem on the [[matrix of the dual map|0ZPB3180AY]].
The theorem on the [[kernel and image of the dual map|7FA499AW3T]] gives $\ker f^* = (\im f)^0$ and $\im f^* = (\ker f)^0$, in every dimension.
So $f$ is injective exactly when $f^*$ is surjective, and $f$ is surjective exactly when $f^*$ is injective.
Finally, the [[naturality lemma|R19758JQWE]] says that $f^{**}$ corresponds to $f$ under the evaluation maps into the double duals.

### Watch out

The first trap is the direction.
For $f \colon V \to W$ the dual map goes from $W^*$ to $V^*$, so for $f \colon \R^2 \to \R^3$ it eats forms on $\R^3$ and returns forms on $\R^2$.

The second trap is the order in a composite.
Take $f(t) = (t, 2t)$ from $\R$ to $\R^2$ and $g(x, y) = x + y$ from $\R^2$ to $\R$.
Then $(g \circ f)^*$ acts on forms on $\R$, while $g^* \circ f^*$ acts on forms on $\R^2$, so they cannot be equal.
The correct rule is $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$, the same reversal as $(BA)^{\mathsf{T}} = A^{\mathsf{T}} B^{\mathsf{T}}$ for matrices.

The third trap is the word adjoint, as the Notation explains: in an orthonormal basis and its dual basis the dual map and the inner product adjoint have the same matrix $A^{\mathsf{T}}$, but in other bases they can differ.

### Try it

First, let $f(x, y) = (2x, \, x + y)$ on $\R^2$ and $\varphi(a, b) = a - b$.
Compute $f^*(\varphi)$.
The answer is $2x - (x + y) = x - y$.

Second, let $f \colon \R^3 \to \R$ be $f(x, y, z) = x + y + z$, and describe the image of $f^* \colon \R^* \to (\R^3)^*$.
Every form on $\R$ is $s \mapsto cs$, and it pulls back to $c(x + y + z)$, so the image is the line of multiples of $f$ itself.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear map sends vectors of one space to vectors of another.
A measuring rule on the second space, such as "take the first coordinate", gives a measuring rule on the first space: apply the map, then measure.
The dual map is this way of turning rules on the target into rules on the source.
For example, if $f(x, y) = (x + y, \, y)$ and the rule is "take the first coordinate", the new rule is "take $x + y$".
So the dual map goes in the opposite direction to the map.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: find the vectors that a map sends into a given plane.
Let $f \colon \R^2 \to \R^3$ be $f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 + x_2, \, x_1 - x_2, \, x_2)$, and let the plane be $y_1 + y_2 - 2y_3 = 0$.
The plane is the kernel of the form $\varphi(y) = y_1 + y_2 - 2y_3$, and $f(x)$ lies on it exactly when $f^*(\varphi)(x) = 0$.
Substitute to find the pulled back form:
$$f^*(\varphi)(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 + x_2) + (x_1 - x_2) - 2x_2 = 2x_1 - 2x_2.$$
So the vectors that land on the plane form the line $x_1 = x_2$.
Check: $f(1, 1) = (2, 0, 1)$, and $2 + 0 - 2 = 0$.
In matrices, the row $(1, 1, -2)$ times the matrix of $f$ gives $(2, -2)$, the same form.
The problem shows the typical use: the preimage of a hyperplane is the kernel of the pulled back form, and one row-times-matrix product finds it.

### L3 human

The rules $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$ and $\id^* = \id$ say that $V \mapsto V^*$, $f \mapsto f^*$ is a [[contravariant functor|RECR96V97B]] from vector spaces over $F$ to themselves.
Over a field this functor is exact, which is the content of the kernel and image theorem.
On finite-dimensional spaces it is an anti-equivalence of categories, because the [[double dual|0ZJM9D7NPS]] is naturally isomorphic to the identity there.
For normed spaces the same formula, restricted to the [[continuous dual spaces|4A82S5FVRT]], gives the Banach space adjoint of a bounded operator.
For Hilbert spaces, composing the dual map with the Riesz identifications of each space with its dual gives the [[Hilbert space adjoint|6HSJBJ8TTQ]].
For finite-dimensional spaces, $\Hom(V, W) \cong V^* \otimes W$, and under this identification $f \mapsto f^*$ exchanges the two factors of the [[tensor product|0BF2DE2SYK]], once $W^{**}$ is identified with $W$.
