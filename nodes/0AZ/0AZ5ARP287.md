## Summary

Let $f \colon V \to W$ be a [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] between vector spaces over a field $F$.
The dual map of $f$ is the map $f^* \colon W^* \to V^*$ between the [[dual spaces|07JBSZE0V4]] given by $f^*(\varphi) = \varphi \circ f$.
It sends a linear form on $W$ to the form on $V$ obtained by first applying $f$.
It is linear, it reverses [[composition|00BKRZKMSS]], $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$, and in dual bases its matrix is the transpose of the matrix of $f$.

## Motivation

A linear map $f \colon V \to W$ moves vectors forward, from $V$ to $W$.
A measurement on $W$, that is, a form $\varphi \in W^*$, can be pulled back to a measurement on $V$: measure $f(v)$ instead of $v$.
This pullback is the dual map, and it goes backward, from $W^*$ to $V^*$.
It gives a coordinate-free meaning to the transpose of a matrix.

## Notation

The dual map is written $f^*$, $f^{\mathsf{T}}$, $f^t$, ${}^t f$ or $f'$.
It is also called the transpose of $f$, and sometimes the adjoint of $f$.
Warning: in this encyclopedia, "adjoint" means the [[adjoint for an inner product|0R9NMSM1ZP]], a map $W \to V$; the dual map $W^* \to V^*$ needs no inner product.
Mathlib writes `Module.Dual.transpose`.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The dual of a matrix map|~f6882d01]]: for $f(x) = Ax$ from $F^n$ to $F^m$, with forms written as rows, $f^*(y) = yA$.
- [[The dual of differentiation on polynomials|~1cf44275]]: for $D \colon P_2(\R) \to P_2(\R)$, $D^*$ sends evaluation at $1$ to the form $p \mapsto p'(1)$.
- The dual of the inclusion $\iota \colon U \to V$ of a subspace is the restriction map $V^* \to U^*$, $\varphi \mapsto \varphi|_U$.
- The dual of the quotient map $\pi \colon V \to V/U$ is the injective map $(V/U)^* \to V^*$ with image the annihilator $U^0$; see the [[dimension of the annihilator|06Q1EFVH0Q]].

### Non-examples

- [[The inner product adjoint is not the dual map|~d9b85092]]: for a non-standard inner product on $\R^2$, the adjoint of a map $A$ has matrix $G^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}} G$, which differs from $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
- The map $W^* \to V^*$, $\varphi \mapsto \varphi \circ f$, needs $f \colon V \to W$. There is no "dual map" $V^* \to W^*$ in the same direction as $f$, unless one chooses extra data such as bases.

### Basic properties

- $f^*$ is linear, and $f \mapsto f^*$ is a linear map $\Hom(V, W) \to \Hom(W^*, V^*)$.
- $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$ and $(\id_V)^* = \id_{V^*}$, by the [[functoriality lemma|~3163ec56]]. So $f^*$ is invertible when $f$ is, with $(f^*)^{-1} = (f^{-1})^*$.
- In dual bases, the matrix of $f^*$ is the transpose of the matrix of $f$, by the theorem on the [[matrix of the dual map|0ZPB3180AY]].
- $\ker f^* = (\im f)^0$ and $\im f^* = (\ker f)^0$, in any dimension, by the theorem on the [[kernel and image of the dual map|~0b4dab92]]. So $f$ is injective if and only if $f^*$ is surjective.
- $f^{**} \circ \operatorname{ev}_V = \operatorname{ev}_W \circ f$, by the [[naturality lemma|~f17af3fe]].

### History

The transpose of a matrix, writing rows as columns, is older than the abstract dual map.
The dual map gave the transpose a meaning that does not depend on bases.
In category theory, the rules $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$ and $\id^* = \id$ say that the dual space is a contravariant functor.

### Common errors

- Writing $f^* \colon V^* \to W^*$. The dual map goes in the opposite direction.
- Writing $(g \circ f)^* = g^* \circ f^*$. The order reverses, as for the transpose of a product of matrices.
- Confusing the dual map with the inner product adjoint. They have the same matrix only in orthonormal bases.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear map sends vectors of one space to vectors of another.
Its dual map works on measuring rules, and it goes the other way.
Given a rule on the target space, it produces a rule on the source space: "first apply the map, then measure".
In coordinates, the dual map is described by the transposed matrix.

### L2 human

We compute a dual map on $\R^2 \to \R^3$.

1. Let $f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1, x_1 + x_2, 2x_2)$, and let $\varphi(y_1, y_2, y_3) = y_1 - y_3$ be a form on $\R^3$.
2. Then $f^*(\varphi)(x_1, x_2) = \varphi(f(x_1, x_2)) = x_1 - 2x_2$.
3. The matrix of $f$ is $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, and $\varphi$ is the row $(1, 0, -1)$.
4. The row of $f^*(\varphi)$ is $(1, 0, -1) A = (1, -2)$, which is the form $x_1 - 2x_2$, as in step 2.
5. In columns, $f^*$ has the matrix $A^{\mathsf{T}}$, and $A^{\mathsf{T}} (1, 0, -1)^{\mathsf{T}} = (1, -2)^{\mathsf{T}}$.

The typical use is to transport equations backward along a map: if $\varphi = 0$ is an equation on $W$, then $f^*(\varphi) = 0$ is the pulled back equation on $V$.
A trap: the word "adjoint" for $f^*$ in older books, and the same symbol $f^*$ for the inner product adjoint.

### L3 human

The assignment $V \mapsto V^*$, $f \mapsto f^*$ is a contravariant functor from vector spaces to vector spaces.
It is exact, and in finite dimension it is an anti-equivalence of categories, with the double dual naturally isomorphic to the identity.
For Banach spaces, the same formula defines the Banach space adjoint between continuous duals.
For Hilbert spaces, combining the dual map with the Riesz isomorphisms gives the Hilbert space adjoint.
