## Statement

Let $g = -dt^2 + a(t)^2 \gamma_k$ be a [[Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric|ECJMS4VDTF]] with [[scale factor|J70F32P86W]] $a$ and Hubble parameter $H = \dot a / a$, in units with $c = 1$.
Let two comoving observers be at comoving distance $\chi$ from each other.
Let $D(t) = a(t)\chi$ be their proper distance on the slice of cosmic time $t$.
Then
$$\frac{dD}{dt}(t) = H(t)\, D(t) \quad \text{for all } t.$$
In particular, at the present time $t_0$ the recession velocity $v = \dot D(t_0)$ of every comoving source is $v = H_0 D$, with $H_0 = H(t_0)$.

## Remarks

The proof is one line: $\dot D = \dot a \chi = (\dot a / a)(a \chi) = H D$.
The law is exact for proper distance and recession velocity in the model, even when $v > 1$ for very distant sources.
For nearby sources the [[cosmological redshift|8K69WPX4GS]] satisfies $1 + z = a(t_0)/a(t_e) \approx 1 + H_0 (t_0 - t_e)$, and $t_0 - t_e \approx D$, so $z \approx H_0 D$; this is the form tested by observation.
Example: in the Einstein–de Sitter model $a \propto t^{2/3}$, so $H = 2/(3t)$ and $\dot D = 2D/(3t)$.
Georges Lemaître derived the linear relation in 1927, and Edwin Hubble reported it from observations of galaxies in 1929; it is also called the Hubble–Lemaître law.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In an expanding universe, galaxies move away from each other, and the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it recedes.
The speed is simply proportional to the distance, and the constant of proportionality is the Hubble constant.
Every observer sees the same law, so there is no centre of the expansion.

### L2 human

Hubble's law is the kinematic content of a homogeneous expansion: a map that scales all distances by the same factor must move points apart at speeds proportional to their distance.
Its observational version, $z \approx H_0 D$, holds only to first order in $z$.
At larger redshifts the relation between redshift and distance depends on the whole history $a(t)$, through the [[distance measures of cosmology|YDWH956TJA]].
The inverse $1/H_0$ sets the time scale of the universe, and the age of most models is of that order.
