## Summary

Fermi liquid theory, due to Lev Landau in 1956, describes interacting fermions, such as electrons in a metal or liquid helium-3, at low temperature.
Its central claim is that the low-energy excitations are quasiparticles in one-to-one correspondence with the excitations of a free [[Fermi gas|663MJA8NAC]].
The quasiparticles have a renormalised mass and interact through a few Landau parameters.
It explains why the free-electron picture of metals works so well, with a heat capacity linear in temperature and a sharp [[Fermi surface|GK3SVTXXKX]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Electrons in a metal repel each other strongly, so it is surprising that the theory of free electrons describes metals well.
Landau's answer is that an electron near the Fermi surface drags a small cloud of disturbance with it.
The electron with its cloud, the quasiparticle, behaves like a free particle with a different mass.
Near the Fermi surface quasiparticles live a long time, because there is little room for them to scatter into.
So at low temperature the metal behaves like a gas of free quasiparticles, with corrected numbers.
