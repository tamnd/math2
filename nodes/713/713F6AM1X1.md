## Example

Let $F$ be a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]] and let $n \ge 0$ be an integer.
The *coordinate space* $F^n$ is the set of $n$-tuples $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ with every $x_i \in F$, and two tuples are equal when they agree in every position.
The operations act entry by entry:
$$(x_1, \dots, x_n) + (y_1, \dots, y_n) = (x_1 + y_1, \dots, x_n + y_n), \qquad a(x_1, \dots, x_n) = (ax_1, \dots, ax_n).$$
We check the axioms of a [[vector space|000AYPA9AJ]], numbered as in the [[definition|05EGHEQZAW]], for $x, y, z \in F^n$ and $a, b \in F$.
In each step we compare entry $i$ of the two sides.

1. Closure: each entry $x_i + y_i$ and $ax_i$ lies in $F$, so the results are $n$-tuples over $F$.
2. Axiom 1: entry $i$ of $x + (y + z)$ is $x_i + (y_i + z_i)$, and entry $i$ of $(x + y) + z$ is $(x_i + y_i) + z_i$; they agree by associativity in $F$.
3. Axiom 2: entry $i$ of $x + y$ is $x_i + y_i = y_i + x_i$, by commutativity in $F$.
4. Axiom 3: the zero vector is $(0, \dots, 0)$, because $x_i + 0 = x_i$ in $F$.
5. Axiom 4: the negative of $x$ is $(-x_1, \dots, -x_n)$, because $x_i + (-x_i) = 0$ in $F$.
6. Axiom 5: entry $i$ of $a(bx)$ is $a(bx_i) = (ab)x_i$, by associativity of multiplication in $F$.
7. Axiom 6: entry $i$ of $1x$ is $1 \cdot x_i = x_i$.
8. Axiom 7: entry $i$ of $a(x + y)$ is $a(x_i + y_i) = ax_i + ay_i$, by distributivity in $F$.
9. Axiom 8: entry $i$ of $(a + b)x$ is $(a + b)x_i = ax_i + bx_i$, by distributivity in $F$.

So $F^n$ is a vector space over $F$.

## Remarks

The main cases are $\mathbb{R}^n$ over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}^n$ over $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$ model the plane and space, as with [[Euclidean vectors|0QN3D80AWP]].
For matrix work we write the elements of $F^n$ as columns, so that $Ax$ is defined for an $m \times n$ matrix $A$.
Over a finite field the space is finite: $\mathbb{F}_2^3$ has $2^3 = 8$ vectors, as the page on [[vector spaces over finite fields|J9VE1X14PV]] counts.
For $n = 0$ the space $F^0$ has one element, the empty tuple, so it is the zero space.

Why call $F^n$ the prototype?
The [[standard basis|C5QA672NPH]] $e_1, \dots, e_n$ shows that $\dim F^n = n$.
Each space of dimension $n$ is [[isomorphic to F^n|059THXBRBB]], by sending a vector to its [[coordinate vector|04CYQHN6MC]], so every finite-dimensional problem can be moved into $F^n$ and solved there with matrices.
That isomorphism depends on the choice of a basis.

The field matters too.
The same set $\mathbb{C}^n$ is also a real vector space, of dimension $2n$, by its [[realification|CT2F31EDH8]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The space $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the space of lists of three real numbers, such as $(2, -1, 5)$.
You add two lists position by position: $(1, 2, 3) + (4, 5, 6) = (5, 7, 9)$.
You scale a list by scaling each entry: $2 \cdot (1, 2, 3) = (2, 4, 6)$.
All the rules of a vector space hold, because they hold for ordinary numbers in each position.

### L2 human

We compute in $\mathbb{C}^2$ over $\mathbb{C}$ with $x = (1 + i, 2)$, $y = (i, -1)$ and $a = i$, and check axiom 7.
First add, then scale, using $i^2 = -1$:
$$x + y = (1 + 2i, 1), \qquad a(x + y) = (i + 2i^2, i) = (-2 + i, i).$$
Now scale each vector first, then add:
$$ax = (i + i^2, 2i) = (-1 + i, 2i), \qquad ay = (i^2, -i) = (-1, -i), \qquad ax + ay = (-2 + i, i).$$
The two sides agree.
The typical use of $F^n$ is as the place where computations happen: after a choice of basis, a question about any finite-dimensional space becomes a question about columns.
Watch out: $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$, since its elements are pairs, not triples; the vectors $(x, y, 0)$ form a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$ that is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^2$.

### L3 human

For any set $I$, the set $F^I$ of all functions $I \to F$ is a vector space, and $F^n$ is the case $I = \{1, \dots, n\}$; see [[spaces of functions|0GZB2SN0PH]].
For infinite $I$, the subspace $F^{(I)}$ of finitely supported functions is the free vector space on $I$, and every vector space is isomorphic to one of these once a basis is chosen.
In categorical terms, $F^n$ is both the product and the coproduct of $n$ copies of $F$, the [[direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] $F \oplus \dots \oplus F$.
