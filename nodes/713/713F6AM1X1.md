## Example

Let $F$ be a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]] and let $n \ge 0$ be an integer.
The *coordinate space* $F^n$ is the set of $n$-tuples $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ with every $x_i \in F$.
Two tuples are equal when they agree in every position.
The operations are entrywise:
$$(x_1, \dots, x_n) + (y_1, \dots, y_n) = (x_1 + y_1, \dots, x_n + y_n), \qquad a(x_1, \dots, x_n) = (ax_1, \dots, ax_n).$$
We check the axioms of a [[vector space|000AYPA9AJ]], numbered as in the [[definition|05EGHEQZAW]], for $x, y, z \in F^n$ and $a, b \in F$.

1. Closure: each entry $x_i + y_i$ and $ax_i$ lies in $F$, so the results are $n$-tuples over $F$.
2. Axiom 1: entry $i$ of $x + (y + z)$ is $x_i + (y_i + z_i)$, and entry $i$ of $(x + y) + z$ is $(x_i + y_i) + z_i$. They are equal by associativity in $F$.
3. Axiom 2: entry $i$ of $x + y$ is $x_i + y_i = y_i + x_i$, by commutativity in $F$.
4. Axiom 3: the zero vector is $0 = (0, \dots, 0)$, because $x_i + 0 = x_i$ in $F$.
5. Axiom 4: the negative of $x$ is $(-x_1, \dots, -x_n)$, because $x_i + (-x_i) = 0$ in $F$.
6. Axiom 5: entry $i$ of $a(bx)$ is $a(bx_i) = (ab)x_i$, by associativity of multiplication in $F$.
7. Axiom 6: entry $i$ of $1x$ is $1 \cdot x_i = x_i$.
8. Axiom 7: entry $i$ of $a(x + y)$ is $a(x_i + y_i) = ax_i + ay_i$, by distributivity in $F$.
9. Axiom 8: entry $i$ of $(a + b)x$ is $(a + b)x_i = ax_i + bx_i$, by distributivity in $F$.

So $F^n$ is a vector space over $F$.

## Remarks

The main cases are $\R^n$ over $\R$ and $\C^n$ over $\C$; the spaces $\R^2$ and $\R^3$ model the plane and ordinary space, as with [[Euclidean vectors|0QN3D80AWP]].
For matrix work we write the elements of $F^n$ as columns $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)^T$, so that $Ax$ is defined for an $m \times n$ matrix $A$.
For $n = 0$ the space $F^0$ has one element, the empty tuple, so it is the zero space.
For $F = \mathbb{F}_2$ and $n = 3$ the space has $2^3 = 8$ vectors, see [[vector spaces over finite fields|J9VE1X14PV]].
The [[standard basis|C5QA672NPH]] $e_1, \dots, e_n$ shows that $\dim F^n = n$.
The space $F^n$ is the prototype of finite dimension: each space of dimension $n$ is [[isomorphic to $F^n$|059THXBRBB]], by sending a vector to its [[coordinate vector|04CYQHN6MC]].
This isomorphism depends on the choice of a basis.
The same space $\C^n$ is also a real vector space, of dimension $2n$, by its [[realification|CT2F31EDH8]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The space $\R^3$ is the space of lists of three real numbers, such as $(2, -1, 5)$.
We add two lists position by position: $(1, 2, 3) + (4, 5, 6) = (5, 7, 9)$.
We scale a list by scaling each entry: $2(1, 2, 3) = (2, 4, 6)$.
All rules of a vector space hold, because they hold for ordinary numbers in each position.

### L2 human

We compute in $\C^2$ over $\C$ with $x = (1 + i, 2)$, $y = (i, -1)$ and $a = i$.

1. $x + y = (1 + 2i, 1)$.
2. $ax = (i(1 + i), 2i) = (-1 + i, 2i)$, because $i^2 = -1$.
3. $ay = (i \cdot i, -i) = (-1, -i)$.
4. Axiom 7 check: $a(x + y) = (i(1 + 2i), i) = (-2 + i, i)$, and $ax + ay = (-2 + i, 2i - i) = (-2 + i, i)$.

The typical use of $F^n$ is as the place where computations happen: after a choice of basis, every finite-dimensional problem becomes a problem in $F^n$.
A trap: $\R^2$ is not a subspace of $\R^3$; the set of vectors $(x, y, 0)$ is a subspace of $\R^3$ that is isomorphic to $\R^2$.

### L3 human

More generally, for any set $I$, the set $F^I$ of all functions $I \to F$ is a vector space, and $F^n$ is the case $I = \set{1, \dots, n}$; see [[spaces of functions|0GZB2SN0PH]].
For infinite $I$ the subspace $F^{(I)}$ of finitely supported functions is the free vector space on $I$, and every vector space is isomorphic to one of these.
In categorical terms, $F^n$ is both the product and the coproduct of $n$ copies of $F$, the [[direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] $F \oplus \cdots \oplus F$.
