## Counterexample

An apportionment problem divides $h$ seats among states with populations $p_1, \dots, p_s$.
The quota of state $i$ is $q_i = h p_i / (p_1 + \cdots + p_s)$.
Hamilton's method (largest remainders) first gives each state $\floor{q_i}$ seats, using the [[floor function|08GR54CKEA]].
It then gives the remaining seats, one each, to the states with the largest fractional parts $q_i - \floor{q_i}$.

Take three states $A, B, C$ with populations $6, 6, 2$, so the total is $14$.

With $h = 10$ seats the quotas are $60/14 \approx 4.29$, $4.29$ and $20/14 \approx 1.43$.
The floors give $4, 4, 1$, and the one remaining seat goes to $C$, which has the largest fractional part.
The apportionment is $(4, 4, 2)$.

With $h = 11$ seats the quotas are $66/14 \approx 4.71$, $4.71$ and $22/14 \approx 1.57$.
The floors give $4, 4, 1$, and the two remaining seats go to $A$ and $B$.
The apportionment is $(5, 5, 1)$.

So when the house grows from $10$ to $11$ seats, state $C$ loses a seat although no population changed.
This is the *Alabama paradox*: Hamilton's method is not house monotone.

## Remarks

Another apportionment paradox is the population paradox: a state that grows faster than another loses a seat to it.
In the new states paradox, adding a state together with its fair share of seats changes the seats of the other states.
The Balinski–Young theorem shows that the population paradox cannot be avoided by any method that keeps every state within its quota.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Dividing seats in proportion to population needs rounding, and rounding can behave strangely.
Here a small state has $2$ seats in a house of $10$ and only $1$ seat in a larger house of $11$, with the same populations.
Such surprises are called apportionment paradoxes.
