## Summary

The algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue says how many times it occurs as a root of the characteristic polynomial.
It is the count that makes an $n \times n$ complex matrix have exactly $n$ eigenvalues.
Let $T$ be an endomorphism of a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ over a field $F$, and let $\lambda$ be an [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $T$.
The *algebraic multiplicity* of $\lambda$ is its multiplicity as a [[root|00EHX6FWC0]] of the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] $p_T$, that is, the largest $m$ with
$$(t - \lambda)^m \text{ dividing } p_T(t).$$
The [[formal definition|0S7A0EMPF4]] states it, and for a square matrix $A$ we use the map $x \mapsto Ax$.

## Motivation

A polynomial can have a repeated root: $1$ is a root of $(t - 1)^2$ twice over.
If we count eigenvalues without repeats, simple facts break.
The [[shear|HTAWRAC3QE]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the single eigenvalue $1$, but its trace is $2$ and its determinant is $1$.
With the eigenvalue counted twice, the [[trace is the sum of the eigenvalues|0NMXDCNNKR]] and the [[determinant is the product|0C2F40MEBC]], as they should be.

The algebraic multiplicity is also one half of the test for diagonalization.
The other half is the [[geometric multiplicity|099TWVJ909]], the number of independent eigenvectors, and a matrix over $\mathbb{C}$ is diagonalizable exactly when the two counts agree for every eigenvalue.
The sub-areas on diagonalization and on the Jordan form build on this comparison.

## Notation

Books write $a(\lambda)$, $\operatorname{am}(\lambda)$, $m_a(\lambda)$ or $\mu_A(\lambda)$.
Watch out for $\mu_A$: many books use the same letter for the minimal polynomial.
Mathlib has no separate name; the number is `Polynomial.rootMultiplicity λ (Matrix.charpoly A)`.

## Remarks

### Reading the multiplicities off a factored polynomial

Take the [[3 by 3 matrix with 4 on the diagonal and 1 elsewhere|7XFGVVTZ2X]].
It is $A = 3I + J$, where $J$ has all entries $1$, so $tI - A = (t - 3)I - J$.
The matrix $J$ has trace $3$, all its $2 \times 2$ minors are $0$ and its determinant is $0$, so $\det(sI - J) = s^3 - 3s^2 = s^2(s - 3)$.
Put $s = t - 3$:
$$p_A(t) = (t - 3)^2 (t - 6).$$
So $3$ has algebraic multiplicity $2$ and $6$ has algebraic multiplicity $1$, and the multiplicities add up to $3$.

### Triangular matrices

For a [[triangular matrix|NHR1K0GPS1]], $tI - A$ is triangular too, and its determinant is the product of its diagonal entries.
For example,
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 & 7 \\ 0 & -1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_A(t) = (t - 2)(t + 1)(t - 2) = (t - 2)^2 (t + 1).$$
So the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ is the number of diagonal places that hold $\lambda$: here $2$ for $\lambda = 2$ and $1$ for $\lambda = -1$.
Check: $2 + 2 - 1 = 3 = \operatorname{tr} A$ and $2 \cdot 2 \cdot (-1) = -4 = \det A$.

### The field decides the total

The multiplicities add up to at most $\dim V$, because $\deg p_T = \dim V$, and they add up to exactly $\dim V$ when $p_T$ splits over $F$.
The [[rotation of the plane|5AE98D7959]] by a right angle, $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, has $p(t) = t^2 + 1$.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ it has no eigenvalue, so the total is $0$.
Over $\mathbb{C}$ the eigenvalues $i$ and $-i$ each have multiplicity $1$, and the total is $2$.

### The rules you can use

The algebraic multiplicity does not depend on the basis, because similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial.
It bounds the geometric multiplicity from above, $1 \le g(\lambda) \le a(\lambda)$, by the theorem that the [[dimension of an eigenspace is at most the algebraic multiplicity|FWY8A9M5HK]].
It also has a geometric meaning of its own: it is the dimension of the generalized eigenspace $\ker(T - \lambda \operatorname{id}_V)^n$ with $n = \dim V$, as the [[decomposition into generalized eigenspaces|0Z3GDY89T5]] shows.

### What it is not

It is not the dimension of the eigenspace.
The shear has $a(1) = 2$, but $\ker(A - I)$ is the line spanned by $(1, 0)$, so $g(1) = 1$.
[[Counting diagonal entries|BX4A1N2PFC]] gives the algebraic count, not the geometric one.

It is not the exponent in the minimal polynomial either.
For the [[identity matrix of size 2|MSQTBBM86Q]], $p = (t - 1)^2$ but the minimal polynomial is $t - 1$, so the exponent there is $1$ while $a(1) = 2$.

### Watch out

Diagonal entries count only for triangular matrices.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has $1$ twice on its diagonal, but $p(t) = (t - 1)^2 - 1 = t(t - 2)$.
So $1$ is not an eigenvalue at all, and $0$ and $2$ each have multiplicity $1$.
Always factor $p_A$ unless the matrix is triangular.

### History

Counting the roots of a polynomial with multiplicity is older than linear algebra, and the fundamental theorem of algebra uses it: a complex polynomial of degree $n$ has $n$ roots counted with multiplicity.
The algebraic multiplicity applies this count to the characteristic polynomial.

### Try it

First, find the algebraic multiplicities for $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 5 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
The matrix is lower triangular with diagonal $5, 5, 1$, so $a(5) = 2$ and $a(1) = 1$.

Second, find them for $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
The trace is $2$ and the determinant is $0 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot (-1) = 1$, so $p(t) = t^2 - 2t + 1 = (t - 1)^2$ and $a(1) = 2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An eigenvalue is a root of the characteristic polynomial, and a root can occur more than once.
For example, $(t - 1)^2 = (t - 1)(t - 1)$ has the root $1$ two times.
The algebraic multiplicity counts how many times.
With this count, a $3 \times 3$ complex matrix always has three eigenvalues, some of which may be equal.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: decide whether the counts of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ allow a basis of eigenvectors.
The matrix is triangular, so $p_A(t) = (t - 2)^2$ and $a(2) = 2$.
The matrix $A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has rank $1$, so the eigenspace has dimension $2 - 1 = 1$.
A basis of eigenvectors would need two independent eigenvectors for the eigenvalue $2$, and there is only one line of them.
So $A$ is not diagonalizable.
Watch out: a repeated root is a warning, not a verdict, since $2I$ has the same $p_A$ and is diagonal.

### L3 human

Over $F[x]$, the space $V$ is a module with $x$ acting as $T$, and the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ is the dimension of its $(x - \lambda)$-primary part.
In the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]] it is the total size of the Jordan blocks for $\lambda$, while the geometric multiplicity is their number and the exponent in the minimal polynomial is the size of the largest one.
The sub-area on canonical forms makes these three numbers precise.
