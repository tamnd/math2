## Summary

Let $T$ be an endomorphism of a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$, and let $\lambda$ be an [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $T$.
The algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ is its multiplicity as a [[root|00EHX6FWC0]] of the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] $p_T$.
It is the largest $m$ such that $(t - \lambda)^m$ divides $p_T$.
It is at least the [[geometric multiplicity|099TWVJ909]], and the algebraic multiplicities add up to $\dim V$ when $p_T$ splits.

## Motivation

A root of a polynomial can occur several times, as $1$ occurs twice in $(t - 1)^2$.
Eigenvalues should be counted in the same way, so that an $n \times n$ complex matrix has exactly $n$ eigenvalues.
With this count, the trace is the sum and the determinant is the product of the eigenvalues.
The comparison with the geometric multiplicity decides whether a matrix can be diagonalised.

## Notation

The formal definition is the [[definition of the algebraic multiplicity|0S7A0EMPF4]].
Common symbols are $a(\lambda)$, $\mu_A(\lambda)$ and $\operatorname{am}(\lambda)$.
Mathlib has no separate name for it; it is `Polynomial.rootMultiplicity λ (Matrix.charpoly A)`.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The 3 by 3 matrix with 4 on the diagonal and 1 elsewhere|7XFGVVTZ2X]]: $p_A = (t - 3)^2 (t - 6)$, so $3$ has algebraic multiplicity $2$ and $6$ has algebraic multiplicity $1$.
- [[The shear of the plane|HTAWRAC3QE]]: $p_A = (t - 1)^2$, so $1$ has algebraic multiplicity $2$.
- [[A triangular matrix|NHR1K0GPS1]]: the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ is the number of times $\lambda$ occurs on the diagonal.
- For $I_n$, the eigenvalue $1$ has algebraic multiplicity $n$.

### Non-examples

- [[The exponent of a root in the minimal polynomial|MSQTBBM86Q]] is not the algebraic multiplicity: for $I_2$, the minimal polynomial is $t - 1$ and the algebraic multiplicity of $1$ is $2$.
- The number of distinct eigenvalues is not a sum of algebraic multiplicities. The shear has one eigenvalue and the sum of multiplicities $2$.

### Basic properties

- $1 \le g(\lambda) \le a(\lambda)$, where $g$ is the geometric multiplicity, by the theorem that the [[dimension of an eigenspace is at most the algebraic multiplicity|FWY8A9M5HK]].
- $\sum_\lambda a(\lambda) \le \dim V$, with equality exactly when $p_T$ splits over $F$.
- It does not depend on the basis, and similar matrices have the same algebraic multiplicities.
- It is the dimension of the [[generalised eigenspace|0Z3GDY89T5]] $\ker(T - \lambda \id_V)^n$, where $n = \dim V$.
- For a triangular matrix, it is the number of diagonal entries equal to $\lambda$.

### History

The idea of counting roots with multiplicity is old, and it is part of the fundamental theorem of algebra in the form "a polynomial of degree $n$ has $n$ complex roots, counted with multiplicity".
The algebraic multiplicity applies this count to the characteristic polynomial.

### Common errors

- Counting the eigenvalue once in the trace or determinant formulas.
- Reading the multiplicity off the minimal polynomial.
- Expecting $a(\lambda)$ independent eigenvectors. There can be fewer.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An eigenvalue is a root of the characteristic polynomial.
A root can occur more than one time, for example $1$ is a double root of $(t - 1)^2$.
The algebraic multiplicity counts how many times the eigenvalue occurs as a root.
With this count, a $3 \times 3$ complex matrix always has three eigenvalues.

### L2 human

We find the algebraic multiplicities for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 & 7 \\ 0 & -1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $A$ is upper triangular, so $p_A(t) = (t - 2)(t + 1)(t - 2) = (t - 2)^2 (t + 1)$.
2. So $2$ has algebraic multiplicity $2$ and $-1$ has algebraic multiplicity $1$.
3. Check: the sum $2 + 2 + (-1) = 3$ is $\tr A$, and the product $2 \cdot 2 \cdot (-1) = -4$ is $\det A$.

The typical use is to compare $a(\lambda)$ with $g(\lambda)$ to test diagonalisability.
A trap: the factor $(t - 2)$ that comes from two different diagonal positions counts twice.

### L3 human

The algebraic multiplicity is the length of the $(x - \lambda)$-primary part of the $F[x]$-module $V$, that is, $\dim$ of the generalised eigenspace.
In the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]], it is the total size of the Jordan blocks for $\lambda$, while the geometric multiplicity is their number.
