## Statement

When we add a matrix of rank one to an invertible matrix, the determinant changes by a single scalar factor that we can compute from the inverse.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $A$ be an [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] $n \times n$ matrix over $K$, and let $u, v \in K^n$ be column vectors.
Then
$$\det(A + u v^{\mathsf{T}}) = (1 + v^{\mathsf{T}} A^{-1} u) \det A,$$
where $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]] and $v^{\mathsf{T}} A^{-1} u$ is a scalar.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, $u = (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $v = (1, 2)^{\mathsf{T}}$, so $\det A = 6$.
The inverse is diagonal, and the factor of the lemma is
$$1 + v^{\mathsf{T}} A^{-1} u = 1 + 1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot 1 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{2}{3} = \tfrac{13}{6}.$$
So the lemma predicts $\det(A + u v^{\mathsf{T}}) = \tfrac{13}{6} \cdot 6 = 13$.
Check: the update is $u v^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, and
$$\det(A + u v^{\mathsf{T}}) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = 15 - 2 = 13.$$

### When the matrix is not invertible

The lemma needs $A^{-1}$, but a form without inverses holds for every square matrix $A$ over $K$:
$$\det(A + u v^{\mathsf{T}}) = \det A + v^{\mathsf{T}} \operatorname{adj}(A)\, u.$$
Here $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ is the [[adjugate|07W9H8QF38]].
For invertible $A$ the two forms agree, because $\operatorname{adj}(A) = \det A \cdot A^{-1}$ by the [[inverse via the adjugate|0ENVM493VK]].
Both sides of the new form are polynomials in the entries of $A$, $u$ and $v$.
They agree for the generic matrix, whose determinant is invertible in the field of fractions, and so they agree over every commutative ring.
In the example above, $\operatorname{adj}(A) = \operatorname{diag}(3, 2)$, and the form gives $6 + (1 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1) = 13$ again.
For $A = 0$ and $n \ge 2$ the adjugate is $0$, so $\det(u v^{\mathsf{T}}) = 0$, as it must be for a matrix of rank at most $1$.

### What it gives you

Since $\det A$ is a unit, the matrix $A + u v^{\mathsf{T}}$ is invertible exactly when $1 + v^{\mathsf{T}} A^{-1} u$ is a unit.
The same factor is the denominator of the [[Sherman-Morrison formula|MDZDEFE53C]] for the inverse $(A + u v^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}$, so both formulas break down at the same time.

Changing one row of $A$ is a rank one update.
If row $i$ changes by a row vector $w^{\mathsf{T}}$, the new matrix is $A + e_i w^{\mathsf{T}}$, and the lemma gives the new determinant as $(1 + w^{\mathsf{T}} A^{-1} e_i) \det A$.
When $A^{-1}$ is known, this costs $O(n^2)$ operations, against about $n^3$ for a fresh elimination.

### Generalizations

Let $U$ and $V$ be $n \times m$ matrices.
Then
$$\det(A + U V^{\mathsf{T}}) = \det(I_m + V^{\mathsf{T}} A^{-1} U) \det A.$$
It follows from the [[Weinstein-Aronszajn identity|0G24VQ1088]] in the same way as the case $m = 1$, as the [[proof|NM2636CNW5]] explains.
So an update of rank $m$ needs only an $m \times m$ determinant.

### Watch out

The factor is $1 + v^{\mathsf{T}} A^{-1} u$, with the inverse of $A$, and not $1 + v^{\mathsf{T}} A u$.
In the example the wrong factor is $1 + 2 + 2 \cdot 3 = 9$, which gives $54$ and not $13$.
The order also matters: $v^{\mathsf{T}} A^{-1} u$ is a number, while $u v^{\mathsf{T}} A^{-1}$ is an $n \times n$ matrix.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose you know the determinant of a matrix and its inverse.
Now you change the matrix by adding a very simple matrix, a column times a row.
The determinant does not have to be computed again from scratch.
It gets multiplied by one number, and that number comes from the inverse in a single short calculation.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: find $\det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
The matrix is $I_3 + u u^{\mathsf{T}}$ with $u = (1, 1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$, so we take $A = I_3$ and $v = u$.
The factor is
$$1 + u^{\mathsf{T}} I_3^{-1} u = 1 + (1 + 1 + 1) = 4,$$
and $\det I_3 = 1$, so the determinant is $4$.
Check by the [[rule of Sarrus|7H2P40X0MV]]:
$$8 + 1 + 1 - 2 - 2 - 2 = 4.$$
In the same way, $\det(c I_n + u u^{\mathsf{T}}) = c^{n-1} (c + n)$ for $u = (1, \dots, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $c \ne 0$.
So matrices of the form "diagonal plus all ones" have determinants that we can read off.

### L3 human

The lemma is the rank one case of the Weinstein-Aronszajn identity, and its general form trades an $n \times n$ determinant for an $m \times m$ one.
In numerical work and in statistics it updates log-determinants of covariance matrices after a rank one change, together with the Sherman-Morrison formula for the inverse.
The form with the adjugate also follows from the multilinearity of the [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] in the columns.
Column $j$ of $A + u v^{\mathsf{T}}$ is $a_j + v_j u$, and every term of the expansion with $u$ in two columns vanishes, so $\det(A + u v^{\mathsf{T}}) = \det A + \sum_j v_j \det A_j(u)$, where $A_j(u)$ is $A$ with column $j$ replaced by $u$.
