## Statement

Let $\mathrm{BW}$ be the following sentence of $L_2$: every sequence $(x_n)_{n \in \N}$ of reals in $[0, 1]$ has a subsequence $(x_{n_k})_{k \in \N}$, with $n_0 < n_1 < \cdots$, that converges to a real $x$.

Then $\mathsf{RCA}_0 \vdash \mathrm{BW} \leftrightarrow \mathsf{ACA}_0$.
So the [[Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem|0KDJD249DT]] is equivalent to [[ACA0|K9XG0Z2N9V]] over [[RCA0|DNXCKXZZ08]], in the sense of [[reverse mathematics|TQTDKMBJ0F]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The sequence must be bounded. The sequence $x_n = n$ has no convergent subsequence.
- Convergence here is without a rate. With the extra demand $\abs{x_{n_k} - x} \le 2^{-k}$ the statement is still equivalent to $\mathsf{ACA}_0$, and the proof below gives this rate.

### Consequences

- In the $\omega$-model of the computable sets, the theorem fails: some computable increasing sequence of rationals in $[0, 1]$ has a limit that computes the halting set.
- Over $\mathsf{RCA}_0$, the monotone convergence theorem and the sequential compactness of $[0, 1]$ are equivalent to $\mathsf{ACA}_0$ by the same proof.

### Converse

Both directions are part of the statement.
The reversal shows that even the case of increasing sequences of rationals implies $\mathsf{ACA}_0$.

### Generalizations

The same holds for bounded sequences in $\R^n$ and for sequences in any compact separable metric space.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem says that every sequence in $[0, 1]$ has a convergent subsequence.
The classical proof keeps a half of the interval that contains infinitely many terms.
Deciding which half does so needs arithmetical comprehension, and the reversal shows that this is unavoidable.

### L2 human

The forward proof forms the set of dyadic intervals that contain infinitely many terms; this set is arithmetical.
The reversal uses the range lemma: from a one-to-one $f$ it builds the increasing sequence $c_n = \sum_{i \le n} 2^{-f(i) - 1}$.
Once the sum is within $2^{-m-1}$ of its limit, the value $m$ can no longer appear, so the limit decides the range of $f$.
A trap is to think that a limit of an increasing sequence comes with a modulus of convergence: the modulus is what the proof extracts from the limit.
