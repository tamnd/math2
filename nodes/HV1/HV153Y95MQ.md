## Statement

The statement that every sequence in the unit interval has a convergent subsequence has exactly the strength of arithmetical comprehension.

Let $\mathrm{BW}$ be the following sentence of $L_2$.
Every sequence $(x_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ of reals in $[0, 1]$ has a subsequence $(x_{n_k})_{k \in \mathbb{N}}$, with $n_0 < n_1 < \cdots$, that converges to a real $x$.

**Theorem.** $\mathsf{RCA}_0 \vdash \mathrm{BW} \leftrightarrow \mathsf{ACA}_0$.

So the [[Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem|0KDJD249DT]] is equivalent to [[ACA0|K9XG0Z2N9V]] over [[RCA0|DNXCKXZZ08]], in the sense of [[reverse mathematics|TQTDKMBJ0F]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The sequence must be bounded.
The sequence $x_n = n$ has no convergent subsequence, since any two of its terms are at distance at least $1$.

The theorem asks for convergence without a rate, but a rate costs nothing more.
With the extra demand $\lvert x_{n_k} - x \rvert \le 2^{-k}$, the statement is still equivalent to $\mathsf{ACA}_0$, and [[the proof|JM71Q0PJ42]] gives this rate.

### What it gives you

In the $\omega$-model of the computable sets, the theorem fails.
For a computable one-to-one $f$ that lists the halting set, the sequence $c_n = \sum_{i \le n} 2^{-f(i)-1}$ is a computable increasing sequence of rationals in $[0, 1]$.
Every subsequence converges to the same limit, and that limit computes the halting set.

### The converse

Both directions are part of the statement.
The reversal shows that even the case of increasing sequences of rationals implies $\mathsf{ACA}_0$.
So the [[monotone convergence theorem|G2S6H789RG]] is also equivalent to $\mathsf{ACA}_0$ over $\mathsf{RCA}_0$, by the same proof.

### Generalizations

The same equivalence holds for bounded sequences in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and for sequential compactness of compact metric spaces, coded as in second-order arithmetic.
In contrast, the [[Heine-Borel theorem|8G2GVRXWG5]] for the unit interval, a different form of compactness, is equivalent only to $\mathsf{WKL}_0$.

### Watch out

A limit of an increasing sequence does not come with a modulus of convergence.
In the reversal, the modulus is what the proof extracts from the limit, and it is the modulus that bounds the search for preimages.

## History

The theorem is named after Bernard Bolzano and Karl Weierstrass.
Its equivalence with $\mathsf{ACA}_0$ over $\mathsf{RCA}_0$ is one of the basic results of reverse mathematics in Simpson's book *Subsystems of Second Order Arithmetic* (1999).

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem says that every sequence in $[0, 1]$ has a convergent subsequence.
The classical proof keeps a half of the interval that contains infinitely many terms.
Deciding which half does so is a question with two number quantifiers, "for every $m$ there is $n > m$".
The reversal shows that this question cannot be avoided.

### L2 human

The forward proof forms the set of dyadic intervals that contain infinitely many terms, which is arithmetical.
The reversal uses the [[range lemma|3MQ39PRCYJ]]: from a one-to-one $f$ it builds the increasing sequence $c_n = \sum_{i \le n} 2^{-f(i) - 1}$.
For $f$ beginning $3, 0, 5, 1$, the first sums are $1/16$, $9/16$, $37/64$ and $53/64$, as in the [[concept of ACA0|NAASTN80QN]].
Once the sum is within $2^{-m-1}$ of its limit, the value $m$ can no longer appear, since its term $2^{-m-1}$ would overshoot.
So the limit decides the range of $f$.
