## Statement

For square matrices over a field, one of the two equations in the definition of the inverse already implies the other.
Let $F$ be a field and let $A$ and $B$ be $n \times n$ matrices over $F$.

1. If $BA = I_n$, then $AB = I_n$, so $A$ is [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] and $A^{-1} = B$.
2. If $AB = I_n$, then $BA = I_n$, so $A$ is invertible and $A^{-1} = B$.

## Remarks

### Half the work

The result halves the work of checking an inverse.
Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
One product is
$$BA = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 & 1 - 1 \\ -2 + 2 & -1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2,$$
and part 1 now gives $AB = I_2$ with no further work.
Check: $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 & -2 + 2 \\ 1 - 1 & -1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$.

### Why the matrices must be square

The result is false for a matrix that is not square.
The $2 \times 3$ matrix $A$ of the [[right inverse example|540E86V3N9]] has a $3 \times 2$ matrix $B$ with $AB = I_2$, but $BA \ne I_3$.
The reason is a count of dimensions: $BA$ is a $3 \times 3$ matrix that factors through $F^2$, so its rank is at most $2$ and it cannot be $I_3$.

### Why the size must be finite

The proofs need a finite number of rows and columns.
On the space of all real sequences, the right shift $S(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ and the left shift $L(x_1, x_2, \dots) = (x_2, x_3, \dots)$ satisfy $LS = \id$.
But $SL(1, 0, 0, \dots) = (0, 0, 0, \dots)$, so $SL \ne \id$.
As infinite matrices, $LS = I$ and $SL \ne I$; the [[shift operators|MRJ5JPW9HN]] work this out.

### What it gives you

A square matrix over a field with a left inverse or a right inverse is invertible.
This gives the converse half of the [[inverse of a product|0S4WTRE9GG]]: if a product of square matrices is invertible, then each factor is invertible.
It also lets the [[invertible matrix theorem|ACDFDABVQK]] list "$A$ has a left inverse" and "$A$ has a right inverse" among its equivalent conditions.

### The converse

The converse holds by the definition: if $A$ is invertible, then $B = A^{-1}$ satisfies both $AB = I_n$ and $BA = I_n$.

### Generalizations

The statement holds over any commutative ring, by the [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]].
If $BA = I_n$, then $\det B \det A = 1$, so $\det A$ is a unit, and $A$ is invertible by the [[determinant criterion|03Q7RE2HXR]].
It can fail over a non-commutative ring, even for $1 \times 1$ matrices: in the ring of linear maps of the space of sequences, $L$ has the right inverse $S$ but no left inverse.
For linear maps, the statement is the fact that an [[injective endomorphism of a finite-dimensional space is surjective|0912EEEVCT]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

For square matrices, we only need to check one of $AB = I$ and $BA = I$.
The other one then holds as well.
This saves half of the work when we check an inverse.

### L2 human

The typical use is to verify a guessed inverse with one product, or to turn a one-sided identity into a two-sided one in a proof.
For example, if $A^2 - A - I_n = 0$, then $A(A - I_n) = I_n$, and the result gives $A^{-1} = A - I_n$ at once.
Watch out: the shortcut needs square matrices, and it fails for the $2 \times 3$ example above.

### L3 human

Two proofs are given.
The [[rank-nullity proof|W4WRRCVKRT]] shows that $x \mapsto Ax$ is injective, hence surjective, hence has a right inverse.
The [[elimination proof|MT0Z14F36B]] shows that $Ax = 0$ has only the trivial solution, so the reduced row echelon form of $A$ is $I_n$.
Both proofs use finite dimension in an essential way, and the shift operators show that the statement fails without it.
In ring language, the matrix ring $M_n(F)$ is Dedekind-finite: $ba = 1$ implies $ab = 1$.
