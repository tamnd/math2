## Statement

Let $F$ be a field and let $A$ and $B$ be $n \times n$ matrices over $F$.

1. If $BA = I_n$, then $AB = I_n$. So $A$ is [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] and $A^{-1} = B$.
2. If $AB = I_n$, then $BA = I_n$. So $A$ is invertible and $A^{-1} = B$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The matrices must be square.
The $2 \times 3$ matrix $A$ of the [[right inverse example|~710ae60f]] has a right inverse $B$ with $AB = I_2$, but $BA \ne I_3$.
The space must be finite-dimensional.
On the space of all sequences, the [[left and right shifts|~aecb4854]] satisfy $LS = \id$ and $SL \ne \id$; as infinite matrices, $LS = I$ and $SL \ne I$.

### Consequences

- To check that $B = A^{-1}$ for a square matrix $A$, one product is enough.
- A square matrix over a field with a left inverse or a right inverse is invertible.
- This gives the converse part of the [[inverse of a product|0S4WTRE9GG]]: if a product of square matrices is invertible, then each factor is invertible.

### Converse

The converse is part of the definition: if $A$ is invertible, then $B = A^{-1}$ satisfies both $AB = I_n$ and $BA = I_n$.

### Generalizations

The statement holds over each commutative ring, by the [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]].
If $BA = I_n$, then $\det B \det A = 1$, so $\det A$ is a unit.
So $A$ is invertible by the [[determinant criterion|03Q7RE2HXR]].
It fails over some non-commutative rings, even for $1 \times 1$ matrices.
In the ring of linear maps of the space of sequences, the left shift has a right inverse and no left inverse.
For linear maps, the same statement is the fact that an [[injective endomorphism of a finite-dimensional space is surjective|0912EEEVCT]].

## History

The fact is standard in the matrix algebra of the nineteenth century.
Modern textbooks give it as part of the [[invertible matrix theorem|~9b681771]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

For square matrices, we only need to check one of $AB = I$ and $BA = I$.
The other one then holds automatically.
This saves half of the work when we check an inverse.

### L2 human

Worked example: let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 & 1 - 1 \\ -2 + 2 & -1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$.
2. By the proposition, $AB = I_2$ without further work.
3. As a check: $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 & -2 + 2 \\ 1 - 1 & -1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$.

The trap is to use this shortcut for non-square matrices, where it fails.

### L3 human

Two proofs are given.
The [[rank-nullity proof|W4WRRCVKRT]] shows that $x \mapsto Ax$ is injective, hence surjective, hence has a right inverse.
The [[elimination proof|~af7c15b5]] shows that $Ax = 0$ has only the trivial solution, so the reduced row echelon form of $A$ is $I_n$.
Both proofs use finite dimension in an essential way, and the shift operators show that the statement fails without it.
In ring language: the matrix ring $M_n(F)$ is Dedekind-finite, that is, $ba = 1$ implies $ab = 1$.
