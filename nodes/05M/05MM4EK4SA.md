## Statement

Let $p$ be a [[prime number|01826KMG2H]] and let $G$ be a [[group|G5S1M9]] of [[order|0B85S2V2DR]] $p$, with identity $e$.
Then:

1. each element $g \neq e$ of $G$ generates $G$, so $G$ is [[cyclic|0KVGH0J5B3]];
2. the only subgroups of $G$ are $\{e\}$ and $G$, so $G$ is a [[simple group|0YMYMNQ0QZ]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The order must be prime. The [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]] has order $4$, and each element other than $e$ has order $2$, so it is not cyclic.
- For order $1$, which is not prime, the group $\{e\}$ is cyclic, but it is not simple, because a simple group must be nontrivial. Also part 1 says nothing, because there is no $g \neq e$.
- For order $6$, which is not prime, the group $S_3$ is not cyclic, and it has the subgroup $A_3$ of order $3$.

### Consequences

- Each group of order $p$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, by the theorem that [[cyclic groups of the same order are isomorphic|0736AXH2ME]].
- So up to isomorphism there is exactly one group of each prime order. The groups of order $2$, $3$, $5$ and $7$ are all cyclic and abelian.
- A group of order $p$ has exactly $p - 1$ generators, namely all elements other than $e$.
- Two distinct subgroups of order $p$ in a group meet only in $\{e\}$. Their intersection is a subgroup of each of them, so it is $\{e\}$ or the whole subgroup.

### Converse

The converse of part 1 is false: a cyclic group need not have prime order, as $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ shows.
The converse of part 2 is true for abelian groups.
In an abelian group each subgroup is normal, so an abelian simple group has no subgroups except $\{e\}$ and itself.
Such a group is generated by each $g \neq e$, and it is finite of prime order, because $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ with $n$ composite have other subgroups.
For nonabelian groups the converse fails: the alternating group $A_5$ of order $60$ is simple.

### Generalizations

- Each group of order $p^2$ is abelian. It is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z}$ or to $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, so it need not be cyclic.
- Each group of order $n$ is cyclic if and only if $\gcd(n, \varphi(n)) = 1$. For example, each group of order $15$ is cyclic, because $\varphi(15) = 8$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A prime number such as $7$ has no divisors except $1$ and $7$.
The size of a subgroup must divide the size of the group.
So a group with $7$ elements has only subgroups with $1$ or $7$ elements.
Take any element other than the identity, and collect all its powers.
They form a subgroup with more than $1$ element, so they fill the whole group.
Thus each group with $7$ elements works like a clock with $7$ hours.

### L2 human

We check part 1 for $G = \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ with addition.

1. Take $g = 2$. Its multiples are $2, 4, 1, 3, 0$.
2. All five elements occur, so $2$ generates $G$.
3. Take $g = 3$. Its multiples are $3, 1, 4, 2, 0$, so $3$ generates $G$ too.
4. The same holds for $1$ and $4$, as the theorem states.

The typical use is in classification: once a group has prime order, its structure is known.
It also appears inside proofs, for example to show that a subgroup of order $p$ is generated by any of its elements other than $e$.

A trap is to extend the result to prime powers.
The group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has order $4 = 2^2$, but it is not cyclic.

### L3 human

The cyclic groups of prime order are exactly the abelian simple groups.
A finite group is solvable if and only if each of its composition factors is cyclic of prime order.
The composition factors of a finite group are unique up to order, by the Jordan-Hölder theorem.
The automorphism group of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is the group of units $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$, which has order $p - 1$.
Groups of order $p$ also appear as the subgroups given by Cauchy's theorem, and the Sylow theorems build on them.
