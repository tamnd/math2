## Summary

A [[group|G5S1M9]] $G$ is locally cyclic if every [[finitely generated|082DWWXMRJ]] [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] of $G$ is [[cyclic|0KVGH0J5B3]].
Equivalently, any two elements $a, b \in G$ generate a cyclic subgroup $\langle a, b \rangle$.
Every cyclic group is locally cyclic, and a finitely generated locally cyclic group is cyclic.
The additive group $\mathbb{Q}$ and the Prüfer groups are locally cyclic but not cyclic.
Every locally cyclic group is [[abelian|082GHNPJ53]], and by a theorem of Ore, a group is locally cyclic exactly when its [[lattice of subgroups|0B8BW4YWTF]] is [[distributive|0HM0HQCK6N]].

## Motivation

A cyclic group is generated by one element.
Many natural groups, such as $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$, are not finitely generated, so they cannot be cyclic.
But in these groups, each finite set of elements lies in a cyclic subgroup.
For example, in $\mathbb{Q}$, the fractions $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $\tfrac{1}{3}$ lie in $\langle \tfrac{1}{6} \rangle$.
Locally cyclic groups are the groups that behave like cyclic groups on every finite set of elements.
They are the largest class of groups whose subgroup lattice has the same simple structure as that of a cyclic group.

## Notation

There is no special symbol.
The word "locally" follows a general convention: a group is locally $\mathcal{P}$ if every finitely generated subgroup has the property $\mathcal{P}$.
Other examples of this convention are locally finite groups and locally nilpotent groups.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. Every cyclic group is locally cyclic, because [[every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic|0KB1ND7XDS]].
2. [[The additive group $\mathbb{Q}$ is locally cyclic|E1EFH8EV3G]]. If $a_i = m_i / n_i$ for $i = 1, \ldots, k$, then each $a_i$ is an integer multiple of $1/(n_1 \cdots n_k)$. So $\langle a_1, \ldots, a_k \rangle$ is a subgroup of the cyclic group $\langle 1/(n_1 \cdots n_k) \rangle$, and it is cyclic.
3. [[The Prüfer group $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ is locally cyclic|PHZ7YGQEEP]].
4. Every subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}$, such as $\mathbb{Z}[1/p] = \{m/p^k : m \in \mathbb{Z},\ k \geq 0\}$, is locally cyclic, by property 3 below.
5. The group $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ is locally cyclic, by property 3, because it is a quotient of $\mathbb{Q}$.

### Non-examples

1. [[The group $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$|VH8C6HD8KP]] is abelian but not locally cyclic: it is finitely generated by $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, and it is not cyclic.
2. [[The Klein four-group|DX6X1D21FE]] is abelian but not locally cyclic: it is generated by two elements and is not cyclic.
3. The group $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ is not locally cyclic. The subgroup $\langle 1, \sqrt{2} \rangle$ is not cyclic, because $1 = kc$ and $\sqrt{2} = lc$ with integers $k \neq 0$, $l$ would give $\sqrt{2} = l/k$, a rational number.
4. The symmetric group $S_3$ is not locally cyclic, because it is not abelian.

### Basic properties

1. Two generators suffice. Suppose $\langle a, b \rangle$ is cyclic for all $a, b \in G$. By induction on $k$, every subgroup $\langle a_1, \ldots, a_k \rangle$ is cyclic: if $\langle a_1, \ldots, a_{k-1} \rangle = \langle c \rangle$, then $\langle a_1, \ldots, a_k \rangle = \langle c, a_k \rangle$, which is cyclic. For $k = 0$ and $k = 1$ the subgroup is $\{e\}$ or $\langle a_1 \rangle$.
2. Abelian. For $a, b \in G$, the subgroup $\langle a, b \rangle$ is cyclic, so it is abelian, and $ab = ba$.
3. Subgroups and quotients. Let $H \leq G$. A finitely generated subgroup of $H$ is a finitely generated subgroup of $G$, so it is cyclic. Let $f : G \to G'$ be a surjective [[homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]]. A subgroup $\langle f(x_1), \ldots, f(x_k) \rangle$ of $G'$ is the image of the cyclic group $\langle x_1, \ldots, x_k \rangle$, so it is cyclic.
4. Torsion or torsion-free. Suppose $x \in G$ has infinite order and $y \in G$ has finite order. Then $\langle x, y \rangle$ is cyclic and infinite, so it is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$, whose only element of finite order is $0$. So $y = e$. Hence $G$ is a [[torsion group|JZNQTTMG58]], or every element other than $e$ has infinite order.
5. Ore's theorem, first direction. If $G$ is locally cyclic, then its lattice of subgroups is distributive: $A \cap (B + C) = (A \cap B) + (A \cap C)$ for all subgroups $A, B, C$. We write $G$ additively, since it is abelian.
   1. The inclusion $(A \cap B) + (A \cap C) \subseteq A \cap (B + C)$ holds in every abelian group, because both $A \cap B$ and $A \cap C$ lie in $A$ and in $B + C$.
   2. First let $G = \mathbb{Z}$. Its subgroups are $x\mathbb{Z}$ for $x \geq 0$, and $x\mathbb{Z} \cap y\mathbb{Z} = \operatorname{lcm}(x, y)\mathbb{Z}$ and $x\mathbb{Z} + y\mathbb{Z} = \gcd(x, y)\mathbb{Z}$, with $\operatorname{lcm}(x, 0) = 0$ and $\gcd(x, 0) = x$.
   3. For a prime $p$, let $v_p(x)$ be the power of $p$ in $x \geq 1$, and put $v_p(0) = \infty$. Then $v_p(\gcd) = \min$ and $v_p(\operatorname{lcm}) = \max$ of the values $v_p$. By the [[fundamental theorem of arithmetic|084HH1NDCB]], two integers $x, y \geq 0$ with $v_p(x) = v_p(y)$ for every prime $p$ are equal.
   4. In the totally ordered set $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots\} \cup \{\infty\}$, we have $\max(u, \min(v, w)) = \min(\max(u, v), \max(u, w))$ for all $u, v, w$, by a check of the cases $v \leq w$ and $w \leq v$. Apply this with $u = v_p(x)$, $v = v_p(y)$ and $w = v_p(z)$, for every prime $p$. By steps 2 and 3, this gives $\operatorname{lcm}(x, \gcd(y, z)) = \gcd(\operatorname{lcm}(x, y), \operatorname{lcm}(x, z))$, which is the distributive law for the subgroups $x\mathbb{Z}, y\mathbb{Z}, z\mathbb{Z}$ of $\mathbb{Z}$.
   5. Every cyclic group $K$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for some $n \geq 0$. By the [[correspondence theorem|0S9ZVZ34MD]], the subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ correspond to the subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$ that contain $n\mathbb{Z}$, and this bijection preserves inclusion. So it preserves intersections and sums, which are the greatest lower bounds and least upper bounds for inclusion. So the distributive law holds for subgroups of every cyclic group.
   6. Now let $G$ be locally cyclic, and let $a \in A \cap (B + C)$. Write $a = b + c$ with $b \in B$ and $c \in C$. The subgroup $K = \langle a, b, c \rangle$ is cyclic.
   7. Apply step 5 to the subgroups $A \cap K$, $B \cap K$ and $C \cap K$ of $K$. Since $a \in A \cap K$ and $a = b + c$ with $b \in B \cap K$ and $c \in C \cap K$, we get $a \in (A \cap B \cap K) + (A \cap C \cap K) \subseteq (A \cap B) + (A \cap C)$.
6. Ore's theorem, second direction. If the lattice of subgroups of a group $G$ is distributive, then $G$ is locally cyclic. We do not prove this direction here. It needs more work, because $G$ is not assumed to be abelian.

### History

Øystein Ore proved in 1938 that a group is locally cyclic if and only if its lattice of subgroups is distributive.

### Common errors

1. A student thinks that locally cyclic means "every element generates a cyclic subgroup". That holds in every group. The condition is about subgroups generated by two or more elements.
2. A student thinks that a locally cyclic group is cyclic. The group $\mathbb{Q}$ is locally cyclic, but it is not finitely generated, so it is not cyclic.
3. A student assumes that every abelian group is locally cyclic. The groups $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ are abelian, but they are not locally cyclic.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A cyclic group is a group in which every element is a multiple of one basic element.
The fractions do not form a cyclic group, because no single fraction has all other fractions as its multiples.
But any finite list of fractions has a common building block: for $\tfrac{1}{2}$, $\tfrac{2}{3}$ and $\tfrac{3}{4}$, every one of them is a multiple of $\tfrac{1}{12}$.
A group with this property, that every finite list of elements has a common building block, is called locally cyclic.

### L2 human

We check that $\langle \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{5}{4} \rangle$ is cyclic in $\mathbb{Q}$ and find a generator.

1. Over the common denominator $12$, we have $\tfrac{2}{3} = \tfrac{8}{12}$ and $\tfrac{5}{4} = \tfrac{15}{12}$.
2. So $\langle \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{5}{4} \rangle = \tfrac{1}{12}\langle 8, 15 \rangle$, where $\langle 8, 15 \rangle$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$.
3. Since $\gcd(8, 15) = 1$, we have $\langle 8, 15 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}$, for example $1 = 2 \cdot 8 - 15$.
4. So $\langle \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{5}{4} \rangle = \langle \tfrac{1}{12} \rangle$.

The typical use of the concept is to transfer facts about cyclic groups to groups that are not finitely generated.
For example, the distributive law for subgroups of $\mathbb{Q}$ follows from the same law in the cyclic groups $\langle 1/n \rangle$.

A trap: in $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$, each element generates a cyclic subgroup, but the two elements $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ do not.

### L3 human

The locally cyclic groups have a full classification.
By property 4, each one is torsion or torsion-free.
A torsion-free locally cyclic group is isomorphic to a subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}$, and these are the torsion-free abelian groups of rank at most $1$.
A torsion locally cyclic group is isomorphic to a subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$.
Its $p$-primary part is, for each prime $p$, either cyclic of order $p^k$ for some $k \geq 0$ or the Prüfer group $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$.

Ore's theorem places locally cyclic groups in lattice theory.
The subgroup lattice of a group is always modular when the group is abelian, but it is distributive only in the locally cyclic case.
For example, the lattice of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has three subgroups of order $2$, and with any two of them as $B$ and $C$ and the third as $A$, we get $A \cap (B + C) = A$, but $(A \cap B) + (A \cap C) = \{0\}$.
For a finite group, Ore's theorem says that the subgroup lattice is distributive exactly when the group is cyclic.
In this case, the lattice is the lattice of the positive divisors of $|G|$, as in the [[fundamental theorem of cyclic groups|0423TXR9AN]].
