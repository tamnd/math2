## Summary

The cross product of two [[vectors|0QN3D80AWP]] $a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$ and $b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the vector
$$a \times b = (a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2, \; a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3, \; a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1),$$
as the [[formal definition|0SEDGXG9S3]] states.
It is [[perpendicular to $a$ and to $b$|0T4QHJXSD4]], its [[length|0XKBKGTCPC]] is the area $\|a\| \, \|b\| \sin \theta$ of the parallelogram with the sides $a$ and $b$, and $a$, $b$, $a \times b$ follow the right-hand rule.
The operation is defined only on $\mathbb{R}^3$; a product with these properties exists in no other dimension except $7$ (and the trivial dimensions $0$ and $1$).
The cross product matters because it produces a normal vector to a plane, measures areas in space, and describes torque, angular momentum and magnetic forces.

## Motivation

In space, two vectors $a$ and $b$ that are not parallel span a plane through the origin.
Many problems need a vector perpendicular to this plane: the normal of a plane, the axis of a rotation, the direction of a torque.
Such a vector $n$ must satisfy $n \cdot a = 0$ and $n \cdot b = 0$, two linear equations in three unknowns.
Solving them by elimination gives, up to a factor, exactly the vector $a \times b$.
Its length then turns out to be the area of the parallelogram spanned by $a$ and $b$, so one vector records both the direction of the plane and the size of the area.

## Notation

- The common notation is $a \times b$, and the name is cross product or vector product.
- Some books, for example in France and in Russia, write $a \wedge b$ or $[a, b]$. In exterior algebra $a \wedge b$ is a different object, a bivector, so the symbol $\wedge$ needs care.
- A memory aid writes the product as a formal determinant with the first row $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$, $\mathbf{k}$:
$$a \times b = \det \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
- In Mathlib the cross product is `crossProduct a b`, written `a ×₃ b`.

## Remarks

### Examples

- $\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}$, $\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{k} = \mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{i} = \mathbf{j}$, by the formula.
- $(1, 2, 3) \times (4, 5, 6) = (-3, 6, -3)$. The [[worked example|P7X5FSFQZ1]] checks perpendicularity and Lagrange's identity.
- The triangle with the vertices $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 2, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 3)$ has the area $\tfrac{7}{2}$, because the cross product of two sides is $(6, 3, 2)$, of length $7$. The [[area example|Q6QW96GXK3]] gives the steps.
- Torque: a force $F = (0, 10, 0)$ newtons at the point $r = (0.5, 0, 0)$ metres gives the torque $r \times F = (0, 0, 5)$ newton metres about the origin.
- For parallel vectors the product is $0$: $(1, 2, 3) \times (2, 4, 6) = (12 - 12, 6 - 6, 4 - 4) = 0$.

### Non-examples

- The cross product is not associative: $(\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{i}) \times \mathbf{j} = 0$, but $\mathbf{i} \times (\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j}) = -\mathbf{j}$. The [[non-associativity example|VRA3B0V8CD]] gives the details.
- There is no cross product on the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$: the only vector perpendicular to $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ is $0$, but the area is $1$. The [[non-example for the plane|D96BEM2GMS]] gives the details.
- The number $a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1$ for $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is sometimes called the "2D cross product", but it is a number, the determinant of $a$ and $b$, not a vector.

### Basic properties

For all $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and real $s$:

- [[Anticommutativity|07ZS685JJC]]: $b \times a = -(a \times b)$, and so $a \times a = 0$.
- Bilinearity: $(sa + c) \times b = s(a \times b) + c \times b$, and the same in the second factor. Each component is a sum of products with one factor from $a$ and one from $b$.
- [[Perpendicularity|0T4QHJXSD4]]: $a \cdot (a \times b) = 0$ and $b \cdot (a \times b) = 0$.
- [[Lagrange's identity|W8V1S8TFWF]]: $\|a \times b\|^2 = \|a\|^2 \|b\|^2 - (a \cdot b)^2$.
- [[Length and area|SAZ353S8W0]]: $\|a \times b\| = \|a\| \, \|b\| \sin \theta$, the area of the parallelogram; and $a \times b = 0$ if and only if $a$ and $b$ are parallel.
- No cancellation: $a \times b = a \times c$ does not give $b = c$. For $a = \mathbf{i}$, $b = \mathbf{j}$ and $c = \mathbf{i} + \mathbf{j}$, both products are $\mathbf{k}$.
- The [[vector triple product|03FTB26QYD]] $a \times (b \times c) = (a \cdot c)\, b - (a \cdot b)\, c$ and the Jacobi identity replace associativity.

### History

Hamilton's quaternions of 1843 contain the cross product as the vector part of the product of two pure quaternions.
Gibbs and Heaviside made the cross product a separate operation in the 1880s, and Gibbs introduced the notation $a \times b$.
Massey showed in 1983 that a bilinear product with the perpendicularity and length properties exists only in dimensions $3$ and $7$, apart from trivial cases.
The case of dimension $7$ comes from the octonions, as the case of dimension $3$ comes from the quaternions.

### Common errors

- Students compute the middle component as $a_1 b_3 - a_3 b_1$. The correct component is $a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3$, the negative of that.
- Students treat the cross product as commutative or associative. Swapping the factors changes the sign, and the grouping of three factors matters.
- Students apply the cross product to vectors of $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^4$. In the plane, embed the vectors as $(a_1, a_2, 0)$ first; in $\mathbb{R}^4$ there is no cross product.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The cross product takes two arrows in space and gives a third arrow.
The new arrow stands at a right angle to both given arrows.
Its length is the area of the parallelogram that the two arrows span.
For the arrows $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$ on the floor, the cross product is $(0, 0, 1)$, which points straight up.
The square with these sides has area $1$, which is the length of $(0, 0, 1)$.

### L2 human

Worked example: a normal vector to the plane through $P = (1, 1, 0)$, $Q = (2, 0, 1)$ and $R = (0, 2, 2)$.

1. Two sides: $a = Q - P = (1, -1, 1)$ and $b = R - P = (-1, 1, 2)$.
2. First component: $a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2 = (-1)(2) - (1)(1) = -3$.
3. Second component: $a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3 = (1)(-1) - (1)(2) = -3$.
4. Third component: $a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 = (1)(1) - (-1)(-1) = 0$.
5. So $n = a \times b = (-3, -3, 0)$.
6. Check: $n \cdot a = -3 + 3 + 0 = 0$ and $n \cdot b = 3 - 3 + 0 = 0$.
7. The triangle $PQR$ has the area $\tfrac{1}{2}\|n\| = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{18} = \tfrac{3\sqrt{2}}{2}$.

The typical uses are normal vectors of planes, areas of triangles in space, and torques.
A trap: the order of the factors fixes the sign; for a normal vector the sign does not matter, but for a torque or an orientation it does.

### L3 human

The cross product is the composite of two constructions: the exterior product $a \wedge b$ in $\Lambda^2 \mathbb{R}^3$, and the Hodge star, which identifies $\Lambda^2 \mathbb{R}^3$ with $\mathbb{R}^3$ by the dot product and the orientation.
This explains why it exists only in dimension $3$ as a product of two vectors: in $\mathbb{R}^n$, $\Lambda^2$ has dimension $\binom{n}{2}$, which equals $n$ only for $n = 3$; the [[exterior algebra|0MDMZ965VE]] keeps the general construction.
With the cross product as bracket, $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a [[Lie algebra|5JJYBJ6A8K]], isomorphic to the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ of infinitesimal rotations; the vector triple product gives the [[Jacobi identity|NDDB9EDN84]].
The seven-dimensional product comes from the multiplication of imaginary [[octonions|1HRMT3DSX2]], and [[Hurwitz's theorem on composition algebras|WZ81H3S4KK]] explains why only $3$ and $7$ occur.
The sub-area on [[determinants|DTBK11SR77]] shows that $a \cdot (b \times c) = \det(a, b, c)$, the signed volume.
