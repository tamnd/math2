## Summary

The cross product turns two vectors in space into a third vector that stands at a right angle to both, with a length equal to the area they span.
For [[vectors|0QN3D80AWP]] $a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$ and $b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$ it is
$$a \times b = (a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2, \; a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3, \; a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1),$$
as the [[formal definition|0SEDGXG9S3]] states.
It is [[perpendicular to $a$ and to $b$|0T4QHJXSD4]], its [[length|0XKBKGTCPC]] is the area $\|a\| \, \|b\| \sin \theta$ of the parallelogram with sides $a$ and $b$, and $a$, $b$, $a \times b$ follow the right-hand rule.
That makes it the standard tool for normal vectors of planes, areas and volumes in space, and torques in mechanics.
The price is that it lives only in $\mathbb{R}^3$: apart from trivial cases, a product with these properties exists only in dimensions $3$ and $7$.

## Motivation

In space, two vectors $a$ and $b$ that are not parallel span a plane through the origin.
Many problems need a vector perpendicular to that plane: the normal of a plane, the axis of a rotation, the direction of a torque.
Such a vector $n$ must satisfy two linear equations,
$$n \cdot a = 0, \qquad n \cdot b = 0,$$
in three unknowns, so the solutions form a line.
Solving the two equations by elimination produces, up to a factor, exactly the vector $a \times b$.
Its length then turns out to be the area of the parallelogram spanned by $a$ and $b$.
So a single vector records both the direction of the plane and the size of the area.

## Notation

The usual notation is $a \times b$, and the operation is also called the vector product.
Books in France write $a \wedge b$, and books in Russia often write $[a, b]$.
In exterior algebra the symbol $a \wedge b$ means a different object, a bivector, so it needs care.
A memory aid writes the product as a formal determinant whose first row holds the unit vectors $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$, $\mathbf{k}$:
$$a \times b = \det \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
In Mathlib the cross product is `crossProduct a b`, written `a ×₃ b`.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Start with the unit vectors.
The formula gives $\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}$, $\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{k} = \mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{i} = \mathbf{j}$, a cyclic pattern worth remembering.

For $a = (1, 2, 3)$ and $b = (4, 5, 6)$ the three components are
$$2 \cdot 6 - 3 \cdot 5 = -3, \qquad 3 \cdot 4 - 1 \cdot 6 = 6, \qquad 1 \cdot 5 - 2 \cdot 4 = -3,$$
so $a \times b = (-3, 6, -3)$.
The [[worked example|P7X5FSFQZ1]] checks that this vector is perpendicular to both factors and that its length obeys Lagrange's identity.
For parallel vectors the product vanishes: $(1, 2, 3) \times (2, 4, 6) = (12 - 12, 6 - 6, 4 - 4) = 0$.

### Areas and torques

The length of the cross product measures area.
The triangle with corners $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 2, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 3)$ has two sides whose cross product is $(6, 3, 2)$, of length $7$, so its area is $\tfrac{7}{2}$.
The [[area example|Q6QW96GXK3]] gives the steps.

In mechanics the torque of a force $F$ applied at the point $r$ is $r \times F$.
A force $F = (0, 10, 0)$ newtons at $r = (0.5, 0, 0)$ meters gives the torque $(0, 0, 5)$ newton meters about the origin, an axis pointing straight up.

### The rules you can use

For all $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and real $s$ the following rules hold.

- [[Anticommutativity|07ZS685JJC]]: $b \times a = -(a \times b)$, and so $a \times a = 0$.
- Bilinearity: $(sa + c) \times b = s(a \times b) + c \times b$, and the same in the second factor, because each component is a sum of products with one factor from $a$ and one from $b$.
- [[Perpendicularity|0T4QHJXSD4]]: $a \cdot (a \times b) = 0$ and $b \cdot (a \times b) = 0$.
- [[Lagrange's identity|W8V1S8TFWF]]: $\|a \times b\|^2 = \|a\|^2 \|b\|^2 - (a \cdot b)^2$.
- [[Length and area|SAZ353S8W0]]: $\|a \times b\| = \|a\| \, \|b\| \sin \theta$, so $a \times b = 0$ exactly when $a$ and $b$ are parallel.

In place of associativity there are the [[vector triple product|03FTB26QYD]] $a \times (b \times c) = (a \cdot c)\, b - (a \cdot b)\, c$ and the [[Jacobi identity|NDDB9EDN84]].

### What the cross product is not

It is not associative.
The product $(\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{i}) \times \mathbf{j}$ is $0$, while $\mathbf{i} \times (\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j}) = \mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{k} = -\mathbf{j}$, as the [[non-associativity example|VRA3B0V8CD]] shows.
It does not allow cancellation either: for $a = \mathbf{i}$, both $a \times \mathbf{j}$ and $a \times (\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{j})$ equal $\mathbf{k}$.

There is no cross product on the plane.
The only vector of $\mathbb{R}^2$ perpendicular to both $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ is $0$, yet their square has area $1$, as the [[non-example for the plane|D96BEM2GMS]] explains.
The number $a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1$, sometimes called the "2D cross product", is the determinant of $a$ and $b$, a number and not a vector.

### Watch out

The middle component is where most errors happen.
It is $a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3$, not $a_1 b_3 - a_3 b_1$; the determinant form explains the minus sign in front of the $\mathbf{j}$ term.
The order of the factors matters too, because swapping them flips the sign.
To use the cross product with vectors of the plane, embed them first as $(a_1, a_2, 0)$; in $\mathbb{R}^4$ there is no cross product of two vectors at all.

### History

Hamilton's quaternions of 1843 contain the cross product as the vector part of the product of two pure quaternions.
Gibbs and Heaviside made it a separate operation in the 1880s, and the notation $a \times b$ comes from Gibbs.
Massey showed in 1983 that a bilinear product with the perpendicularity and length properties exists only in dimensions $3$ and $7$, apart from trivial cases.
Dimension $7$ comes from the octonions, just as dimension $3$ comes from the quaternions.

### Try it

Compute $(2, 0, 1) \times (1, 3, 0)$.
The components are $0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 3 = -3$, then $1 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 0 = 1$, then $2 \cdot 3 - 0 \cdot 1 = 6$, so the answer is $(-3, 1, 6)$.
Check that its dot products with both factors are $0$.

Find the area of the parallelogram with sides $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$.
The cross product is $(1, -1, 1)$, so the area is $\sqrt{3}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The cross product takes two arrows in space and gives a third arrow.
The new arrow stands at a right angle to both given arrows.
Its length is the area of the parallelogram that the two arrows span.
For the arrows $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$ on the floor, the cross product is $(0, 0, 1)$, which points straight up.
The square with these sides has area $1$, which is the length of $(0, 0, 1)$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical problem: find a normal vector to the plane through $P = (1, 1, 0)$, $Q = (2, 0, 1)$ and $R = (0, 2, 2)$.
Two sides from $P$ are $a = Q - P = (1, -1, 1)$ and $b = R - P = (-1, 1, 2)$.
The three components of $a \times b$ are
$$(-1)(2) - (1)(1) = -3, \qquad (1)(-1) - (1)(2) = -3, \qquad (1)(1) - (-1)(-1) = 0,$$
so $n = (-3, -3, 0)$.
Check: $n \cdot a = -3 + 3 + 0 = 0$ and $n \cdot b = 3 - 3 + 0 = 0$.
As a bonus, the triangle $PQR$ has area $\tfrac{1}{2}\|n\| = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{18} = \tfrac{3\sqrt{2}}{2}$.
For a normal vector the sign does not matter, but for a torque or an orientation it does, so keep the order of the factors.

### L3 human

The cross product combines two constructions: the exterior product $a \wedge b$ in $\Lambda^2 \mathbb{R}^3$, and the Hodge star, which identifies $\Lambda^2 \mathbb{R}^3$ with $\mathbb{R}^3$ using the dot product and the orientation.
This explains why a product of two vectors of this kind is special to dimension $3$: in $\mathbb{R}^n$ the space $\Lambda^2$ has dimension $\binom{n}{2}$, which equals $n$ only for $n = 3$, and the [[exterior algebra|0MDMZ965VE]] keeps the general construction.
With the cross product as bracket, $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a [[Lie algebra|5JJYBJ6A8K]], isomorphic to the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ of infinitesimal rotations.
The seven-dimensional product comes from the multiplication of imaginary [[octonions|1HRMT3DSX2]], and [[Hurwitz's theorem on composition algebras|WZ81H3S4KK]] explains why only $3$ and $7$ occur.
The sub-area on [[determinants|DTBK11SR77]] shows that $a \cdot (b \times c) = \det(a, b, c)$, the signed volume.
