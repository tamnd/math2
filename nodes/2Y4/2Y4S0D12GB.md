## Statement

Let $G = (V, E)$ be a finite [[graph|00C0BR7D7H]] and let $s \colon E \to \set{+1, -1}$ assign a sign to each edge, so that $(G, s)$ is a signed graph.
Call $(G, s)$ *balanced* if every [[cycle|0Z05VCBQ7Y]] of $G$ has an even number of negative edges.
Equivalently, the product of the signs along each cycle is $+1$.
Then $(G, s)$ is balanced if and only if $V$ is the disjoint union of two sets $V_1$ and $V_2$, possibly one empty, with these properties:

1. every positive edge joins two vertices of the same set;
2. every negative edge joins a vertex of $V_1$ to a vertex of $V_2$.

## Remarks

If such a split exists, a cycle crosses between $V_1$ and $V_2$ an even number of times, so it has an even number of negative edges.
Conversely, it suffices to treat each [[connected|0KWFFMDF8T]] component.
Fix a vertex $r$ in it.
Put $v$ in $V_1$ or $V_2$ according to the parity of the number of negative edges on a [[path|03NAMV1DHJ]] from $r$ to $v$.
Balance makes this parity the same for all paths, and the split then has the required property.
Example: three people where $a$ and $b$ are friends and both dislike $c$ form a balanced triangle, with $V_1 = \set{a, b}$ and $V_2 = \set{c}$.
A triangle with exactly one negative edge is not balanced, and no split works for it.
When all edges are negative, balance means having no odd cycle.
So in that case the theorem is the characterisation of bipartite graphs by odd cycles.
For a complete graph it suffices to check that every triangle has an even number of negative edges.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Fritz Heider's balance theory says that "the friend of my friend is my friend" and "the enemy of my enemy is my friend".
A group where these rules hold everywhere is called balanced.
The theorem says that a balanced group is either fully friendly or splits into two camps, with friendship inside each camp and hostility between them.

### L2 human

The theorem is due to Frank Harary and was brought into social psychology by Dorwin Cartwright and Harary.
It is a generalisation of the characterisation of bipartite graphs by odd cycles, which is the all-negative case.
The proof is a parity labelling, like the 2-colouring of a bipartite graph by breadth-first search, and it gives a linear-time test for balance.
Weaker notions, such as Davis's clusterability into more than two camps, drop the rule that the enemy of my enemy is my friend.
