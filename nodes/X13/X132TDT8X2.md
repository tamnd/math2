## Example

Let $\iota \colon \R^2 \to \R^3$ be the map $\iota(x, y) = (x, y, 0)$, with the dot product on both spaces.
We check that $\iota$ is a [[linear isometry|0717XXPWM6]] that is not onto, and we compute its [[adjoint|0ST8QF9T3T]].

1. The map is linear, because $\iota\big(a(x, y) + b(x', y')\big) = (ax + bx', ay + by', 0) = a\,\iota(x, y) + b\,\iota(x', y')$.
2. It keeps norms, because $\norm{\iota(x, y)}^2 = x^2 + y^2 + 0^2 = \norm{(x, y)}^2$.
3. Its matrix in the standard bases is the $3 \times 2$ matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. The standard bases are orthonormal, so the matrix of $\iota^*$ is $A^T$, by the [[matrix of the adjoint|0DBTE6HVBD]]. So $\iota^*(x, y, z) = (x, y)$.
5. The product $A^TA$ is $I_2$, so $\iota^*\iota = I$, as the [[equivalent conditions|95ST99SEW7]] predict.
6. The other product is $AA^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \ne I_3$, so $\iota\iota^* \ne I$.
7. The vector $(0, 0, 1)$ is not in the range of $\iota$, because every image has third entry $0$. So $\iota$ is not surjective.

## Remarks

The example shows that conditions 6 and 7 of the equivalent conditions need $\dim V = \dim W$.
A linear isometry between spaces of different dimensions has a left inverse $\iota^*$ but no right inverse.

The map $\iota\iota^*$ is the orthogonal projection of $\R^3$ onto the plane $z = 0$.
This holds in general: for every linear isometry $T$ between finite-dimensional inner product spaces, $TT^*$ is the orthogonal projection onto the range of $T$.

Change one entry and the example breaks.
The map $(x, y) \mapsto (x, y, x)$ is still linear and injective, but it sends $(1, 0)$ to $(1, 0, 1)$, of length $\sqrt{2}$, so it is no isometry.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Lay a flat sheet of paper in space at height $0$.
Each point of the sheet keeps its two coordinates and gets a third coordinate $0$.
Distances on the sheet do not change, so the map is an isometry.
But the map never reaches the points above or below the sheet.

### L2 human

Follow one vector through both products.
The vector $v = (3, 4)$ has length $5$, and $\iota(v) = (3, 4, 0)$ has length $\sqrt{9 + 16 + 0} = 5$.
Going back, $\iota^*\iota(v) = \iota^*(3, 4, 0) = (3, 4) = v$.
In the other order, a vector of space loses its height:
$$\iota\iota^*(1, 2, 3) = \iota(1, 2) = (1, 2, 0) \ne (1, 2, 3).$$
Watch out: for square matrices $A^TA = I$ gives $AA^T = I$, but $A$ here is $3 \times 2$, and that step fails.
