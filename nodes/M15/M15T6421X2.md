## Statement

Let $s \in \C^n$ be a nonzero known signal.
Let $w$ be a [[random vector|0PNRKMRC55]] in $\C^n$ with [[mean|0WQNRCNY3M]] $0$ and [[covariance matrix|0V7G6WMFK0]] $R = E[w w^*]$, where $R$ is positive definite.
For a nonzero filter $h \in \C^n$, define the output signal-to-noise ratio
$$\mathrm{SNR}(h) = \frac{\abs{h^* s}^2}{E\big[\abs{h^* w}^2\big]} = \frac{\abs{h^* s}^2}{h^* R h}.$$
Then $\mathrm{SNR}(h) \le s^* R^{-1} s$ for every nonzero $h$, with equality if and only if $h = c R^{-1} s$ for some nonzero $c \in \C$.
In particular, for white noise $R = \sigma^2 I$ with $\sigma > 0$, the maximum is $\norm{s}^2 / \sigma^2$, attained exactly by the multiples of $s$.

## Remarks

The idea of the proof: write $h^* s = (R^{1/2} h)^* (R^{-1/2} s)$.
By the [[Cauchy–Schwarz inequality|0RFWW56Y22]], $\abs{h^* s}^2 \le (h^* R h)(s^* R^{-1} s)$, with equality exactly when $R^{1/2} h$ and $R^{-1/2} s$ are proportional.
Dividing by $h^* R h$ gives the claim.

Example: with $s = (1, 1, -1)$ and white noise of variance $\sigma^2 = 1$, the matched filter $h = s$ gives $\mathrm{SNR} = 3$.
The filter $h = (1, 0, 0)$, which looks at the first sample only, gives $\mathrm{SNR} = 1$.

The hypothesis that $R$ is positive definite is needed for $R^{-1}$ to exist.
If $R$ were singular with $s$ outside its range, a filter that cancels all noise but keeps part of $s$ would give an infinite ratio.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To detect a known pulse buried in noise, the best linear detector compares the received signal with a copy of the pulse itself.
This is the matched filter: it adds up the samples weighted by how strongly the pulse is present at each one.
Among all linear filters it gives the largest ratio of pulse strength to noise strength.

### L2 human

In continuous time, the matched filter for a pulse $s(t)$ in white noise has impulse response $s^*(T_0 - t)$, a time-reversed and conjugated copy of the pulse.
Its output at time $T_0$ is the correlation of the input with $s$.
With coloured noise one first whitens the input by $R^{-1/2}$, then matches to the whitened signal, which gives $h \propto R^{-1} s$.
For Gaussian noise the matched filter output is a sufficient statistic for deciding whether $s$ is present, so the optimal detector thresholds it.
Radar and digital receivers use matched filters, often with pulses such as Barker codes whose autocorrelation has a sharp peak.
