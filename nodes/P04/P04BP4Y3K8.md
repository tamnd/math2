## Example

The [[Pythagorean theorem|0VP3A1290K]] says: in a triangle with sides $a$, $b$, $c$, if the angle $C$ between $a$ and $b$ is a right angle, then $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$.
The [[law of cosines|060ZVZBV3E]] drops the hypothesis on the angle.
It says: in each triangle with sides $a$, $b$, $c$ and angle $C$ between $a$ and $b$,
$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab \cos C.$$
We check that the law of cosines is a generalization of the Pythagorean theorem, and we test it on two triangles.

1. The law of cosines has fewer hypotheses: it asks only for a triangle, not for a right angle.
2. For a right angle, $C = 90^\circ$ and $\cos 90^\circ = 0$. So the law gives $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 0 = a^2 + b^2$, which is the Pythagorean theorem.
3. So the Pythagorean theorem is the special case $C = 90^\circ$ of the law of cosines.
4. Test with $a = 3$, $b = 4$, $C = 90^\circ$: $c^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$, so $c = 5$. This is the $3, 4, 5$ right triangle.
5. Test with $a = 3$, $b = 4$, $C = 60^\circ$: $\cos 60^\circ = \frac{1}{2}$, so $c^2 = 9 + 16 - 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 25 - 12 = 13$, and $c = \sqrt{13}$.
6. Test of the sign: for $C = 120^\circ$, $\cos 120^\circ = -\frac{1}{2}$, so $c^2 = 25 + 12 = 37$. A wider angle gives a longer opposite side, as expected.

## Remarks

The example shows a generalization by weakening a hypothesis.
The correction term $-2ab \cos C$ measures how far the angle is from a right angle.
It is negative for an acute angle, zero for a right angle and positive for an obtuse angle.
So the law of cosines also gives the converse of the Pythagorean theorem: $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$ forces $\cos C = 0$, so $C = 90^\circ$.

The generalization does not stop here.
In a real inner product space, the same formula reads $|u - v|^2 = |u|^2 + |v|^2 - 2 \langle u, v \rangle$.
On a sphere and in the hyperbolic plane there are spherical and hyperbolic laws of cosines.
For small triangles they reduce to the plane law.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Pythagorean theorem works only when the triangle has a square corner.
The law of cosines works for each triangle.
It adds a correction to the Pythagorean formula.
When the corner is square, the correction is zero, and we get back the old rule.
For two sides of length $3$ and $4$ at a square corner, the third side is $5$; at a narrower corner of $60$ degrees, it is about $3.6$.

### L2 human

We find the third side of a triangle with $a = 5$, $b = 8$ and $C = 60^\circ$.

1. We use $\cos 60^\circ = \frac{1}{2}$.
2. The law of cosines gives $c^2 = 25 + 64 - 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 8 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 89 - 40 = 49$.
3. So $c = 7$.

The typical use is to compute a side from two sides and the angle between them, or an angle from three sides.
A trap is to use the angle that is not between the two given sides.
The formula needs the angle $C$ opposite to the unknown side $c$.
