## Example

Take a triangle with sides $a$, $b$ and $c$, and let $C$ be the angle between the sides $a$ and $b$, so that $c$ is the side opposite $C$.
The [[Pythagorean theorem|0VP3A1290K]] says that if $C$ is a right angle, then $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$.
The [[law of cosines|060ZVZBV3E]] drops the hypothesis on the angle, and says that in every triangle
$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab \cos C.$$
We check that the law of cosines is a generalization of the Pythagorean theorem, and then test it on numbers.

1. The law of cosines has fewer hypotheses, since it asks only for a triangle and not for a right angle.
2. For a right angle, $C = 90^\circ$ and $\cos 90^\circ = 0$, so the law gives $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 0 = a^2 + b^2$.
3. So the Pythagorean theorem is the special case $C = 90^\circ$ of the law of cosines.
4. With $a = 3$, $b = 4$ and $C = 90^\circ$, we get $c^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$, so $c = 5$, the familiar $3, 4, 5$ right triangle.
5. With $a = 3$, $b = 4$ and $C = 60^\circ$, we use $\cos 60^\circ = \frac{1}{2}$:
$$c^2 = 9 + 16 - 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 25 - 12 = 13, \qquad c = \sqrt{13} \approx 3.61.$$
6. With $C = 120^\circ$, we use $\cos 120^\circ = -\frac{1}{2}$, so $c^2 = 25 + 12 = 37$ and $c = \sqrt{37} \approx 6.08$.
   A wider angle gives a longer opposite side, as it should.

## Remarks

The example is a generalization by weakening a hypothesis.
The correction term $-2ab \cos C$ measures how far the angle is from a right angle.
It is negative for an acute angle, zero for a right angle and positive for an obtuse angle, which is why step 5 gave less than $25$ and step 6 gave more.

The wider statement also gives something new about the old one.
If $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, then the law of cosines forces $2ab \cos C = 0$, so $\cos C = 0$ and $C = 90^\circ$.
That is the converse of the Pythagorean theorem, which the theorem alone does not state.

The generalization can go further.
In a real inner product space the same formula reads $\|u - v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2 - 2 \langle u, v \rangle$.
On a sphere and in the hyperbolic plane there are spherical and hyperbolic laws of cosines, and for very small triangles they approach the plane law.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Pythagorean theorem works only when the triangle has a square corner.
The law of cosines works for every triangle, because it adds a correction to the Pythagorean formula.
When the corner is square, the correction is zero, and we get back the old rule.
For two sides of length $3$ and $4$ at a square corner, the third side is $5$.
At a narrower corner of $60$ degrees, it is about $3.6$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: find the third side of a triangle with $a = 5$, $b = 8$ and $C = 60^\circ$.
Put $\cos 60^\circ = \frac{1}{2}$ into the law of cosines:
$$c^2 = 25 + 64 - 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 8 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 89 - 40 = 49.$$
So $c = 7$.
Check: the angle is acute, so $c^2$ should be less than $a^2 + b^2 = 89$, and $49 < 89$.
The law computes a side from two sides and the angle between them, or an angle from three sides.
Watch out: the angle in the formula must be the one between $a$ and $b$, which is the angle opposite the side $c$ that you want.
