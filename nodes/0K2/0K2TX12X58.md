## Definition

The singular values of a matrix are the square roots of the eigenvalues of the positive semidefinite matrix $A^* A$.
Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ complex matrix, and let $A^*$ be its [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]].
The $n \times n$ matrix $A^* A$ is [[positive semidefinite|01MMVW8CKC]] by [[the Gram matrix theorem|0AM050KCAW]].
So its [[eigenvalues|0ZND7NQ970]] are real and nonnegative, by the [[eigenvalue test|0HYC2RHD3M]].
List them with multiplicity in decreasing order, $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n \ge 0$.
The *singular values* of $A$ are the numbers
$$\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i} \qquad (1 \le i \le n).$$

## Notation

We write $\sigma_i(A)$, or $\sigma_i$ when $A$ is fixed.
For a real matrix, $A^* A = A^{\mathsf{T}} A$.
Some books keep only the first $\min(m, n)$ values, or only the nonzero ones; the values they drop are all $0$.

## Remarks

Each part of the definition has a job.
The product $A^* A$ is used because $A$ itself may be rectangular, and $A^* A$ is always square.
Positive semidefiniteness is what makes the square roots possible: it gives real eigenvalues that are at least $0$, so $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ is a real number.
The decreasing order is a convention, and it makes "the largest singular value" $\sigma_1$ well defined.
An equivalent form uses the positive semidefinite [[square root|EQ98KW1ZN4]]: the singular values are the eigenvalues of $(A^* A)^{1/2}$.

For a small example, take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Then
$$A^{\mathsf{T}} A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (2 - t)^2 - 1 = (t - 3)(t - 1),$$
so $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 1$, and the singular values are $\sqrt3$ and $1$.

The definition does not use the eigenvalues of $A$, and replacing them gives a wrong answer.
For the [[shear with rows (1, 1) and (0, 1)|S3ZGYKVBRR]] the eigenvalues are $1, 1$, while $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ has trace $3$ and determinant $1$, so the singular values are $\tfrac{\sqrt5 \pm 1}{2}$.
The [[concept page|09VMDSRXWJ]] explains what the numbers measure and lists their properties.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The singular values of a matrix are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$.
They are never negative.
They tell how much the matrix stretches space in its main directions.
For the $3 \times 2$ matrix above they are $\sqrt3 \approx 1.73$ and $1$.

### L2 human

To compute singular values, form $A^* A$, find its eigenvalues, and take square roots.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ we get $A^{\mathsf{T}} A = \operatorname{diag}(0, 9)$, so the singular values are $3$ and $0$.
Watch out: the eigenvalues of this $A$ are $0$ and $0$, so they are not the singular values, even though $A$ is square.

### L3 human

The definition uses the Gram matrix $A^* A$ of the columns of $A$.
So the numbers $\sigma_i^2$ are the eigenvalues of the Hermitian form $x \mapsto \norm{Ax}^2$ relative to the standard inner product.
This form, not $A$ itself, carries the metric information of $A$, which is why unitary changes of basis on either side leave the singular values unchanged.
