## Definition

Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ complex matrix, and let $A^*$ be its [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]].
The matrix $A^* A$ is [[positive semidefinite|01MMVW8CKC]] by [[the Gram matrix theorem|0AM050KCAW]].
So its [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]] $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n \ge 0$ are real and nonnegative, by the [[eigenvalue test|0HYC2RHD3M]].
They are listed with multiplicity.
The *singular values* of $A$ are the numbers
$$\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i} \qquad (1 \le i \le n).$$

## Notation

We write $\sigma_i(A)$, or $\sigma_i$ when $A$ is fixed.

## Remarks

- For a real matrix, $A^* A = A^T A$.
- Some authors list only the first $\min(m, n)$ values, or only the nonzero ones. The extra values in this definition are $0$.
- An equivalent definition uses the positive semidefinite square root: the singular values are the eigenvalues of $(A^* A)^{1/2}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The singular values of a matrix are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^T A$.
They are never negative.
They measure how much the matrix stretches space in its main directions.

### L2 human

To compute singular values, form $A^* A$, find its eigenvalues, and take square roots.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $A^T A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $3$ and $1$, so the singular values are $\sqrt3$ and $1$.
A trap: the singular values of $A$ are not its eigenvalues, even when $A$ is square.

### L3 human

The definition uses the Gram matrix $A^* A$ of the columns of $A$, which is positive semidefinite.
So $\sigma_i^2$ are the eigenvalues of the quadratic form $x \mapsto \norm{Ax}^2$ with respect to the standard inner product.
This form, not $A$ itself, carries the metric information of $A$.
