## Proof

We choose $d$ so that the moduli are pairwise coprime and larger than the entries, and then the Chinese remainder theorem gives $c$.
Let $a_0, \ldots, a_n$ be given, and write $m_i = 1 + (i + 1) d$ for the moduli.

1. Put $s = \max(n, a_0, \ldots, a_n)$ and $d = s!$.
   Then $s \le d$, and every $k$ with $1 \le k \le s$ divides $d$.
2. Each modulus is coprime to $d$.
   If a prime $p$ divides both $d$ and $m_i = 1 + (i + 1) d$, then $p$ divides $m_i - (i + 1) d = 1$, which is impossible.
3. The moduli $m_0, \ldots, m_n$ are pairwise coprime.
   Let $0 \le i < j \le n$, and suppose that a prime $p$ divides $m_i$ and $m_j$.
4. Then $p$ divides $m_j - m_i = (j - i) d$.
   By step 2, $p$ does not divide $d$, so $p$ divides $j - i$, because $p$ is prime.
5. But $1 \le j - i \le n \le s$, so $j - i$ divides $d$ by step 1, and then $p$ divides $d$.
   This contradicts step 2, so $m_i$ and $m_j$ have no common prime factor.
6. Each entry is smaller than its modulus, since $a_i \le s \le d < 1 + (i + 1) d = m_i$.
7. By the [[Chinese remainder theorem|0FNN9B4W64]] for the pairwise coprime moduli $m_0, \ldots, m_n$, there is $c \in \mathbb{N}$ with $c \equiv a_i \pmod{m_i}$ for $i = 0, \ldots, n$.
8. For each $i$, the remainder of $c$ on division by $m_i$ is the unique $r$ with $0 \le r < m_i$ and $r \equiv c \pmod{m_i}$.
   By steps 6 and 7, $r = a_i$, so $\beta(c, d, i) = a_i$.

## Idea

The moduli $1 + d, 1 + 2d, \ldots, 1 + (n + 1) d$ form an arithmetic progression whose difference $d$ is divisible by every small number.
A common prime factor of two of them would divide their difference, a small multiple of $d$, and so it would divide $d$ itself.
But each modulus is $1$ more than a multiple of $d$, so no prime factor of $d$ divides it.
So the moduli are pairwise coprime, and the Chinese remainder theorem gives one number $c$ with the prescribed remainders.
For the list $2, 0, 2, 1$, the choice $d = 3! = 6$ gives the moduli $7, 13, 19, 25$.

## Variants

Any $d$ that is divisible by $1, 2, \ldots, n$ and at least $\max_i a_i$ works, for example a large enough multiple of the least common multiple of $1, \ldots, n$.
The proof above only uses these two properties of $s!$.
Some presentations combine $c$ and $d$ into a single number with a pairing function, so that $\beta$ has two arguments instead of three.
