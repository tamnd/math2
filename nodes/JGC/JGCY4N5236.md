## Statement

Let $A(t) \ge 0$ and $B(t) \ge 0$ be the sizes of two fighting forces at time $t \ge 0$.
Let $\alpha > 0$ and $\beta > 0$ be the fighting effectiveness of one unit of $A$ and of $B$.

1. *Square law*: if $A' = -\beta B$ and $B' = -\alpha A$ while both are positive, then $\alpha A(t)^2 - \beta B(t)^2$ is constant.
   If moreover $\alpha A(0)^2 > \beta B(0)^2$, then $B$ reaches $0$ at a finite time.
   At that time $A = \sqrt{A(0)^2 - (\beta/\alpha) B(0)^2}$.
2. *Linear law*: if $A' = -\beta A B$ and $B' = -\alpha A B$, then $\alpha A(t) - \beta B(t)$ is constant.
   Hence if $\alpha A(0) > \beta B(0)$, then $B(t) \to 0$ and $A(t) \to A(0) - (\beta/\alpha) B(0)$ as $t \to \infty$.

## Remarks

Both invariants follow by differentiation: $\frac{d}{dt}(\alpha A^2 - \beta B^2) = -2\alpha\beta AB + 2\alpha\beta AB = 0$ and $\frac{d}{dt}(\alpha A - \beta B) = -\alpha\beta AB + \alpha\beta AB = 0$.
In the square law the system is a linear [[ordinary differential equation|0NT64JV96A]].
Its solutions are combinations of $\cosh(\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\, t)$ and $\sinh(\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\, t)$, which gives the finite time.
Example of the square law: with $\alpha = \beta$, a force of $5$ units against a force of $4$ ends with $\sqrt{25 - 16} = 3$ units.
So doubling numbers is worth as much as quadrupling effectiveness.
In the linear law, numbers and effectiveness count equally: with $\alpha = \beta$, $5$ units against $4$ end with $1$ unit.
The square law models aimed fire, where each unit can target any enemy; the linear law models unaimed fire into an area, where losses are proportional to both sizes.

## Explanations

### L1 human

These are simple equations for how two armies wear each other down.
When every soldier can shoot at any enemy, a bigger army both deals out more losses and spreads the enemy's fire over more targets.
So strength grows with the square of numbers, and concentrating forces pays off.
When fire is unaimed, strength grows only in proportion to numbers.

### L2 human

Frederick Lanchester published these models during the First World War, and they are a classic starting point of the mathematical modelling of combat in operations research.
The conserved quantities play the role of energy in mechanics: they give the outcome without solving the equations.
Many variants mix the two laws or add reinforcements and non-combat losses, and fits to historical battles are only approximate.
