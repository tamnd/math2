## Summary

KK-theory, introduced by Kasparov, assigns an abelian group $KK(A, B)$ to each pair of C*-algebras $A$ and $B$, with $A$ separable.
It is contravariant in $A$ and covariant in $B$, and it contains both K-theory, $KK(\C, B) \cong K_0(B)$, and [[K-homology|41XX3KF72X]], $KK(A, \C) \cong K^0(A)$.
Its key feature is the Kasparov product $KK(A, D) \times KK(D, B) \to KK(A, B)$, which makes separable C*-algebras into an additive category.
KK-theory is a main tool for index theory, for the Novikov and Baum–Connes conjectures, and for the classification of C*-algebras.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An element of $KK(A, B)$ is a generalized morphism from $A$ to $B$ that only remembers K-theoretic information.
Every $*$-homomorphism $A \to B$ gives such an element, but so do families of elliptic operators and many other analytic objects.
The data is a Hilbert module over $B$ on which $A$ acts, together with an operator that almost commutes with $A$ and is almost a symmetry.
For $A = B = \C$, an element is essentially a Fredholm operator, and $KK(\C, \C) \cong \Z$ by the Fredholm index.
