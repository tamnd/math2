## Example

The word "any" is a [[quantifier|EGTZPA6QH3]] whose meaning depends on the sentence around it.
We compare two sentences and show that "any" changes sense between them, a case of [[ambiguity in mathematical language|8BZPKYF912]].

1. Sentence A: "$x^2 \geq 0$ for any real $x$".
   Here "any" means "every".
   The statement is $\forall x \in \mathbb{R}, \, x^2 \geq 0$, and it is true.
2. Sentence B: "if $p(x) = 0$ for any real $x$, then $p$ has a real root".
   Here "any" means "some".
   The hypothesis is $\exists x \in \mathbb{R}, \, p(x) = 0$, and sentence B is true for every polynomial $p$.
3. Read B with "every" instead: "if $p(x) = 0$ for every real $x$, then $p$ has a real root".
   The hypothesis now says that $p$ is the zero polynomial, which is a much stronger condition.
4. The two readings of B are different statements.
   With "some", B is a claim about every polynomial with one zero.
   With "every", B is a claim about the zero polynomial only.
5. The pattern is this.
   In a positive claim, "any" is a [[universal quantifier|0GHS47PDAQ]].
   After "if", after "not", or in a question, "any" is often an [[existential quantifier|0MSRQHCNN9]].

## Remarks

Negation shows the same split.
"$f$ is not zero at any point" means "for every point $x$, $f(x) \neq 0$", that is, "there is no point where $f$ is zero".
"$f$ is not zero at every point" means "there is a point where $f$ is not zero".
So "not any" and "not every" have different meanings, and a swap of "any" for "every" in a negated sentence changes the claim.
Check with $f(x) = x$: it is zero at the point $0$, so "not zero at any point" is false, but "not zero at every point" is true.

The safe rule, which Paul Halmos also gave, is to write "every" or "some" and not "any".
Sentence A becomes "$x^2 \geq 0$ for every real $x$".
Sentence B becomes "if $p(x) = 0$ for some real $x$, then $p$ has a real root".

## Explanations

### L1 human

"Any child can join the club" means every child can join.
"Is any child here?" asks whether at least one child is here.
The same word "any" means "every" in the first sentence and "at least one" in the second.
In mathematics, the words "every" and "some" say each meaning with no doubt.

### L2 human

Rewrite three sentences that contain "any", using "every" or "some".

1. "Any continuous function on $[0, 1]$ is bounded."
   This is a general claim, so "any" means "every".
   The rewrite is "every continuous function on $[0, 1]$ is bounded".
2. "If $f(x) > 1$ for any $x$ in $[0, 1]$, then $f$ is not bounded above by $1$."
   This is a hypothesis after "if", so "any" means "some".
   The rewrite is "if $f(x) > 1$ for some $x \in [0, 1]$, then $f$ is not bounded above by $1$".
3. "$f$ has no root in any interval of length $1$."
   This is a negation, and "no ... any" means "for every interval $I$ of length $1$, $f$ has no root in $I$".

Do this rewriting when you read a definition or a theorem that contains "any", before the proof starts.
Watch out: in step 3, "any" is universal, but it sits inside a negation.
The negation of the whole sentence is "there is an interval of length $1$ that contains a root of $f$".
