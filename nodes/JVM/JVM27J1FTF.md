## Summary

A normal mode of a vibrating linear system is a motion in which all parts oscillate with one frequency and a fixed phase relation.
The relative amplitudes of the parts stay fixed.
For the equations $M \ddot x + K x = 0$ of [[small oscillations|WVRPFFPD0Y]], the normal modes come from the solutions of $K v = \omega^2 M v$.
Their frequencies $\omega$ are the natural frequencies of the system.
Every free vibration is a superposition of normal modes, which is the basis of [[modal analysis|D14DP6XQJ6]].
The idea extends to continuous systems such as strings and membranes, where the modes are standing waves.
See the [[definition|WZGRC78VDB]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two equal pendulums joined by a weak spring can swing together, in step, or exactly against each other.
These two special motions are the normal modes.
In each of them, the motion repeats exactly with one frequency, and the shape of the motion does not change.
The in-step mode has the frequency of a single pendulum, since the spring is never stretched; the opposite mode is faster, since the spring adds to gravity.
Any other motion, for example starting with one pendulum at rest, is a mix of the two, and the energy then passes back and forth between the pendulums.
