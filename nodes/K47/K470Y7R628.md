## Example

Count how many times we can double $a$ and stay at or below $b$.
For $a = 3$ and $b = 100$ we can double five times:
$$3 \cdot 2^5 = 96 \le 100 < 192 = 3 \cdot 2^6.$$
So the count is $5$.
In a non-standard model, the count is infinite exactly when a cut closed under doubling fits between $a$ and $b$.

Let $M$ be a countable [[non-standard model|1QWN5CV4CG]] of $\mathsf{PA}$, and let $\mathcal{K}$ be the class of the cuts $I$ of $M$ with $2x \in I$ for every $x \in I$.
Write $a^+ = \max(a, 1)$.
Let $Y(a, b) = \max \{ c : a^+ \cdot 2^c \le b \}$ when $a^+ \le b$, and $Y(a, b) = 0$ otherwise.
We check that $Y$ is an [[indicator|JWTZ8NVVGK]] for $\mathcal{K}$.

1. $Y$ is definable without parameters, because $x \mapsto 2^x$ is definable in $\mathsf{PA}$.
   The maximum exists, because $a^+ \cdot 2^c \le b$ gives $c < 2^c \le b$.
2. So $Y(a, b) \le b$.
   If $a' \le a \le b \le b'$, then $a'^+ \le a^+$, so each $c$ with $a^+ \cdot 2^c \le b$ has $a'^+ \cdot 2^c \le b'$, and $Y$ is monotone.
3. Let $1 \le a \le b$ with $c = Y(a, b)$ non-standard.
   Let $I$ be the set of the $x$ with $x \le a \cdot 2^k$ for some standard $k$.
4. $I$ is closed downward and under doubling, because $x \le a \cdot 2^k$ gives $2x \le a \cdot 2^{k+1}$.
   It is closed under $x \mapsto x + 1$, because $x + 1 \le 2x$ for $x \ge 1$, and $0 + 1 \le a$.
5. $I$ contains each standard $k$, since $k < 2^k \le a \cdot 2^k$, and $a \in I$ with $k = 0$.
6. For a standard $k$ we have $k < c$, so $a \cdot 2^k < a \cdot 2^c \le b$.
   So $b \notin I$, and $I \in \mathcal{K}$ with $a \in I < b$.
7. Conversely, let $I \in \mathcal{K}$ with $a \in I < b$, and $a \ge 1$.
   Then $a \cdot 2^k \in I$ for each standard $k$, by doubling $k$ times.
8. So $a \cdot 2^k < b$ for each standard $k$, and $Y(a, b) \ge k$ for each standard $k$.
   So $Y(a, b)$ is non-standard.
9. For $a = 0$, every cut contains $0$ and $1$, so steps 3 to 8 with $a^+ = 1$ give condition 3.

## Remarks

The [[difference indicator|1T6K132VVA]] measures the distance from $a$ to $b$ by counting additions of $1$, and this one counts doublings.
For a non-standard $c$, the pair $(c, 2c)$ shows the difference: $2c - c = c$ is non-standard, but $Y(c, 2c) = 1$, and indeed every cut closed under doubling that contains $c$ also contains $2c$.

Faster operations give indicators for smaller classes of cuts.
Closure under doubling is still far from being a model of $\mathsf{PA}$, as the [[non-example|Q1WSA5TPC2]] shows with the pair $(c, c^2)$.
An indicator for the models of $\mathsf{PA}$ needs a combinatorial principle, such as the one in the [[Paris-Harrington proof|D0Q498H0R5]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Here two infinite numbers are separated when we can double $a$ any finite number of times and still stay below $b$.
The number of doublings that fit below $b$ is $Y(a, b)$.
For ordinary numbers, $Y(3, 100) = 5$, since $96$ fits and $192$ does not.
The count is infinite exactly when a cut closed under doubling lies between $a$ and $b$.

### L2 human

We compute $Y$ on three pairs.
For $(5, 200)$ we have $5 \cdot 2^5 = 160 \le 200 < 320$, so $Y(5, 200) = 5$.
For a non-standard $c$, the pair $(c, 2c)$ gives $Y = 1$, so no cut closed under doubling separates $c$ from $2c$.
The pair $(c, c \cdot 2^c)$ gives $Y = c$, which is non-standard, so a cut closed under doubling lies between $c$ and $c \cdot 2^c$.

The typical use of this example is to see how the closure condition on the cuts changes the indicator.
The trap is to take it as an indicator for models of $\mathsf{PA}$: closure under doubling is much weaker than that.
