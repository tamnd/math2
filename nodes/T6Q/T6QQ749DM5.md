## Summary

Every mathematician has seen a "proof" that $2 = 1$ and felt the small shock of not finding the error at once.
Hunting for that error is one of the best ways to learn what a proof must do, and this part of [[reasoning and proof|SGSVYFDSYK]] is a collection of such hunts.
Each page opens with a short false proof for you to try, then names the error, with its formal name from logic and its informal name from teaching where both exist, and then gives the correct version next to it.
The errors come in three families.
Some break a rule of algebra, geometry or analysis outside its range, such as dividing by zero or trusting a figure.
Some break a rule of logic, such as assuming the claim, reading an implication backward, generalizing from cases, or swapping quantifiers.
The last family is historical: proofs by famous mathematicians that experts accepted and that later turned out to have a gap.

## Remarks

### Reading order

1. [[Mathematical fallacies|S2BW0FDHV8]]: what makes a proof invalid, and how a howler can reach a true answer by a wrong route.
2. [[Division by zero fallacies|V7601AG5G3]]: how to spot a cancelled factor that is secretly zero.
3. [[Fallacies with roots and powers|75ZEQ21AG2]]: the exact ranges where the rules for roots, powers and logarithms hold.
4. [[Fallacies with infinite series and integrals|KDXKVT5K1E]]: which finite rules survive passage to a limit, and which need a theorem.
5. [[Geometric fallacies|5CJ4KD4D96]]: why every fact read from a figure needs a proof.
6. [[Circular reasoning|JPHE53PABT]]: how to check that a proof does not rest on its own conclusion.
7. [[Affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent|J7KFCFQRBT]]: the two invalid ways to read a conditional, next to the valid ones.
8. [[Proof by example|ZX6ERT3SW7]]: when examples prove something, and when they are only evidence.
9. [[Quantifier shift fallacy|CRW82NARQV]]: why the order of "for all" and "there exists" matters, with pointwise and uniform statements as the main case.
10. [[Non-reversible steps in solving equations|641ZP4RA5S]]: how extraneous solutions arise, and two ways to avoid them.
11. [[Famous incorrect proofs|1CV4HTA6C8]]: what the errors of Kempe, Lamé, Legendre and others teach about checking proofs.
12. [[Proof by intimidation and handwaving|D8A1K052XV]]: names for arguments that hide a gap, and the test of proving too much.

### Prerequisites

The reader should know the [[basic proof methods|958EF46EYJ]] and the [[language of mathematical statements|WMY13MXY3W]].
Errors in proofs by induction, such as the paradox that [[all horses are the same color|AJ2FDJJGTH]], are treated with [[induction and well-ordering as proof methods|EEH2F76CYQ]].
