## Statement

Let $B$ be a continuously differentiable [[magnetic field|XRED3C547P]] on $\R^3$.
Then its [[divergence|0MWV6ZTK6S]] vanishes:
$$\nabla \cdot B = 0.$$
Equivalently, for every bounded open set $\Omega \subset \R^3$ with $C^1$ boundary and outward unit normal $n$, the [[flux|026J1MG2NW]] of $B$ through $\partial \Omega$ is zero:
$$\int_{\partial \Omega} B \cdot n \, dA = 0.$$

## Remarks

The law is one of [[Maxwell's equations|9BWWD4QK8Y]]; as a physical law it expresses that no magnetic monopoles have been observed.
The two forms are equivalent by the [[divergence theorem|0JX86PSSA9]].
For a field given by the [[Biot–Savart law|RA4GE6185Q]] it is a theorem: such a field is a curl, and the divergence of a curl is zero.
Example: the field of a long straight wire, $B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2 \pi} \frac{(-x_2, x_1, 0)}{x_1^2 + x_2^2}$, has divergence $0$ away from the wire.
Non-example: the field $q x / \abs{x}^3$ of a point source has flux $4 \pi q$ through every sphere around $0$, so it cannot be a magnetic field if $q \ne 0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Magnetic field lines never begin or end at a point, unlike electric field lines, which start and end on charges.
So as much magnetic field enters any closed surface as leaves it.
Cutting a bar magnet in two never isolates a north pole; one gets two smaller magnets.

### L2 human

Since $\nabla \cdot B = 0$ on $\R^3$, the field is the curl of a vector potential, $B = \nabla \times A$.
This is the reason for the magnetic vector potential and, in the relativistic theory, for the four-potential.
The law also gives the continuity of the normal component of $B$ across any interface.
If magnetic monopoles existed, the right side would be a magnetic charge density.
