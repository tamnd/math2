## Statement

The identity matrix is never a commutator of two matrices over the rational, real or complex numbers.
Let $F$ be a field of characteristic $0$ and let $n \ge 1$.
Then there are no $n \times n$ matrices $A$ and $B$ over $F$ with
$$AB - BA = I_n,$$
where $I_n$ is the [[identity matrix|6NHZRCKJNW]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The [[proof|DPEARYXCP7]] takes traces: the [[cyclic property|0Y4NF1DRKD]] gives $\operatorname{tr}(AB - BA) = 0$, while $\operatorname{tr} I_n = n \cdot 1_F$.
So it needs only $n \cdot 1_F \neq 0$, that is, that the characteristic of $F$ does not divide $n$.
When the characteristic $p$ divides $n$, solutions exist.
Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, [[this example|KZD8TFB85C]] gives $2 \times 2$ matrices with $AB - BA = I_2$.
For $n = kp$, taking $k$ copies of a $p \times p$ solution along the diagonal gives an $n \times n$ solution, by the block multiplication rule.

### A solution in characteristic p

On $V = \mathbb{F}_p[x]/(x^p)$ with basis $1, x, \dots, x^{p-1}$, let $D$ be the derivative $d/dx$ and $X$ the multiplication by $x$.
For $0 \le k < p - 1$ the product rule gives
$$(DX - XD)(x^k) = (k + 1) x^k - k x^k = x^k.$$
For $k = p - 1$ the product $x \cdot x^{p-1} = x^p$ is $0$ in $V$, so
$$(DX - XD)(x^{p-1}) = 0 - (p - 1) x^{p-1} = x^{p-1},$$
because $-(p - 1) = 1$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$.
So the matrices of $D$ and $X$ satisfy $DX - XD = I_p$.

### What it gives you

The same trace argument shows that over any field, $AB - BA = cI_n$ forces $nc = 0$.
In characteristic $0$ this rules out every nonzero multiple of the identity.
A commutator $AB - BA$ measures how far $A$ and $B$ are from [[commuting|8G3X8MPG7E]], and in characteristic $0$ this failure can never be a nonzero scalar matrix.

### Generalizations

No bounded operators on a nonzero Hilbert space satisfy $AB - BA = I$, a theorem of Wintner and Wielandt.
So the [[canonical commutation relation|HKP9E7N7AB]] of quantum mechanics needs unbounded operators.
In the other direction, a matrix over a field is a commutator $AB - BA$ if and only if its trace is $0$.
Shoda proved this in characteristic $0$, and Albert and Muckenhoupt extended it to all fields.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix $AB - BA$ measures how far $A$ and $B$ are from commuting.
Over the real or complex numbers it can never be the identity matrix.
The reason is that its diagonal entries always add up to $0$, while those of $I_n$ add up to $n$.

### L2 human

The trace is a [[linear form|0GJWQZCPZM]] that vanishes on every commutator.
For example, with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$,
$$AB - BA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix},$$
which has trace $0$ and so cannot be $I_2$, whose trace is $2$.
Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ the same matrices give $I_2$, because there $-1 = 1$ and $2 = 0$.

### L3 human

In quantum mechanics, position and momentum satisfy $QP - PQ = i\hbar I$.
The theorem shows that no finite matrices can do this, and the Wintner and Wielandt theorem rules out bounded operators too.
Algebraically, the Weyl algebra generated by $x$ and $d/dx$ with $[d/dx, x] = 1$ has no nonzero finite-dimensional representations in characteristic $0$, for the same trace reason.
