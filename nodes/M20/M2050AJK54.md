## Example

A recipe says that fresh eggs and $30$ minutes of baking make the cake rise, and the cake did not rise, but that does not prove the eggs were bad.
The [[boundedness theorem|0ATVXC3NCC]] has the same shape: if $f$ is continuous and its domain is a closed bounded interval $[a, b]$, then $f$ is bounded.
Let $f(x) = 1/x$ on the domain $(0, 1]$.
A student argues: "$f$ is unbounded, so by the boundedness theorem $f$ is not continuous."
We check that this argument is not an instance of [[modus tollens|32AQPJQQAM]], and that its conclusion is false.

1. Let $p$ be "$f$ is continuous", $q$ be "the domain of $f$ is a closed bounded interval", and $r$ be "$f$ is bounded".
   The theorem is $(p \land q) \to r$.
2. Modus tollens on $(p \land q) \to r$ and $\neg r$ gives $\neg(p \land q)$, that is, $\neg p \lor \neg q$.
   It does not give $\neg p$.
3. The valuation with $p$ true, $q$ false and $r$ false makes both premises true and the conclusion $\neg p$ false.
   So it is a [[countermodel|FWHBT4HABW]], and the form is invalid.
4. The function $1/x$ on $(0, 1]$ realizes this valuation.
   It is continuous at each point of $(0, 1]$, as a quotient of continuous functions with a nonzero denominator, so $p$ is true.
5. The domain $(0, 1]$ does not contain $0$, so it is not a closed bounded interval, and $q$ is false.
6. For each $K > 0$, the point $x = \min(1, 1/(K+1))$ gives $f(x) \ge K + 1 > K$, so $f$ is unbounded and $r$ is false.
7. So the premises are true and the conclusion "$f$ is not continuous" is false.

## Remarks

### What the example shows

Denying the consequent of a theorem refutes only the conjunction of all its hypotheses.
The correct conclusion here is "$f$ is not continuous, or the domain is not a closed bounded interval", and the second part is the true reason.
If we add the premise $q$, so that the domain is known to be $[a, b]$, then $\neg p$ follows from $\neg p \lor \neg q$ and $q$.
For example, $g(x) = 1/x$ for $x \in (0, 1]$ with $g(0) = 0$ is defined on $[0, 1]$ and unbounded, so modus tollens and $q$ give that $g$ is not continuous on $[0, 1]$.

### Watch out

Read the negated conclusion as "at least one hypothesis fails", and then find which one.
Do not blame the hypothesis that you most want to refute without checking the others.

### Try it

Every odd perfect square is congruent to $1$ modulo $8$, and $4 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 8$.
What does modus tollens give for $n = 4$?
It gives "$4$ is not odd, or $4$ is not a perfect square", and the first part is the true one, since $4 = 2^2$ is even.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A recipe says: "if the eggs are fresh and the cake bakes for $30$ minutes, the cake rises".
The cake did not rise.
We may conclude that the eggs were not fresh or the baking time was wrong, but we cannot tell which.
Blaming the eggs alone is the error of this example.

### L2 human

The theorem "if $n$ is odd and $n$ is a perfect square, then $n \equiv 1 \pmod 8$" holds because $m^2 - 1 = (m - 1)(m + 1)$ is a product of two consecutive even numbers for odd $m$, and one of them is divisible by $4$.
For $n = 4$ the conclusion fails, since $4 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 8$.
Modus tollens gives only that $4$ is not odd or $4$ is not a perfect square.
The step "so $4$ is not a perfect square" would be wrong, because $4 = 2^2$.
The failure is explained by the hypothesis "odd", because $4$ is even.
