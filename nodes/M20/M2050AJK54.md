## Example

The [[boundedness theorem|0ATVXC3NCC]] has two hypotheses: if $f$ is continuous and its domain is a closed bounded interval $[a, b]$, then $f$ is bounded.
Let $f(x) = 1/x$ on the domain $(0, 1]$.
A student argues: "$f$ is unbounded, so by the boundedness theorem $f$ is not continuous."
We check that this argument is not an instance of [[modus tollens|32AQPJQQAM]], and that its conclusion is false.

1. Let $p$ be "$f$ is continuous", $q$ be "the domain of $f$ is a closed bounded interval", and $r$ be "$f$ is bounded". The theorem is $(p \land q) \to r$.
2. Modus tollens on $(p \land q) \to r$ and $\neg r$ gives $\neg(p \land q)$, that is, $\neg p \lor \neg q$. It does not give $\neg p$.
3. The form "$(p \land q) \to r$, $\neg r$, therefore $\neg p$" is invalid. The valuation with $p$ true, $q$ false and $r$ false makes both premises true and the conclusion false, so it is a [[countermodel|FWHBT4HABW]].
4. The function $1/x$ on $(0, 1]$ realizes this valuation. It is continuous at each point of $(0, 1]$, as a quotient of continuous functions with a nonzero denominator, so $p$ is true.
5. The domain $(0, 1]$ does not contain $0$, so it is not a closed bounded interval, and $q$ is false.
6. For each $K > 0$, the point $x = \min(1, 1/(K+1))$ gives $f(x) \ge K + 1 > K$, so $f$ is unbounded and $r$ is false.
7. So the premises are true and the student's conclusion "$f$ is not continuous" is false.

## Remarks

The example shows that denying the consequent of a theorem refutes only the conjunction of all its hypotheses.
The correct conclusion here is "$f$ is not continuous or the domain is not a closed bounded interval", and the second part is the true reason.
If we add the premise $q$, so that the domain is known to be $[a, b]$, then $\neg p$ follows: from $\neg p \lor \neg q$ and $q$ we get $\neg p$.
For example, $g(x) = 1/x$ for $x \in (0, 1]$ with $g(0) = 0$ is defined on $[0, 1]$ and unbounded, so modus tollens and $q$ give that $g$ is not continuous on $[0, 1]$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A recipe says: "if the eggs are fresh and the cake bakes for $30$ minutes, the cake rises".
The cake did not rise.
We may conclude that the eggs were not fresh or the baking time was wrong, but we cannot tell which.
Blaming the eggs alone is the error of this example.

### L2 human

We repair a wrong use of modus tollens in a proof.

1. Theorem: for every integer $n$, if $n$ is odd and $n$ is a perfect square, then $n \equiv 1 \pmod 8$. Reason: $n = m^2$ with $m$ odd, and $m^2 - 1 = (m - 1)(m + 1)$ is a product of two consecutive even numbers, one of them divisible by $4$.
2. For $n = 4$ we have $4 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 8$, so the conclusion of the theorem fails.
3. Modus tollens gives: $4$ is not odd, or $4$ is not a perfect square.
4. The step "so $4$ is not a perfect square" would be wrong, because $4 = 2^2$.
5. The first disjunct is the true one: $4$ is even. So the failure of the conclusion is explained by the hypothesis "odd".

The typical use is to read the negated conclusion as "at least one hypothesis fails" and then to find which one.
A trap is to blame the hypothesis that we most want to refute, without checking the others.
