## Example

On $\mathbb{R}^2$ let
$$B(x, y) = x_1 y_1 - x_2 y_2,$$
whose [[matrix|0P24YW565F]] in the standard basis is $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$.
We show that $B$ is [[nondegenerate|01NHFZF2SK]], find its [[isotropic vectors|MZHFGPV1YT]], and find a basis in which it is a [[hyperbolic plane|1X5PZSK44P]].

1. The matrix has $\det A = -1 \neq 0$, so $B$ is nondegenerate by the [[criteria for nondegeneracy|3ND2FNJ38Y]].
2. For $v = (a, b)$ the value on the diagonal factors as $B(v, v) = a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$.
3. So $B(v, v) = 0$ exactly when $b = a$ or $b = -a$. The isotropic vectors are the nonzero vectors on the two diagonal lines $b = a$ and $b = -a$.
4. Let $W$ be the line through $u = (1, 1)$. For $v = (v_1, v_2)$ we get $B(u, v) = v_1 - v_2$.
5. So the [[orthogonal complement|05Q4JD6XEH]] $W^\perp = \{v : B(u, v) = 0\}$ is the line $v_1 = v_2$, which is $W$ itself.
6. The dimensions add up as the [[dimension statement|M2JH84VEY9]] says for a nondegenerate form: $\dim W + \dim W^\perp = 1 + 1 = 2$. But $W \cap W^\perp = W \neq \{0\}$, so $\mathbb{R}^2 \neq W \oplus W^\perp$.
7. Now let $v = \tfrac{1}{2}(1, -1)$, a vector on the other diagonal. Then $B(u, v) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot (-1)) = 1$ and $B(v, v) = \tfrac{1}{4}(1 - 1) = 0$.

Put $u$ and $v$ as the columns of $P$, and compute the new matrix $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$:
$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 \\ 1 & -\tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad P^{\mathsf{T}} A P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 \\ 1 & -\tfrac12 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

So in the basis $(u, v)$ the matrix of $B$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and $(u, v)$ is a hyperbolic pair.

## Remarks

The two diagonals are the light cone of special relativity with one space dimension: with $x_1$ as time, $x_2$ as space and units in which light has speed $1$, a light ray moves along $x_2 = \pm x_1$, as for [[the Minkowski form on the plane|TFGECQ5C32]].

The isotropic vectors together with $0$ do not form a subspace.
The sum $(1, 1) + (1, -1) = (2, 0)$ has $B((2, 0), (2, 0)) = 4 \neq 0$.

The example also separates two ideas that are easy to mix up.
The restriction of $B$ to the line $W$ is the zero form, yet $B$ on the whole plane is nondegenerate.
Nondegenerate means that no nonzero vector pairs to $0$ with every vector, and $u = (1, 1)$ pairs to $1$ with $(1, 0)$.

If the minus sign becomes a plus sign, the form is the dot product, which has no isotropic vector at all.
If the matrix becomes $\operatorname{diag}(1, -c)$ with $c > 0$, the isotropic lines are $b = \pm a / \sqrt{c}$, and the form is still a hyperbolic plane over $\mathbb{R}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The rule $x_1 y_1 - x_2 y_2$ pairs some nonzero vectors with themselves to give $0$.
For example, $(3, 3)$ gives $9 - 9 = 0$, and $(2, -2)$ gives $4 - 4 = 0$.
These vectors lie on the two diagonals of the plane.
Each diagonal is at a "right angle" to itself for this rule, which never happens for the dot product.

### L2 human

The method above works for any form in two variables.
Write $B(v, v)$ as a quadratic polynomial in the coordinates, then factor it or solve it for one coordinate.
The solutions other than $0$ are the isotropic vectors.
For example, $B(v, v) = a^2 - 4b^2 = (a - 2b)(a + 2b)$ gives the lines $a = 2b$ and $a = -2b$.
Two vectors on different isotropic lines, scaled so that they pair to $1$, form a basis in which the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Watch out: a form that vanishes on a line is not degenerate for that reason, because degenerate means a nonzero vector that pairs to $0$ with every vector.
