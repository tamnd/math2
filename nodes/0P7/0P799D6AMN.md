## Definition

A bilinear form is a pairing of two vectors to a scalar that is linear in each vector separately.

Let $F$ be a field and let $V$ be a [[vector space|05EGHEQZAW]] over $F$.
A *bilinear form* on $V$ is a map $B \colon V \times V \to F$ such that, for all $u, v, w \in V$ and all $a \in F$:

1. $B(u + v, w) = B(u, w) + B(v, w)$ and $B(au, w) = a B(u, w)$;
2. $B(u, v + w) = B(u, v) + B(u, w)$ and $B(u, aw) = a B(u, w)$.

Condition 1 says that $B$ is linear in the first slot, and condition 2 says that $B$ is linear in the second slot.

## Notation

We write $B(u, v)$, or $\langle u, v \rangle$ when only one form is in use.
Mathlib writes `LinearMap.BilinForm F V` for the type of bilinear forms on `V`, and `B u v` for the value.

## Remarks

Each condition holds one slot fixed and asks for linearity in the other slot.
Neither condition implies the other, so a check must cover both slots.
For example, the [[map $x_1 y_1 + x_2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$|5JPJW2GJR6]] satisfies condition 1, but it fails condition 2 because $B((0, 1), 0) = 1 \ne 0$.

The definition asks for nothing more.
It does not ask that $B(u, v) = B(v, u)$, which defines a [[symmetric form|0AXZNE5XNH]], and it does not ask that $B(v, v) > 0$, which only makes sense over an ordered field such as $\mathbb{R}$.
A small example on $F^2$ is $B(x, y) = x_1 y_2$.
For fixed $y$ it is the linear map $x \mapsto y_2 x_1$, and for fixed $x$ it is the linear map $y \mapsto x_1 y_2$, so both conditions hold.
It is not symmetric, since $B(e_1, e_2) = 1$ and $B(e_2, e_1) = 0$.

The same two conditions on a map $V \times W \to F$, with two different spaces, define a bilinear pairing.
The [[bilinear form|0SQZMXB6GP]] concept gives the standard examples and the first properties.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A bilinear form takes two vectors and gives a number.
If you double the first vector, the number doubles, and the same holds for the second vector.
The dot product is the main example: $(1, 0)$ and $(2, 5)$ give $2$.
Bilinear forms need not be symmetric or positive, so the order of the two vectors can matter.

### L2 human

To check that a map $B$ is bilinear, fix $v$ and check that $u \mapsto B(u, v)$ is linear, then fix $u$ and check that $v \mapsto B(u, v)$ is linear.
For $B(x, y) = 2 x_1 y_1 - x_2 y_1$ on $\mathbb{Q}^2$, fixing $y$ gives $(2 y_1) x_1 - y_1 x_2$, and fixing $x$ gives $(2 x_1 - x_2) y_1$.
Both are linear, so $B$ is bilinear.
Watch out: the map $B(x, y) = x_1 y_1 + 1$ fails both conditions, since $B(0, y) = 1 \ne 0$.
