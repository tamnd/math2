## Definition

Let $F$ be a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]].
Take a [[system of linear equations|0GT4WWGWH2]] over $F$ with $m$ equations in $n$ unknowns, where equation $i$ is $a_{i1} x_1 + \cdots + a_{in} x_n = b_i$.
The *coefficient matrix* of the system is the $m \times n$ [[matrix|0VD9ZSWWJG]] $A = (a_{ij})$.
Its entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is the coefficient of $x_j$ in equation $i$.
When $x_j$ does not appear in equation $i$, the entry $a_{ij}$ is $0$.

## Notation

1. With $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)^T$ and $b = (b_1, \dots, b_m)^T$, the system is the matrix equation $Ax = b$.
2. Here $Ax$ is the [[matrix-vector product|0PDVQEP0ZC]]: its entry $i$ is $a_{i1} x_1 + \cdots + a_{in} x_n$.
3. Some books write $(a_{ij})_{1 \le i \le m,\, 1 \le j \le n}$ or $[a_{ij}]$ for $A$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The system $2x + y = 5$, $x - 3y = 0$ has the coefficient matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$.
2. The system $2y + x = 5$, $3z - y = 1$, $x = 4z$ has the coefficient matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$, see [[the example with missing unknowns|2E4TZ2ZV57]].
3. The single equation $x_1 - x_3 = 7$ in the unknowns $x_1, x_2, x_3$ has the $1 \times 3$ coefficient matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$.
4. The system $x + y + z = 6$, $2x - y + z = 3$, $x + 2y - z = 2$ has $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$, see [[the three planes example|YM0PB9F9HG]].

### Non-examples

1. For $x + y = 2x + 1$, $3y = x - 2$, the array $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ read off the left sides is not the coefficient matrix, see [[the example with unknowns on both sides|PE7H79RRY9]].
2. The matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 5 \\ 1 & -3 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ of example 1 with the constants added is the [[augmented matrix|0T6MWGZSXA]], not the coefficient matrix.

### Basic properties

1. The coefficient matrix depends on the order of the unknowns: changing the order permutes the columns.
2. Changing the order of the equations permutes the rows, and it does not change the solution set.
3. $Ax$ is the [[linear combination|0V8F3E48E6]] $x_1 a_1 + \cdots + x_n a_n$ of the columns $a_j$ of $A$, see [[linear systems as vector equations|E7SAHXQEVC]].
4. The homogeneous system $Ax = 0$ depends only on $A$; its solution set is the [[null space|0PHDYNR24D]] of $A$.
5. If $A$ is square and [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]], the system has exactly one solution, $x = A^{-1} b$.
6. Whether the solution is unique, when it exists, depends only on $A$: it is unique exactly when every column of $A$ has a pivot, see [[the existence and uniqueness theorem|BKA191KD1S]].

### Common errors

1. Reading the coefficients before all unknowns are on the left side.
2. Forgetting the zero entries for unknowns that do not appear in an equation.
3. Writing the unknowns in a different order in different rows.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The coefficient matrix keeps only the numbers in front of the unknowns, in a rectangular table.
Each row is one equation, and each column is one unknown.
For $2x + y = 5$ and $x - 3y = 0$, the first row is $2, 1$ and the second row is $1, -3$.
This table lets us work with the whole system as one object.

### L2 human

We write $x_1 - 2x_3 = 4$, $3x_2 + x_1 = 0$ as $Ax = b$.

1. Order the unknowns as $x_1, x_2, x_3$ in each equation: $x_1 + 0x_2 - 2x_3 = 4$ and $x_1 + 3x_2 + 0x_3 = 0$.
2. Read the rows: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -2 \\ 1 & 3 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, and $b = (4, 0)^T$.
3. Check with $x = (4, -\tfrac43, 0)$: $Ax = (4 + 0 - 0, 4 - 4 + 0) = (4, 0) = b$.

The typical use is to pass from a word problem to a matrix that a computer can row reduce.
A trap: the order of the unknowns must be the same in every row, or the columns mix different unknowns.

### L3 human

The coefficient matrix is the matrix of the [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] $F^n \to F^m$, $x \mapsto Ax$, in the standard bases.
So questions about the system become questions about this map: existence of solutions is about its image, and uniqueness is about its kernel.
Row operations replace $A$ by $EA$ with $E$ invertible, which changes the basis of the target $F^m$ but keeps the kernel.
