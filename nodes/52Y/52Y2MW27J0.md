## Summary

A student copies the proof that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational from the board without a single error.
Then the teacher asks, "Why does $a^2 = 2b^2$ force $a$ to be even?", and the student has no answer.
The student can reproduce the proof but does not understand it.

Proof comprehension is the understanding that a reader gains of a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] that the reader takes to be correct.
Proof validation is the process by which a reader decides whether an argument is a correct proof at all.
Research in mathematics education studies how students and mathematicians do both, and how teachers can test and improve them.
The reader's own methods are on the pages [[reading proofs|40GCSM2G56]] and [[checking a proof|4M98ZZNK0E]], and the research behind those methods is described below.

## Motivation

In a lecture course, students read and hear many proofs and write only a few.
If they do not understand the proofs that the lecturer gives, most of the course is lost.
Yet for a long time, tests in proof courses asked students to reproduce a proof, which a student can do from memory.
So researchers asked three questions.
What does it mean to understand a proof?
How can a test measure that understanding?
And how do experts and novices differ when they read and check a proof?

## Notation

The research uses "validation" for the reading of an argument to decide whether it is a proof, a term from Selden and Selden.
"Comprehension" is used for the understanding of a proof already accepted as correct.
In the comprehension model of Mejía-Ramos and coauthors, "local" questions concern single statements or steps, and "holistic" questions concern the proof as a whole.

## Remarks

### Comprehension and validation on one proof

Take Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many primes.
A local comprehension question asks: "Why is $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1$ not divisible by $p_1$?"
The answer is that it leaves remainder $1$ on division by $p_1$, and the question tests the reason for one step.
A holistic question asks: "Give the main idea of the proof in two sentences."
That tests whether the reader sees the shape of the argument and not only its lines.

A validation task gives four arguments for the same theorem, one of them with a wrong step, and asks which arguments are proofs.
Selden and Selden used tasks of this kind in their interviews.
Other studies record the eye movements of students and of mathematicians while they check a proof, as Inglis and Alcock did.

### What does not count

A test that asks students to "state and prove the theorem from the lecture" tests memory of a proof, not comprehension.
A student can reproduce a proof that the student does not understand, as in the opening story.
A computer that checks a formal proof in a [[proof assistant|KGPHY3F0GC]] does validate it, but by a mechanical check of rules, not by the human reading that this research studies.

### Research on validation

Annie Selden and John Selden (2003) introduced the name "validation" for the reading and checking of proofs.
They interviewed eight undergraduates in a transition-to-proof course, who judged four arguments for one theorem.
At first the students often could not tell valid from invalid arguments, and their judgments improved as they reflected during the interview.

Keith Weber (2008) observed research mathematicians as they checked proofs.
They tested claims with examples as well as by deduction.
Matthew Inglis and Lara Alcock (2012) used eye tracking on undergraduates and mathematicians reading the same proofs.
The students spent relatively more time on formulas, and the mathematicians moved their attention back and forth between lines more often, which suggests that they were inferring unstated reasons.
The mathematicians also did not all agree on which proofs were valid.

Inglis, Mejía-Ramos, Weber and Alcock (2013) asked a large group of mathematicians to judge one short proof, and again the judgments differed.
So validity, in practice, is a judgment that experts can disagree on, at least for proofs that leave steps implicit.

### Research on comprehension

Jurie Conradie and John Frith (2000) proposed comprehension tests on proofs as a form of assessment in university courses.
Juan Pablo Mejía-Ramos, Evan Fuller, Keith Weber, Kathryn Rhoads and Aron Samkoff (2012) proposed a model for assessing proof comprehension.
Its local part asks about the meaning of terms, the logical status of statements and the justification of claims.
Its holistic part asks for a summary, the division of the proof into modules, the transfer of its ideas to other problems, and illustration with examples.

Mejía-Ramos, Kristen Lew, Jimmy de la Torre and Weber (2017) built comprehension tests on this model and described how they checked the quality of the tests.
Mark Hodds, Lara Alcock and Matthew Inglis (2014) found in experiments with undergraduates that a short training in self-explanation improved proof comprehension.
The page [[self-explanation of a proof that a square is 0 or 1 modulo 4|8FG8SXKP0B]] shows the technique.

### History

Research on proof grew first around the writing of proofs, and the reading of proofs became a strong line of research in the 2000s.
The Seldens' study of 2003 started a series of studies on validation, and the model of 2012 started a series on comprehension tests.
Eye tracking and surveys of large groups of mathematicians came in the 2010s.

### Watch out

Do not assume that a student who reproduces a proof understands it.
Comprehension questions often show the opposite, as with the student who copied the $\sqrt{2}$ proof.
Do not assume either that mathematicians agree on every judgment of validity, since the studies above found disagreement even on short proofs.
And when you check a proof yourself, do not stop at "each line looks right": the experts in these studies asked what justifies each line and tested claims on examples.

### Try it

Write one local and one holistic question on the proof that the sum of two odd integers is even.
A local question is "Why can we write the first odd integer as $2k + 1$?", and its answer is the definition of odd; a holistic question is "Would the same idea show that the sum of two even integers is even?", and its answer is yes, with $2k + 2m = 2(k + m)$.

Is "Reproduce the proof that there are infinitely many primes" a comprehension question?
No, because a student can answer it from memory without understanding any step.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Reading a proof and understanding it are two different things.
A student can read each line and still not know why the proof works.
Researchers ask students questions such as "why is this step true?" and "what is the main idea?".
They also ask students to find the mistake in a wrong proof.
Students often do less well than they expect, and short training in careful reading can help.

### L2 human

We build a short comprehension test for the proof that [[the square root of 2 is irrational|0DZGSDHD92]], following the model of Mejía-Ramos and coauthors.
The proof assumes $\sqrt{2} = a/b$ with integers $a$ and $b \neq 0$ in lowest terms, derives $a^2 = 2b^2$, concludes that $a$ and $b$ are both even, and reaches a contradiction.

For the meaning of terms, ask what "in lowest terms" means; the answer is that $a$ and $b$ have no common factor greater than $1$.
For logical status, ask what the proof assumes and what it shows; it assumes that $\sqrt{2}$ is rational and derives a contradiction.
For justification, ask why $a^2 = 2b^2$ makes $a$ even; if $a$ were odd, then $a^2$ would be odd.
For a summary, ask for the idea in one sentence; a fraction for $\sqrt{2}$ could always be reduced further, which is impossible.
For transfer, ask whether the same proof works for $\sqrt{3}$; it does, with "divisible by $3$" in place of "even".
For illustration, ask why the proof fails for $\sqrt{4}$; the equation $a^2 = 4b^2$ has the solution $a = 2$, $b = 1$ in lowest terms, so no contradiction appears.

A test like this can replace "reproduce the proof" in a proof-based course.
Watch the transfer question: a student who answers "yes" with no reason has not shown transfer.

### L3 human

Comprehension and validation are two aims of reading, and they use the same acts: finding the reason for each step and testing claims on examples.
The research links the cognitive study of single readers with the social study of how proofs are accepted.
The disagreement of mathematicians on validity supports the view that acceptance of a proof depends on a community, a theme of the [[philosophy of mathematics in education|174ES70CS8]].
The [[Toulmin model|RVZCSFDB2Y]] gives a language for the unstated warrants that expert readers supply.
