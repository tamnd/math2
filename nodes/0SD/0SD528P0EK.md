## Definition

A minor is the determinant of a square submatrix that keeps its rows and columns in their original order.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $A = (a_{ij})$ be an $m \times n$ [[matrix|0N1MY1T0W9]] over $K$, and let $1 \le k \le \min(m, n)$.
Choose row indices $i_1 < i_2 < \dots < i_k$ and column indices $j_1 < j_2 < \dots < j_k$, and put $I = \{ i_1, \dots, i_k \}$ and $J = \{ j_1, \dots, j_k \}$.
Let $A_{I,J}$ be the $k \times k$ matrix whose entry in row $r$ and column $s$ is $a_{i_r j_s}$.
The *minor of order $k$* of $A$ with rows $I$ and columns $J$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]] $\det A_{I,J}$.

For a square matrix with $n \ge 2$, the *first minor* $M_{ij}$ is the minor with rows $\{ 1, \dots, n \} \setminus \{ i \}$ and columns $\{ 1, \dots, n \} \setminus \{ j \}$.
A minor is *principal* when $I = J$, and *leading principal* when $I = J = \{ 1, \dots, k \}$.

## Remarks

Each condition in the definition has a job.
The ring is commutative so that the determinant of $A_{I,J}$ is defined by the Leibniz formula.
The bound $k \le \min(m, n)$ is needed because we must find $k$ distinct rows and $k$ distinct columns.
The indices are listed in increasing order because the order fixes the sign: listing two columns the other way round swaps two columns of $A_{I,J}$ and negates its determinant.

A small example: for $A$ with rows $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 4)$, the first minor $M_{12}$ deletes row $1$ and column $2$, so $M_{12} = \det(3) = 3$.
A case that fails: rows $1, 2$ and columns $2, 1$ of the same matrix give the array with rows $(2, 1)$ and $(4, 3)$, whose determinant $2$ is not a minor, since the only minor of order $2$ is $\det A = -2$.

The minors of order $1$ are the entries of $A$, and the signed first minors are the [[cofactors|0XPKR18APA]].
Over a field, the [[rank|0ZC9YTXZRY]] of $A$ is the largest $k$ such that $A$ has a nonzero minor of order $k$, by the theorem on [[rank by minors|HVHQ50TJB1]].
The [[concept page|0BT9BYWVRY]] works out examples and uses.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A minor is the determinant of a smaller square piece of a table of numbers.
For a $3 \times 3$ table, we delete one row and one column and take the $2 \times 2$ determinant of the four numbers that remain.
For the table with rows $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 4)$, deleting the first row and the second column leaves the single number $3$, and that is a minor of order $1$.
Minors appear in the expansion of determinants and in the formula for the inverse.

### L2 human

Here is a typical computation of one minor of order $2$ of the $3 \times 3$ matrix with rows $(1, 0, 2)$, $(3, 1, 4)$ and $(0, 5, 6)$.
Choose rows $1, 3$ and columns $2, 3$, and keep them in this order:
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix} = 0 \cdot 6 - 2 \cdot 5 = -10.$$
This is also the first minor $M_{21}$, since it deletes row $2$ and column $1$.
Watch out: the columns taken in the order $3, 2$ would give $+10$, which is not a minor.
