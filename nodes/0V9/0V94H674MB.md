## Definition

The determinant of $n$ vectors in a basis is the determinant of the square matrix of their coordinates in that basis.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $B = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ be a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] of $V$.
For vectors $v_1, \dots, v_n \in V$, let $M$ be the $n \times n$ matrix whose column $j$ is the [[coordinate vector|0BMVBHTWV2]] $[v_j]_B$.
The *determinant of $(v_1, \dots, v_n)$ in the basis $B$* is
$$\det_B(v_1, \dots, v_n) = \det M,$$
the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]] of $M$.

## Notation

We write $\det_B(v_1, \dots, v_n)$, or $\det_B(v)$ for the family $v = (v_1, \dots, v_n)$.
For a second basis $B'$, $\det_B(B')$ is the determinant of the vectors of $B'$, taken in their order.

## Remarks

The conditions have simple reasons.
The family must have exactly $n = \dim V$ vectors, so that $M$ is square.
The basis $B$ must be fixed in advance, because the coordinates and so the value depend on it.
The columns of $M$ follow the order of the family, and another order can change the sign.

As a function of the family, $\det_B \colon V^n \to F$ is multilinear and alternating, because the determinant of matrices has these properties in the columns.
It takes the value $1$ on $B$, since the coordinate matrix of $B$ itself is $I_n$.
By the [[uniqueness of the normalized alternating form|1C8RRWHD45]], these properties determine $\det_B$ completely.
The vectors form a basis exactly when the value is nonzero, by the [[basis criterion|5D1CHPV9Z3]], and the [[change of basis rule|T1WN2DHS63]] gives $\det_{B'} = \det_{B'}(B) \cdot \det_B$.

For a small example, in $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the standard basis, $\det((3, 0), (1, 2)) = 3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 0 = 6$.
A case that fails a condition is a pair of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, whose coordinate matrix is $3 \times 2$, so [[it has no determinant|CECRXV7KAZ]].
The same definition works for a free module of rank $n$ over a commutative ring.
The [[concept page|05CJSSXPR3]] explains the geometric meaning.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Write $n$ vectors in the coordinates of a fixed basis, and put the coordinates side by side as columns.
The determinant of this square matrix measures the signed volume of the box that the vectors span.
The unit of volume is the box of the basis vectors.

### L2 human

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ take $B = ((1, 1), (0, 1))$, with $v_1 = (1, 1)$ and $v_2 = (1, 0)$.

1. Since $v_1 = 1 \cdot (1, 1) + 0 \cdot (0, 1)$, the coordinate vector is $[v_1]_B = (1, 0)$.
2. Since $v_2 = 1 \cdot (1, 1) - 1 \cdot (0, 1)$, the coordinate vector is $[v_2]_B = (1, -1)$.
3. So the definition gives
$$\det_B(v_1, v_2) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = -1.$$
4. Check in the standard basis $E$: $\det_E(v_1, v_2) = 1 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1 = -1$ and $\det_E(B) = 1$, so the two values agree, as the change of basis rule requires.

The typical use is to measure volume or to test for a basis in a space without a standard basis.
Watch out: the coordinates go into the columns in the order of the family, and the order $v_2, v_1$ gives $+1$.

### L3 human

For the free module $K^n$ over a commutative ring $K$, $\det_B$ generates the module of alternating $n$-linear forms, which is free of rank one.
