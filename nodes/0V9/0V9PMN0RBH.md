## Description

The matrices of determinant $1$ form a subgroup of the general linear group, the special linear group.
Let $F$ be a field and let $n \ge 1$.
The *special linear group* is the set
$$\mathrm{SL}_n(F) = \{ A \in \mathrm{GL}_n(F) : \det A = 1 \}$$
of $n \times n$ matrices over $F$ with [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] $1$, with matrix multiplication as operation.
It is a subgroup of the [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$, as the [[subgroup example|PDX10T7N7Z]] checks.
The same definition works over each commutative ring $R$, and gives $\mathrm{SL}_n(R)$.

## Remarks

### Kernel of the determinant

The group $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$ is the kernel of the group homomorphism $\det \colon \mathrm{GL}_n(F) \to F^{\times}$, so it is a [[normal subgroup|0CB0DX5BVV]].
The determinant is surjective, since $\det \operatorname{diag}(c, 1, \dots, 1) = c$ for each $c \ne 0$.
So the quotient $\mathrm{GL}_n(F) / \mathrm{SL}_n(F)$ is isomorphic to $F^{\times}$.
For $n = 1$ the group is trivial, since the only $1 \times 1$ matrix with determinant $1$ is $(1)$.

### Generators

Over a field, $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$ is generated by the [[transvections|0DTCBCKEJP]] $T_{ij}(c)$, by the theorem on [[generation by transvections|YSS4XNY9RC]].
For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $4 - 3 = 1$, and
$$T_{12}(1)\, T_{21}(1)\, T_{12}(1) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Row exchanges and scalings are not needed, and in general they are not in the group: $\det P_{ij} = -1$, and $\det D_i(c) = c$.

### Geometry

Over $\mathbb{R}$, the number $\abs{\det A}$ is the factor by which $A$ scales areas and volumes, and the sign of $\det A$ says whether $A$ keeps orientation.
So $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ consists of the linear maps that keep both volume and orientation.
Shears $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and the squeeze maps $\operatorname{diag}(t, t^{-1})$ with $t > 0$ are typical elements, while rotations of the plane are elements too.

### Over the integers and beyond

The group $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ consists of the integer $2 \times 2$ matrices with determinant $1$, and it is a group because the inverse $\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$ of such a matrix again has integer entries.
The examples [[$\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$|Q0GBCB9RA4]] and [[$\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$|J58ED1GV6N]] treat the real and complex cases.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathrm{SL}_n$ is a [[matrix Lie group|36KSZ7GA5S]]; that view belongs to Lie theory.

### Watch out

The special linear group is not the set of matrices with determinant $\pm 1$.
That set is also a group, and it is larger than $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$ whenever $1 \ne -1$ in $F$; it contains $\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1)$, which reverses orientation.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the group of all matrices with determinant $\pm 1$ is $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$, and $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$ has index $2$ in it.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The special linear group collects the matrices with determinant $1$.
In the real case, these maps keep the area or volume of every region, and they do not flip the space over.
A shear, which slides each horizontal line along itself by an amount proportional to its height, is a typical element.

### L2 human

Here is a membership check: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\det A = 4 - 3 = 1$, so $A \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Q})$.
Its inverse is $A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $4 - 3 = 1$ again, as the subgroup property requires.
The matrix $\operatorname{diag}(2, \tfrac{1}{2})$ is in $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Q})$, but $\operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$ is not.
A typical use of the group is to normalize a problem: since $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$, over $\mathbb{C}$ every invertible matrix is a scalar multiple of one in $\mathrm{SL}_n$.

### L3 human

$\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$ is the commutator subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$, except when $n = 2$ and $F$ has $2$ elements.
Its quotient by its center, the projective special linear group, is simple for $n \ge 2$, except for $n = 2$ and $F$ with $2$ or $3$ elements.
These facts belong to group theory.
