## Definition

Use units with $c = \hbar = 1$ and coordinates $(t, \mathbf{x}) \in \R \times \R^3$.
Let $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$ be the [[Pauli matrices|XAQCW3D4QQ]] and write $\boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \nabla = \sum_{k=1}^3 \sigma_k\, \partial / \partial x_k$.
The *Weyl equations* for a function $\psi \colon \R \times \R^3 \to \C^2$ are
$$\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \nabla \psi = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} - \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \nabla \psi = 0.$$
With $\sigma^\mu = (I_2, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3)$ and $\bar\sigma^\mu = (I_2, -\sigma_1, -\sigma_2, -\sigma_3)$, they read $\sigma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi = 0$ and $\bar\sigma^\mu \partial_\mu \psi = 0$.

## Remarks

Since $(\boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \nabla)^2 = \Delta$, one has $(\partial_t - \boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\nabla)(\partial_t + \boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\nabla) = \partial_t^2 - \Delta$, so every component of a solution of either equation solves the wave equation.

Example: let $\mathbf{p} \in \R^3 \setminus \set{0}$ and let $u \in \C^2$ be an eigenvector of $\boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{p}$ with eigenvalue $\abs{\mathbf{p}}$.
Then $\psi = u\, e^{-i(\abs{\mathbf{p}} t - \mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x})}$ satisfies $\partial_t \psi + \boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\nabla\psi = \left(-i\abs{\mathbf{p}} + i\, \boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{p}\right)\psi = 0$.
It is a positive-energy solution of the first equation whose spin points along $\mathbf{p}$.

Non-example: if $u$ is instead the eigenvector of $\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{p}$ with eigenvalue $-\abs{\mathbf{p}}$, then $\psi = u\, e^{-i(\abs{\mathbf{p}} t - \mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x})}$ still solves the wave equation, but $\partial_t\psi + \boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\nabla\psi = -2i\abs{\mathbf{p}}\psi \ne 0$; it solves the second Weyl equation instead.

Which of the two equations is called right-handed and which left-handed depends on conventions.
In the Weyl representation of the gamma matrices, the [[Dirac equation|XNZX7VXXR2]] with mass $m = 0$ decouples into the two Weyl equations for the upper and lower pairs of components.
