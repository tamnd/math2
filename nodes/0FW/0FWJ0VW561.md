## Definition

An orthonormal basis is a basis whose vectors are unit vectors at right angles to each other.
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional [[inner product space|01EK923SKP]] over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, of dimension $n$.
An *orthonormal basis* of $V$ is a family $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ of vectors of $V$ such that:

1. $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ is a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] of $V$;
2. $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ is an [[orthonormal family|09VYHEF2VT]], that is, $\ip{e_i}{e_j} = \delta_{ij}$ for all $i, j$.

## Notation

Mathlib writes `OrthonormalBasis ι 𝕜 E` for a finite index type `ι`.
It stores a linear isometry from `E` onto `EuclideanSpace 𝕜 ι`, the space $\mathbb{K}^\iota$ with the standard inner product, so it is a variant of the definition above.
For a square matrix, "orthogonal" over $\mathbb{R}$ and "unitary" over $\mathbb{C}$ mean that the columns form an orthonormal basis.

## Remarks

Condition 1 is the linear part of the definition, and condition 2 is the metric part.
They are not independent of each other.
An orthonormal family is linearly independent, so condition 1 can be replaced by "the family spans $V$" or by "the family has $n$ members".
In practice you check condition 2 and count.

For example, $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ have squared norms $\tfrac{1 + 1}{2} = 1$ and dot product $\tfrac{1 - 1}{2} = 0$.
There are $2 = \dim \mathbb{R}^2$ of them, so they form an orthonormal basis.
The vector $e_1$ alone satisfies condition 2 and fails condition 1, since it does not span $\mathbb{R}^2$.
The pair $(1, 1)$, $(1, -1)$ satisfies condition 1 and fails condition 2, as the [[orthogonal basis that is not orthonormal|72YCTDMG60]] shows.

Condition 2 depends on the inner product.
The standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is orthonormal for the dot product, but not for $2x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$, which gives $\ip{e_1}{e_1} = 2$.
The Mathlib form agrees with ours: the isometry sends $e_i$ to the $i$-th standard vector, and an isometry preserves the values $\ip{e_i}{e_j}$.
In infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, "orthonormal basis" usually means a maximal orthonormal family, which is in general not a basis in the algebraic sense.
The concept page on the [[orthonormal basis|0S9GGTR0EX]] gives the uses and more examples.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An orthonormal basis is a basis whose arrows have length $1$ and meet at right angles.
The axes $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ of the plane are one.
Each coordinate of a vector is then its inner product with one basis arrow.

### L2 human

To check the definition for $n$ vectors in a space of dimension $n$, it is enough to check orthonormality.
Take $e_1 = (\tfrac35, \tfrac45)$ and $e_2 = (-\tfrac45, \tfrac35)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
The squared norms are $\tfrac{9 + 16}{25} = 1$ and $\tfrac{16 + 9}{25} = 1$, and the dot product is $\tfrac{-12 + 12}{25} = 0$.
So the pair is orthonormal, and since it has $2$ members it is an orthonormal basis, with no need to row reduce.

### L3 human

An orthonormal basis is the same as a linear isometry $V \to \mathbb{K}^n$, where $\mathbb{K}^n$ has the standard inner product.
This is the form that Mathlib uses, and it shows that every inner product space of dimension $n$ is isometric to $\mathbb{K}^n$, once an orthonormal basis exists.
The two conditions of the definition separate the linear structure, a basis, from the metric structure, orthonormality.
