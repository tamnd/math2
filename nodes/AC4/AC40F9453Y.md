## Example

Consider the two sentences "all $S$ are $P$" and "all $P$ are $S$".
We show that they stand in none of the four relations of the [[square of opposition|VYNBSVZ2TH]].

1. The corners of one square share the same subject term and the same predicate term.
   The first sentence has subject $S$ and the second has subject $P$, so they are not two corners of one square.
2. Each relation of the square excludes some pair of truth values.
   Contradictories exclude (true, true) and (false, false), contraries exclude (true, true), subcontraries exclude (false, false), and subalternation excludes (true, false).
3. Both true: let $S$ and $P$ both be the squares, and "all squares are squares" is true twice.
4. First true, second false: let $S$ be the squares and $P$ the rectangles.
   "All squares are rectangles" is true, and "all rectangles are squares" is false, as the rectangle with sides $1$ and $2$ shows.
5. First false, second true: let $S$ be the rectangles and $P$ the squares, which is step 4 with the roles exchanged.
6. Both false: let $S$ be the even numbers and $P$ the multiples of $3$.
   "All even numbers are multiples of $3$" is false because of $2$, and "all multiples of $3$ are even" is false because of $3$.
7. All four pairs of truth values occur, so every relation of the square fails.
   Every term in steps 3 to 6 is non-empty, so this holds in the traditional and in the modern reading.

## Remarks

The second sentence is the converse of the first, and the two are logically independent: the truth value of one tells nothing about the other.
Confusing a theorem with its converse is a common error in proofs, and this is its syllogistic form.

The E and I forms behave differently.
"No $S$ is $P$" and "no $P$ is $S$" both say $S \cap P = \varnothing$, and "some $S$ is $P$" and "some $P$ is $S$" both say $S \cap P \neq \varnothing$.
For these forms conversion is valid, and the converse is the same corner of the square in another wording.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"All squares are rectangles" is true, but "all rectangles are squares" is false.
Swapping the two groups can change a true sentence into a false one.
It can also change a false sentence into a true one, or keep both true or both false.
So the two sentences are not opposites in any sense of the square: knowing one tells us nothing about the other.

### L2 human

We show the independence with sets.

1. "All $S$ are $P$" says $S \subseteq P$, and "all $P$ are $S$" says $P \subseteq S$.
2. Both hold exactly when $S = P$.
3. Only the first holds when $S$ is a proper subset of $P$.
4. Only the second holds when $P$ is a proper subset of $S$.
5. Neither holds when each set has an element outside the other, as for the even numbers and the multiples of $3$.

A proof of $S \subseteq P$ says nothing about $P \subseteq S$, and set equality needs both.
The trap is to treat "all $P$ are $S$" as the negation of "all $S$ are $P$": the negation is "some $S$ is not $P$".
