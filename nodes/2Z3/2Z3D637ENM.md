## Statement

A group of $n$ voters, with $n$ odd, decides between two options, exactly one of which is correct.
Each voter votes for the correct option with probability $p$, independently of the other voters.
Let $P_n$ be the probability that a strict majority of the $n$ votes is for the correct option.

1. If $p > 1/2$, then $P_n$ is strictly increasing in $n$ (over odd $n$) and $P_n \to 1$ as $n \to \infty$.
2. If $p < 1/2$, then $P_n$ is strictly decreasing in $n$ (over odd $n$) and $P_n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.
3. If $p = 1/2$, then $P_n = 1/2$ for every odd $n$.

## Remarks

The number of correct votes has the [[binomial distribution|0WCWA1KT3M]] with parameters $n$ and $p$, so
$$P_n = \sum_{k = (n+1)/2}^{n} \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}.$$
The limits follow from the [[law of large numbers|0ZVEAYVRC3]]: the fraction of correct votes tends to $p$ in probability.
Monotonicity follows by adding two voters at a time: the majority changes only if the first $n$ votes were split by one, and then $P_{n+2} - P_n$ has the sign of $p - 1/2$.

Example: with $p = 0.6$, one voter is right with probability $0.6$, and three voters are right by majority with probability $0.6^3 + 3 \cdot 0.6^2 \cdot 0.4 = 0.648$.

Independence is needed.
If all voters copy one leader, the majority is right with probability $p$ for every $n$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose each juror is a bit better than a coin flip at finding the truth, and the jurors judge independently.
Then a large jury is very likely to be right by majority.
More jurors help as long as each is more often right than wrong.
If each is more often wrong, a bigger jury makes things worse.

### L2 human

The theorem is a basic argument for majority rule as a way of tracking truth, and an early application of probability to collective decisions.
It is a direct consequence of the law of large numbers for Bernoulli trials.
Extensions treat voters with different competences, correlated votes, and strategic voting.
