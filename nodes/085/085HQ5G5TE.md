## Statement

When all the eigenvalues of a square matrix lie in the field, the matrix is similar to a block diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks, and the blocks are unique up to order.
Let $F$ be a field and $A \in M_n(F)$.
Assume that the [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]] of $A$ splits over $F$:
$$\chi_A = (x - \lambda_1)^{m_1} \cdots (x - \lambda_p)^{m_p}, \qquad \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p \in F \text{ distinct}.$$

1. *Existence.* $A$ is [[similar|027KDW2K72]] over $F$ to a [[block diagonal matrix|0M0SNKZ482]] $J = J_{k_1}(\mu_1) \oplus \dots \oplus J_{k_s}(\mu_s)$ of [[Jordan blocks|0PBFS92AJP]], with each $\mu_i \in \{\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p\}$.
2. *Uniqueness.* $J$ is unique up to the order of the blocks. For each $\lambda \in F$ and each $j \ge 1$, the number of blocks $J_j(\lambda)$ in $J$ is $r_{j-1} - 2 r_j + r_{j+1}$, where $r_j = \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)^j$ is the [[rank|0EWQEYRVA8]] and $r_0 = n$.
3. *Algebraically closed fields.* If $F$ is [[algebraically closed|02SJG1HC32]], for example $F = \mathbb{C}$, then the hypothesis holds for every $A \in M_n(F)$.

The matrix $J$ is the *Jordan normal form* of $A$.
So two matrices in $M_n(F)$ whose characteristic polynomials split over $F$ are similar if and only if they have the same Jordan form, up to the order of the blocks.

## Remarks

### Why the polynomial must split

A Jordan matrix is upper triangular, and its diagonal entries are the roots of its characteristic polynomial.
So a matrix over $F$ that is similar to a Jordan matrix over $F$ has a characteristic polynomial that splits over $F$, and the hypothesis cannot be dropped.
The [[quarter turn of the real plane|PFZF3KRBBB]] has characteristic polynomial $x^2 + 1$, which has no real root, so it has no Jordan form over $\mathbb{R}$.
The [[companion matrix of x² − 2 over the rationals|KFFR8JXBH9]] fails in the same way over $\mathbb{Q}$, although it has a Jordan form over $\mathbb{R}$.
Over any field, the [[rational canonical form|042JWAR3Q0]] needs no such hypothesis, and over $\mathbb{R}$ the [[real Jordan form|MPFT45RFGV]] repairs the defect with $2 \times 2$ rotation-scaling blocks.

### What the blocks tell you

The Jordan form puts the main invariants of a matrix on display.
For an eigenvalue $\lambda$, the number of blocks with eigenvalue $\lambda$ is the geometric multiplicity $\dim \ker(A - \lambda I)$, since each block contributes one eigenvector.
The sizes of these blocks add up to the algebraic multiplicity $m_\lambda$, and the largest of them is the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of the minimal polynomial.
So $A$ is diagonalizable exactly when every block has size $1$.
Splitting $J$ into its diagonal and its part above the diagonal and conjugating back gives $A = D + N$ with $D$ diagonalizable, $N$ nilpotent and $DN = ND$, the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]].
Powers, polynomials and the exponential of $A$ reduce to those of single blocks, by the [[lemma on powers of a Jordan block|B9CHNGJV5K]].

### A worked use

Take the matrix of the [[3 by 3 example with two eigenvalues|S56B41JF5A]]:
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & -2 \\ -1 & 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \chi_A = (x - 1)^2 (x - 3).$$
For $\lambda = 1$ the ranks of $(A - I)^j$ are $r_0 = 3$, $r_1 = 2$, $r_2 = 1$ and $r_3 = 1$.
The formula of part 2 gives $r_0 - 2 r_1 + r_2 = 0$ blocks $J_1(1)$ and $r_1 - 2 r_2 + r_3 = 1$ block $J_2(1)$.
The eigenvalue $3$ is a simple root, so it has one block of size $1$, and the Jordan form is $J_2(1) \oplus J_1(3)$.
The [[method for computing the Jordan form|7M1W0ZA49Q]] also finds the basis that achieves it.

### Watch out

Having $n$ eigenvalues counted with multiplicity does not make a matrix diagonalizable.
The block $J_2(0)$ has the eigenvalue $0$ twice, but its eigenspace is a line, so its Jordan form is the block itself.
Also, the Jordan form is unique only up to the order of the blocks, so $J_2(1) \oplus J_1(3)$ and $J_1(3) \oplus J_2(1)$ are the same answer.

### The converse

Every Jordan matrix with diagonal entries in $F$ occurs, as its own Jordan form.
If $A$ is similar over $F$ to any triangular matrix, then $\chi_A$ splits over $F$, so the splitting hypothesis is also necessary.

### Generalizations

The theorem is the case $R = F[x]$ of the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, in its elementary divisor form.
The [[rational canonical form|042JWAR3Q0]] is the same theorem in its invariant factor form, and it works over every field.
For a compact operator on a Banach space, each nonzero eigenvalue has a finite-dimensional generalized eigenspace, and the operator has a Jordan form on it.

## History

The theorem is named after Camille Jordan, who published it in 1870 in his treatise on substitutions and algebraic equations.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every complex square matrix becomes almost diagonal in a well chosen basis.
The only entries off the diagonal are some ones just above it, inside the Jordan blocks.
For example, a $3 \times 3$ matrix might become $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, with a block of size $2$ for the eigenvalue $1$ and a block of size $1$ for $3$.
Two complex matrices are similar exactly when they give the same picture, up to the order of the blocks.

### L2 human

Here is a typical decision: are the matrices
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$
similar over $\mathbb{Q}$?
Both have $\chi = (x - 2)^3$, so we compare the ranks of the powers of $A - 2I$ and $B - 2I$.
Each of these has rank $1$, and each square is zero, because the image of $B - 2I$ is spanned by $(1, 1, 0)$ and $B - 2I$ kills that vector.
So both have ranks $3, 1, 0, 0$, which gives $3 - 2 + 0 = 1$ block of size $1$ and $1 - 0 + 0 = 1$ block of size $2$.
Both Jordan forms are $J_2(2) \oplus J_1(2)$, so $A$ and $B$ are similar.
The trap is to compare only the characteristic polynomials: $2I$ also has $\chi = (x - 2)^3$, but it is not similar to $A$.

### L3 human

View $F^n$ as an $F[x]$-module, with $x$ acting as $A$.
When $\chi_A$ splits, the elementary divisors are powers $(x - \lambda)^k$, and $J_k(\lambda)$ is the matrix of $x$ on $F[x]/((x - \lambda)^k)$ in the basis $(x - \lambda)^{k-1}, \dots, x - \lambda, 1$.
So the Jordan form is the elementary divisor decomposition of this module, written in bases adapted to the factors $x - \lambda$.
The [[proof by generalized eigenspaces and chains|6GSKMHQTD1]] avoids modules, and the [[cyclic decomposition theorem|R3Z20J19BR]] gives the module route.
The [[classification of matrices up to similarity|H3E52HTEZ2]] places both forms in one picture.
