## Statement

Let $F$ be a field and $A \in M_n(F)$.
Assume that the [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]] of $A$ splits over $F$: $\chi_A = (x - \lambda_1)^{m_1} \cdots (x - \lambda_p)^{m_p}$ with distinct $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p \in F$.

1. *Existence.* $A$ is [[similar|027KDW2K72]] over $F$ to a [[block diagonal matrix|0M0SNKZ482]]
$$J = J_{k_1}(\mu_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus J_{k_s}(\mu_s)$$
of [[Jordan blocks|0PBFS92AJP]], with each $\mu_i \in \{\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p\}$.
2. *Uniqueness.* $J$ is unique up to the order of the blocks. For each eigenvalue $\lambda$ and each $j \ge 1$, the number of blocks $J_j(\lambda)$ is $r_{j-1} - 2 r_j + r_{j+1}$, where $r_j = \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)^j$ is the [[rank|0EWQEYRVA8]] and $r_0 = n$.
3. If $F$ is [[algebraically closed|02SJG1HC32]], for example $F = \mathbb{C}$, the hypothesis holds for every $A$.

The matrix $J$ is the *Jordan normal form* of $A$.
Two matrices whose characteristic polynomials split over $F$ are similar if and only if they have the same Jordan form, up to the order of the blocks.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The characteristic polynomial must split.
The [[rotation by a quarter turn over $\mathbb{R}$|PFZF3KRBBB]] has characteristic polynomial $x^2 + 1$.
It is not similar over $\mathbb{R}$ to any real Jordan matrix, since a Jordan matrix is triangular and has its eigenvalues on the diagonal.
Over any field, the [[rational canonical form|042JWAR3Q0]] needs no such hypothesis.
The diagonal entries of $J$ are the roots of $\chi_A$, so they must lie in $F$ for $J$ to be a matrix over $F$.

### Consequences

- For an eigenvalue $\lambda$, the number of blocks with eigenvalue $\lambda$ is its geometric multiplicity $\dim \ker(A - \lambda I)$.
- The sum of the sizes of these blocks is the algebraic multiplicity $m_\lambda$.
- The size of the largest block for $\lambda$ is the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of the minimal polynomial.
- $A$ is diagonalisable if and only if all its blocks have size $1$.
- $A = D + N$ with $D$ diagonalisable, $N$ nilpotent and $DN = ND$: take $D$ and $N$ as the diagonal and the off-diagonal parts of $J$, conjugated back. This is the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]].
- $A$ is similar to $A^{\mathsf T}$, since $J_k(\lambda)^{\mathsf T}$ is similar to $J_k(\lambda)$ by the reversal matrix.
- Powers, polynomials and the exponential of $A$ reduce to those of single Jordan blocks.

### Converse

Every Jordan matrix with eigenvalues in $F$ occurs, as its own Jordan form.
If $A$ is similar over $F$ to a triangular matrix, in particular to a Jordan matrix, then $\chi_A$ splits over $F$, so the hypothesis is also necessary.

### Generalizations

- The [[rational canonical form|042JWAR3Q0]] works over every field.
- The [[real Jordan form|MPFT45RFGV]] replaces each pair of complex conjugate blocks by a real block of size $2k$.
- The theorem is the case $R = F[x]$ of the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, in its elementary divisor form.
- For compact operators on Banach spaces, the nonzero part of the spectrum has a similar decomposition into finite-dimensional generalized eigenspaces.

## History

The Jordan normal form is named after Camille Jordan, who first stated the Jordan decomposition theorem in 1870.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every complex square matrix becomes almost diagonal in a good basis.
The only entries off the diagonal are some ones just above it, inside the Jordan blocks.
Two complex matrices are similar exactly when they have the same Jordan form, up to the order of the blocks.

### L2 human

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & -2 \\ -1 & 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, worked out in [[a $3 \times 3$ example|S56B41JF5A]].

1. Its characteristic polynomial is $(x - 1)^2 (x - 3)$.
2. The ranks of $(A - I)^j$ are $r_0, r_1, r_2, r_3 = 3, 2, 1, 1$. So the number of blocks $J_1(1)$ is $r_0 - 2 r_1 + r_2 = 0$, and the number of blocks $J_2(1)$ is $r_1 - 2 r_2 + r_3 = 1$.
3. The eigenvalue $3$ is a simple root, so it has one block, of size $1$.
4. The Jordan form is $J_2(1) \oplus J_1(3)$.

The trap is to forget that $A$ must have all its eigenvalues in $F$.

### L3 human

View $F^n$ as an $F[x]$-module, with $x$ acting as $A$.
The elementary divisors are the powers $(x - \lambda)^k$, and the Jordan block $J_k(\lambda)$ is the matrix of $x$ on $F[x]/((x - \lambda)^k)$.
So the Jordan form is the elementary divisor decomposition of this module, written in bases adapted to the factors $x - \lambda$.
