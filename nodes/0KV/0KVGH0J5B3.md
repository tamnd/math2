## Summary

A [[group|G5S1M9]] $G$ is cyclic when there is an element $g \in G$ with $G = \langle g \rangle$.
Here $\langle g \rangle = \{g^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is the [[cyclic subgroup generated by $g$|08ABT85339]].
Such an element $g$ is a generator of $G$.
So each element of a cyclic group is a power of one fixed element.
Up to isomorphism, the cyclic groups are $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ and the groups $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, +)$ for $n \geq 1$.
They are the simplest groups, and many other groups are built from them.

## Motivation

To describe a group, we can list a few elements and say that all the other elements come from them.
The smallest case is one element: all the elements are powers of a single $g$.
Then the whole group depends on one question: when is $g^a = g^b$?
The answer depends only on the [[order|0WE2NHZJ7B]] of $g$.
So a cyclic group is known completely once we know its size.
Cyclic groups also appear inside every group: each element $g$ of any group generates the cyclic group $\langle g \rangle$.

## Notation

1. We write $G = \langle g \rangle$ when $g$ generates $G$.
2. The notations $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}/n$, $\mathbb{Z}_n$ and $C_n$ all denote the cyclic group of order $n$.
3. We use $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for the additive group of [[integers modulo $n$|03VFZ2WETT]], and $C_n$ for a cyclic group of order $n$ in multiplicative notation.
4. The infinite cyclic group is $\mathbb{Z}$, also written $C_\infty$.
5. Warning: in number theory, $\mathbb{Z}_p$ denotes the ring of [[$p$-adic integers|V6TJAFZ4MD]] for a prime $p$. This is an infinite group, not the cyclic group of order $p$. When a text writes $\mathbb{Z}_p$, check which meaning it uses.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The [[infinite cyclic group|02B1T50JD1]] $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ has the generators $1$ and $-1$.
2. The group [[$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic|FB79N7AHZA]], with generator $1$. It is the standard model of the [[cyclic group of order $n$|0G0B3DRG65]].
3. The [[group of $n$th roots of unity|0WX11EA3F3]] in $\mathbb{C}^\times$ is cyclic of order $n$, with generator $e^{2\pi i/n}$.
4. The rotations of a regular $n$-gon about its center form a cyclic group of order $n$, generated by the rotation through $2\pi/n$.
5. The group $\{1, -1\}$ under multiplication is cyclic of order $2$, and [[it is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$|7FCN2KNWC0]].
6. The group $(\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z})^\times$ of nonzero residues modulo $7$ under multiplication is cyclic with generator $3$. The powers $3^1, \ldots, 3^6$ are $3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1$ modulo $7$.
7. Each group of prime order is cyclic, by [[this corollary of Lagrange's theorem|05MM4EK4SA]].

### Non-examples

1. The [[Klein four-group is abelian but not cyclic|DX6X1D21FE]]: each element generates a subgroup with at most $2$ elements.
2. The group $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$ is [[not cyclic|E1EFH8EV3G]], although each finitely generated subgroup of it is cyclic.
3. The group [[$\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ is not cyclic|VH8C6HD8KP]], although two elements generate it.
4. The [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$ is not cyclic, because it is not [[abelian|082GHNPJ53]] and [[cyclic groups are abelian|04RG02S6BA]].
5. The group $(\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})^\times = \{1, 3, 5, 7\}$ under multiplication is not cyclic: $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$ for each of its $4$ elements.

### Basic properties

Let $G = \langle g \rangle$ be cyclic.

1. $G$ is abelian, by [[the theorem that cyclic groups are abelian|04RG02S6BA]].
2. The [[order|0B85S2V2DR]] of $G$ equals the order of $g$, by [[this theorem|031M8HX7DA]].
3. $G$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ or to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and two cyclic groups of the same order are isomorphic, by [[the classification of cyclic groups|0736AXH2ME]].
4. Each subgroup of $G$ is cyclic, by [[this theorem|0KB1ND7XDS]]. If $|G| = n$ is finite, then $G$ has exactly one subgroup of order $d$ for each divisor $d$ of $n$, by [[the fundamental theorem of cyclic groups|0423TXR9AN]].
5. Each homomorphic image of $G$ is cyclic: if $f \colon G \to H$ is a [[group homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]], then $f(G) = \langle f(g) \rangle$, because $f(g^k) = f(g)^k$.
6. If $G$ is infinite, then $G$ has exactly two generators, $g$ and $g^{-1}$. This is proved on the page of the [[infinite cyclic group|02B1T50JD1]].
7. The generators of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ are the residues $k$ with $\gcd(k, n) = 1$, that is, the [[units|0XVEZDE9J8]] of the ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. So there are $\varphi(n)$ generators, where $\varphi$ is [[Euler's totient function|0DAMHCT17R]]. The proof of property 7 follows.

Proof of property 7.
Let $n \geq 1$ and $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

1. Suppose $\gcd(k, n) = 1$. By [[Bézout's identity|0DQVHMYAGA]], there are integers $a, b$ with $ak + bn = 1$.
2. Then $ak \equiv 1 \pmod n$. So the residue $1$ is the sum of $a$ copies of $k$ (of $\abs{a}$ copies of $-k$ if $a < 0$), and $1 \in \langle k \rangle$.
3. The residue $1$ generates $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. Since $\langle k \rangle$ is a subgroup that contains $1$, it contains $\langle 1 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. So $k$ is a generator.
4. Conversely, suppose $k$ generates $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. Then $1 \in \langle k \rangle$, so $ak \equiv 1 \pmod n$ for some integer $a$.
5. So $ak - 1 = -bn$ for some integer $b$, that is, $ak + bn = 1$.
6. Let $d = \gcd(k, n)$. Then $d$ divides $ak + bn = 1$, so $d = 1$. $\blacksquare$

In the same way, in $G = \langle g \rangle$ of order $n$, the element $g^k$ generates $G$ if and only if $\gcd(k, n) = 1$.
This also follows from [[the order of a power|0VVPQNVM8S]]: $g^k$ has order $n/\gcd(n, k)$.

### Common errors

1. A student assumes that each abelian group is cyclic. The Klein four-group is a counterexample.
2. A student assumes that each nonidentity element of a cyclic group is a generator. In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, the element $2$ generates only $\{0, 2, 4\}$.
3. A student assumes that a group with an element of infinite order is infinite cyclic. The group $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ is a counterexample.
4. A student reads $\mathbb{Z}_p$ as the cyclic group of order $p$ in a text where it means the $p$-adic integers.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A cyclic group is a group where one element produces all the others.
Combine this element with itself again and again, and also combine its inverse with itself.
You reach every element of the group.
A clock is a good picture: start at $0$ and keep adding one hour.
After $12$ steps you have visited every hour, and you are back at the start.
The integers under addition are also cyclic: from $1$ you reach every positive number by adding, and every negative number by subtracting.

### L2 human

We find all the generators of $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$.

1. By property 7, the residue $k$ is a generator exactly when $\gcd(k, 12) = 1$.
2. The residues from $0$ to $11$ with this property are $1, 5, 7, 11$. So there are $4 = \varphi(12)$ generators.
3. Check $k = 5$: the multiples $5, 10, 15, 20, \ldots$ reduce to $5, 10, 3, 8, 1, 6, 11, 4, 9, 2, 7, 0$, which are all $12$ residues.
4. Check $k = 8$: $\gcd(8, 12) = 4$, and the multiples of $8$ are only $8, 4, 0$.

The typical use: to show that a group is cyclic, find one element whose order equals the order of the group.
To show that a group is not cyclic, show that it is not abelian, or, for a finite group $G$, that each element has order smaller than $|G|$.

A trap: a cyclic group of order $n$ has $\varphi(n)$ generators, so most elements are often not generators.
In $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, only $4$ of the $12$ elements generate the group.

### L3 human

A cyclic group is a quotient of $\mathbb{Z}$: if $G = \langle g \rangle$, the map $k \mapsto g^k$ is a surjective homomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to G$, so $G \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for some $n \geq 0$.
In module language, the cyclic groups are the cyclic $\mathbb{Z}$-modules, and the [[structure theorem|0RY0B72XAS]] writes each finitely generated abelian group as a direct sum of cyclic groups.
Each finite subgroup of the multiplicative group of a field is cyclic.
So $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is cyclic for each prime $p$, and its generators are the [[primitive roots modulo $p$|0F33KX9Q6F]].
More generally, $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is cyclic exactly when $n$ is $1$, $2$, $4$, $p^k$ or $2p^k$ with $p$ an odd prime and $k \geq 1$.
The [[automorphism group of a cyclic group|0G4C8SJ6PV]] of order $n$ is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$.
The inverse limit of the groups $\mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}$ is the group of $p$-adic integers, which explains the clash of notation for $\mathbb{Z}_p$.
