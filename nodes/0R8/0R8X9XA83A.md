## Statement

To give a linear map, it is enough to say where the basis vectors go, and these images can be chosen freely.

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over $F$, and let $(b_i)_{i \in I}$ be a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] of $V$.

1. Uniqueness: if $S, T \colon V \to W$ are [[linear maps|0RJF668P1T]] with $S(b_i) = T(b_i)$ for all $i \in I$, then $S = T$.
2. Existence: for each family $(w_i)_{i \in I}$ of vectors of $W$, there is a linear map $T \colon V \to W$ with $T(b_i) = w_i$ for all $i \in I$.

By part 1, the map of part 2 is unique.
It is given by $T\left(\sum_i a_i b_i\right) = \sum_i a_i w_i$, where only finitely many $a_i$ are nonzero.

## Remarks

### Why the hypotheses are needed

Part 1 uses only that the family spans $V$, and it fails without spanning.
With the single vector $b_1 = (1, 0)$ in $\R^2$, both $(x, y) \mapsto x$ and $(x, y) \mapsto x + y$ send $b_1$ to $1$.
Part 2 uses that the family is linearly independent, and it fails without independence.
In $\R$ the family $b_1 = 1$, $b_2 = 2$ spans, but no linear map has $T(1) = 1$ and $T(2) = 0$, since $T(2) = 2\,T(1)$.

### What it gives you

Each linear map $F^n \to F^m$ is a [[matrix transformation|W6KB0J3SZZ]], as one sees by taking the standard basis.
In finite dimension, choosing bases of $V$ and $W$ gives the [[matrix of a linear map|0YQSGNYE3K]].
The [[space of linear maps|0X9MBPP2Z3]] $\Hom(V, W)$ is in bijection with the set $W^I$ of families indexed by the basis.
If $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional, this gives $\dim \Hom(V, W) = \dim V \cdot \dim W$.
Two spaces with bases of the same size are isomorphic, by sending one basis to the other, and this is the core of the [[classification by dimension|0VJBSK15SN]].

### The converse

Suppose a family $(b_i)$ in $V$ has both properties for every $W$.
Uniqueness for $W = F$ forces the family to span $V$, and existence forces it to be independent, so the family is a basis.
This is the universal property of a basis.

### Generalizations

For a module over a ring, a basis has the same property, and a module with a basis is called free.
In the language of categories, a vector space with basis indexed by $I$ is the free vector space on the set $I$.

## History

The theorem is the abstract form of the older fact that a linear substitution of variables is fixed by its table of coefficients.
The abstract notion of a linear map on a vector space goes back to Peano in 1888.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear map is known as soon as the images of the basis vectors are known.
Every other vector is a combination of basis vectors, and linearity says where it goes.
The images of the basis vectors can be chosen freely.

### L2 human

Find the linear map $T \colon \R^2 \to \R^2$ with $T(1, 1) = (2, 0)$ and $T(1, -1) = (0, 4)$.

1. The vectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ form a basis of $\R^2$.
2. Each vector has the coordinates $(x, y) = \tfrac{x + y}{2}(1, 1) + \tfrac{x - y}{2}(1, -1)$.
3. So $T(x, y) = \tfrac{x + y}{2}(2, 0) + \tfrac{x - y}{2}(0, 4) = (x + y, \; 2x - 2y)$.
4. Check: $T(1, 1) = (2, 0)$ and $T(1, -1) = (0, 2 + 2) = (0, 4)$.

Watch out: values prescribed on a dependent family may contradict each other, so prescribe them on a basis.

### L3 human

A basis $(b_i)_{i \in I}$ gives a bijection $\Hom_F(V, W) \to W^I$, $T \mapsto (T(b_i))_i$.
This bijection is linear, so it is an isomorphism of vector spaces.
Every vector space has a basis, by the [[basis theorem|00MS9VMQ9Z]], so every vector space is free.
In Mathlib the map built in part 2 is `Basis.constr`.
