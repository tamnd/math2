## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over $F$, and let $(b_i)_{i \in I}$ be a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] of $V$.

1. Uniqueness. If $S, T \colon V \to W$ are [[linear maps|0RJF668P1T]] with $S(b_i) = T(b_i)$ for all $i \in I$, then $S = T$.
2. Existence. For each family $(w_i)_{i \in I}$ of vectors of $W$, there is a linear map $T \colon V \to W$ with $T(b_i) = w_i$ for all $i \in I$.

By part 1, the map of part 2 is unique.
It is given by $T\left(\sum_i a_i b_i\right) = \sum_i a_i w_i$, where only finitely many $a_i$ are nonzero.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Part 1 holds for any spanning family, not only for a basis.
- Part 2 needs the family to be linearly independent. In $\R$, the family $b_1 = 1$, $b_2 = 2$ spans, but no linear map has $T(1) = 1$ and $T(2) = 0$, since $T(2) = 2T(1)$.
- Part 2 needs the family to span $V$ for uniqueness. With only $b_1 = (1, 0)$ in $\R^2$, both $(x, y) \mapsto x$ and $(x, y) \mapsto x + y$ send $b_1$ to $1$.

### Consequences

- Each linear map $F^n \to F^m$ is a [[matrix transformation|W6KB0J3SZZ]]: take the standard basis.
- In finite dimension, choosing bases gives the [[matrix of a linear map|0YQSGNYE3K]].
- The [[space of linear maps|0X9MBPP2Z3]] $\Hom(V, W)$ is in bijection with the set $W^I$ of families indexed by the basis. For finite $I$, this gives $\dim \Hom(V, W) = \dim V \cdot \dim W$.
- Two spaces with bases of the same size are isomorphic: send one basis to the other. This is the core of the [[classification by dimension|0VJBSK15SN]].
- Each linear form on a subspace extends to $V$, by extending a basis of the subspace.

### Converse

If a family $(b_i)$ has both properties for every $W$, then it is a basis.
Uniqueness for $W = F$ forces the family to span, and existence forces it to be independent.
This is the universal property of a basis.

### Generalizations

- For a module over a ring, a basis has the same property; such a module is called free.
- In the language of categories, $V$ with basis indexed by $I$ is the free vector space on the set $I$.

## History

The theorem is the abstract form of the statement that a linear substitution is fixed by its coefficients.
Peano stated the abstract notion of a linear map in 1888; the free choice of images of a basis is used in the textbooks of the 20th century, such as Halmos's book of 1942.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear map is known as soon as the images of the basis vectors are known.
Every other vector is a combination of basis vectors, and linearity says where it goes.
The images of the basis vectors can be chosen freely.

### L2 human

Find the linear map $T \colon \R^2 \to \R^2$ with $T(1, 1) = (2, 0)$ and $T(1, -1) = (0, 4)$.

1. The vectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ form a basis of $\R^2$.
2. Write $(x, y) = \tfrac{x + y}{2}(1, 1) + \tfrac{x - y}{2}(1, -1)$.
3. So $T(x, y) = \tfrac{x + y}{2}(2, 0) + \tfrac{x - y}{2}(0, 4) = (x + y,\ 2x - 2y)$.
4. Check: $T(1, 1) = (2, 0)$ and $T(1, -1) = (0, 4)$.

The trap is to prescribe values on a dependent family, where the values may contradict each other.

### L3 human

The theorem says that a basis $(b_i)_{i \in I}$ gives a bijection $\Hom_F(V, W) \to W^I$, $T \mapsto (T(b_i))_i$.
This bijection is linear, so it is an isomorphism of vector spaces.
Every vector space has a basis, by the [[basis theorem|00MS9VMQ9Z]], so every vector space is free.
In Mathlib this is `Module.Basis.constr`.
