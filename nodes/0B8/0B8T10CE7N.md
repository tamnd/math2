## Summary

A linear isomorphism is a dictionary between two vector spaces that matches their vectors one to one and respects sums and scalar multiples.
Precisely, it is a [[bijective|0GG9TVW90M]] [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] $T \colon V \to W$, as in the [[formal definition|0XSJ25Z9M4]].
Its inverse $T^{-1} \colon W \to V$ is again linear.
Two spaces are isomorphic when an isomorphism between them exists, and then they have the same linear algebra.

## Motivation

Many vector spaces look different but behave the same.
The polynomials $a + bx + cx^2$, the triples $(a, b, c)$ and the symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrices all form spaces of dimension $3$.
An isomorphism translates one of these spaces into another, and each statement of linear algebra that is true in one space then transfers to the other.

This is how matrix methods reach spaces that are not made of columns.
A question about polynomials becomes a question about triples, and row reduction answers it.

## Notation

Two isomorphic spaces are written $V \cong W$.
The isomorphisms $V \to V$ are the automorphisms of $V$, and they form the group $GL(V)$ under composition.
Mathlib writes `V ≃ₗ[F] W` for the type of linear isomorphisms, called `LinearEquiv`.

## Remarks

### Examples

The [[coefficient map of quadratic polynomials|9DWJ0QE48F]] $P_2 \to F^3$, $a + bx + cx^2 \mapsto (a, b, c)$, is an isomorphism.
So is the [[transpose|QSVZNS1V01]] $M_{m \times n}(F) \to M_{n \times m}(F)$.
For an [[invertible matrix|0EB9N0S33C]] $A$, the map $x \mapsto Ax$ of $F^n$ is an isomorphism with inverse $x \mapsto A^{-1}x$.
For a basis $b_1, \dots, b_n$ of $V$, the map $F^n \to V$, $(a_i) \mapsto \sum_i a_i b_i$, is an isomorphism whose inverse is the [[coordinate map|059THXBRBB]].

### What it is not

The [[cube map x ↦ x³ on R|T4286X5GA4]] is bijective but not linear.
The projection $(x, y) \mapsto x$ from $\R^2$ to $\R$ is linear and surjective but not injective.
The [[right shift|N1G1WQW59J]] on sequences is linear and injective but not surjective.
Each of these misses one of the three requirements: linear, injective, surjective.

### Properties

The inverse of a linear isomorphism is linear, by the [[lemma on inverses|E08G7CVKPK]].
The composite of two isomorphisms is an isomorphism, with $(T \circ S)^{-1} = S^{-1} \circ T^{-1}$, so $\cong$ is an equivalence relation on vector spaces over $F$.
A linear map is an isomorphism exactly when it sends some basis to a basis, and then it sends every basis to a basis.
In terms of kernel and image, $T$ is an isomorphism exactly when $\ker T = \{0\}$ and $\im T = W$.
Finite-dimensional spaces are isomorphic exactly when they have the same dimension, by the [[classification by dimension|0VJBSK15SN]].

### History

Peano gave the axioms of a vector space and the notion of a linear map in 1888.
Some modern texts, such as Hefferon's, treat isomorphisms before general linear maps.
They first show that spaces of the same dimension behave alike, and only then study maps that lose information.

### Watch out

Linear and injective is not enough in general, and surjectivity must be checked as well unless $\dim V = \dim W < \infty$.
An isomorphism $V \cong F^n$ is not unique: it depends on the choice of a basis.
To prove that $T^{-1}$ is linear there is no need to find a formula for it, since the lemma on inverses gives linearity at once.

### Try it

First, is $T(a, b, c) = (a + b, \; b + c, \; a + c)$ an isomorphism of $\R^3$?
Yes: $T = 0$ gives $a = -b$ and $c = -b$, then $a + c = -2b = 0$, so $b = 0$ and the kernel is $\{0\}$, and equal dimensions give surjectivity.

Second, is differentiation $P_2 \to P_2$ an isomorphism?
No: it sends the constant $1$ to $0$, so its kernel is not $\{0\}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear isomorphism is a perfect translation between two vector spaces.
It matches the vectors one to one and respects addition and scaling.
Isomorphic spaces have the same linear algebra, even when their vectors look different.

### L2 human

To show that $T \colon V \to W$ is an isomorphism in finite dimension, check three things: $T$ is linear, $\ker T = \{0\}$, and $\dim V = \dim W$.
The rank-nullity theorem then gives surjectivity at no extra cost.
For example, $T(a, b) = (a + b, \; a - b)$ on $\R^2$ has kernel $\{0\}$, since $a + b = 0$ and $a - b = 0$ force $a = b = 0$.
So $T$ is an isomorphism, with inverse $T^{-1}(c, d) = \left(\tfrac{c + d}{2}, \; \tfrac{c - d}{2}\right)$.
Check: $T\left(\tfrac{c + d}{2}, \tfrac{c - d}{2}\right) = (c, d)$.
Watch out: the shortcut fails when $\dim V \ne \dim W$ or when the dimension is infinite.

### L3 human

Linear isomorphisms are the isomorphisms in the category of vector spaces over $F$.
By the classification by dimension, the finite-dimensional spaces form a category equivalent to one with objects $0, 1, 2, \dots$ and matrices as arrows.
In Mathlib, `LinearEquiv` stores the map, its inverse and the two inverse laws, so an isomorphism carries its inverse as data.
