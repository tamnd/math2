## Summary

A linear isomorphism is a [[bijective|0GG9TVW90M]] [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] $T \colon V \to W$.
Its inverse $T^{-1} \colon W \to V$ is again linear.
Two spaces are isomorphic when an isomorphism between them exists; then they have the same linear algebra.

## Motivation

Many vector spaces look different but behave the same.
The polynomials $a + bx + cx^2$, the triples $(a, b, c)$ and the symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrices all form spaces of dimension $3$.
An isomorphism is a dictionary between two such spaces that respects sums and scalar multiples.
Each statement of linear algebra true in one space then transfers to the other.

## Notation

Two isomorphic spaces are written $V \cong W$.
The set of isomorphisms $V \to W$ is sometimes written $\operatorname{Iso}(V, W)$.
The isomorphisms $V \to V$ are the automorphisms of $V$, and they form the group $GL(V)$.
Mathlib writes `V ≃ₗ[F] W` for the type of linear isomorphisms.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The coefficient map of quadratic polynomials|9DWJ0QE48F]] $P_2 \to F^3$, $a + bx + cx^2 \mapsto (a, b, c)$.
- [[The transpose|QSVZNS1V01]] $M_{m \times n}(F) \to M_{n \times m}(F)$.
- For an [[invertible matrix|0EB9N0S33C]] $A$, the map $x \mapsto Ax$ of $F^n$, with inverse $x \mapsto A^{-1}x$.
- For a basis $b_1, \dots, b_n$ of $V$, the map $F^n \to V$, $(a_i) \mapsto \sum_i a_i b_i$. Its inverse is the [[coordinate map|059THXBRBB]].

### Non-examples

- [[The cube map $x \mapsto x^3$ on $\R$|T4286X5GA4]] is bijective but not linear.
- The projection $(x, y) \mapsto x$ from $\R^2$ to $\R$ is linear and surjective but not injective.
- The [[right shift|N1G1WQW59J]] on sequences is linear and injective but not surjective.

### Basic properties

- The inverse of a linear isomorphism is linear, by the [[lemma on inverses|E08G7CVKPK]].
- The composite of two isomorphisms is an isomorphism, with $(T \circ S)^{-1} = S^{-1} \circ T^{-1}$.
- So $\cong$ is an equivalence relation on vector spaces over $F$.
- A linear map is an isomorphism if and only if it sends some basis, or each basis, to a basis.
- Finite-dimensional spaces are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension, by the [[classification by dimension|0VJBSK15SN]].
- A linear map $T \colon V \to W$ is an isomorphism if and only if $\ker T = \{0\}$ and $\im T = W$.

### History

Peano gave the axioms of a vector space and the notion of a linear map in 1888.
Some modern texts, such as Hefferon's, treat isomorphisms before general linear maps.
They first show that spaces of the same dimension behave alike, and only then study maps that lose information.

### Common errors

- Checking only that $T$ is linear and injective. Without $\dim V = \dim W < \infty$, surjectivity must be checked as well.
- Thinking that an isomorphism $V \cong F^n$ is unique or natural. It depends on the choice of a basis.
- Proving that $T^{-1}$ is linear by computing a formula for it. The general lemma makes this unnecessary.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear isomorphism is a perfect translation between two vector spaces.
It matches the vectors one to one and respects addition and scaling.
Isomorphic spaces have the same linear algebra, even when their vectors look different.

### L2 human

To show that $T \colon V \to W$ is an isomorphism in finite dimension, check three things: $T$ is linear, $\ker T = \{0\}$, and $\dim V = \dim W$.
Rank-nullity then gives surjectivity for free.
For example, $T(a, b) = (a + b,\ a - b)$ on $\R^2$ has kernel $\{0\}$, since $a + b = 0$ and $a - b = 0$ force $a = b = 0$.
So $T$ is an isomorphism, with inverse $T^{-1}(c, d) = \left(\tfrac{c + d}{2},\ \tfrac{c - d}{2}\right)$.
The trap is to use this shortcut when $\dim V \ne \dim W$ or when the dimension is infinite.

### L3 human

Linear isomorphisms are the isomorphisms in the category of vector spaces over $F$.
By the classification by dimension, the finite-dimensional spaces form a category equivalent to one with objects $0, 1, 2, \dots$ and matrices as arrows.
In Mathlib, `LinearEquiv` stores the map, its inverse and the two inverse laws, so an isomorphism carries its inverse as data.
