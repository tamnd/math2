## Statement

The rank of a sum is at most the sum of the ranks.
Let $F$ be a field and let $A$ and $B$ be $m \times n$ matrices over $F$.
Then
$$\rank(A + B) \le \rank A + \rank B,$$
where $\rank$ is the [[rank of a matrix|0ZC9YTXZRY]].

## Remarks

### A first check

Take
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A + B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Both $A$ and $B$ have rank $1$, and $\det(A + B) = 2 - 1 = 1 \ne 0$, so $\rank(A + B) = 2 = 1 + 1$.
Here the bound is reached.
The [[proof|F890ZBVDZV]] shows why the bound holds in general: each column of $A + B$ lies in the sum of the two column spaces.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The two matrices must have the same size, so that $A + B$ is defined.
No other hypothesis is needed, and the statement holds over every field.

### When the inequality is strict

For $B = -A$ with $A \ne 0$, the left side is $\rank 0 = 0$ and the right side is $2 \rank A > 0$.
A milder case: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ have rank $1$ each, and $A + B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ also has rank $1$.
In both cases the column spaces of $A$ and $B$ overlap.
Equality holds exactly when the column spaces of $A$ and $B$ meet only in $0$ and the row spaces of $A$ and $B$ also meet only in $0$.

### What it gives you

Applying the statement to $A = B + (A - B)$ gives $\rank A - \rank B \le \rank(A - B)$, and exchanging $A$ and $B$ gives
$$\lvert \rank A - \rank B \rvert \le \rank(A - B).$$
So changing one entry of a matrix changes its rank by at most $1$, since the change is a multiple of a matrix unit, which has rank at most $1$.
A matrix of rank $r$ is never a sum of fewer than $r$ matrices of rank $1$, and the [[rank factorization|5JGAG62VN8]] shows that $r$ of them always suffice.

### Generalizations

For linear maps $S, T \colon V \to W$ of finite rank, $\rank(S + T) \le \rank S + \rank T$, with the same proof: $\operatorname{im}(S + T) \subseteq \operatorname{im} S + \operatorname{im} T$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Adding two matrices cannot create more independent columns than the two matrices have together.
If $A$ has columns along one line and $B$ has columns along another line, then the columns of $A + B$ lie in the plane of those two lines.
So the rank of $A + B$ is at most $2$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: show that $I_n + uv^{\mathsf{T}}$ has rank at least $n - 1$ for any vectors $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
Write $I_n = (I_n + uv^{\mathsf{T}}) + (-uv^{\mathsf{T}})$.
Subadditivity gives
$$n = \rank I_n \le \rank(I_n + uv^{\mathsf{T}}) + \rank(uv^{\mathsf{T}}) \le \rank(I_n + uv^{\mathsf{T}}) + 1.$$
So $\rank(I_n + uv^{\mathsf{T}}) \ge n - 1$.
Both values occur: for $u = v = e_1$ the matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(2, 1, \dots, 1)$ of rank $n$, and for $u = -e_1$, $v = e_1$ it is $\operatorname{diag}(0, 1, \dots, 1)$ of rank $n - 1$.
Watch out: subadditivity is an upper bound for the sum, and you get a lower bound only by moving a term to the other side, as here.
