## Summary

Laminar–turbulent transition is the process by which a smooth, orderly flow becomes [[turbulent|VY307F8Z0S]].
It happens when the [[Reynolds number|JH9EJ0HA0R]] is raised, or along a flow whose local Reynolds number grows, for example downstream along a plate.
Transition may start from a linear instability that grows and breaks down.
It may also be triggered directly by finite disturbances in flows that are linearly stable.
Predicting and controlling it matters for drag, heat transfer and mixing in engineering.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Smoke rising from a candle first goes up in a smooth thread, then starts to wave, and higher up it breaks into swirling, irregular puffs.
That change from smooth to irregular is laminar–turbulent transition.
In 1883 Osborne Reynolds showed it with dye in water flowing through a glass pipe.
At low speeds the dye made a straight line; at high speeds it quickly spread through the whole pipe.
The change depends on a single number, the Reynolds number, which combines speed, pipe size and viscosity.
