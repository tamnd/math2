## Summary

A term is an expression of a first-order language that names an object.
Terms are built from variables and constant symbols by applying function symbols of the [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]].
In a [[structure|983GZBQYT4]], once the variables have values, each term has a value, which is an element of the structure.
Terms play the role of nouns in logic, while formulas play the role of statements.

## Motivation

Mathematical statements compare objects that are built by operations, such as $x \cdot (y + 1)$ and $x \cdot y + x$.
A formal language needs a precise class of such expressions before it can state equations and inequalities between them.
Terms are this class.
Their recursive definition lets one compute values, substitute, and prove facts about all terms by induction.

## Notation

1. The formal notation is prefix with brackets, $f(t_1, \dots, t_n)$. Binary symbols are usually written infix, as in $(t_1 + t_2)$, with the usual rules to drop brackets.
2. Enderton writes terms in Polish notation, $f t_1 \cdots t_n$, without brackets or commas.
3. $\operatorname{Var}(t)$ is the set of variables that occur in $t$. A term with $\operatorname{Var}(t) = \emptyset$ is closed, or ground.
4. The value of $t$ in a structure $\mathcal{M}$ under an assignment $s$ is written $t^{\mathcal{M}}[s]$ or $\bar{s}(t)$. It is defined by [[term evaluation|DH30WTA26T]].
5. In Mathlib terms are `FirstOrder.Language.Term L α`, with variables from the type `α`. Inside a bounded formula the variables are `α ⊕ Fin n`, and the numbered ones are bound.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The closed terms of zero and successor|VK6WWSDMXK]] are exactly the numerals $0, S(0), S(S(0)), \dots$.
2. [[The ring term $x \cdot (y + 1)$|BZP11VY05S]] has one formation tree, with root $\cdot$.
3. In the signature of groups, $(x \cdot y)^{-1}$ and $y^{-1} \cdot x^{-1}$ are two different terms. They have the same value in every group, but they are different strings.
4. A variable alone and a constant symbol alone are terms.

### Non-examples

1. [[The strings $+(x)$ and $S(x, y)$|J5BD7FZFFA]] are not terms, because the number of arguments differs from the arity.
2. In the signature of ordered sets, $x < y$ is not a term. It is an [[atomic formula|MCZRXR3SVB]], a statement and not a name.
3. The expression $\sum_{i=1}^{n} i$ is not a term of the language of arithmetic. The symbol $\sum$ binds the variable $i$, and terms have no binders.

### Basic properties

1. Unique readability: each term is a variable, a constant symbol, or $f(t_1, \dots, t_n)$ with unique $f$ and $t_i$. See [[unique readability|3QPV4XW8MC]].
2. Over a countable signature there are countably many terms, since each term is a finite [[string|1P6EP01HZF]] over a countable alphabet.
3. The terms over $\sigma$ with variables in $X$ form the [[term algebra|3YYKGWJJND]] $T_\sigma(X)$. It is the free $\sigma$-algebra on $X$: every map from $X$ to a $\sigma$-structure extends uniquely to a homomorphism.
4. The value of a term depends only on the values of its variables. This fact is part of the coincidence lemma, in the topic [[structures, satisfaction and truth|NJDME6F845]].
5. Closed terms exist exactly when $\sigma$ has a constant symbol.

### History

Frege's Begriffsschrift of 1879 already separated names of objects from statements.
Later textbooks of logic gave the recursive definition of terms used today, with function symbols of fixed arity.
The algebraic view of terms as the free algebra comes from Birkhoff's work on universal algebra in 1935.

### Common errors

1. A student treats a term as a formula, for example writes $\neg (x + 1)$. A connective applies to formulas, not to terms.
2. A student identifies a term with its value. The terms $2 + 3$ and $5$ have the same value in $\N$, but they are different terms.
3. A student forgets that terms depend on the signature. In the signature of groups, $x + y$ is not a term.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In the language of arithmetic, $x + 1$ and $(x \cdot y) + 0$ are terms.
They stand for numbers once one says what $x$ and $y$ are.
For example, with $x = 3$ the term $x + 1$ has value $4$.
An expression such as $x < y$ is not a term: it does not name a number but makes a claim.

### L2 human

We compute the value of the term $S(x) \cdot S(S(0))$ in $\N$, where $S$ is "add one", at $x = 4$.

1. The term $0$ has value $0$, so $S(0)$ has value $1$ and $S(S(0))$ has value $2$.
2. The term $x$ has value $4$, so $S(x)$ has value $5$.
3. The product has value $5 \cdot 2 = 10$.
4. At $x = 0$ the same term has value $1 \cdot 2 = 2$. The value changes with $x$, because $x$ occurs in the term.

The typical use of terms is in atomic formulas, such as the equation $S(x) \cdot S(S(0)) = x + x + 2$.
A trap: the evaluation always goes from the variables and constants up to the root, by the formation tree of the term.

### L3 human

The terms of a signature form the free algebra, and evaluation in a structure is the unique homomorphism from it that extends an assignment.
[[Substitution|NCDZ969V7X]] of terms for variables is the same homomorphism with values in the term algebra itself.
Equational logic studies identities $t_1 = t_2$ between terms, and Birkhoff's theorem says that the classes defined by identities are exactly the classes closed under homomorphic images, substructures and products.
[[Unification|RJJ9D0WF53]] solves equations between terms by a substitution, and it is the core of resolution and of logic programming.
In proof assistants, first-order terms are an inductive type, and in Mathlib `FirstOrder.Language.Term` has the two constructors `var` and `func`.
In higher-order logic and in the lambda calculus, terms may also bind variables, which first-order terms never do.
