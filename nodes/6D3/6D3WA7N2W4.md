## Summary

A term is an expression that names an element, the way a noun names a thing.
Here is one, built in the ring signature in three steps:
$$x, \ 1 \quad \leadsto \quad (x + 1) \quad \leadsto \quad ((x + 1) \cdot y).$$
First $x$ and $1$ are terms, then $+$ joins them, and then $\cdot$ joins the result with $y$.
The steps form a tree with $\cdot$ at the root, and once $x$ and $y$ have values the tree gives the term a value: with $x = 2$ and $y = 5$ it is $(2 + 1) \cdot 5 = 15$.

Exactly, let $\sigma$ be a [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]] and $V$ a set of variables.
The [[terms|BQQ0FPFZ67]] over $\sigma$ are the smallest set of [[strings|1P6EP01HZF]] that contains every variable and every constant symbol, and that contains $f(t_1, \dots, t_n)$ whenever $f$ is a function symbol of arity $n \ge 1$ and $t_1, \dots, t_n$ are terms.
A term with no variables is closed.
In a [[structure|983GZBQYT4]], once each variable has a value, each term has a value, which is an element of the structure.

## Motivation

Mathematical statements compare objects built by operations, such as $x \cdot (y + 1)$ and $x \cdot y + x$.
Before a formal language can state an equation between them, it needs an exact class of such expressions, and terms are that class.
The recursive definition is what makes terms useful.
It lets us compute the value of a term from its parts, replace a variable by another term, and prove a fact about all terms at once by [[structural induction|12MQ2NJ29A]].
Terms then fill the slots of [[atomic formulas|MCZRXR3SVB]], and every formula of the language is built on them.

## Notation

The official form is prefix with brackets, $f(t_1, \dots, t_n)$, and binary symbols are usually written infix, as $(t_1 + t_2)$, with the usual rules for dropping brackets.
Enderton writes terms in Polish notation, $f t_1 \cdots t_n$, with no brackets or commas.
$\operatorname{Var}(t)$ is the set of variables that occur in $t$, and $t$ is closed, or ground, when $\operatorname{Var}(t) = \emptyset$.
The value of $t$ in a structure $\mathcal{M}$ under an assignment $s$ is written $t^{\mathcal{M}}[s]$ or $\bar{s}(t)$, and [[term evaluation|DH30WTA26T]] defines it.
In Mathlib, terms are `FirstOrder.Language.Term L α`, with variables from the type `α`; inside a bounded formula the variables are `α ⊕ Fin n`, and the numbered ones are bound.

## Remarks

### Trees and values

The tree of a term is the record of how it was built.
For [[the ring term $x \cdot (y + 1)$|BZP11VY05S]] the root is $\cdot$, its left child is the leaf $x$, and its right child is a node $+$ with leaves $y$ and $1$.
Values are computed from the leaves up.
With $x = 3$ and $y = 4$ the node $+$ gets $4 + 1 = 5$, and then the root gets $3 \cdot 5 = 15$.
The term $x \cdot y + 1$ has the same symbols in a different tree, with root $+$, and its value at the same point is $13$.
The [[unique readability theorem|3QPV4XW8MC]] says that each term has exactly one tree, so its value is well defined.

### Closed terms

A closed term names a fixed element, with no choice of values needed.
In the signature $\{ 0, S \}$, [[the closed terms are exactly the numerals|VK6WWSDMXK]] $0, S(0), S(S(0)), \dots$, one for each natural number.
In the ring signature, $(1 + 1) \cdot (1 + 1)$ is closed and has the value $4$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ and the value $0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
A signature with no constant symbols has no closed terms at all, since every leaf is then a variable.

### What is not a term

[[The strings $+(x)$ and $S(x, y)$|J5BD7FZFFA]] are not terms, because the number of arguments differs from the arity.
In the signature of orders, $x < y$ is not a term: it is an [[atomic formula|MCZRXR3SVB]], a statement and not a name.
The sum $\sum_{i=1}^{n} i$ is not a term of arithmetic either, because $\sum$ binds the variable $i$, and first-order terms have no binders.

### The first facts

Each term is a variable, a constant symbol, or $f(t_1, \dots, t_n)$ for exactly one choice of $f$ and $t_1, \dots, t_n$; this is unique readability.
Over a countable signature there are countably many terms, since each is a finite string over a countable alphabet.
The terms over $\sigma$ with variables in $X$ form the [[term algebra|3YYKGWJJND]] $T_\sigma(X)$, the free $\sigma$-algebra on $X$: every map from $X$ into a $\sigma$-structure extends to exactly one homomorphism on $T_\sigma(X)$.
The value of a term depends only on the values of the variables in it, which is the term part of the coincidence lemma in [[structures, satisfaction and truth|NJDME6F845]].

### Watch out

A term is not its value.
The terms $2 + 3$ and $5$ have the same value in $\mathbb{N}$, but they are different strings, and in $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ the closed terms $1 + 1 + 1 + 1$ and $0$ even have the same value while one is much longer.
A term is not a formula either: $\neg (x + 1)$ is meaningless, since connectives join formulas, not terms.
And terms depend on the signature, so $x + y$ is not a term in the signature of groups $\{ \cdot, e, {}^{-1} \}$.

### History

Frege's Begriffsschrift of 1879 already separated expressions that name objects from expressions that make claims.
Birkhoff's work on abstract algebras in 1935 treated the terms of a signature as a free algebra, which is the view of the term algebra today.

### Try it

Is $S(+(x, 0))$ a term of $\{ 0, S, + \}$ with arities $0, 1, 2$, and what are its variables?
Yes: $+(x, 0)$ is a term since $+$ gets two terms, and then $S$ gets one term; its only variable is $x$.

Compute the value of $(x + 1) \cdot (x + 1)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ at $x = -3$.
The node $x + 1$ gets $-2$, and the root gets $(-2) \cdot (-2) = 4$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In the language of arithmetic, $x + 1$ and $(x \cdot y) + 0$ are terms.
They stand for numbers once you say what $x$ and $y$ are.
For example, with $x = 3$ the term $x + 1$ has the value $4$.
An expression such as $x < y$ is not a term: it does not name a number but makes a claim.

### L2 human

We compute the value of $S(x) \cdot S(S(0))$ in $\mathbb{N}$ at $x = 4$, where $S$ means "add one".
Work from the leaves up.
The leaf $0$ has the value $0$, so $S(0)$ has the value $1$ and $S(S(0))$ has the value $2$.
The leaf $x$ has the value $4$, so $S(x)$ has the value $5$.
The root multiplies:
$$5 \cdot 2 = 10.$$
At $x = 0$ the same term has the value $1 \cdot 2 = 2$, so the value changes with $x$, because $x$ occurs in the term.
The typical use of a term is inside an atomic formula, such as the equation $S(x) \cdot S(S(0)) = x + x + S(S(0))$.
Check at $x = 4$: the right side is $4 + 4 + 2 = 10$, the same as the left side.

### L3 human

The terms of a signature form the free algebra, and evaluation in a structure is the unique homomorphism from it that extends an assignment.
[[Substitution|NCDZ969V7X]] of terms for variables is the same kind of homomorphism, with values in the term algebra itself.
Equational logic studies identities $t_1 = t_2$, and Birkhoff's theorem says that the classes defined by identities are exactly the classes closed under homomorphic images, substructures and products.
[[Unification|RJJ9D0WF53]] solves equations between terms by a substitution, and it is the core of resolution and of logic programming.
In Mathlib, `FirstOrder.Language.Term` is an inductive type with the two constructors `var` and `func`.
In higher-order logic and the lambda calculus terms may bind variables, which first-order terms never do.
