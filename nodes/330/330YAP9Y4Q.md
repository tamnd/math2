## Definition

Primitive recursive arithmetic is the quantifier-free formal system with one function symbol for each way of building a [[primitive recursive function|XZV7BD3D66]].
We write it $\mathsf{PRA}$ and define it in seven parts.

1. Function symbols.
   The constant $0$ has arity $0$, the successor $S$ has arity $1$, and for $1 \le i \le n$ the projection $\pi^n_i$ has arity $n$.
   If $g$ has arity $m$ and $h_1, \ldots, h_m$ have arity $n$, then $\mathrm{Comp}(g, h_1, \ldots, h_m)$ is a function symbol of arity $n$.
   If $g$ has arity $n$ and $h$ has arity $n + 2$, then $\mathrm{Rec}(g, h)$ is a function symbol of arity $n + 1$.
2. Terms are built from the variables $x_0, x_1, \ldots$ by the function symbols.
3. An atomic formula is an equation $s = t$ between terms.
   The formulas are built from atomic formulas by $\neg$, $\land$, $\lor$ and $\to$ only, with no quantifiers.
4. The logical axioms are the substitution instances of propositional tautologies and the equality axioms.
   These are $x = x$, $x = y \to (x = z \to y = z)$, and $x = y \to f(\ldots, x, \ldots) = f(\ldots, y, \ldots)$ for each function symbol $f$ and each argument place.
5. The successor axioms are $\neg (Sx = 0)$ and $Sx = Sy \to x = y$.
6. The defining equations, with $\vec y = y_1, \ldots, y_n$, are $\pi^n_i(\vec y) = y_i$ and $\mathrm{Comp}(g, h_1, \ldots, h_m)(\vec y) = g(h_1(\vec y), \ldots, h_m(\vec y))$.
   For recursion they are $\mathrm{Rec}(g, h)(0, \vec y) = g(\vec y)$ and $\mathrm{Rec}(g, h)(Sx, \vec y) = h(x, \mathrm{Rec}(g, h)(x, \vec y), \vec y)$.
7. The rules are modus ponens, substitution (from $\varphi(x)$ infer $\varphi(t)$ for every variable $x$ and term $t$), and the induction rule.
   The induction rule says: from $\varphi(0)$ and $\varphi(x) \to \varphi(Sx)$ infer $\varphi(x)$, for every formula $\varphi$.

A theorem of $\mathsf{PRA}$ is the last formula of a finite sequence in which each formula is an axiom or follows from earlier formulas by a rule.

## Notation

We give the symbols their usual names.
Let $R = \mathrm{Rec}(\pi^1_1, \mathrm{Comp}(S, \pi^3_2))$, so that $R(0, y) = y$ and $R(Sx, y) = S(R(x, y))$.
Then $x + y$ is the symbol $\mathrm{Comp}(R, \pi^2_2, \pi^2_1)$, whose value is $R(y, x)$, so $x + 0 = x$ and $x + Sy = S(x + y)$ are theorems.
In the same way $x \cdot y$ is defined from $+$ by $x \cdot 0 = 0$ and $x \cdot Sy = x \cdot y + x$, and $x!$ by $0! = S0$ and $(Sx)! = x! \cdot Sx$.
A numeral $\overline{n}$ is the term $S \cdots S 0$ with $n$ copies of $S$.

## Remarks

The function symbols mirror the five clauses that generate the primitive recursive functions, so every term denotes a primitive recursive function and every such function has a symbol.
A symbol names a definition, not a function.
So $\mathrm{Comp}(\pi^1_1, S)$ and $S$ are different symbols, but $\mathsf{PRA}$ proves $\mathrm{Comp}(\pi^1_1, S)(x) = Sx$ from the defining equations.

The induction rule needs no restriction to quantifier-free formulas, because every formula of $\mathsf{PRA}$ is quantifier-free.
What it does need is the form of a rule.
There is no formula for "$\varphi(0)$ and the step imply $\varphi(x)$" with the step quantified, so the rule fires only after both premises are proved.
If we instead take classical first-order logic with the same symbols and the induction schema for quantifier-free formulas, the quantifier-free theorems stay the same.

The successor axioms cannot be dropped.
Without $\neg (Sx = 0)$, the one-element structure in which every term has the value $0$ satisfies all the other axioms and rules, and in it $S0 = 0$ holds.

A small case: the factorial symbol gives $(S0)! = 0! \cdot S0 = S0 \cdot S0$, and the equations of $\cdot$ and $+$ reduce this to $S0$, so $1! = 1$.
The [[concept page|WCXH72T8D8]] explains what $\mathsf{PRA}$ proves and why it is the usual formal model of finitism.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The definition lists the symbols, the starting facts and the allowed steps of a proof.
Each function symbol comes with equations that compute it, so you can evaluate every term without variables by hand.
For example, $S0 + S0$ becomes $S(S0 + 0)$ and then $SS0$, which is the number $2$.
The only way to prove a general law is the induction rule: prove it for $0$, and prove that it passes from $x$ to $x + 1$.

### L2 human

We evaluate $\overline{2} \cdot \overline{2}$ with the defining equations, writing $2$ as $SS0$.
The second equation of $\cdot$, used twice, and the first equation once give
$$SS0 \cdot SS0 = SS0 \cdot S0 + SS0 = (SS0 \cdot 0 + SS0) + SS0 = (0 + SS0) + SS0.$$
The equations of $+$ give $0 + SS0 = S(0 + S0) = SS(0 + 0) = SS0$.
They also give
$$SS0 + SS0 = S(SS0 + S0) = SS(SS0 + 0) = SSSS0.$$
So $\mathsf{PRA}$ proves $\overline{2} \cdot \overline{2} = \overline{4}$, using the equality axioms to put the pieces together.

Watch out: the law $0 + x = x$ holds for each numeral by computation, but for the variable $x$ it needs the induction rule.
Using it before it is proved is the usual slip.
