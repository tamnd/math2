## Example

A [[conic|00HJCMC2CD]] is the set of points of the plane with
$$ax^2 + bxy + cy^2 + dx + ey + f = 0,$$
where $a$, $b$ and $c$ are not all $0$.
Its quadratic part $Q(x, y) = ax^2 + bxy + cy^2$ has the symmetric matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b/2 \\ b/2 & c \end{pmatrix}$, with half of $b$ in each off-diagonal place.
The method has three stages.
First, when $\det A \ne 0$, move the origin to the center, which removes the linear terms.
Second, rotate the axes by [[the principal axis theorem|0PR5P9Z4KM]], which removes the $xy$ term.
Third, read the type and the size from the eigenvalues and the constant.
We carry this out for a hyperbola, a parabola and an ellipse.

**A hyperbola.** Let $x^2 + 4xy + y^2 - 6x - 6y + 3 = 0$.

1. The matrix is $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvalue $3$ on $(1, 1)$ and $-1$ on $(-1, 1)$.
2. The center is the point where the gradient of the left side vanishes, so it solves $2A(x, y)^{\mathsf{T}} = (6, 6)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
   This gives the center $(1, 1)$, since $2A(1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} = (6, 6)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
3. At the center the left side is $1 + 4 + 1 - 6 - 6 + 3 = -3$.
4. With $x = 1 + X$ and $y = 1 + Y$ the linear terms cancel, and the equation becomes $X^2 + 4XY + Y^2 - 3 = 0$.
5. The rotation $X = (u - v)/\sqrt{2}$ and $Y = (u + v)/\sqrt{2}$ gives $3u^2 - v^2 = 3$.
6. So the curve is the [[hyperbola|0WN5G117YC]] $u^2 - v^2/3 = 1$, with vertices at $(u, v) = (\pm 1, 0)$ and asymptotes $v = \pm \sqrt{3}\, u$.
7. In the original coordinates the vertices are $(x, y) = (1 \pm 1/\sqrt{2}, \, 1 \pm 1/\sqrt{2})$, with the same sign in both places.
   Check with the plus sign: $X = Y = 1/\sqrt{2}$, and $X^2 + 4XY + Y^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} + 2 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 3$.

**A parabola.** Let $x^2 + 2xy + y^2 + 2x - 6y + 5 = 0$.

1. The matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvalue $2$ on $(1, 1)$ and $0$ on $(-1, 1)$.
2. Its determinant is $0$, so there is no unique center, and we rotate first.
3. The rotation $x = (u - v)/\sqrt{2}$ and $y = (u + v)/\sqrt{2}$ gives $x + y = \sqrt{2}\, u$, so $x^2 + 2xy + y^2 = (x + y)^2 = 2u^2$.
4. The linear part becomes $2x - 6y = (2u - 2v - 6u - 6v)/\sqrt{2} = -2\sqrt{2}\, u - 4\sqrt{2}\, v$.
5. So the equation is $2u^2 - 2\sqrt{2}\, u - 4\sqrt{2}\, v + 5 = 0$.
6. Completing the square gives $2u^2 - 2\sqrt{2}\, u = 2(u - 1/\sqrt{2})^2 - 1$, so the equation becomes $2(u - 1/\sqrt{2})^2 = 4\sqrt{2}\, (v - 1/\sqrt{2})$.
7. This is a [[parabola|04S2GMHX82]] with vertex at $u = v = 1/\sqrt{2}$, which is the point $(x, y) = (0, 1)$.
   Check: at $(0, 1)$ the left side is $0 + 0 + 1 + 0 - 6 + 5 = 0$.
8. The axis of the parabola passes through $(0, 1)$ in the direction $(-1, 1)$, the eigenvector of the eigenvalue $0$.

**An ellipse.** Let $5x^2 + 4xy + 2y^2 - 14x - 8y + 5 = 0$.

1. The matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvalue $6$ on $(2, 1)$ and $1$ on $(-1, 2)$.
2. The center solves $2A(x, y)^{\mathsf{T}} = (14, 8)^{\mathsf{T}}$, and $2A(1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} = (14, 8)^{\mathsf{T}}$, so the center is $(1, 1)$.
3. At the center the left side is $5 + 4 + 2 - 14 - 8 + 5 = -6$.
4. With $x = 1 + X$ and $y = 1 + Y$ the equation becomes $5X^2 + 4XY + 2Y^2 = 6$.
5. The rotation of [[the ellipse example|GP4XSY4FNH]] gives $6u^2 + v^2 = 6$.
6. So the curve is the [[ellipse|0VKCCEXBJ1]] $u^2 + v^2/6 = 1$, with semi-axis $1$ along $(2, 1)$ and semi-axis $\sqrt{6}$ along $(-1, 2)$.

## Remarks

### The type from the eigenvalues

For a conic that is not degenerate, the signs of the eigenvalues of $A$ give the type.
Two eigenvalues of one sign give an ellipse, two of opposite signs give a hyperbola, and one zero eigenvalue gives a parabola.
The product of the eigenvalues is $\det A = ac - b^2/4 = -\tfrac{1}{4}(b^2 - 4ac)$.
So the rule is [[the classification by the discriminant|0N5NR32NQG]]: an ellipse when $b^2 - 4ac < 0$, a parabola when $b^2 - 4ac = 0$, and a hyperbola when $b^2 - 4ac > 0$.
For the three conics above, $b^2 - 4ac$ is $16 - 4 = 12$, then $4 - 4 = 0$, then $16 - 40 = -24$.

### The degenerate cases

The same steps also find the degenerate conics, and each one has a model after the change of coordinates:

- $x^2 - y^2 = 0$ is a pair of lines that meet, and $x^2 = 1$ is a pair of parallel lines;
- $x^2 + y^2 = 0$ is a single point, and $x^2 = 0$ is a single line, counted twice;
- $x^2 + y^2 = -1$ and $x^2 = -1$ have no real points at all.

They appear when the constant after centering is $0$, or has the wrong sign, or when the linear term along the zero eigenvector vanishes in the parabolic case.
For example, $x^2 + 2xy + y^2 - 1 = 0$ is $(x + y)^2 = 1$, two parallel lines, although its matrix is the one of the parabola above.
The geometry of the curves themselves, with foci and eccentricity, is in [[the topic on conic sections|NNJH85SH0H]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A tilted conic has an $xy$ term in its equation, and a conic away from the origin has $x$ and $y$ terms.
A shift of the origin removes the $x$ and $y$ terms, and a turn of the axes removes the $xy$ term.
What is left is a short equation such as $u^2 - v^2/3 = 1$, which shows at once the type and the size of the curve.

### L2 human

The usual order is: find the center first if $\det A \ne 0$, then rotate.
If $\det A = 0$, rotate first and then complete the square, as for the parabola.
Watch out for the constant: the value of the left side at the center moves to the right side with its sign changed, so $-3$ becomes the right side $3$ for the hyperbola.
Watch out for the axes of the ellipse as well: in $6u^2 + v^2 = 6$ the semi-axis is $\sqrt{6/6} = 1$ along the eigenvector of $6$ and $\sqrt{6/1}$ along the eigenvector of $1$.
