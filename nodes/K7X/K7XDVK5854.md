## Summary

Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a [[structure|983GZBQYT4]] for a signature $L$, and let $\sigma$ be an $L$-[[sentence|DKFGQTRYZE]], that is, a formula without free variables.
The sentence $\sigma$ is true in $\mathcal{M}$, written $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$, when every variable assignment in $\mathcal{M}$ [[satisfies|2RBQPV02AS]] $\sigma$; since $\sigma$ has no free variables, this holds exactly when some assignment satisfies $\sigma$.
Otherwise $\sigma$ is false in $\mathcal{M}$, and then $\mathcal{M} \models \neg \sigma$.
The theory of $\mathcal{M}$ is the set $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M}) = \set{\sigma : \sigma \text{ is an } L\text{-sentence and } \mathcal{M} \models \sigma}$.
Truth in a structure is the meeting point of syntax and mathematics: "the group $G$ is abelian" means exactly that the sentence $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x \cdot y = y \cdot x)$ is true in $G$.

## Motivation

Satisfaction $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi[s]$ depends on an assignment $s$.
For the statements of mathematics, such as "every element has an inverse", this dependence is a nuisance, because the statements have no free variables.
For a sentence, the [[coincidence lemma|~7b107606]] shows that the assignment plays no role.
So a sentence is simply true or simply false in each structure, and we can collect all true sentences into one object, the theory of the structure.
This lets us compare structures by the sentences that they make true, and ask which properties of a structure first-order logic can express.

## Notation

We write $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$ for "$\sigma$ is true in $\mathcal{M}$" and $\mathcal{M} \not\models \sigma$ for "$\sigma$ is false in $\mathcal{M}$".
Other readings are "$\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $\sigma$" and "$\sigma$ holds in $\mathcal{M}$".
For a formula $\varphi$ with free variables, many books (Enderton, Mendelson) write $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi$ when every assignment satisfies $\varphi$; this is the same as the truth of the universal closure of $\varphi$.
Other books use $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi$ only for sentences; this page follows the first convention and says so when it uses it.
The theory of $\mathcal{M}$ is written $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$.
In Mathlib, truth of a sentence is `FirstOrder.Language.Sentence.Realize`, with the notation `M ⊨ φ`, and the theory of a structure is `FirstOrder.Language.completeTheory`.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The [[sentence $\forall x \, \exists y \, (x < y)$|~3f437639]] is true in $(\N, <)$, because $n + 1$ is larger than $n$, and false in $(\set{0, 1, 2}, <)$, because no element is larger than $2$.
2. The [[sentence "there are at least three elements"|~b15b5238]], $\exists x \, \exists y \, \exists z \, (\neg x = y \wedge \neg x = z \wedge \neg y = z)$, is true exactly in the structures with at least three elements.
3. The sentence $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x \cdot y = y \cdot x)$ is true in the group $(\Z, +, 0, -)$, read with $\cdot$ as $+$, and false in the symmetric group $S_3$.
4. The sentence $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x < y \to \exists z \, (x < z \wedge z < y))$ is true in $(\Q, <)$, with the witness $(x + y)/2$, and false in $(\Z, <)$, with the pair $0, 1$.

### Non-examples

1. The [[formula $x < 1$ in $(\N, <)$|~2c4f7e5f]] is neither true nor false: the condition that fails is that $x < 1$ is not a sentence, and assignments disagree on it.
2. The sentence $\exists x \, \forall y \, (x < y \vee x = y)$ is not true in $(\Z, <)$: the condition that fails is the existence of a least element; see the [[integers have no least element|~32355403]].

### Basic properties

1. Bivalence. For each sentence $\sigma$, exactly one of $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$ and $\mathcal{M} \models \neg \sigma$ holds. Indeed $\sigma$ is satisfied either by all assignments or by none, by the coincidence lemma, and the domain is nonempty, so assignments exist.
2. So $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$ is complete: for each sentence $\sigma$, it contains $\sigma$ or $\neg \sigma$, and not both.
3. $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$ is closed under [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]]: if every sentence of $\Gamma \subseteq \operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$ is true in $\mathcal{M}$ and $\Gamma \models \sigma$, then $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$.
4. Truth is preserved by isomorphisms: if $\mathcal{M} \cong \mathcal{N}$, then $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M}) = \operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{N})$. This is [[isomorphism invariance of truth|~5166a0b3]].
5. Structures with the same theory are called [[elementarily equivalent|P3B1V182N4]], written $\mathcal{M} \equiv \mathcal{N}$. By property 4, isomorphic structures are elementarily equivalent; the converse fails, for example for $(\Q, <)$ and $(\R, <)$, a fact proved in model theory.
6. A structure $\mathcal{M}$ is a [[model|MVCH6N9SNW]] of a set $T$ of sentences exactly when $T \subseteq \operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$.

### History

Alfred Tarski defined truth for formalized languages in his 1933 monograph, through the satisfaction of formulas by sequences of objects.
His adequacy condition, convention T, asks that a definition of truth imply each instance of "'snow is white' is true if and only if snow is white"; see the [[semantic conception of truth|8FBF0EFFC9]].
Truth relative to an arbitrary structure became the standard notion with the model theory of the 1950s.

### Common errors

- Calling a formula with free variables true or false without saying which convention is used. Under the convention of this page, $x < 1$ is not true in $(\N, <)$, and $\neg (x < 1)$ is not true either.
- Thinking that $\mathcal{M} \not\models \varphi$ implies $\mathcal{M} \models \neg \varphi$ for formulas. This holds for sentences only; the formula $x < 1$ shows the failure.
- Confusing truth in one structure with [[validity|5SDS9T8E7J]]. The sentence $\forall x \, \exists y \, (x < y)$ is true in $(\N, <)$ but not valid, because it fails in $(\set{0}, <)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A sentence is a complete statement in a formal language, with no unknown letters left over.
Once we fix a structure, such as the whole numbers with "less than", every sentence is either true or false there.
"For every number there is a bigger number" is true for the whole numbers $0, 1, 2, \ldots$.
The same sentence is false for the three numbers $0, 1, 2$ alone, because nothing is bigger than $2$.
The list of all sentences that are true in a structure is called its theory.

### L2 human

We decide the truth of $\forall x \, \exists y \, (y < x)$ in two structures.

1. In $(\Z, <)$: for each value $a$ of $x$, the variant $y \mapsto a - 1$ gives $a - 1 < a$. So every variant of $x$ satisfies $\exists y \, (y < x)$, and the sentence is true.
2. In $(\N, <)$: the variant $x \mapsto 0$ needs some $b \in \N$ with $b < 0$. There is none, so the sentence is false.
3. By bivalence, $(\N, <) \models \neg \forall x \, \exists y \, (y < x)$.

So this sentence belongs to $\operatorname{Th}(\Z, <)$ and its negation belongs to $\operatorname{Th}(\N, <)$.
The typical use is to show that two structures are not isomorphic, by a sentence that is true in one and false in the other.
A trap is to use a formula with a free variable for this purpose: a formula can be satisfied in both structures by some assignments and fail in both by others.

### L3 human

The map $\mathcal{M} \mapsto \operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$ sends structures to complete theories, and its fibres are the classes of elementarily equivalent structures.
The [[completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]] describes its image: every complete consistent theory that is closed under consequence is $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$ for some $\mathcal{M}$.
A theory $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$ can be decidable, as for $(\R, +, \cdot, 0, 1, <)$ by Tarski's quantifier elimination, or undecidable, as for $(\N, +, \cdot)$.
Tarski's undefinability theorem says that $\operatorname{Th}(\N, +, \cdot)$, coded by numbers, is not definable in $(\N, +, \cdot)$ itself.
In abstract model theory, truth is the satisfaction relation of a logic, and other logics, such as second-order or infinitary logic, give finer relations than $\equiv$.
