## Example

Let $G = \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ be the [[direct product|00HF4RJ5QN]] of two copies of the [[additive group of integers|0BSCJA5G53]].
Its elements are the pairs $(m, n)$ of integers, with $(m, n) + (m', n') = (m + m', n + n')$ and identity $(0, 0)$.
We check three properties.

1. [[Finitely generated|082DWWXMRJ]]: each element is $(m, n) = m(1, 0) + n(0, 1)$. So the finite set $\{(1, 0), (0, 1)\}$ is a [[generating set|0MBV2VHMXA]] of $G$.
2. Not [[cyclic|0KVGH0J5B3]]: suppose $G = \langle (a, b) \rangle$. Then $(1, 0) = k(a, b)$ and $(0, 1) = l(a, b)$ for integers $k$ and $l$.
3. From $(1, 0) = (ka, kb)$ we get $ka = 1$, so $k \neq 0$, and $kb = 0$, so $b = 0$.
4. From $(0, 1) = (la, lb)$ we get $lb = 1$. With $b = 0$ this says $0 = 1$, which is false. So no single element generates $G$.
5. Not [[locally cyclic|RXWA8TF2RM]]: a locally cyclic group is a group whose finitely generated subgroups are all cyclic. By step 1, $G$ is a finitely generated subgroup of itself, and by step 4 it is not cyclic.

So $G$ is finitely generated, but it is not cyclic and not locally cyclic.

## Remarks

The example shows that a group with two generators need not have one generator, even when it is abelian and infinite.
The smallest number of generators is $d(\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}) = 2$.

Other pairs also generate $G$.
A pair $\{(a, b), (c, d)\}$ generates $G$ exactly when $ad - bc = \pm 1$.
The reason is that the pair generates $G$ exactly when the matrix with rows $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$ has an inverse with integer entries.
For example, $\{(2, 1), (1, 1)\}$ generates $G$: $(2, 1) - (1, 1) = (1, 0)$ and $2(1, 1) - (2, 1) = (0, 1)$.
The pair $\{(2, 0), (0, 1)\}$ does not generate $G$: it generates only the pairs $(m, n)$ with $m$ even, and here $ad - bc = 2$.

The group $\mathbb{Z}^n$ is generated by its $n$ standard basis vectors, and it is not generated by fewer than $n$ elements.
The reduction modulo $2$ maps $\mathbb{Z}^n$ onto $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^n$, which has $2^n$ elements, and $k$ elements of $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^n$ generate at most $2^k$ elements.
Each subgroup of $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ is again finitely generated, by the theory of [[finitely generated abelian groups|RQNAAJF6XY]].
The finite groups $\mathbb{Z}/m \times \mathbb{Z}/n$ behave in a different way: such a group is cyclic exactly when $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of the points with whole-number coordinates in the plane, such as $(3, -2)$.
We add points coordinate by coordinate.
Every point comes from the two steps "one to the right" and "one up": $(3, -2)$ is three steps right and two steps down.
But one step alone is not enough.
Any single step moves along one line through the origin, and its multiples stay on that line.
So the points need two building blocks, and not one.

### L2 human

We check that $\{(3, 2), (1, 1)\}$ generates $G$.

1. $(3, 2) - 2(1, 1) = (1, 0)$.
2. $(1, 1) - (1, 0) = (0, 1)$.
3. So $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ lie in $\langle (3, 2), (1, 1) \rangle$, and by step 1 of the example they generate $G$.
4. The test with the determinant agrees: $3 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 1 = 1$.

The typical use of this example is as the first infinite group that is finitely generated but not cyclic.
It shows that the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups needs more than one cyclic factor.

A trap: in step 2 of the example, it is not enough to say "$G$ is infinite, so it is not cyclic".
The group $\mathbb{Z}$ is infinite and cyclic.
The proof must show that no single element produces both $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$.
