## Example

Many operations that you already know from calculus and from matrix algebra are [[linear maps|0RJF668P1T]].
Let $F$ be a field.
For each map below we check additivity $T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v)$ and homogeneity $T(cu) = c\,T(u)$, and we compute one value.

Differentiation comes first.
Let $D \colon F[x] \to F[x]$ be the [[formal derivative|0EGN1E1JPB]] on the [[polynomial ring|0SDC934R52]], given by
$$D\Bigl(\sum_k a_k x^k\Bigr) = \sum_{k \ge 1} k a_k x^{k-1}.$$
For $p = \sum a_k x^k$ and $q = \sum b_k x^k$, the coefficient of $x^{k-1}$ in $D(p + q)$ is $k(a_k + b_k) = k a_k + k b_k$, which is the coefficient in $D(p) + D(q)$.
In $D(cp)$ that coefficient is $k(c a_k) = c(k a_k)$, so $D$ is linear.
For example, $D(x^3 + 2x) = 3x^2 + 2$.

Integration is the second map.
Over $F = \R$, let $J \colon \R[x] \to \R[x]$ be $J(p)(x) = \int_0^x p(t)\, dt$, so that $J(x^k) = x^{k+1}/(k + 1)$.
The integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals, and a constant factor moves out of the integral, so $J$ is linear.
For example, $J(3x^2 + 1) = x^3 + x$.

The shifts act on the [[space of sequences|XYTN19EPF2]] $F^{\N}$, whose elements $(x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ are added and scaled entry by entry.
The right [[shift operator|MBK6AE6734]] and the left shift are
$$R(x_0, x_1, \dots) = (0, x_0, x_1, \dots), \qquad L(x_0, x_1, \dots) = (x_1, x_2, \dots).$$
Each entry of $R(x + y)$ is either $0$ or an entry $x_i + y_i$ of $x + y$, so $R(x + y) = R(x) + R(y)$, and the same reading gives $R(cx) = c\,R(x)$.
The same check works for $L$; for example, $L(1, 2, 3, 4, \dots) = (2, 3, 4, \dots)$.

Evaluation sends a polynomial to a number.
Fix $a \in F$ and let $\mathrm{ev}_a \colon F[x] \to F$ be $\mathrm{ev}_a(p) = p(a)$, as in the [[evaluation homomorphism|0FCCDXH97W]].
The rules $(p + q)(a) = p(a) + q(a)$ and $(cp)(a) = c\,p(a)$ are the definitions of the sum and the scalar multiple of polynomials, so $\mathrm{ev}_a$ is linear.
For example, $\mathrm{ev}_2(x^2 - 3) = 4 - 3 = 1$.

The [[trace|0XC1RDBY7A]] $\tr \colon M_n(F) \to F$ adds up the diagonal entries.
The diagonal of $A + B$ is the sum of the two diagonals, and the diagonal of $cA$ is $c$ times the diagonal of $A$, so the trace is linear.
For example, $\tr \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = 1 + 4 = 5$.

The [[transpose|09DXWYRD43]] $A \mapsto A^{\mathsf T}$ is a map $M_{m \times n}(F) \to M_{n \times m}(F)$.
The entry $(i, j)$ of $(A + B)^{\mathsf T}$ is $a_{ji} + b_{ji}$, and the entry $(i, j)$ of $(cA)^{\mathsf T}$ is $c\,a_{ji}$, so the transpose is linear.
For example, the transpose of the row $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ is the column with entries $1, 2, 3$.

Multiplication by a matrix is the last map.
Fix $A \in M_{m \times n}(F)$ and let $T_A \colon F^n \to F^m$ be $T_A(x) = Ax$, the [[matrix-vector product|0PDVQEP0ZC]].
The rules $A(x + y) = Ax + Ay$ and $A(cx) = c\,Ax$ of matrix algebra say that $T_A$ is linear.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ we get $T_A(1, 1) = (1 + 2, \; 0 + 1) = (3, 1)$.

## Remarks

### What the list shows

In every case the check works one coefficient, one entry or one coordinate at a time.
That is because the operations in these spaces are themselves defined one coefficient, entry or coordinate at a time.
The list also shows that a linear map need not be given by a matrix formula: the first six maps are linear with no matrix in sight.
Still, the [[theorem on matrix transformations|W6KB0J3SZZ]] says that the last map covers every linear map $F^n \to F^m$, and with bases every map between finite-dimensional spaces becomes a matrix.

### Differentiation in a basis

On the space $P_n$ of [[polynomials of degree at most n|0JGZCDT5DN]], $D$ maps $P_n$ to itself, and $D^{n+1} = 0$ because each application lowers the degree by one.
In the basis $1, x, x^2$ of $P_2$ we have $D(1) = 0$, $D(x) = 1$ and $D(x^2) = 2x$, so the [[matrix of D|0YQSGNYE3K]] has these coordinate vectors as columns:
$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Over $\R$, differentiation is also linear on the space of differentiable functions, by the [[linearity of differentiation|0M0TKFRX2A]].

### What changes with the field or the order

Over a field of characteristic $0$, the [[kernel|0S23E6YE83]] of $D$ is the space of constant polynomials.
Over $\mathbb{F}_p$ the kernel is larger, since $D(x^p) = p\,x^{p-1} = 0$.
The order of composition matters as well: $D \circ J$ is the identity on $\R[x]$, but $J \circ D$ is not, since $J(D(1)) = J(0) = 0$; the [[composites of differentiation and integration|25N6VYJ127]] work this out.
The shifts behave in the same lopsided way and give the standard [[counterexample in infinite dimension|N1G1WQW59J]]: $R$ is injective but not surjective.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Many operations in mathematics are linear maps.
Taking a derivative, taking an integral, adding up a diagonal and flipping a matrix all respect sums and scalar multiples.
For example, the derivative of $x^2 + x^3$ is $2x + 3x^2$, which is the derivative of $x^2$ plus the derivative of $x^3$.
The check is always the same: apply the map to a sum, and compare with the sum of the images.

### L2 human

The examples are useful because their kernels and images are easy to find, and these are the next ideas of the sub-area.
On $P_3$, the derivative $D$ sends $a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3$ to $a_1 + 2a_2 x + 3a_3 x^2$.
This is $0$ exactly when $a_1 = a_2 = a_3 = 0$, so the kernel is the constants, of dimension $1$.
The values are all polynomials of degree at most $2$, a space of dimension $3$, and $1 + 3 = 4 = \dim P_3$ as the [[rank-nullity theorem|0W4FSW1TVB]] predicts.
The two maps $\mathrm{ev}_a$ and $\tr$ have the field as target, so they are linear forms, the elements of the [[dual space|07JBSZE0V4]].
Watch out: the source and the target can differ in dimension, and they can even be infinite-dimensional, as for the shifts.
