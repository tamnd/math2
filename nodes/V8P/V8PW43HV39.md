## Proof

For part 1 we build a basis of $W$ one vector at a time, and the [[Steinitz exchange lemma|08ZDRN6H41]] stops the process; parts 2 and 3 then compare bases.
Steps 1 to 8 prove part 1, steps 9 to 11 prove part 2, and steps 12 to 14 prove part 3.

1. Let $n = \dim V$, the [[dimension|05G2G0P224]] of $V$, and let $c_1, \dots, c_n$ be a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] of $V$. In particular $c_1, \dots, c_n$ [[span|0BJKVF90Z6]] $V$.
2. Every [[linearly independent|0EVHJFTG9Z]] list in $V$ has at most $n$ vectors, by the Steinitz exchange lemma with the spanning list $c_1, \dots, c_n$.
3. Let $v_1, \dots, v_k \in W$ be independent, and let $v \in W$ lie outside their [[linear span|0HR573CHNM]]. If $a_1 v_1 + \dots + a_k v_k + a v = 0$ and $a \ne 0$, then $v = -a^{-1}(a_1 v_1 + \dots + a_k v_k)$ lies in the span, which is false.
4. So $a = 0$ in step 3, and then all $a_i = 0$ because $v_1, \dots, v_k$ are independent. Hence $v_1, \dots, v_k, v$ are independent.
5. Start with the empty list, which is independent. As long as the current list does not span $W$, choose a vector of $W$ outside its span and append it.
6. By steps 3 and 4, every list produced in step 5 is independent in $W$, and so in $V$, since a relation in $W$ is a relation in $V$. By step 2, its length is at most $n$.
7. Each append adds one vector, so by step 6 the process stops after at most $n$ appends. It stops only when the current list $v_1, \dots, v_k$ spans $W$, so this list is a basis of $W$ with $k \le n$.
8. So $W$ has a finite spanning list, and it is [[finite-dimensional|018W3V3TCT]]. By the [[dimension theorem|0WK0Y8RH1M]], every basis of $W$ has $k$ vectors, so $\dim W = k \le n = \dim V$.
9. For part 2, let $\dim W = n$. By step 7, $W$ has a basis $v_1, \dots, v_n$, and by step 6 it is independent in $V$.
10. By the [[right-size criterion|096V7H13H9]], $n$ independent vectors in the $n$-dimensional space $V$ form a basis of $V$, so $v_1, \dots, v_n$ span $V$.
11. Each $v_i$ lies in the subspace $W$, so every linear combination of them lies in $W$, and $V = \operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_n) \subseteq W$. With $W \subseteq V$, this gives $W = V$.
12. For part 3, let $V$ be arbitrary. By the theorem that [[every vector space has a basis|00MS9VMQ9Z]], $W$ has a basis $B_W$.
13. The set $B_W$ is independent in $V$, as in step 6. By the [[extension theorem|0M4SXE5WW3]], there is a basis $B$ of $V$ with $B_W \subseteq B$.
14. So $\abs{B_W} \le \abs{B}$. By the dimension theorem, $\dim W = \abs{B_W}$ and $\dim V = \abs{B}$, so $\dim W \le \dim V$.

## Idea

Inside $V$, an independent list never has more than $\dim V$ vectors, by the exchange lemma.
So if we keep adding vectors of $W$ that lie outside the current span, we must stop, and when we stop we hold a basis of $W$ with at most $\dim V$ vectors.
For equality, a basis of $W$ with $\dim V$ vectors is already a basis of $V$, so it spans $V$ while staying inside $W$.
In infinite dimension we cannot count steps, so we extend a basis of $W$ to a basis of $V$ instead and compare the two sets.

## Variants

Part 1 also follows from the extension theorem in finite form: a basis of $W$ is independent in $V$, so it extends to a basis of $V$, which has $\dim V$ vectors.
This route needs to know first that $W$ has a finite basis, which steps 5 to 7 supply.
When $V = F^n$ and $W$ is the span of finitely many given vectors, there is also a matrix proof.
Write the vectors as the columns of a matrix with $n$ rows, and the number of pivots is $\dim W$; each row holds at most one pivot, so $\dim W \le n$.
