## Summary

Let $T$ be a [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]] operator on a finite-dimensional [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] space $V$.
The Rayleigh quotient of a nonzero vector $v$ is the real number $R(v) = \ip{Tv}{v} / \ip{v}{v}$.
For a [[Hermitian matrix|0EKZCX4K3P]] $A$ and $x \neq 0$, it is $R_A(x) = x^* A x / x^* x$.
Its maximum and its minimum are the largest and the smallest eigenvalue, by [[the extreme value statement|T7MFPSY009]].
[[The Courant-Fischer min-max theorem|CJK2HS2508]] gives all the other eigenvalues as min-max values of $R$.

## Motivation

At an eigenvector $v$ with $Tv = \lambda v$, the quotient is $R(v) = \lambda$.
So $R$ extends the eigenvalue from eigenvectors to all nonzero vectors.
This gives eigenvalue information without the characteristic polynomial.
A test vector $v$ gives the bound $\lambda_{\max} \geq R(v)$, and a test vector near an eigenvector gives a good estimate of its eigenvalue.
The maximization of $R$ also gives [[a proof of the real spectral theorem|GHGBEV51GZ]].

## Notation

- Common symbols are $R(v)$, $R_A(x)$, $R(T, v)$ and $\rho(v)$.
- $R(v)$ is the value of the [[quadratic form|0RBQS1JJCR]] $\ip{Tv}{v}$ at the unit vector $v / \norm{v}$.
- Some books define the quotient for every square matrix. For a non-symmetric real matrix it then describes the symmetric part only.

## Remarks

### Examples

- For [[the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$|3FXAQ6P15S]], $R(1, 1) = \tfrac{11}{2}$, $R(2, 1) = 6$ and $R(1, -2) = 1$. The eigenvalues are $6$ and $1$.
- For a diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_n)$, the quotient is $\sum_j d_j \abs{x_j}^2 / \sum_j \abs{x_j}^2$, a weighted average of the $d_j$.
- For the identity, $R(v) = 1$ for every $v \neq 0$.
- [[Near an eigenvector|S1XDFTT962]], $R$ is a quadratically accurate estimate of the eigenvalue.

### Non-examples

- [[The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$|39XSQ9MTZ3]] is not symmetric. Its quotient takes the values $3$ and $-1$, but its only eigenvalue is $1$.
- For a complex matrix that is not Hermitian, $x^* A x$ need not be real. For $A = iI$, $x^* A x = i \norm{x}^2$.

### Basic properties

- $R(cv) = R(v)$ for every scalar $c \neq 0$. So $R$ is determined by its values on the unit sphere.
- In an orthonormal eigenbasis, $R(v) = \sum_j \lambda_j \abs{c_j}^2 / \sum_j \abs{c_j}^2$ with $c_j = \ip{v}{u_j}$.
- [[Extreme values|T7MFPSY009]]: $\lambda_{\min} \leq R(v) \leq \lambda_{\max}$, with equality exactly at the eigenvectors of the extreme eigenvalues.
- The critical points of $R$ on the nonzero vectors are exactly the eigenvectors.
- [[Courant-Fischer|CJK2HS2508]]: $\lambda_k$ is the maximum over subspaces $U$ of dimension $k$ of the minimum of $R$ on $U \setminus \set{0}$.
- For real symmetric matrices, the extreme values are also [[the extreme values of the quadratic form on unit vectors|YS4HT1EYE5]].

### History

The Wikipedia article says that the quotient is also called the Rayleigh-Ritz ratio, after Rayleigh and Ritz.
It calls the bound of the extreme value statement the Rayleigh-Ritz principle.

### Common errors

- Students forget the division by $\ip{v}{v}$. The form $\ip{Tv}{v}$ alone changes with the length of $v$, so it does not bound the eigenvalues.
- Students apply the eigenvalue bounds to non-symmetric matrices, as in the non-example above.
- Students think that $R(v) = \lambda$ for an eigenvalue $\lambda$ makes $v$ an eigenvector. This holds only for the extreme eigenvalues. For $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2, 3)$ and $v = (1, 0, 1)$, $R(v) = \tfrac{1 + 3}{2} = 2$, but $v$ is not an eigenvector.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Rayleigh quotient tells how much a symmetric matrix stretches a vector in the direction of the vector itself.
For an eigenvector, it is the eigenvalue.
For other vectors, it lies between the smallest and the largest eigenvalue.

### L2 human

Worked example: we bound the largest eigenvalue of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. Compute $x^T A x = 5 x_1^2 + 4 x_1 x_2 + 2 x_2^2$.
2. At $x = (3, 1)$, we get $\tfrac{45 + 12 + 2}{10} = 5.9$. So the largest eigenvalue is at least $5.9$.
3. At $x = (2, 1)$, we get $\tfrac{30}{5} = 6$. The characteristic polynomial $(t - 6)(t - 1)$ confirms that $6$ is the largest eigenvalue.

The typical use is a fast bound: each value $R(x)$ is a lower bound for the largest eigenvalue and an upper bound for the smallest one.
A trap: the quotient bounds the eigenvalues only for symmetric or Hermitian matrices.

### L3 human

For a self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space, the supremum and the infimum of $R$ are the ends of the spectrum.
The Rayleigh-Ritz method approximates eigenvalues by the extreme values of $R$ on a finite-dimensional subspace.
For any operator, the set of values of $R$ is the numerical range, and it is convex by the Toeplitz-Hausdorff theorem.
