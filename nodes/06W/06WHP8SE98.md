## Summary

The Rayleigh quotient asks one question of a vector: how much does the operator stretch it along its own direction?
For an eigenvector the answer is the eigenvalue, and for a self-adjoint operator every answer lies between the smallest and the largest eigenvalue.
Let $T$ be a [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]] operator on a finite-dimensional [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] space $V$.
The *Rayleigh quotient* of a vector $v \ne 0$ is the real number
$$R(v) = \frac{\ip{Tv}{v}}{\ip{v}{v}}.$$
For a [[Hermitian matrix|0EKZCX4K3P]] $A$ and $x \in \mathbb{C}^n$ with $x \ne 0$, it is $R_A(x) = x^* A x / x^* x$.
Its maximum and its minimum are the largest and the smallest eigenvalue, by [[the extreme value theorem for the quotient|T7MFPSY009]].

## Motivation

Eigenvalues are roots of the characteristic polynomial, and for a large matrix that polynomial is hard to compute.
The Rayleigh quotient gives eigenvalue information from a single matrix-vector product.
Every test vector $v$ gives the bounds $\lambda_{\min} \le R(v) \le \lambda_{\max}$, and a test vector close to an eigenvector gives a very good estimate of its eigenvalue.

The quotient began in mechanics.
For a vibrating system, $R(v)$ compares the potential energy of a shape $v$ with its kinetic energy, and its smallest value gives the square of the lowest frequency.
In linear algebra, maximizing $R$ gives [[a proof of the real spectral theorem|GHGBEV51GZ]], and the [[Courant-Fischer min-max theorem|CJK2HS2508]] describes every eigenvalue, not only the extreme ones, by maxima and minima of $R$ over subspaces.
In numerical analysis, Rayleigh quotient iteration uses $R(v)$ as the current eigenvalue estimate.

## Notation

Books write $R(v)$, $R_A(x)$, $R(T, v)$ or $\rho(v)$.
The numerator is the [[quadratic form|0RBQS1JJCR]] $\ip{Tv}{v}$, and $R(v)$ is its value at the unit vector $v / \norm{v}$.
Some books define the quotient for every square matrix.
For a real matrix that is not symmetric, the quotient then sees only the symmetric part of the matrix, so the eigenvalue bounds fail.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take [[the symmetric matrix|3FXAQ6P15S]] $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, whose eigenvalues are $6$ and $1$.
For $x = (x_1, x_2)$ the quotient is
$$R(x) = \frac{5 x_1^2 + 4 x_1 x_2 + 2 x_2^2}{x_1^2 + x_2^2}.$$
At $x = (1, 1)$ it is $\tfrac{11}{2}$, at $x = (1, 0)$ it is $5$, and at $x = (0, 1)$ it is $2$.
At the eigenvector $(2, 1)$ it is $\tfrac{20 + 8 + 2}{5} = 6$, and at the eigenvector $(1, -2)$ it is $\tfrac{5 - 8 + 8}{5} = 1$.
Every value lies in $[1, 6]$, and the ends are reached exactly at the eigenvectors.

### Why it is an average of eigenvalues

Write $v$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis $u_1, \dots, u_n$ with $T u_j = \lambda_j u_j$, so that $v = \sum_j c_j u_j$.
Orthonormality turns the quotient into
$$R(v) = \frac{\sum_j \lambda_j \abs{c_j}^2}{\sum_j \abs{c_j}^2},$$
a weighted average of the eigenvalues with the weights $\abs{c_j}^2$.
An average lies between the smallest and the largest value, and it equals the largest value only when all the weight sits on the largest value.
That is the whole content of [[the extreme value theorem|T7MFPSY009]].
For a diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_n)$ the average is visible at once: $R(x) = \sum_j d_j \abs{x_j}^2 / \sum_j \abs{x_j}^2$.

### The rules you can use

The quotient does not see the length of $v$: $R(cv) = R(v)$ for every scalar $c \ne 0$, because $c$ appears as $\abs{c}^2$ above and below.
So $R$ is determined by its values on the unit sphere.
The critical points of $R$ on the nonzero vectors are exactly the eigenvectors, and its critical values are the eigenvalues.
Near an eigenvector, the error of $R$ is quadratic: [[a test vector at angle about epsilon|S1XDFTT962]] gives an eigenvalue error of size about $\varepsilon^2$.
The [[Courant-Fischer theorem|CJK2HS2508]] says that, with $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n$, the number $\lambda_k$ is the maximum over subspaces $U$ of dimension $k$ of the minimum of $R$ on the nonzero vectors of $U$.

### What it is not

For [[the non-symmetric matrix with rows (1, 4) and (0, 1)|39XSQ9MTZ3]], the only eigenvalue is $1$, but $R(1, 1) = 3$ and $R(1, -1) = -1$.
The condition that fails is self-adjointness, and the quotient there measures the symmetric part $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ instead.
For a complex matrix that is not Hermitian, $x^* A x$ need not even be real: for $A = iI$ we get $x^* A x = i \norm{x}^2$.

### Watch out

The division by $\ip{v}{v}$ matters.
For the matrix above, the numerator at $(2, 2)$ is $44$, which is far above the largest eigenvalue $6$, while $R(2, 2) = \tfrac{44}{8} = \tfrac{11}{2}$.

A value $R(v) = \lambda$ for an eigenvalue $\lambda$ makes $v$ an eigenvector only when $\lambda$ is the largest or the smallest eigenvalue.
For $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2, 3)$ and $v = (1, 0, 1)$ we get $R(v) = \tfrac{1 + 3}{2} = 2$, but $Av = (1, 0, 3)$ is not a multiple of $v$.

### History

Rayleigh used the quotient in The Theory of Sound (1877) to estimate the frequencies of vibrating systems.
The quotient is also called the Rayleigh-Ritz ratio, and its bounds the Rayleigh-Ritz principle, after Rayleigh and Ritz.

### Try it

First, compute $R(1, 2)$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
The numerator is $5 + 8 + 8 = 21$ and the denominator is $5$, so $R(1, 2) = \tfrac{21}{5} = 4.2$, which lies in $[1, 6]$.

Second, give a lower bound for the largest eigenvalue of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ without solving any equation.
At $(1, 1)$ the quotient is $\tfrac{1 + 6 + 1}{2} = 4$, so the largest eigenvalue is at least $4$; in fact it equals $4$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a symmetric matrix as a machine that stretches arrows.
The Rayleigh quotient of an arrow tells how much the machine stretches it along its own direction.
For the matrix with rows $(5, 2)$ and $(2, 2)$, the arrow $(1, 0)$ is stretched by $5$ along itself, and the arrow $(2, 1)$ by exactly $6$.
No arrow is stretched by more than $6$ or less than $1$, and those two numbers are the eigenvalues.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: bound the largest eigenvalue of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ by test vectors.
The numerator is $x^{\mathsf{T}} A x = 5 x_1^2 + 4 x_1 x_2 + 2 x_2^2$.
At $x = (3, 1)$ it is $45 + 12 + 2 = 59$, and $x^{\mathsf{T}} x = 10$, so
$$\lambda_{\max} \ge R(3, 1) = \frac{59}{10} = 5.9.$$
At $x = (2, 1)$ the quotient is $\tfrac{30}{5} = 6$.
The characteristic polynomial $t^2 - 7t + 6 = (t - 6)(t - 1)$ confirms that $6$ is the largest eigenvalue.
Each test vector gives a lower bound for $\lambda_{\max}$ and an upper bound for $\lambda_{\min}$, and good test vectors give nearly exact values.
Watch out: these bounds need a symmetric or Hermitian matrix.

### L3 human

For a self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space, the supremum and the infimum of $R$ are the ends of the spectrum.
The Rayleigh-Ritz method approximates eigenvalues by the extreme values of $R$ on a finite-dimensional subspace, and this is the basis of the finite element method for vibration problems.
For an arbitrary operator, the set of values of $R$ on a complex space is the numerical range, and it is convex by the Toeplitz-Hausdorff theorem.
The sub-area on positive operators and singular values uses $R$ for $A^* A$, whose maximum is the square of the largest singular value.
