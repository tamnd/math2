## Example

Claim: for every positive integer $n$, $2^n > n^2$.
A student checks the claim for $n = 1, 5, 6, 7$ and concludes that it is true for all $n$.
This is not a [[proof of a universal statement|CX38X989TB]], and the claim is false.
Here are the values for $n = 1$ to $7$.

| $n$ | $2^n$ | $n^2$ | $2^n > n^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | 1 | true |
| 2 | 4 | 4 | false |
| 3 | 8 | 9 | false |
| 4 | 16 | 16 | false |
| 5 | 32 | 25 | true |
| 6 | 64 | 36 | true |
| 7 | 128 | 49 | true |

1. The student's four checks are correct.
2. The checks do not use an arbitrary $n$.
   They are four separate facts about four numbers, and they say nothing about $n = 2$.
3. At $n = 2$ both sides equal $4$, and $4 > 4$ is false, so $n = 2$ is a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]].
4. The claim also fails at $n = 3$, where $8 < 9$, and at $n = 4$, where $16 = 16$.

## Remarks

The true statement is: for every integer $n \ge 5$, $2^n > n^2$.
A generic argument alone does not give it, but [[mathematical induction|0ASJVEFA9J]] does.
The base case is $2^5 = 32 > 25 = 5^2$.
For the step, assume $2^n > n^2$ with $n \ge 5$, and double both sides:
$$2^{n+1} = 2 \cdot 2^n > 2n^2 = (n + 1)^2 + \big((n - 1)^2 - 2\big).$$
For $n \ge 5$ we have $(n - 1)^2 - 2 \ge 16 - 2 = 14 > 0$, so $2^{n+1} > (n + 1)^2$.
Check the identity at $n = 5$: $2 \cdot 25 = 50$ and $36 + 14 = 50$.

The example teaches two lessons.
Checks that happen to avoid the bad values can make a false claim look true.
And a true universal statement can need a hypothesis, here $n \ge 5$, that a few checks do not reveal.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Doubling grows fast: $2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64$.
Squares grow more slowly at first: $1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36$.
From the fifth term on, the doubling list is ahead.
At the second, third and fourth terms it is not ahead: $4$ against $4$, $8$ against $9$, and $16$ against $16$.
A person who checks only the first term and some later terms will miss this.

### L2 human

Which positive integers $n$ satisfy $2^n > n^2$?
The table shows that the inequality holds at $n = 1$, fails at $n = 2, 3, 4$, and holds at $n = 5$.
The induction in the remarks carries it from $n = 5$ to every larger $n$, so the exact set is
$$\{1\} \cup \{n \in \mathbb{Z} : n \ge 5\}.$$
The typical use of such a table is to form a correct conjecture, which a proof must then confirm.
Watch out: after $n = 2$ fails, a student may "fix" the claim to "for all $n \ge 3$", but $n = 3$ fails too, so each new conjecture needs its own check and its own proof.
