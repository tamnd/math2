## Definition

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional [[inner product space|01EK923SKP]] over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be a linear [[operator|0PTF1PTJK0]].
The operator $T$ is *self-adjoint* when $T^* = T$, where $T^*$ is the [[adjoint|0ST8QF9T3T]] of $T$.
Equivalently, $T$ is self-adjoint when
$$\ip{Tv}{w} = \ip{v}{Tw} \qquad \text{for all } v, w \in V.$$

## Notation

- A self-adjoint operator is also called Hermitian. Over $\mathbb{R}$, some books call it symmetric.
- The Lean declaration `LinearMap.IsSymmetric` is the second form, $\ip{Tv}{w} = \ip{v}{Tw}$. In finite dimension it is equivalent to $T^* = T$.
- An operator with $T^* = -T$ is called skew-adjoint.

## Remarks

- The two forms are equivalent by uniqueness of the adjoint. If $\ip{Tv}{w} = \ip{v}{Tw}$ for all $v$ and $w$, then $T$ satisfies the defining identity of $T^*$, so $T^* = T$.
- In an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]], $T$ is self-adjoint if and only if its matrix is [[symmetric|0N28QN4CYA]] over $\mathbb{R}$ or [[Hermitian|0EKZCX4K3P]] over $\mathbb{C}$. This follows from [[the matrix of the adjoint|0DBTE6HVBD]].
- The condition depends on the inner product. A map can be self-adjoint for one inner product and not for another, as [[the weighted example|~6b5e5bb9]] shows.
- Every self-adjoint operator is [[normal|0ENZN5G4CV]], since $T^* T = T^2 = T T^*$.
- The word "symmetric operator" has a different meaning for unbounded operators on Hilbert spaces, see [[symmetric operator|H971WGMV6C]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A self-adjoint operator can move from one side of the inner product to the other without change.
For real matrices and the dot product, these are the symmetric matrices.
For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is symmetric, and $(Ax) \cdot y = x \cdot (Ay)$ for all $x$ and $y$.

### L2 human

Worked example: we check the definition for a complex matrix.

1. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2i \\ -2i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ act on $\mathbb{C}^2$ with the standard inner product.
2. The conjugate transpose is $A^* = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \overline{-2i} \\ \overline{2i} & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2i \\ -2i & 1 \end{pmatrix} = A$.
3. The standard basis is orthonormal, so the operator $x \mapsto Ax$ is self-adjoint.
4. The plain transpose $A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2i \\ 2i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ differs from $A$. So $A$ is self-adjoint but not symmetric as a complex matrix.

The typical use is to check $T^* = T$ in an orthonormal basis.
A trap: over $\mathbb{C}$, the test is $A^* = A$ with conjugation, not $A^T = A$.

### L3 human

In Mathlib the predicate `LinearMap.IsSymmetric` makes sense in any inner product space, also infinite-dimensional.
On a Hilbert space, an operator that is defined everywhere and has this property is bounded and self-adjoint, by the Hellinger-Toeplitz theorem.
In a star algebra, the general predicate is `IsSelfAdjoint`, the condition $a^* = a$.
