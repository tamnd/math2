## Definition

A self-adjoint operator is an operator that equals its own adjoint.
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional [[inner product space|01EK923SKP]] over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be a linear [[operator|0PTF1PTJK0]].
The operator $T$ is *self-adjoint* when $T^* = T$, where $T^*$ is the [[adjoint|0ST8QF9T3T]] of $T$.
Equivalently, $T$ is self-adjoint when
$$\ip{Tv}{w} = \ip{v}{Tw} \qquad \text{for all } v, w \in V.$$

## Notation

A self-adjoint operator is also called Hermitian, and over $\mathbb{R}$ many books call it symmetric.
The Lean declaration `LinearMap.IsSymmetric` is the second form, $\ip{Tv}{w} = \ip{v}{Tw}$, and in finite dimension it is equivalent to $T^* = T$.
An operator with $T^* = -T$ is called skew-adjoint.

## Remarks

The two forms of the definition say the same thing because the adjoint is unique.
If $\ip{Tv}{w} = \ip{v}{Tw}$ for all $v$ and $w$, then $T$ itself satisfies the identity that defines $T^*$, so $T^* = T$.
Conversely, $T^* = T$ turns the identity $\ip{Tv}{w} = \ip{v}{T^* w}$ into the second form.

The condition depends on the inner product, not only on the map.
In an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]], $T$ is self-adjoint exactly when its matrix is [[symmetric|0N28QN4CYA]] over $\mathbb{R}$ or [[Hermitian|0EKZCX4K3P]] over $\mathbb{C}$, by [[the matrix of the adjoint|0DBTE6HVBD]].
In other bases, or for other inner products, a symmetric matrix can fail the test, as [[the weighted inner product example|CPQB5HABC6]] shows for adjoints in general.

The hypothesis of finite dimension makes the adjoint exist for every operator.
The second form makes sense in any inner product space, and there it is the definition that Mathlib uses.
On an infinite-dimensional space, the name [[symmetric operator|H971WGMV6C]] is used for unbounded operators that satisfy the identity only on a domain, and such an operator need not be self-adjoint.

A small example: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product is self-adjoint, because $(Ax) \cdot y = x^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} y = x \cdot (Ay)$.
A case that fails: $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $(N e_2) \cdot e_1 = 1$ but $e_2 \cdot (N e_1) = 0$.
Every self-adjoint operator is [[normal|0ENZN5G4CV]], since $T^* T = T^2 = T T^*$.
The [[concept page|0NEGN5CRSC]] develops the properties and the examples.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A self-adjoint operator can move from one side of the inner product to the other without change.
For real matrices and the dot product, these are the symmetric matrices.
For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is symmetric, so $(Ax) \cdot y = x \cdot (Ay)$ for all $x$ and $y$.
For $x = (1, 0)$ and $y = (0, 1)$ both sides equal $3$.

### L2 human

Here is a check of the definition for a complex matrix.
Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2i \\ -2i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ act on $\mathbb{C}^2$ with the standard inner product.
Conjugate each entry and transpose:
$$A^* = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \overline{-2i} \\ \overline{2i} & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2i \\ -2i & 1 \end{pmatrix} = A.$$
The standard basis is orthonormal, so the operator $x \mapsto Ax$ is self-adjoint.
The plain transpose $A^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2i \\ 2i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ differs from $A$, so $A$ is self-adjoint but not symmetric as a complex matrix.
Watch out: over $\mathbb{C}$ the test is $A^* = A$, with conjugation, and never $A^{\mathsf{T}} = A$.

### L3 human

In Mathlib, the predicate `LinearMap.IsSymmetric` makes sense in every inner product space, also in infinite dimension.
On a Hilbert space, an operator that is defined everywhere and has this property is bounded and self-adjoint, by the Hellinger-Toeplitz theorem.
In a star algebra, the general predicate is `IsSelfAdjoint`, the condition $a^* = a$.
