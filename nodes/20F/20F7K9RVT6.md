## Summary

What counts as a proof has changed over time, and each change answered a concrete failure of the older standard.
The history of proof and [[rigor|E1PE9R8DBB]] follows those changes, from Greek geometry to proofs checked by computer.

One theorem shows the whole path.
The [[intermediate value theorem|00KAFBR32E]] says that a continuous function which is negative at one end of an interval and positive at the other has a zero in between.
Eighteenth-century authors used it without proof, because the graph makes it look evident.
Bernard Bolzano gave a proof in 1817, and Augustin-Louis Cauchy gave one in 1821.
In 1872 Richard Dedekind constructed the real numbers by [[cuts|0G3RRM41X9]], so that the completeness on which the proof rests became a theorem.
Today the result is part of the Mathlib library of the Lean proof assistant, where a program checks every step.
The theorem stayed true throughout, and what changed was the standard that its proof had to meet.

In outline, Greek mathematics made deductive proof from stated starting points the ideal.
Other traditions, in China, India and the Islamic world, justified their methods in their own ways.
In the 19th century analysis was rebuilt on exact definitions, and around 1900 geometry and arithmetic received complete systems of [[axioms|GD2JBXJV5Z]].
In the 20th century the ideal became a derivation in a formal system, and from 1976 computers took part in proofs.

## Motivation

A student learns that a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] must justify each step, and the history shows where this rule comes from.
Each change in the standard came from a specific problem.
Paradoxes of the infinite made the Greeks careful with limits.
False results about infinite series pushed analysts to define convergence.
Doubts about the parallel postulate showed that Euclid's axioms were not the only possible ones.
Very long proofs and computer proofs now test the idea that one person checks each step.
Knowing these episodes helps you see why a modern definition has exactly the conditions it has.

## Notation

Historians use a few names for the main changes.
The *axiomatic method* derives all results of a theory from a list of axioms, as [[the axiomatic method|GD2JBXJV5Z]] explains.
The *arithmetization of analysis* is the 19th-century program of defining limits, continuity and the real numbers through the natural numbers and inequalities.
The *foundations of mathematics* is the study, from about 1900 on, of systems in which all of mathematics can be derived.

## Remarks

### Greek deductive proof

Greek tradition credits Thales and Hippocrates of Chios with some of the first proofs in geometry.
Eudoxus developed the method of exhaustion, which handles areas and volumes by finite approximations from inside and outside.
Around 300 BC [[Euclid's Elements|83WHDAV68J]] arranged geometry and number theory as definitions, postulates and propositions, each proposition with a proof.
For more than two thousand years the *Elements* was the model of a rigorous text.
Its figures sometimes did work that its axioms did not, as [[Euclid's first proposition|GQCW6RG3GB]] shows with two circles whose meeting point no postulate supplies.

### Proof in other traditions

Chinese mathematics passed on methods through problem texts such as the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*.
In 263 CE Liu Hui wrote a commentary on the *Nine Chapters* that explains why its methods give correct results.
In the Kerala school in India, Jyeṣṭhadeva wrote the *Yuktibhāṣā* in Malayalam around 1530, with justifications of the [[Madhava series|QF83S57V8Y]] for the sine, the cosine and the arctangent.
In the Islamic world, around 1000, al-Karaji used an early form of induction to justify the formula for the sum of the first $n$ cubes.
These traditions justified their results, but they did not take the axiomatic form of the *Elements* as their ideal.

### The 19th-century reform of analysis

The calculus of Newton and Leibniz worked with infinitely small quantities, and its foundations were disputed.
In 1734 George Berkeley attacked these foundations in *The Analyst*.
Eighteenth-century analysts found many correct results, and also some false ones, because they treated infinite series like finite sums.
Bolzano's proof of 1817 and Cauchy's *Cours d'analyse* of 1821 began to define limits and continuity and to prove theorems from the definitions.
Cauchy's book also states that a convergent series of continuous functions has a continuous sum, and in 1826 Niels Henrik Abel gave a series that does not obey it.
The repair needed [[uniform convergence|08VZ57PT26]], which Seidel, Weierstrass and others made precise.
In 1872 Karl Weierstrass presented a [[continuous function that is nowhere differentiable|01A0MK1730]], against the intuition that a continuous curve is smooth at most points.
The analytic side of this story has its own page, [[history of rigor in analysis|4VC87RNB8H]].

### Axioms and foundations

Lobachevsky and Bolyai published [[non-Euclidean geometry|SPCYMFN5V2]] around 1830, and Eugenio Beltrami's models of 1868 showed that the [[parallel postulate|2PC3XY0PYZ]] does not follow from Euclid's other axioms.
In 1899 David Hilbert published his [[axioms of geometry|KM1FB8XZ2R]], which state the assumptions that Euclid's figures had hidden.
Gottlob Frege published a formal language for logic in 1879, and Whitehead and Russell derived much of mathematics in a formal system in *Principia Mathematica*, from 1910 to 1913.
In 1931 [[Gödel's incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]] showed that no consistent system of this kind with enough arithmetic proves all true statements of arithmetic.
After this work most mathematicians came to regard [[Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory|0HE5WDQ9FJ]] as the base in which their proofs could in principle be written.

### Bourbaki

In the 1930s a group of French mathematicians began to write under the collective name Nicolas Bourbaki.
Their volumes, published from 1939, present mathematics in an axiomatic style, from set theory through algebra, topology and analysis.
Their style of exact definitions, general structures and complete proofs shaped the teaching and writing of mathematics in the following decades.

### The computer age

In 1976 Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken announced a proof of the [[four color theorem|0M406JC8J1]] that relied on a computer check of many cases.
In 1998 Thomas Hales and Samuel Ferguson announced a proof of the [[Kepler conjecture|AFMBQT06ZV]], also with a large computer part, and the referees could not check all of it.
Proof assistants later checked formal versions of both proofs: the four color theorem in Coq in 2005, and the Kepler conjecture in 2014.
[[Computer-assisted proof|MC18C7BMFT]] and [[long proofs and surveyability|RM0S65BHWH]] take up the questions that these cases raise.

### What the episodes share

Each change in the standard answered a specific failure: a paradox, a false theorem, or an object that intuition did not expect.
A higher standard does not make earlier results false, and most theorems of Euler and Cauchy survive with new proofs.
The standard is set by the community of mathematicians, and it can differ between fields at the same time.

### Watch out

Do not conclude that only Greek mathematics had proofs.
Liu Hui and Jyeṣṭhadeva gave justifications too, in forms that differ from the *Elements*.

Do not conclude that 18th-century analysis was wrong.
Most of its results were correct, and its arguments failed only the later standard.

Do not conclude that rigor reached a final form in 1900.
The debates on computer proofs and long proofs show that the standard still changes.

### Try it

First, put these in order of date: Dedekind's cuts, Berkeley's *The Analyst*, Hilbert's axioms of geometry, Bolzano's proof of the intermediate value theorem.
The order is Berkeley (1734), Bolzano (1817), Dedekind (1872), Hilbert (1899).

Second, a check by computer that Goldbach's conjecture holds for all even numbers up to a large bound is not a proof of the conjecture.
Why not?
It covers finitely many cases and derives nothing about the infinitely many that remain, as [[the Goldbach check|VE6ACCC4F7]] explains.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The ancient Greeks decided that a fact of geometry must be shown from a few starting rules, step by step.
Euclid's book collected this idea, and it guided mathematics for two thousand years.
Mathematicians in China and India also explained why their methods work, in their own ways.
In the 1800s mathematicians found mistakes in arguments about infinite sums, so they wrote exact definitions for words like "limit".
Around 1900 they wrote down all the starting rules of mathematics.
Since 1976 computers have helped to prove some theorems, and mathematicians still discuss when such a proof counts.

### L2 human

We follow Abel's series of 1826 to see why the definitions had to change.
The series is
$$\sin x - \tfrac{1}{2} \sin 2x + \tfrac{1}{3} \sin 3x - \tfrac{1}{4} \sin 4x + \dots$$
Each term is continuous, and the series converges at every real $x$.
Its sum is $x/2$ for $-\pi < x < \pi$, and every term is $0$ at $x = \pi$, so the sum there is $0$.
As $x$ increases toward $\pi$, the sum $x/2$ tends to $\pi/2$, while the value at $\pi$ is $0$, so the sum jumps.
Check at $x = \pi/2$: the terms are $1, 0, -\tfrac{1}{3}, 0, \tfrac{1}{5}, \dots$, and $1 - \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{5} - \dots = \pi/4$, which is $x/2$.

The flaw lies in the order of quantifiers.
Cauchy's claim needs one index $N$ that works for all $x$ at once, which is uniform convergence, and near $x = \pi$ no such $N$ exists.
With uniform convergence added as a hypothesis, the theorem is true.
Watch out for the phrase "Cauchy made a careless error", since historians still discuss what his definitions meant; the safe claim is that the theorem as we read it needs uniform convergence.

### L3 human

The history reads as a sequence of failures and repairs, and each repair also opened a new field.
The study of convergence led to real analysis and to [[Fourier series|G12KXDHPPS]] as a subject of its own.
The search for foundations led to set theory, [[mathematical logic|DYJ0KGTPMR]] and proof theory.

The present standard has two levels.
In practice, a proof is accepted when experts agree that each gap is routine, as [[acceptance of proofs|MJ3EHQJJHX]] explains.
In principle, a proof is a derivation in set theory, and proof assistants now check some proofs at that level, as [[formal and informal proofs|8VZ77R4R8S]] explains.
In 1993 and 1994 Arthur Jaffe, Frank Quinn and William Thurston argued about how these levels relate to speculation and understanding, in [[the debate on theoretical mathematics|K3M3TEX7SJ]].
