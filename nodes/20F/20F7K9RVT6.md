## Summary

The history of proof and rigour is the story of how the standard for an acceptable [[mathematical proof|0B94TKFQJX]] arose and changed.
Greek mathematics made deductive proof from stated starting points the ideal.
Other traditions, in China, India and the Islamic world, justified their methods by arguments of their own kinds.
In the 19th century analysis was rebuilt on precise definitions, and around 1900 geometry and arithmetic were given complete systems of [[axioms|GD2JBXJV5Z]].
In the 20th century the standard became, in principle, a derivation in a formal system, and computers began to take part in proofs.
The history matters because it shows that [[rigour|E1PE9R8DBB]] is a standard that mathematicians set and revise, in response to errors and to new kinds of objects.

## Motivation

A student learns that a proof must justify each step.
The history shows where this rule comes from and why it has its present form.
Each change in the standard came from a concrete problem.
Paradoxes of the infinite made the Greeks careful about limits.
Wrong results about infinite series pushed analysts to define convergence.
Doubts about the parallel postulate showed that Euclid's axioms were not the only possible ones.
Very long and computer proofs now test the idea that a single human checks each step.

## Notation

Historians use a few terms for the main changes.
The "axiomatic method" is the practice of deriving all results of a theory from a list of axioms; the page [[axiomatic method|GD2JBXJV5Z]] treats it.
The "arithmetization of analysis" is the 19th-century programme of defining limits, continuity and the real numbers through the natural numbers and inequalities.
The "foundations of mathematics" is the study, from about 1900 on, of the systems in which all of mathematics can be derived.

## Remarks

### Greek deductive proof

Thales and Hippocrates of Chios are credited with some of the first known proofs of theorems in geometry.
Eudoxus developed the method of exhaustion, which treats areas and volumes by finite approximations from inside and outside.
Around 300 BC [[Euclid's Elements|83WHDAV68J]] arranged geometry and number theory as definitions, postulates and propositions, each with a proof.
For more than two thousand years the Elements was the model of a rigorous text.

### Proof in other traditions

Chinese mathematics transmitted methods through problem texts such as the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art.
In 263 CE Liu Hui wrote a commentary on the Nine Chapters that explains why its methods give correct results.
In the Kerala school in India, Jyeṣṭhadeva wrote the Yuktibhāṣā in Malayalam around 1530, with justifications of the [[series of Madhava|QF83S57V8Y]] for the sine, the cosine and the arctangent.
In the Islamic world, around 1000, al-Karaji used an early form of induction to prove facts about arithmetic progressions and the binomial coefficients.
These traditions justified results, but they did not take the axiomatic form of the Elements as their ideal.

### The 19th-century rigour movement

The calculus of Newton and Leibniz worked with infinitely small quantities, and its foundations were disputed.
In 1734 George Berkeley attacked these foundations in his book The Analyst.
Eighteenth-century analysts obtained many correct results, and also some wrong ones, because they treated infinite series like finite sums.
In 1817 Bolzano proved the [[intermediate value theorem|00KAFBR32E]], and in 1821 Cauchy's Cours d'analyse defined limits and continuity and proved theorems from these definitions.
Cauchy's book also contains a theorem that a convergent sum of continuous functions is continuous, and Abel gave a counterexample in 1826.
The repair needed the notion of [[uniform convergence|08VZ57PT26]], which Weierstrass and others made precise.
In 1872 Weierstrass presented a [[continuous function that is nowhere differentiable|01A0MK1730]], against the intuition that a continuous curve is smooth at most points.
In the same year Dedekind published his construction of the real numbers by [[cuts|0G3RRM41X9]], which gives the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ as a theorem.

### Axioms and foundations

The discovery of [[non-Euclidean geometry|SPCYMFN5V2]] around 1830 showed that the [[parallel postulate|2PC3XY0PYZ]] does not follow from Euclid's other axioms.
In 1899 Hilbert published the [[axioms of geometry|KM1FB8XZ2R]] that bear his name, and they fill the gaps that Euclid's figures had hidden.
In 1879 Frege published a formal language for logic, and Whitehead and Russell derived much of mathematics in a formal system in Principia Mathematica, 1910 to 1913.
In 1931 [[Gödel's incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]] showed that no such system of the usual kind can prove all true statements of arithmetic.
After this work, most mathematicians came to regard [[Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory|0HE5WDQ9FJ]] as the standard base, in which their proofs could in principle be written.

### Bourbaki

From the 1930s a group of French mathematicians wrote under the collective name Nicolas Bourbaki.
Their volumes, published from 1939, present mathematics in an axiomatic style, from set theory through algebra, topology and analysis.
Their style of precise definitions, general structures and complete proofs influenced the teaching and writing of mathematics in the following decades.

### The computer age

In 1976 Appel and Haken announced a proof of the [[four colour theorem|0M406JC8J1]] that relied on a computer check of many cases.
In 1998 Hales and Ferguson announced a proof of the [[Kepler conjecture|AFMBQT06ZV]], also with a large computer part, and the referees could not check all of it.
Proof assistants then checked formal versions of both proofs: the four colour theorem in Coq in 2005, and the Kepler conjecture in 2014.
These cases are treated in [[computer-assisted proof|MC18C7BMFT]] and [[long proofs and surveyability|RM0S65BHWH]].

### Examples

- The intermediate value theorem: earlier authors took it as evident, and Bolzano gave it a proof in 1817. [[This example|ARWPBPW0Y3]] shows what the proof had to add.
- Euclid's first proposition: rigorous by Greek standards, it uses a step that his axioms do not justify. [[This example|GQCW6RG3GB]] shows the gap.
- Cauchy's theorem on sums of continuous functions: a published proof with a false general claim, repaired by a new definition.

### Non-examples

- A list of solved problems with no justification, such as a table of results, is not part of the history of proof. The condition that fails: no argument is given.
- A computer search that checks a conjecture up to a bound, such as for the [[Goldbach conjecture|063JNBSMJ0]], is evidence and not a proof. The condition that fails: the general claim is not derived.

### Basic properties

- Each change in the standard answered a specific failure: a paradox, a false theorem, or an object that intuition did not expect.
- A raised standard does not make earlier results false. Most results of Euler and Cauchy survive, with new proofs.
- The standard is set by the community, and it can differ between fields at one time.

### History

The sections above are the history.
In short: deductive proof in Greece, justifications in other traditions, rigorous analysis in the 19th century, axioms and formal logic around 1900, and computer proofs from 1976.

### Common errors

- To think that only Greek mathematics had proofs. Chinese, Indian and Islamic mathematicians gave justifications, in forms that differ from the Elements.
- To think that 18th-century analysis was wrong. Most of its results were correct; its arguments did not meet the later standard.
- To think that rigour reached a final form in 1900. The debates on computer proofs and long proofs show that the standard still changes.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The ancient Greeks decided that a fact in geometry must be shown from a few starting rules, step by step.
This idea, collected in Euclid's book, guided mathematics for two thousand years.
Mathematicians in China and India also explained why their methods work, in their own ways.
In the 1800s mathematicians found errors in arguments about infinite sums, so they wrote exact definitions for words like "limit".
In the 1900s they wrote down all the starting rules of mathematics.
Since 1976 computers have helped to prove some theorems, and people still discuss when such a proof counts.

### L2 human

We follow one claim through the change of standard, the claim of Cauchy's 1821 book that a convergent sum of continuous functions is continuous.

1. Abel's counterexample of 1826 uses a Fourier series of the form $\sin x - \tfrac{1}{2}\sin 2x + \tfrac{1}{3}\sin 3x - \cdots$.
2. Each term is continuous, and the series converges at each real $x$.
3. Its sum is $x/2$ for $-\pi < x < \pi$, and $0$ at $x = \pi$.
4. So the sum jumps at $x = \pi$: as $x$ increases to $\pi$, the sum tends to $\pi/2$, while its value at $\pi$ is $0$.
5. The flaw is in the order of quantifiers. Cauchy's argument needs one index $N$ that works for all $x$ at once, which is uniform convergence.
6. With uniform convergence as a hypothesis, the theorem is true, and its proof is now standard.

The typical use of this case is to show why precise definitions matter: the words "the series converges" had two meanings, and only one makes the theorem true.
A trap is to say that Cauchy "made a careless error"; historians still discuss what his definitions meant, and the safe claim is that the theorem as read today needs uniform convergence.

### L3 human

The history can be read as a sequence of crises and repairs, but each repair also opened new fields.
The study of convergence led to real analysis and to [[Fourier series|G12KXDHPPS]] as a subject of its own.
The search for foundations led to set theory, [[mathematical logic|DYJ0KGTPMR]] and proof theory.
The analytic part of this story is told in more detail in the topic [[history of rigour in analysis|4VC87RNB8H]].

The present standard has two levels.
In practice, a proof is accepted when experts agree that each gap is routine, as the page [[acceptance of proofs|MJ3EHQJJHX]] explains.
In principle, a proof is a derivation in set theory, and proof assistants now check some proofs at this level, as the page [[formal and informal proofs|8VZ77R4R8S]] explains.
The debate between Jaffe and Quinn and Thurston in 1993 and 1994 asked how these levels should relate to speculation and understanding; see [[the Jaffe-Quinn and Thurston debate|K3M3TEX7SJ]].
