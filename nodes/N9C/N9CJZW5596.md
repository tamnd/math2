## Example

On $V = \mathbb{Q}^2$ let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B(x, y) = x^{\mathsf{T}} A y = x_1 y_1 + 2 x_1 y_2 + 3 x_2 y_2.$$
Let $e_1, e_2$ be the standard basis and $e_1^*, e_2^*$ the [[dual basis|0ZD2CF6ZS0]].
We check that $B$ is a bilinear form with matrix $A$, show that it is not symmetric, and compute its two maps into the dual space.

1. $B$ is a [[bilinear form|0P799D6AMN]], because the matrix product $x^{\mathsf{T}} A y$ is linear in $x$ for fixed $y$ and linear in $y$ for fixed $x$.
2. The values on the basis pairs are $B(e_1, e_1) = 1$, $B(e_1, e_2) = 2$, $B(e_2, e_1) = 0$ and $B(e_2, e_2) = 3$. So the [[matrix of $B$|0P24YW565F]] is $A$.
3. $B$ is not symmetric. Take $u = (2, 1)$ and $v = (1, -1)$:
$$B(u, v) = 2 - 4 - 3 = -5, \qquad B(v, u) = 2 + 2 - 3 = 1.$$
4. The map $L_B \colon V \to V^*$, $u \mapsto B(u, \cdot)$, sends $e_1$ to the linear form $y \mapsto y_1 + 2 y_2$, which is $e_1^* + 2 e_2^*$. It sends $e_2$ to $y \mapsto 3 y_2$, which is $3 e_2^*$.
5. The columns of the matrix of $L_B$ are the coordinates of $L_B(e_1)$ and $L_B(e_2)$, namely $(1, 2)$ and $(0, 3)$. So this matrix is $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
6. The map $R_B \colon V \to V^*$, $v \mapsto B(\cdot, v)$, sends $e_1$ to $x \mapsto x_1$, which is $e_1^*$, and $e_2$ to $x \mapsto 2 x_1 + 3 x_2$, which is $2 e_1^* + 3 e_2^*$. Its matrix has the columns $(1, 0)$ and $(2, 3)$, so it is $A$.
7. $\det A = 3 \ne 0$, so both maps are isomorphisms and $B$ is [[nondegenerate|01NHFZF2SK]].

## Remarks

The example shows that the two maps $V \to V^*$ of a form are different when the form is not symmetric.
Their matrices are $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $A$, as the [[correspondence with linear maps into the dual|ZVMJAF7D53]] states.
They still have the same rank, here $2$, because a matrix and its transpose have the same rank.

Orthogonality also becomes one-sided.
We have $B(e_2, e_1) = 0$, so $e_2$ is orthogonal to $e_1$ when $e_2$ sits in the first slot.
But $B(e_1, e_2) = 2$, so $e_1$ is not orthogonal to $e_2$ in the same sense.
Forms for which this cannot happen are the [[reflexive forms|M0G958M917]].

If we change the entry $0$ to $2$, the matrix becomes symmetric, the form becomes $x_1 y_1 + 2 x_1 y_2 + 2 x_2 y_1 + 3 x_2 y_2$, and $L_B = R_B$.
The same matrix $A$ appears in the [[change of basis example|2X09GT6SMD]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

This rule takes two pairs of numbers and gives one number.
It is linear in each pair, so it is a bilinear form.
But the order matters: the pairs $(2, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ give $-5$ in one order and $1$ in the other order.
The dot product never does this, since $3 \cdot 4$ and $4 \cdot 3$ are the same.

### L2 human

Here is how you read the two maps into the dual space: you fix one slot and look at what is left.
Fix the first slot at $e_1$:
$$y \mapsto B(e_1, y) = y_1 + 2 y_2.$$
Fix the second slot at $e_1$:
$$x \mapsto B(x, e_1) = x_1.$$
The two linear forms differ, because the form is not symmetric.
The kernel of the first one is the line spanned by $(-2, 1)$, and the kernel of the second one is the line spanned by $(0, 1)$.
Check: $B(e_1, (-2, 1)) = -2 + 2 = 0$ and $B((0, 1), e_1) = 0$.
Watch out: the matrix of $u \mapsto B(u, \cdot)$ is $A^{\mathsf{T}}$, not $A$, because its columns come from the rows of $A$.
