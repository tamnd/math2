## Example

On $V = \Q^2$ let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and
$$B(x, y) = x^T A y = x_1 y_1 + 2x_1 y_2 + 3x_2 y_2.$$
Let $e_1, e_2$ be the standard basis and $e_1^*, e_2^*$ the [[dual basis|0ZD2CF6ZS0]].

1. $B$ is a [[bilinear form|0P799D6AMN]]. For fixed $y$ it is linear in $x$, and for fixed $x$ it is linear in $y$, because $x^T A y$ is a product of matrices.
2. The [[matrix of $B$|0P24YW565F]] is $A$, because $B(e_1, e_1) = 1$, $B(e_1, e_2) = 2$, $B(e_2, e_1) = 0$ and $B(e_2, e_2) = 3$.
3. $B$ is not symmetric: $B((2, 1), (1, -1)) = 2 - 4 - 3 = -5$, but $B((1, -1), (2, 1)) = 2 + 2 - 3 = 1$.
4. The map $L_B \colon V \to V^*$, $u \mapsto B(u, \cdot)$, sends $e_1$ to $B(e_1, \cdot) = e_1^* + 2e_2^*$ and $e_2$ to $3e_2^*$.
5. So the matrix of $L_B$ in the bases $(e_1, e_2)$ and $(e_1^*, e_2^*)$ has the columns $(1, 2)$ and $(0, 3)$. This is $A^T$.
6. The map $R_B \colon V \to V^*$, $v \mapsto B(\cdot, v)$, sends $e_1$ to $e_1^*$ and $e_2$ to $2e_1^* + 3e_2^*$. Its matrix has the columns $(1, 0)$ and $(2, 3)$, which is $A$.
7. $\det A = 3 \neq 0$, so both maps are isomorphisms and $B$ is [[nondegenerate|01NHFZF2SK]].

## Remarks

The example shows that the two maps $V \to V^*$ of a form are different when the form is not symmetric.
Their matrices are $A^T$ and $A$, as the [[correspondence with linear maps into the dual|ZVMJAF7D53]] states.
The vector $e_2$ is orthogonal to $e_1$ from the left, but $e_1$ is not orthogonal to $e_2$ from the left.
This one-sided orthogonality is the subject of [[reflexive forms|M0G958M917]].
If we change the entry $0$ to $2$, the matrix becomes symmetric, the form becomes symmetric, and $L_B = R_B$.
The same matrix serves for the [[change of basis example|2X09GT6SMD]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

This rule takes two pairs of numbers and gives one number.
It is linear in each pair, so it is a bilinear form.
But the order matters: the pairs $(2, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ give $-5$ in one order and $1$ in the other order.
The dot product never does this.

### L2 human

To see the two maps into the dual space, fix one slot of the form.

1. Fix the first slot at $e_1$. The function $y \mapsto B(e_1, y) = y_1 + 2y_2$ is the linear form $e_1^* + 2e_2^*$.
2. Fix the second slot at $e_1$. The function $x \mapsto B(x, e_1) = x_1$ is the linear form $e_1^*$.
3. The two results differ, because the form is not symmetric.

The typical use of these maps is the test of nondegeneracy: $B$ is nondegenerate when they are injective.
A trap: the matrix of $u \mapsto B(u, \cdot)$ is $A^T$, not $A$.
