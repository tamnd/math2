## Definition

The conjugate transpose of a complex matrix is its transpose with every entry replaced by its complex conjugate.
Let $A = (a_{ij})$ be an $m \times n$ matrix with entries in $\mathbb{C}$.
The *conjugate transpose* of $A$ is the $n \times m$ matrix $A^*$ with entries
$$(A^*)_{ij} = \overline{a_{ji}} \qquad (1 \le i \le n,\ 1 \le j \le m),$$
where $\overline{z}$ denotes the complex conjugate of $z \in \mathbb{C}$.

## Notation

Other common notations are $A^{\mathsf{H}}$, $A^H$ and $A^\dagger$.
In Mathlib, `Matrix.conjTranspose A` is written `Aᴴ`, and it is defined for matrices over any ring with a `star` operation.

## Remarks

The definition does two things at once.
The swap of the indices, $a_{ji}$ in place of $a_{ij}$, is the [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]], and it is why an $m \times n$ matrix becomes $n \times m$.
The bar conjugates each entry, and it is there so that $x^* x = \sum_k |x_k|^2$ is a real number that is $0$ only for $x = 0$.
The two operations do not interfere, so $A^* = \overline{A}^{\mathsf{T}} = \overline{A^{\mathsf{T}}}$.

For example, the conjugate transpose of the $2 \times 3$ matrix
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i & 0 \\ 2 - i & 3 & 4i \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{is} \quad A^* = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 + i \\ -i & 3 \\ 0 & -4i \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check one entry: $(A^*)_{21} = \overline{a_{12}} = \overline{i} = -i$.

If you keep the swap and drop the bar, you get the plain transpose, and [[the isotropic vector example|5AG0M5GR5T]] shows that it no longer measures length.
For a real matrix the bar has no effect, so $A^* = A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
The [[concept page|0DXKE10PHF]] gives the rules $(A^*)^* = A$, $(cA)^* = \overline{c} A^*$ and $(AB)^* = B^* A^*$, and the identity $\langle Ax, y \rangle = \langle x, A^* y \rangle$ for the standard inner product of $\mathbb{C}^n$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The conjugate transpose flips a complex matrix over its diagonal and changes the sign of every imaginary part.
For example, the conjugate transpose of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -i & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
The entry $i$ moved from the top right to the bottom left and became $-i$.

### L2 human

To compute $A^*$, go through the rows of $A$ one at a time.
Row $j$ of $A$, with every entry conjugated, becomes column $j$ of $A^*$.
For the row $(2 - i, 3, 4i)$ above, the conjugates are $2 + i$, $3$ and $-4i$, and they form column 2 of $A^*$.
Watch out: conjugating only the off-diagonal entries is wrong, since a diagonal entry like $2i$ must become $-2i$.
