## Example

Add the first few cubes: $1 = 1$, $1 + 8 = 9$, $9 + 27 = 36$, $36 + 64 = 100$.
Every total is a perfect square, $1^2$, $3^2$, $6^2$, $10^2$, and that is the start of a conjecture.
Let $s_n = 1^3 + 2^3 + \dots + n^3$.
We [[form a conjecture|R2T42N5XGK]] for $s_n$ from data, test it on data we did not use, and then prove it.

1. We compute the data:
$$s_1, \dots, s_{10} = 1, 9, 36, 100, 225, 441, 784, 1296, 2025, 3025.$$
2. We take differences of $s_1, \dots, s_7$ until they become constant:
$$\begin{array}{ll} \text{first:} & 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343 \\ \text{second:} & 19, 37, 61, 91, 127 \\ \text{third:} & 18, 24, 30, 36 \\ \text{fourth:} & 6, 6, 6 \end{array}$$
3. Constant fourth differences suggest that $s_n$ is a polynomial in $n$ of degree $4$.
   Its leading coefficient would be $6/4! = 1/4$.
4. [[Newton's forward difference formula|DDHCMKHD2T]], with the first entries $1, 8, 19, 18, 6$ of the rows, gives the fitted polynomial
$$s_n = 1 + 8\binom{n-1}{1} + 19\binom{n-1}{2} + 18\binom{n-1}{3} + 6\binom{n-1}{4}.$$
5. We look for a simpler form.
   The roots of the squares in step 1 are $1, 3, 6, 10, 15, \dots, 55$, the [[triangular numbers|WHN4CBYZXD]] $n(n+1)/2$.
   So the conjecture is
$$s_n = \left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2,$$
   and expanding both sides shows that this polynomial equals the fit of step 4.
6. We test on new data.
   The values $s_8$, $s_9$, $s_{10}$ were not used in step 4, and the conjecture gives $36^2 = 1296$, $45^2 = 2025$ and $55^2 = 3025$, which agree with step 1.
7. We prove the conjecture by induction.
   For $n = 1$ both sides equal $1$.
   If $s_n = n^2(n+1)^2/4$, then
$$s_{n+1} = \frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4} + (n+1)^3 = \frac{(n+1)^2(n^2 + 4n + 4)}{4} = \frac{(n+1)^2(n+2)^2}{4}.$$

So the conjecture of step 5 is a theorem, the identity of the [[squared triangular number|23WBA59VWP]].

## Remarks

### Which steps are evidence and which are proof

Steps 2 to 6 produce evidence, and only step 7 is a proof.
Seven values were differenced, and a sequence can have constant fourth differences on a finite range without being a polynomial.
Ten values agreeing with a formula is a pattern, and a pattern that holds in many cases is not a proof.
The induction of step 7 is what settles the question for every $n$.
The chain of compute, detect, test and prove is [[experimental mathematics|BPEPTN0TT9]] on a small scale.

### Why the simpler form helps

The polynomial of step 4 is correct but opaque.
The form of step 5 has a meaning, the square of $1 + 2 + \dots + n$, and that meaning suggested a proof.
A good conjecture is often a rewriting of a fit into a form that a reader can explain.

### Watch out

The fit of step 4 matches $s_1, \dots, s_5$ by construction, because a polynomial of degree $4$ is fixed by $5$ values.
So agreement at those $5$ values tests nothing, and only values beyond them, such as $s_6$ to $s_{10}$, test the fit.

### Try it

Use the conjecture to find $s_{20}$.
It gives $(20 \cdot 21/2)^2 = 210^2 = 44100$.

Do the sums $1^2 + 2^2 + \dots + n^2$ have constant differences, and at which order?
The values $1, 5, 14, 30, 55$ have third differences $2, 2$, so the sum is a cubic in $n$, namely $n(n+1)(2n+1)/6$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Add the cubes: $1$, then $1 + 8 = 9$, then $9 + 27 = 36$, then $36 + 64 = 100$.
The answers $1, 9, 36, 100$ are the squares of $1, 3, 6, 10$.
These are the numbers $1$, $1 + 2$, $1 + 2 + 3$ and $1 + 2 + 3 + 4$.
So we guess that the sum of the first $n$ cubes is the square of the sum of the first $n$ numbers.
We test the guess on more cases, and then we prove it by induction, because more cases alone would never be enough.

### L2 human

We check the fit of step 4 at $n = 5$.
The binomial coefficients are $\binom41 = 4$, $\binom42 = 6$, $\binom43 = 4$ and $\binom44 = 1$, so
$$s_5 = 1 + 8 \cdot 4 + 19 \cdot 6 + 18 \cdot 4 + 6 \cdot 1 = 1 + 32 + 114 + 72 + 6 = 225.$$
The conjecture gives the same value:
$$\left(\frac{5 \cdot 6}{2}\right)^2 = 15^2 = 225.$$
The typical use: Newton's formula turns a difference table into a polynomial with no algebra beyond binomial coefficients.
Watch out: this check is at a value the fit was built from, so it confirms the arithmetic, not the conjecture.
