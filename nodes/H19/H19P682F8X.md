## Example

Let $s_n = 1^3 + 2^3 + \cdots + n^3$.
We [[form a conjecture|R2T42N5XGK]] for $s_n$ from data, test it, and prove it.

1. The data. $s_1, \ldots, s_{10} = 1, 9, 36, 100, 225, 441, 784, 1296, 2025, 3025$.
2. The differences. For $s_1, \ldots, s_7$:
   - first differences: $8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343$;
   - second differences: $19, 37, 61, 91, 127$;
   - third differences: $18, 24, 30, 36$;
   - fourth differences: $6, 6, 6$.
3. The first conjecture. Constant fourth differences suggest that $s_n$ is a polynomial of degree $4$ with leading coefficient $6/4! = 1/4$.
4. The fit. [[Newton's forward difference formula|DDHCMKHD2T]] with $\Delta^j s_1 = 1, 8, 19, 18, 6$ gives $s_n = 1 + 8\binom{n-1}{1} + 19\binom{n-1}{2} + 18\binom{n-1}{3} + 6\binom{n-1}{4}$.
5. The simpler form. Every value in step 1 is a square: $1^2, 3^2, 6^2, 10^2, 15^2, \ldots, 55^2$. The roots $1, 3, 6, 10, 15$ are the [[triangular numbers|WHN4CBYZXD]] $n(n+1)/2$. So the conjecture is $s_n = \left(\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2$, and this polynomial agrees with the fit of step 4.
6. The test. The data of step 1 for $n = 8, 9, 10$ were not used in step 4. The conjecture gives $36^2 = 1296$, $45^2 = 2025$ and $55^2 = 3025$, which agree.
7. The proof. For $n = 1$ both sides are $1$. If $s_n = n^2(n+1)^2/4$, then $s_{n+1} = \dfrac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4} + (n+1)^3 = \dfrac{(n+1)^2(n^2 + 4n + 4)}{4} = \dfrac{(n+1)^2(n+2)^2}{4}$.

So the conjecture of step 5 is a theorem, the identity of the [[squared triangular number|23WBA59VWP]].

## Remarks

Step 3 is a conjecture and not a deduction.
Only seven values were differenced, and a sequence can have constant fourth differences on a finite range without being a polynomial.
The proof of step 7 is what settles the question.
Step 5 replaced a correct but opaque polynomial by a form with a meaning, which suggested the proof.

The page is also an instance of [[experimental mathematics|BPEPTN0TT9]] on a small scale: compute, detect a pattern, test, prove.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Add the cubes: $1$, then $1 + 8 = 9$, then $9 + 27 = 36$, then $36 + 64 = 100$.
The answers $1, 9, 36, 100$ are the squares of $1, 3, 6, 10$.
These are the numbers $1$, $1 + 2$, $1 + 2 + 3$ and $1 + 2 + 3 + 4$.
So we guess: the sum of the first $n$ cubes is the square of the sum of the first $n$ numbers.
We test the guess on more cases, and then prove it by induction.

### L2 human

We check the fit of step 4 at $n = 5$.

1. $\binom41 = 4$, $\binom42 = 6$, $\binom43 = 4$, $\binom44 = 1$.
2. $s_5 = 1 + 8 \cdot 4 + 19 \cdot 6 + 18 \cdot 4 + 6 \cdot 1 = 1 + 32 + 114 + 72 + 6 = 225$.
3. The conjecture gives $(5 \cdot 6/2)^2 = 15^2 = 225$.

The typical use: Newton's formula turns a difference table into a polynomial with no algebra beyond binomial coefficients.
A trap: the polynomial of step 4 fits the first five values by construction, so only values beyond them test it.
