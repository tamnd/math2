## Problem

Twin $A$ stays at rest in an inertial frame.
Twin $B$ travels at constant speed $v$, with $0 < v < c$, to a star at distance $L$ in that frame.
Then $B$ turns around and comes back at the same speed.
By [[time dilation|KRJAF5K1YA]] each twin, while moving uniformly, judges the clock of the other to run slow by the [[Lorentz factor|2E9AJVZS6E]] $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$.
When they meet again, which twin is younger, and why does the symmetric argument fail?

## Remarks

The travelling twin $B$ is younger.
The time each twin ages is the [[proper time|YG74WHC3S9]] along its [[world line|Z788G6MJ9E]] in [[Minkowski space|G49G4R1K04]].
For $A$ it is $2L/v$, and for $B$ it is $2L/(\gamma v)$, which is smaller.
The situation is not symmetric: the world line of $A$ is a straight line.
The world line of $B$ has a corner at the turnaround, where $B$ changes inertial frames.
Among all timelike world lines between two events, the straight one has the longest proper time; this is the reversed triangle inequality of Minkowski geometry.
At the turnaround the notion of simultaneity of $B$ jumps, which accounts for the missing time in the naive argument.
Einstein derived in 1905 that a clock moved away and back lags behind one that stayed.
Langevin gave the round trip of a traveller in 1911.

## Explanations

### L1 human

One twin stays home and the other flies to a distant star and back at nearly the speed of light.
When they meet again, the traveller has aged less.
It seems paradoxical, because from the traveller's view it is the home twin who was moving.
But only the traveller had to turn around, and this breaks the symmetry.
In a spacetime picture the home twin follows a straight path, and in relativity the straight path between two events is the one with the most elapsed time.
