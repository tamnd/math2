## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$.
Then
$$A \operatorname{adj}(A) = \operatorname{adj}(A) A = \det(A)\, I_n,$$
where $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ is the [[adjugate|0GMPYM0NSR]] of $A$, $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]], and $I_n$ is the [[identity matrix|06DW9BAWB1]].
In terms of cofactors: $\sum_j a_{ij} C_{kj} = \delta_{ik} \det A$ and $\sum_j a_{ji} C_{jk} = \delta_{ik} \det A$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The ring must be commutative, since the proof uses the Laplace expansion.
No other hypothesis is needed: the identity holds for singular matrices and over every commutative ring, because it uses no division.

### Consequences

- If $\det A$ is a unit, then $A^{-1} = (\det A)^{-1} \operatorname{adj} A$; see the [[inverse via the adjugate|0ENVM493VK]].
- If $\det A = 0$, then $A \operatorname{adj} A = 0$, so the columns of $\operatorname{adj} A$ solve $Ax = 0$.
- A one-sided inverse is two-sided: if $AB = I_n$, then $\det A$ is a unit and $B = A^{-1}$.
- [[Cramer's rule|0977KZ6PQB]] is the identity applied to a vector $b$.

### Converse

For invertible $A$ over a field, $\operatorname{adj} A$ is the only matrix $B$ with $AB = (\det A) I_n$.
For singular $A$ this fails: for $A = 0$ with $n \ge 2$, every $B$ satisfies $AB = 0$.

### Generalizations

The identity is the case $k = n - 1$ of the relations among compound matrices.
The Cayley-Hamilton theorem over a commutative ring is often proved by applying the identity to $xI - A$ over $K[x]$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Multiplying a matrix by its adjugate gives the determinant times the identity matrix.
So, when the determinant is not zero, dividing the adjugate by it gives the inverse.
For a singular matrix, the product is the zero matrix.

### L2 human

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, $\operatorname{adj} A = \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $A \operatorname{adj} A = \begin{pmatrix} ad - bc & -ab + ba \\ cd - dc & -cb + da \end{pmatrix} = (ad - bc) I_2$.
2. The same product in the other order gives $(ad - bc) I_2$ as well.

A $3 \times 3$ case, checked entry by entry, is in the [[worked example|BDHT6DBGC9]].
The typical use is a proof over a ring, for example the Cayley-Hamilton theorem.
A trap: the identity uses the transpose of the cofactor matrix; see the [[non-example|2BJC4DHCMK]].

### L3 human

Applied to $tI - A$ over $K[t]$, the identity reads $(tI - A) \operatorname{adj}(tI - A) = \chi_A(t) I$, with the characteristic polynomial $\chi_A$.
Comparing coefficients in this identity of polynomials with matrix coefficients proves $\chi_A(A) = 0$.
