## Statement

A square matrix times its adjugate, in either order, is the determinant times the identity matrix, over every commutative ring.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A = (a_{ij})$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$ with cofactors $C_{ij}$.
Then
$$A \operatorname{adj}(A) = \operatorname{adj}(A) A = \det(A)\, I_n,$$
where $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ is the [[adjugate|0GMPYM0NSR]] of $A$, $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]], and $I_n$ is the [[identity matrix|06DW9BAWB1]].
In terms of cofactors, for all $i$ and $k$,
$$\sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} C_{kj} = \delta_{ik} \det A, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ji} C_{jk} = \delta_{ik} \det A.$$

## Remarks

### A first check

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ the adjugate is $\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$.
Multiply:
$$A \operatorname{adj} A = \begin{pmatrix} ad - bc & -ab + ba \\ cd - dc & -cb + da \end{pmatrix} = (ad - bc) I_2.$$
The entries off the diagonal vanish because $ab = ba$ and $cd = dc$, which is where commutativity enters.
The [[worked example|BDHT6DBGC9]] checks all nine entries for a $3 \times 3$ matrix with determinant $-3$.

### Why it holds

Entry $(i, k)$ of $A \operatorname{adj}(A)$ is $\sum_j a_{ij} C_{kj}$: the entries of row $i$ against the cofactors of row $k$.
When $i = k$, this is the [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]] of $\det A$ along row $i$.
When $i \neq k$, it is the expansion along row $k$ of the matrix that has row $i$ copied into row $k$, and that matrix has two equal rows, so the sum is $0$.
The other product works the same way with columns.
The [[proof|Q47MWN8W57]] writes this out.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Commutativity is the only hypothesis.
The Leibniz formula for the determinant and the cofactors assumes that the order of the factors does not matter, and the $2 \times 2$ check above already uses $ab = ba$.
No invertibility is needed, because the identity involves no division.
For a singular matrix it says that $A \operatorname{adj} A = 0$, and for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ indeed
$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 - 4 & -2 + 2 \\ 8 - 8 & -4 + 4 \end{pmatrix} = 0.$$

### What it gives you

If $\det A$ is a unit, dividing by it gives $A^{-1} = (\det A)^{-1} \operatorname{adj} A$, the [[inverse via the adjugate|0ENVM493VK]].
If $\det A = 0$, then the columns of $\operatorname{adj} A$ are solutions of $Ax = 0$, and over a field this finds the kernel of a matrix of rank $n - 1$.
Applied to a vector $b$, the identity gives $(\det A) x = \operatorname{adj}(A) b$ for every solution of $Ax = b$, which is [[Cramer's rule|0977KZ6PQB]].

### The converse

For invertible $A$ over a field, $\operatorname{adj} A$ is the only matrix $B$ with $AB = (\det A) I_n$, since $B = A^{-1} (\det A) I_n$.
For singular $A$ this uniqueness fails.
For $A = 0$ with $n \ge 2$, every matrix $B$ satisfies $AB = 0 = (\det A) I_n$, while $\operatorname{adj} 0 = 0$.

### Generalizations

Applied to $tI_n - A$ over the polynomial ring $K[t]$, the identity reads
$$(t I_n - A) \operatorname{adj}(t I_n - A) = \chi_A(t) I_n,$$
where $\chi_A$ is the [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]].
This is the starting point of a standard proof of the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]] over any commutative ring.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Multiplying a matrix by its adjugate gives the determinant times the identity matrix.
For $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ the adjugate is $\begin{pmatrix} 6 & -7 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, and the product is $10$ times the identity, since the determinant is $24 - 14 = 10$.
So, when the determinant is not zero, dividing the adjugate by it gives the inverse.
When the determinant is zero, the product is the zero matrix.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: over a commutative ring, a one-sided inverse of a square matrix is automatically two-sided.
Suppose that $AB = I_n$.

1. By the [[product rule for determinants|04CPJ6AF00]], $\det A \det B = \det I_n = 1$, so $\det A$ is a unit.
2. Put $A' = (\det A)^{-1} \operatorname{adj} A$.
   By the identity, $A' A = I_n$.
3. Then $A' = A' (A B) = (A' A) B = B$, so $BA = A' A = I_n$.

This shows what the identity is for: it produces a left inverse from the determinant alone, with no row reduction, and so it works over rings such as $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.
Watch out: the identity needs the transpose of the cofactor matrix, and with the cofactor matrix itself the product is not a multiple of $I_n$, as the [[non-example|2BJC4DHCMK]] shows.

### L3 human

Write $\operatorname{adj}(t I_n - A) = B_0 + B_1 t + \dots + B_{n-1} t^{n-1}$ with matrices $B_k$ over $K$.
Comparing the coefficients of each power of $t$ on both sides of the identity for $t I_n - A$ gives $n + 1$ matrix equations.
Multiplying the equation for $t^k$ by $A^k$ on the left and adding them all makes the left side telescope to $0$, and the right side becomes $\chi_A(A)$.
So $\chi_A(A) = 0$, which is the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, and the proof needs no field.
The adjugate also appears in calculus: over $\mathbb{R}$, [[Jacobi's formula|357FEAZ0Q4]] writes the derivative of $\det$ in terms of the adjugate.
