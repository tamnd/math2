## Definition

An annihilating polynomial of a linear map is a polynomial that gives the zero map when we substitute the map for the variable.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be an endomorphism.
A polynomial $p \in F[x]$ *annihilates* $T$ when $p(T) = 0$ in $\operatorname{End}_F(V)$, where $p(T)$ is the [[value of p at T|0BNEXSKNRV]].
Such a polynomial is an *annihilating polynomial* of $T$, and the set of all of them is written
$$I_T = \{ p \in F[x] : p(T) = 0 \}.$$
For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over $F$, a polynomial $p$ annihilates $A$ when $p(A) = 0$ in $M_n(F)$.

## Notation

Mathlib has no separate name for $I_T$.
It is the kernel `RingHom.ker (Polynomial.aeval T)`, and the condition $p \in I_T$ reads `Polynomial.aeval T p = 0`.

## Remarks

The condition $p(T) = 0$ is an equation between maps, so it must hold on every vector of $V$, not only on some of them.
The zero polynomial annihilates every $T$, so $I_T$ is never empty.
The theorem that [[the ideal of annihilating polynomials is nonzero and principal|AH34SFJ8SF]] shows that $I_T$ is an ideal, and that it contains a nonzero polynomial when $V$ is finite-dimensional.

For example, let $R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the reflection that swaps the coordinates.
Its square is
$$R^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 & 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 \\ 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = I,$$
so $R^2 - I = 0$ and $x^2 - 1$ annihilates $R$.
The polynomial $x - 1$ does not, because $R - I \ne 0$, although $1$ is an eigenvalue of $R$ with the eigenvector $(1, 1)$.
The [[concept page|0PBN8VEKY8]] has more examples and the uses of the idea.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An annihilating polynomial turns into the zero map when you put the map in place of $x$.
A reflection $R$ undoes itself, so $R^2 = I$, and the polynomial $x^2 - 1$ annihilates it.
The polynomial $x - 1$ does not, because $R$ is not the identity.

### L2 human

To test whether $p(x) = x^2 - 3x + 2$ annihilates $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$, evaluate it on each diagonal entry, since powers of a diagonal matrix are taken entry by entry.
This gives $p(1) = 1 - 3 + 2 = 0$ and $p(2) = 4 - 6 + 2 = 0$, so $p(A) = \operatorname{diag}(0, 0) = 0$.
Watch out: for a matrix that is not diagonal you must compute the matrix $p(A)$ itself, because testing a few vectors is not enough.
