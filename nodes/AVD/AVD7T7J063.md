## Statement

Let $L$ be a first-order language with equality, given by a [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]].
Its [[formulas|HYJ10AF6RT]] use $\neg$, $\to$ and $\forall$; the other connectives and $\exists$ are abbreviations.
$\alpha^x_t$ is the [[substitution|NCDZ969V7X]] of $t$ for the free occurrences of $x$ in $\alpha$.
The Hilbert system $H$ has one rule, [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] (MP).
Its logical axioms are the generalizations $\forall y_1 \cdots \forall y_k \, \lambda$ ($k \ge 0$) of the following formulas $\lambda$.

1. Tautologies, with the atomic formulas and the formulas $\forall x \, \gamma$ as prime parts.
2. $\forall x \, \alpha \to \alpha^x_t$, where $t$ is substitutable for $x$ in $\alpha$: no variable of $t$ is captured.
3. $\forall x \, (\alpha \to \beta) \to (\forall x \, \alpha \to \forall x \, \beta)$.
4. $\alpha \to \forall x \, \alpha$, where $x$ is not free in $\alpha$.
5. $x = x$.
6. $x = y \to (\alpha \to \alpha')$, where $\alpha$ is atomic and $\alpha'$ replaces some occurrences of $x$ in $\alpha$ by $y$.

$\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ means that a finite list ends with $\varphi$, and each entry is a logical axiom, a member of $\Gamma$, or follows from earlier entries by MP.
$\Gamma \models \varphi$ is [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]]: each [[structure|983GZBQYT4]] $\mathcal{A}$ and assignment $s$ that [[satisfy|2RBQPV02AS]] all of $\Gamma$ also satisfy $\varphi$.

**Theorem (soundness).** For every set $\Gamma$ of formulas of $L$ and every formula $\varphi$ of $L$:

1. If $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, then $\Gamma \models \varphi$.
2. If $\vdash \varphi$, then $\varphi$ is valid: true in every structure under every assignment.
3. Every satisfiable set $\Gamma$ is [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]]: no $\psi$ has $\Gamma \vdash \psi$ and $\Gamma \vdash \neg \psi$.

The proof is [[the proof by induction on derivations|Q2F1N068BV]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The proof uses two facts: every logical axiom is valid, and MP sends true formulas to a true formula.
The side conditions in groups 2 and 4 are what make the axioms valid.

- Group 2 without the condition on $t$: take $\alpha = \exists y \, \neg (x = y)$ and $t = y$. The formula $\forall x \, \exists y \, \neg(x = y) \to \exists y \, \neg (y = y)$ has a true hypothesis in every structure with two elements and a false conclusion in every structure.
- Group 4 without the condition on $x$: the formula $x = c \to \forall x \, (x = c)$ is false in the structure $\set{0, 1}$ with $c = 0$, under an assignment with $s(x) = 0$.
- A rule of generalization without a restriction: many books have the rule "from $\varphi$ infer $\forall x \, \varphi$". With it, $x = c \vdash \forall x \, (x = c)$, but $x = c \not\models \forall x \, (x = c)$ by the structure of the previous item. Books with this rule either forbid its use on variables free in $\Gamma$, or take $\Gamma$ to be a set of sentences. The system $H$ has no such rule; the axioms are closed under generalization instead.

### Consequences

- To show $\Gamma \not\vdash \varphi$, it is enough to give one structure and one assignment that satisfy $\Gamma$ and not $\varphi$.
- For example, the group axioms do not derive $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x \cdot y = y \cdot x)$. The symmetric group $S_3$ satisfies the group axioms, and the transpositions $(1\,2)$ and $(1\,3)$ do not commute.
- First-order logic is consistent: the formula $\neg (x = x)$ is not derivable, because it is false in every structure.
- A theory that has a model is consistent. This is the usual way to prove a relative [[consistency|VM8TQ70CVJ]] result.

### Converse

The converse is [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]]: if $\Gamma \models \varphi$, then $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$.
It is true, and its proof is much harder than the proof of soundness.
Together the two theorems give $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ if and only if $\Gamma \models \varphi$.

### Generalizations

- The same theorem holds for the other usual proof systems of first-order logic: other Hilbert systems, natural deduction, the [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] and tableaux. Each proof is an induction on derivations.
- The [[soundness theorem for propositional logic|XD629S54CS]] is the special case without quantifiers, and [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]] is the general notion for any logic with a proof system and a semantics.
- For a proof system of a modal logic, soundness for a class of Kripke frames is proved in the same way: each axiom is valid in the class, and each rule keeps validity.

## History

The axiom system $H$ in this form is the one of Herbert Enderton's textbook "A Mathematical Introduction to Logic" (first edition 1972).
It has all tautologies as axioms and modus ponens as its only rule.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A proof system is a set of rules for writing proofs on paper.
Soundness says that these rules never lead from true statements to a false one.
If the starting statements are true in some situation, every statement that we prove from them is true in that situation too.
For example, from "every number in this list is even" and "$6$ is in this list", the rules give "$6$ is even", and this is true whenever the two starting statements are true.
So a proof is a safe way to find new truths.

### L2 human

We show that the group axioms do not derive $\exists x \, \neg (x = e)$, where $e$ is the identity symbol.

1. Let $\Gamma$ be the group axioms in the language with $\cdot$, ${}^{-1}$ and $e$.
2. The trivial group $\set{e}$ is a structure for this language, and it satisfies every member of $\Gamma$.
3. In this structure every element is $e$, so $\exists x \, \neg (x = e)$ is false.
4. If $\Gamma \vdash \exists x \, \neg (x = e)$, then soundness would give $\Gamma \models \exists x \, \neg(x = e)$, and the formula would be true in $\set{e}$.
5. Steps 3 and 4 contradict each other, so $\Gamma \not\vdash \exists x \, \neg(x = e)$.

The typical use is this one: a single model shows that a formula is not derivable.
The trap is to forget the side conditions of groups 2 and 4.
A careless substitution, as in the first item of "Why each hypothesis is needed", gives an unsound axiom.

### L3 human

Soundness is a statement about a single induction: the axioms lie in the set of valid formulas, and this set is closed under the rules.
The same pattern proves soundness for every logic with a compositional semantics, for example intuitionistic logic for Kripke models and modal logics for frames.
In categorical logic, soundness says that the interpretation of formulas as subobjects in a suitable category respects the rules.
The hard direction is the converse, the [[completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]], which needs the [[Henkin construction|RY8KRKR097]] or another way to build models from syntax.
