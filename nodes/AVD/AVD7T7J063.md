## Statement

Soundness says that a formal proof never leads from true hypotheses to a false conclusion, so everything we derive is a logical consequence.

Let $L$ be a first-order language with equality, given by a [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]].
Its [[formulas|HYJ10AF6RT]] use $\neg$, $\to$ and $\forall$, and the other connectives and $\exists$ are abbreviations.
We write $\alpha^x_t$ for the [[substitution|NCDZ969V7X]] of the term $t$ for the free occurrences of $x$ in $\alpha$.
The [[Hilbert-style system|N95HV0VWSM]] $H$ has one rule, [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] (MP).
Its logical axioms are the generalizations $\forall y_1 \cdots \forall y_k \, \lambda$, with $k \ge 0$, of the formulas $\lambda$ in these six groups.

1. Tautologies, with the atomic formulas and the formulas $\forall x \, \gamma$ as prime parts.
2. $\forall x \, \alpha \to \alpha^x_t$, where $t$ is substitutable for $x$ in $\alpha$, that is, no variable of $t$ is captured by a quantifier of $\alpha$.
3. $\forall x \, (\alpha \to \beta) \to (\forall x \, \alpha \to \forall x \, \beta)$.
4. $\alpha \to \forall x \, \alpha$, where $x$ is not free in $\alpha$.
5. $x = x$.
6. $x = y \to (\alpha \to \alpha')$, where $\alpha$ is atomic and $\alpha'$ comes from $\alpha$ by replacing some occurrences of $x$ by $y$.

We write $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ when there is a finite list that ends with $\varphi$ in which each entry is a logical axiom, a member of $\Gamma$, or follows from two earlier entries by MP.
We write $\Gamma \models \varphi$ for [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]]: every [[structure|983GZBQYT4]] $\mathcal{A}$ and assignment $s$ that [[satisfy|2RBQPV02AS]] all members of $\Gamma$ also satisfy $\varphi$.

**Theorem (soundness).** For every set $\Gamma$ of formulas of $L$ and every formula $\varphi$ of $L$, the following hold.

1. If $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, then $\Gamma \models \varphi$.
2. If $\vdash \varphi$, then $\varphi$ is valid, that is, true in every structure under every assignment.
3. Every satisfiable set $\Gamma$ is [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]]: there is no $\psi$ with $\Gamma \vdash \psi$ and $\Gamma \vdash \neg \psi$.

The [[proof by induction on derivations|Q2F1N068BV]] checks that each axiom is valid and that MP keeps truth.

## Remarks

### A first use: commutativity is not a theorem of group theory

Can the group axioms derive the law $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x \cdot y = y \cdot x)$?
Soundness answers this with one structure.
The symmetric group $S_3$ satisfies the group axioms, and in it the transpositions $(1\,2)$ and $(1\,3)$ do not commute.
Compose them both ways:
$$(1\,2)(1\,3) = (1\,3\,2), \qquad (1\,3)(1\,2) = (1\,2\,3).$$
Check: the first product, applied right to left, sends $1 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 3$, so it sends $1$ to $3$, while the second sends $1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 2$.
So the law is false in a model of the axioms, and part 1 of the theorem shows that no derivation of it exists.

This is the typical use of soundness.
To show $\Gamma \not\vdash \varphi$, we do not search through all derivations, which is impossible.
We give one structure and one assignment that satisfy $\Gamma$ and not $\varphi$.

### Why the side conditions are needed

The proof rests on two facts: every logical axiom is valid, and MP takes true formulas to a true formula.
The side conditions of groups 2 and 4 are exactly what makes those axioms valid.

Drop the condition of group 2, and take $\alpha = \exists y \, \neg (x = y)$ with $t = y$.
The instance becomes $\forall x \, \exists y \, \neg(x = y) \to \exists y \, \neg (y = y)$.
Its hypothesis is true in every structure with at least two elements, and its conclusion is false in every structure.
The variable $y$ of $t$ was captured by $\exists y$, and the meaning changed.

Drop the condition of group 4, and take the formula $x = c \to \forall x \, (x = c)$.
In the structure with universe $\{ 0, 1 \}$ and $c = 0$, under an assignment with $s(x) = 0$, the hypothesis is true and the conclusion is false.

### Watch out: the rule of generalization

Many books have the rule "from $\varphi$ infer $\forall x \, \varphi$".
Used without a restriction, it gives $x = c \vdash \forall x \, (x = c)$, and the structure above shows $x = c \not\models \forall x \, (x = c)$.
So such books either forbid generalization on a variable that is free in a hypothesis, or allow only sentences in $\Gamma$.
The system $H$ has no rule of generalization; its axioms are closed under $\forall$ instead, and the [[generalization theorem|VKSK35Y52Z]] recovers the rule in the safe form.

### What it gives you

First-order logic itself is consistent: $\neg (x = x)$ has no derivation, because it is false in every structure.
More generally, a theory with a model is consistent, by part 3.
This is the standard way to prove that an axiom system is consistent, and also the standard way to prove that an axiom is independent of the others: find a model of the others in which it fails.

### The converse

The converse of part 1 is [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]]: if $\Gamma \models \varphi$, then $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$.
It is true, and its proof is much harder, because it has to build a model out of syntax.
Together the two theorems give $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ if and only if $\Gamma \models \varphi$.
In the same way, the converse of part 3, "every consistent set is satisfiable", is the [[model existence theorem|G0DTR159RN]].

### Generalizations

The same theorem holds, with the same kind of proof, for the other usual proof systems of first-order logic: other Hilbert systems, [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]], the [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] and [[semantic tableaux|WGWBBVE8GX]].
The [[soundness theorem for propositional logic|XD629S54CS]] is the special case without quantifiers, and [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]] is the general notion for any logic with a proof system and a semantics.
For a modal logic and a class of Kripke frames the plan is again the same: each axiom is valid on the class, and each rule keeps validity.

### Try it

First, show that the group axioms do not derive $\exists x \, \neg (x = e)$, where $e$ is the identity symbol.
The trivial group $\{ e \}$ is a model of the axioms in which every element equals $e$, so the sentence is false there, and soundness rules out a derivation.

Second, show that the axioms of a linear order do not derive "there is a least element".
The integers with their usual order satisfy the axioms and have no least element.

## History

The axiom system $H$ in this form is the one of Herbert Enderton's textbook "A Mathematical Introduction to Logic", first published in 1972.
It takes all tautologies as axioms and has modus ponens as its only rule.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A proof system is a set of rules for writing proofs on paper.
Soundness says that these rules never lead from true statements to a false one.
If the starting statements are true in some situation, every statement that we prove from them is true in that situation too.
For example, from "every number in this list is even" and "$6$ is in this list", the rules give "$6$ is even", and this is true whenever the two starting statements are true.
So a proof is a safe way to find new truths, and a single situation where the conclusion fails shows that no proof exists.

### L2 human

We show that the group axioms do not derive $\exists x \, \neg (x = e)$.
Let $\Gamma$ be the group axioms in the language with $\cdot$, ${}^{-1}$ and $e$.
The trivial group $\{ e \}$ is a structure for this language, and it satisfies every member of $\Gamma$.
In this structure every element is $e$, so $\exists x \, \neg (x = e)$ is false.
If $\Gamma \vdash \exists x \, \neg (x = e)$ held, soundness would make the formula true in $\{ e \}$, which it is not.
So $\Gamma \not\vdash \exists x \, \neg(x = e)$.

The typical use is always this one: to prove that a formula is not derivable, exhibit a model of the hypotheses where it fails.
Watch out: a model shows that something is not derivable, but it cannot show that something is derivable.
For that you need a derivation, or the completeness theorem.

### L3 human

Soundness is a statement about one induction: the axioms lie in the set of valid formulas, and this set is closed under the rules.
The same pattern proves soundness for every logic with a compositional semantics, for example intuitionistic logic for Kripke models and modal logics for frames.
In categorical logic, soundness says that the interpretation of formulas as subobjects in a suitable category respects the rules.
The hard direction is the converse, the [[completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]], which needs the [[Henkin construction|RY8KRKR097]] or another way to build models from syntax.
