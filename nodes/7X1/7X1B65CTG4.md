## Statement

Let $F$ be a field and let $T$ be an $n \times n$ [[triangular matrix|GTG8MJGDKC]] over $F$.

1. $T$ is an [[invertible matrix|0EB9N0S33C]] if and only if every entry on its main diagonal is nonzero.
2. If $T$ is [[upper triangular|0N4XY7Z3NN]] and invertible, then $T^{-1}$ is upper triangular, and its diagonal entries are $t_{11}^{-1}, \dots, t_{nn}^{-1}$.
3. If $T$ is [[lower triangular|00J3KZP8Y0]] and invertible, then $T^{-1}$ is lower triangular, with the same diagonal entries $t_{ii}^{-1}$.
4. The invertible upper triangular $n \times n$ matrices form a subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$. So do the invertible lower triangular matrices, the unit upper triangular matrices (diagonal entries $1$) and the unit lower triangular matrices.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The matrix must be triangular for part 1.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has zero diagonal entries and is invertible.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has nonzero diagonal entries and is singular.

### Consequences

- The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries, by the [[triangular determinant theorem|0K1KKMFHYH]]. Part 1 agrees with the determinant criterion.
- A triangular system $Tx = b$ with nonzero diagonal is solved by back substitution, with no row exchanges.
- The factors $L$ and $U$ of an [[LU decomposition|0RKG5YW66Y]] can be inverted inside their own classes.

### Converse

Part 1 is an equivalence.
Part 2 has a converse: if $T$ is invertible and $T^{-1}$ is upper triangular, then $T = (T^{-1})^{-1}$ is upper triangular, by part 2 applied to $T^{-1}$.

### Generalizations

Parts 2 to 4 hold over each commutative ring, with "nonzero" replaced by "a unit".
The same holds for block triangular matrices with invertible square diagonal blocks.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A triangular matrix has zeros on one side of its diagonal.
It has an inverse exactly when no diagonal entry is zero.
The inverse is triangular of the same kind.

### L2 human

Worked example: let $T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, a unit upper triangular matrix.

1. Row reduce $[T \mid I_3]$. $R_2 \to R_2 - 4R_3$ gives row 2 equal to $(0, 1, 0 \mid 0, 1, -4)$.
2. $R_1 \to R_1 - 3R_3$ gives row 1 equal to $(1, 2, 0 \mid 1, 0, -3)$.
3. $R_1 \to R_1 - 2R_2$ gives row 1 equal to $(1, 0, 0 \mid 1, -2, 5)$.
4. So $T^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & -4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, again unit upper triangular.
5. Check row 1 of $TT^{-1}$: $(1, 2, 3)$ gives $1$, $-2 + 2 = 0$ and $5 - 8 + 3 = 0$. Row 2: $(0, 1, 4)$ gives $0$, $1$ and $-4 + 4 = 0$. Row 3 is $(0, 0, 1)$.

A trap: the entry $5$ of $T^{-1}$ is not $-3$.
The inverse of a triangular matrix is not found by changing signs above the diagonal, except for $2 \times 2$ matrices.

### L3 human

Part 4 says that the invertible upper triangular matrices form a subgroup $B$ of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$, the standard Borel subgroup.
The unit upper triangular matrices form a normal subgroup $U$ of $B$.
The quotient $B/U$ is isomorphic to the group of invertible diagonal matrices, by the map that keeps the diagonal.
The same results hold over each commutative ring when "nonzero" is replaced by "a unit", with the same proof by back substitution.
