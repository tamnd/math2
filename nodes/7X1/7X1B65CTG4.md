## Statement

A triangular matrix is invertible exactly when its diagonal has no zeros, and then its inverse is triangular of the same kind.
Let $F$ be a field and let $T$ be an $n \times n$ [[triangular matrix|GTG8MJGDKC]] over $F$.

1. $T$ is an [[invertible matrix|0EB9N0S33C]] if and only if every entry on its main diagonal is nonzero.
2. If $T$ is [[upper triangular|0N4XY7Z3NN]] and invertible, then $T^{-1}$ is upper triangular, and its diagonal entries are $t_{11}^{-1}, \dots, t_{nn}^{-1}$.
3. If $T$ is [[lower triangular|00J3KZP8Y0]] and invertible, then $T^{-1}$ is lower triangular, with diagonal entries $t_{11}^{-1}, \dots, t_{nn}^{-1}$.
4. The invertible upper triangular $n \times n$ matrices form a subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$. So do the invertible lower triangular matrices, the unit upper triangular matrices, whose diagonal entries are all $1$, and the unit lower triangular matrices.

## Remarks

### The statement at work

Take the unit upper triangular matrix $T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Its diagonal is $1, 1, 1$, so part 1 says it is invertible.
Reducing $[T \mid I_3]$ needs only the clearing above the pivots: $R_2 \to R_2 - 4R_3$, then $R_1 \to R_1 - 3R_3$, then $R_1 \to R_1 - 2R_2$.
The result is
$$T^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & -4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
again unit upper triangular, as part 2 predicts.
Check the last column of $TT^{-1}$: $5 - 8 + 3 = 0$, $-4 + 4 = 0$ and $1$.

### Watch out

The entry $5$ in the corner of $T^{-1}$ is not $-3$.
Changing the signs above the diagonal inverts a unit triangular $2 \times 2$ matrix, but not a larger one in general, because the corner entry of the inverse collects a product of two off-diagonal entries: here $5 = -3 + 2 \cdot 4$.
For a triangular matrix whose diagonal is not all $1$, even the $2 \times 2$ case needs more:
$$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & d \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} a^{-1} & -b\,a^{-1}d^{-1} \\ 0 & d^{-1} \end{pmatrix}.$$

### Why triangularity is needed

For a general matrix, the diagonal says nothing about invertibility.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has zeros on its diagonal and is invertible, and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has no zeros on its diagonal and is singular.

### What it gives you

The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries, by the [[triangular determinant theorem|0K1KKMFHYH]], so part 1 agrees with the determinant criterion.
A triangular system $Tx = b$ with nonzero diagonal is solved by back substitution, with no row exchanges.
The factors $L$ and $U$ of an [[LU decomposition|0RKG5YW66Y]] can be inverted, and multiplied, without leaving their classes, by part 4.

### The converse and generalizations

Part 1 is already an equivalence.
Part 2 has a converse: if $T$ is invertible and $T^{-1}$ is upper triangular, then $T = (T^{-1})^{-1}$ is upper triangular, by part 2 applied to $T^{-1}$.
Over a commutative ring, all four parts hold with "nonzero" replaced by "a unit"; for part 1, use that the determinant $t_{11} \cdots t_{nn}$ is a unit exactly when each factor is.
A block triangular matrix with square diagonal blocks is invertible over a field exactly when each diagonal block is invertible.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A triangular matrix has zeros on one side of its diagonal.
It has an inverse exactly when no diagonal entry is zero.
The inverse is triangular of the same kind, and its diagonal entries are the reciprocals of the old ones.

### L2 human

To invert a triangular matrix, use back substitution, column by column, or reduce $[T \mid I_n]$ starting from the bottom row.
Before you start, look at the diagonal: one zero means there is no inverse.
A useful check: the diagonal of $T^{-1}$ must be $t_{11}^{-1}, \dots, t_{nn}^{-1}$, so an error there shows at once.

### L3 human

Part 4 says that the invertible upper triangular matrices form a subgroup $B$ of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$, the standard Borel subgroup.
The unit upper triangular matrices form a normal subgroup $U$ of $B$.
The quotient $B/U$ is isomorphic to the group of invertible diagonal matrices, by the map that keeps the diagonal.
