## Definition

Resolution is the proof system that shows a CNF to be unsatisfiable by deriving the empty clause with one rule.
For example, the clauses $\{a\}$, $\{\neg a, b\}$, $\{\neg b, c\}$ and $\{\neg b, \neg c\}$ are refuted in three steps:
$$\{\neg b, c\}, \{\neg b, \neg c\} \vdash \{\neg b\}, \qquad \{\neg b\}, \{\neg a, b\} \vdash \{\neg a\}, \qquad \{\neg a\}, \{a\} \vdash \square.$$

Let $F$ be a finite set of [[clauses|T3SXVH3ZZC]], that is, a [[CNF|0B3D4C8YF2]].
A *resolution derivation* of a clause $C$ from $F$ is a sequence of clauses $\pi = (C_1, \dots, C_s)$ with $C_s = C$.
Each $C_i$ is a clause of $F$, an *axiom*, or it follows from two earlier clauses $C_j, C_k$ with $j, k < i$ by the [[resolution rule|ZDW6N1PGJ7]]:
$$\frac{A \cup \{x\} \qquad B \cup \{\neg x\}}{A \cup B}.$$
The variable $x$ is the *pivot* of the step.
A *resolution refutation* of $F$ is a derivation of the empty clause $\square$ from $F$.

The *graph* of $\pi$ has an edge from $j$ to $i$ when $C_j$ is a premise of the step that gives $C_i$.

1. $\pi$ is *tree-like* if every clause other than $C_s$ is a premise of at most one step.
   An axiom that is needed twice is written twice, so the graph is a tree.
2. $\pi$ is *dag-like*, or *general*, without this condition.
3. $\pi$ is *regular* if on every path of the graph from $C_s$ back to an axiom, no variable is the pivot of two steps.

The *size* $S(\pi) = s$ is the number of clauses.
The *width* of a clause is $w(C) = \lvert C \rvert$, the width of $\pi$ is $w(\pi) = \max_i w(C_i)$, and $w(F)$ is the largest width of a clause of $F$.
We write $S(F \vdash \square)$, $S_T(F \vdash \square)$ and $w(F \vdash \square)$ for the least size of a refutation, the least size of a tree-like refutation, and the least width of a refutation of $F$.

## Notation

Many books write $F \vdash_{\mathrm{Res}} \square$, and $\mathrm{Res}$, $\mathrm{Res}_T$ and $\mathrm{RegRes}$ for general, tree-like and regular resolution.
Some books measure size by the number of literals, which is at most $2n$ times the number of clauses when there are $n$ variables.
Some books add the *weakening* rule "from $C$ infer $C \cup D$".
It can be removed without an increase of size or width, so the measures do not change.
In the sub-area on proof complexity, the size of a resolution refutation always means its number of clauses.

## Remarks

### Reading the first refutation

The refutation in the definition has $4$ axioms and $3$ derived clauses, so its size is $7$.
Its widest clauses have $2$ literals, so its width is $2$.
Each derived clause is used once, so it is tree-like, and the pivots along every path are $c$, then $b$, then $a$, so it is regular.

No refutation of these four clauses is smaller.
Each set of three of them is satisfiable: drop $\{a\}$ and take $a = b = 0$, drop $\{\neg a, b\}$ and take $a = 1, b = 0$, drop $\{\neg b, c\}$ and take $a = b = 1, c = 0$, or drop $\{\neg b, \neg c\}$ and take $a = b = c = 1$.
So a refutation uses all four axioms.
Each step joins the parts of the graph that its two premises belong to, so it lowers the number of parts by at most one, and at least $3$ steps are needed.
Check: $4 + 3 = 7$, which is the size we found.

### DPLL runs and CDCL runs

A run of the [[DPLL algorithm|K7R9D390SP]] on an unsatisfiable CNF is a tree-like resolution refutation in disguise.
Label each leaf of the search tree with a clause that the leaf falsifies, and label each branching node with the resolvent of its two children on the branching variable.
[[A tree-like refutation from a DPLL run|889KFFK1N3]] does this for a formula with four clauses and checks every label.
So the number of nodes of a DPLL search tree is at least $S_T(F \vdash \square)$, and lower bounds for tree-like resolution are lower bounds on the running time of DPLL.

A conflict-driven clause-learning (CDCL) solver learns clauses and uses them again, so its runs give dag-like refutations.
Each learned clause follows from the current clauses by a chain of resolution steps.
Conversely, Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche, and Atserias, Fichte and Thurley, proved in 2011 that CDCL with restarts p-simulates general resolution.
So lower bounds for general resolution bound the running time of CDCL solvers on unsatisfiable inputs; see [[SAT and SMT solving|3BW08M1EX7]].

### What is not a resolution refutation

[[An extended resolution refutation|DP7RSJETHC]] adds clauses that define a new variable $z \leftrightarrow (a \land b)$.
These clauses are neither axioms nor resolvents, so the sequence fails the definition, although it is a sound refutation in a stronger system.
[[Resolving two variables at once|C7ZTS8N0VB]] fails in a different way: from $\{x, y\}$ and $\{\neg x, \neg y\}$ it would give $\square$, but $x = 1, y = 0$ satisfies both clauses.
So the rule removes exactly one complementary pair.

### Three refinements

Every tree-like refutation is dag-like, and a tree-like refutation of least size is regular.
Exponential separations are known at both steps.
Bonet, Esteban, Galesi and Johannsen separated tree-like from regular resolution in 2000, and Alekhnovich, Johannsen, Pitassi and Urquhart separated regular from general resolution in 2002.
The Davis-Putnam procedure removes one variable at a time, as in [[a run of the Davis-Putnam procedure|20PVEKMM24]], and the refutation it gives is regular.
All three refinements keep their size under restrictions: a refutation of $F$ and a partial assignment $\rho$ give a refutation of $F|_\rho$ that is no larger, by the [[restriction lemma|7RX0Z6S1KZ]].

### Lower bounds for general resolution

Exponential lower bounds on $S(F \vdash \square)$ in general resolution are known for three families.
[[Haken's theorem|4PCEYEW3JG]] covers the [[pigeonhole formulas|RJ571F7FF0]], [[Urquhart's theorem|HF1GHVRDSD]] covers the [[Tseitin formulas|JXTCC6KXNG]] of expander graphs, and Chvátal and Szemerédi covered random $3$-CNFs with $\Delta n$ clauses for a large constant $\Delta$.
The [[size-width relation|GWDGRPAQSM]] of Ben-Sasson and Wigderson reduces all three to lower bounds on width.
Two further facts concern proof search and circuits.
Resolution is not automatable unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$, as explained under [[automatizability|WBECDG163W]], and it has [[feasible interpolation|59GJCM8C66]], even by monotone circuits.
Resolution is sound and complete for unsatisfiable CNFs, by the [[soundness and completeness of resolution|DH1P95R390]], and checking a refutation takes polynomial time, so it is a [[propositional proof system|3V24CYWDT6]] in the sense of Cook and Reckhow.

### Watch out

Size in clauses and size in literals differ by a factor of at most $2n$, so the choice does not matter for exponential bounds, but it matters for exact counts.
A lower bound for resolution says nothing about a stronger system.
The pigeonhole formulas need exponential size in resolution, and they have polynomial-size refutations in [[cutting planes|KKCX2HQP2K]].
In a tree-like refutation, a derived clause that is used twice must be derived twice, and the copies count in the size.

### History

Davis and Putnam used resolution on clauses in 1960, and Robinson made resolution the basis of automated theorem proving in 1965.
Tseitin proved the first lower bounds, for regular resolution, in 1968.
Haken proved the first exponential lower bound for general resolution in 1985.

### Try it

Refute $\{p, q\}$, $\{\neg p, q\}$, $\{\neg q\}$ and give the size and the width.
Resolve the first two clauses on $p$ to get $\{q\}$, then resolve $\{q\}$ with $\{\neg q\}$ to get $\square$; the size is $5$ and the width is $2$.

Why does every refutation of the four clauses of the definition have width at least $2$?
It must use all four clauses, as shown above, and three of them have two literals.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Resolution is the proof system behind modern SAT solvers.
It shows that a list of clauses has no solution by combining two clauses into a new one, step by step, until it reaches the empty clause.
From "$p$ or $q$" and "not $p$ or $q$" it gets "$q$", since $q$ holds in both cases for $p$.
Proof complexity asks how many such steps a proof needs.
For some families of lists, the least number of steps grows exponentially with the length of the list.

### L2 human

The order of the steps can change the tree-like size, so we refute the four clauses of the definition in the forward order.
Resolve $\{a\}$ with $\{\neg a, b\}$ to get $\{b\}$.
Then resolve $\{b\}$ with $\{\neg b, c\}$ to get $\{c\}$, and $\{b\}$ with $\{\neg b, \neg c\}$ to get $\{\neg c\}$.
Last, resolve $\{c\}$ with $\{\neg c\}$ to get $\square$.
Written as a sequence with $\{b\}$ used twice, this refutation is dag-like:
$$\{a\}, \ \{\neg a, b\}, \ \{b\}, \ \{\neg b, c\}, \ \{c\}, \ \{\neg b, \neg c\}, \ \{\neg c\}, \ \square.$$
Its size is $8$.
To make it tree-like we must derive $\{b\}$ a second time, which adds $\{a\}$, $\{\neg a, b\}$ and $\{b\}$ again, so the size becomes $11$.
The backward order of the definition is tree-like with size $7$, so the tree-like cost of $11$ came from a bad order, not from the formula.
This is the typical use of the measures: fix the refinement and the measure first, and only then compare refutations.

### L3 human

Resolution is the weakest of the standard proof systems, and the one whose lower bounds are best understood.
Its lines are clauses, so it is the lowest level of [[bounded-depth Frege|41QJ1WEMJ3]], in the form of the [[sequent calculus with atomic cuts|BJ4T6B8Q2M]].
Lower bounds for tree-like resolution are often proved by games on partial assignments, such as the Prover-Delayer game of Pudlák and Impagliazzo.
The [[algebraic proof systems|ZERBQSTZB5]] and [[cutting planes|KKCX2HQP2K]] extend resolution in two directions, by polynomials and by linear inequalities.
Cutting planes p-simulates resolution, and the polynomial calculus simulates it with degree in the role of width.
