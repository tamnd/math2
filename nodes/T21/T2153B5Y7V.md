## Proof

We check that the given $S$ and $K$ work, and then show that any decomposition must use them; let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over a commutative ring $R$ in which $2$ is invertible.

1. Put $S = \tfrac12 (A + A^{\mathsf{T}})$ and $K = \tfrac12 (A - A^{\mathsf{T}})$, which make sense because $\tfrac12 \in R$.
2. The [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]] is additive, commutes with scalars and satisfies $(A^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}} = A$, so $S^{\mathsf{T}} = \tfrac12 (A^{\mathsf{T}} + A) = S$, and $S$ is [[symmetric|0N28QN4CYA]].
3. By the same rules, $K^{\mathsf{T}} = \tfrac12 (A^{\mathsf{T}} - A) = -K$, so $K$ is [[skew-symmetric|03RNVFDEAN]].
4. Adding, $S + K = \tfrac12 A + \tfrac12 A^{\mathsf{T}} + \tfrac12 A - \tfrac12 A^{\mathsf{T}} = A$, which proves existence.
5. For uniqueness, suppose that $A = S' + K'$ with $S'$ symmetric and $K'$ skew-symmetric.
6. Transposing this equation gives $A^{\mathsf{T}} = S'^{\mathsf{T}} + K'^{\mathsf{T}} = S' - K'$.
7. Adding the equations of steps 5 and 6 gives $A + A^{\mathsf{T}} = 2S'$, and since $2$ is invertible, $S' = \tfrac12 (A + A^{\mathsf{T}}) = S$.
8. Subtracting them gives $A - A^{\mathsf{T}} = 2K'$, so $K' = \tfrac12 (A - A^{\mathsf{T}}) = K$.
9. So the decomposition exists by step 4 and is unique by steps 7 and 8.

## Idea

Transposing $A = S + K$ gives $A^{\mathsf{T}} = S - K$.
These are two linear equations in the unknowns $S$ and $K$, and adding and subtracting them solves for both.
The solution divides by $2$, which is where the hypothesis on $R$ enters.

## Variants

Uniqueness can also be proved from the fact that only $0$ is both symmetric and skew-symmetric.
If $A = S + K = S' + K'$, then $M = S - S' = K' - K$ is symmetric and skew-symmetric, so $M = M^{\mathsf{T}} = -M$ and $2M = 0$.
This gives $M = 0$ whenever $2$ is not a zero divisor, a weaker hypothesis than invertibility.
