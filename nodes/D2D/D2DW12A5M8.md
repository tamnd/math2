## Example

Let $D \ge 0$ be an integer.
We prove that every finite simple [[graph|0JD5C82QMB]] $G$ in which each vertex has [[degree|0T0F6CCS9W]] at most $D$ has a proper [[coloring|0874138YP6]] with the colors $1, 2, \ldots, D + 1$.
A proper coloring gives each vertex a color so that the two ends of each edge get different colors.
The proof is a [[minimal counterexample|JGRCNPKNGG]] argument, and the size of a graph is its number of vertices.

1. Suppose that the claim is false.
   Among all counterexamples, choose a graph $G$ with the least number $n$ of vertices.
2. The graph with no vertices has a proper coloring, the empty one.
   So $n \ge 1$, and $G$ has a vertex $v$.
3. Let $G - v$ be the graph that we get from $G$ when we delete $v$ and the edges at $v$.
   It has $n - 1$ vertices.
4. Deleting a vertex does not raise the degree of any other vertex.
   So each vertex of $G - v$ has degree at most $D$, and $G - v$ satisfies the hypothesis of the claim.
5. The graph $G - v$ has fewer vertices than $G$, so by the choice of $G$ it is not a counterexample.
   So $G - v$ has a proper coloring $c$ with the colors $1, \ldots, D + 1$.
6. The vertex $v$ has at most $D$ neighbors, and they use at most $D$ of the $D + 1$ colors.
   So some color $k$ is used by no neighbor of $v$.
7. Give $v$ the color $k$, and keep the color $c(u)$ of every other vertex $u$.
8. An edge of $G - v$ has ends of different colors by step 5.
   An edge $vu$ has ends of different colors by step 6.
   So this is a proper coloring of $G$ with $D + 1$ colors.
9. This contradicts the choice of $G$ as a counterexample, so the claim is true.

## Remarks

Step 4 is the check that the smaller graph lies in the class of the claim.
Without it, step 5 could not use the minimality of $G$.
If the proof had added an edge or merged two vertices, the degrees could rise above $D$, and the argument would break at this point.

The bound $D + 1$ cannot be lowered in general.
The complete graph on $D + 1$ vertices has maximum degree $D$, and its vertices are pairwise adjacent, so they need $D + 1$ different colors.
For $D = 2$, the cycle on $5$ vertices also needs $3$ colors, because $2$ colors would have to alternate around a cycle of odd length.
[[Brooks' theorem|0Y4TV2D5RQ]] says that these are the only connected exceptions: a connected graph that is not complete and not an odd cycle has a coloring with $D$ colors.

The proof is also an algorithm.
Delete the vertices one by one, then put them back in the reverse order and give each the first free color.
This is the [[greedy coloring|04AAKZNXHD]], and the argument above shows that it never needs more than $D + 1$ colors.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Imagine a party where each guest knows at most $3$ other guests.
We want to give each guest one of $4$ hats so that two guests who know each other never wear the same hat.
Send one guest out of the room, and suppose that the others already wear good hats.
The guest who comes back knows at most $3$ people, so at most $3$ hats are taken among them.
One of the $4$ hats is free, and the guest takes it.

### L2 human

We follow the proof on the cycle $C_5$ with vertices $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ and edges $12, 23, 34, 45, 51$.
Here $D = 2$, so we may use the colors $1$, $2$ and $3$.
Delete vertex $5$, and the path $1, 2, 3, 4$ remains.
Color the path by alternating: vertices $1$ and $3$ get color $1$, and vertices $2$ and $4$ get color $2$.
Vertex $5$ has the neighbors $4$ and $1$, with the colors $2$ and $1$, so color $3$ is free and vertex $5$ takes it.

Check: the five edges $12, 23, 34, 45, 51$ join the colors $1$ and $2$, $2$ and $1$, $1$ and $2$, $2$ and $3$, $3$ and $1$, all different.
The typical use of the method in graph theory is to delete a vertex or an edge, use the smaller graph, and repair the coloring.
Watch out: in proofs that need a vertex of small degree, the choice of the deleted vertex is a part of the proof and must be justified.
