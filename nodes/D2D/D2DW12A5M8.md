## Example

Let $D \ge 0$ be an integer.
Claim: every finite simple [[graph|0JD5C82QMB]] $G$ in which each vertex has [[degree|0T0F6CCS9W]] at most $D$ has a proper [[colouring|0874138YP6]] with the colours $1, 2, \ldots, D + 1$.
A proper colouring gives each vertex a colour so that the two ends of each edge get different colours.
We prove the claim with a [[minimal counterexample|JGRCNPKNGG]], and the size of a graph is its number of vertices.

1. Suppose that the claim is false. Among all counterexamples, choose a graph $G$ with the least number $n$ of vertices.
2. The graph with no vertices has a proper colouring, the empty one. So $n \ge 1$, and $G$ has a vertex $v$.
3. Let $G - v$ be the graph that we get from $G$ when we delete $v$ and the edges at $v$. It has $n - 1$ vertices.
4. Deleting a vertex does not raise the degree of any other vertex. So each vertex of $G - v$ has degree at most $D$.
5. By steps 3 and 4, $G - v$ satisfies the hypothesis and has fewer vertices than $G$. By the choice of $G$ in step 1, $G - v$ is not a counterexample.
6. So $G - v$ has a proper colouring $c$ with the colours $1, \ldots, D + 1$.
7. The vertex $v$ has at most $D$ neighbours, and they use at most $D$ of the $D + 1$ colours. So some colour $k$ is used by no neighbour of $v$.
8. Give $v$ the colour $k$, and keep the colour $c(u)$ of every other vertex $u$.
9. An edge of $G - v$ has ends with different colours by step 6. An edge $vu$ has ends with different colours by step 7. So this is a proper colouring of $G$.
10. This contradicts the choice of $G$ as a counterexample. So the claim is true.

## Remarks

1. Step 4 is the check that the smaller graph lies in the class of the claim. Without it, step 5 could not use the minimality of $G$.
2. The bound is sharp: the complete graph on $D + 1$ vertices has maximum degree $D$, and its $D + 1$ vertices are pairwise adjacent, so they need $D + 1$ colours.
3. [[Brooks' theorem|0Y4TV2D5RQ]] improves the bound to $D$ colours for every connected graph that is not a complete graph and not an odd cycle.
4. The proof turns into an algorithm: delete the vertices one by one, then colour them in the reverse order. This is the [[greedy colouring|04AAKZNXHD]].
5. For $D = 2$ the claim says that every graph of maximum degree $2$ has a colouring with $3$ colours. The cycle on $5$ vertices needs all $3$ colours, because a colouring with $2$ colours would alternate around a cycle of odd length.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Imagine a party where each guest knows at most $3$ other guests.
We want to give each guest one of $4$ hats so that two guests who know each other never have the same hat.
Send one guest out of the room, and suppose that the others already have good hats.
The guest who comes back knows at most $3$ people, so at most $3$ hats are taken among them.
One of the $4$ hats is free, and the guest takes it.

### L2 human

We colour the cycle $C_5$ with vertices $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ and edges $12, 23, 34, 45, 51$.
Here $D = 2$, so we have the colours $\{1, 2, 3\}$.

1. Delete vertex $5$. The rest is the path $1, 2, 3, 4$.
2. Colour the path: vertex $1$ gets colour $1$, vertex $2$ gets colour $2$, vertex $3$ gets colour $1$, vertex $4$ gets colour $2$.
3. Vertex $5$ has the neighbours $4$ and $1$, with the colours $2$ and $1$.
4. The colour $3$ is free, so vertex $5$ gets colour $3$.
5. Each of the $5$ edges now joins two different colours.

The typical use of the method in graph theory is to delete a vertex or an edge, use the smaller graph, and repair the colouring.
A trap is to delete a vertex of large degree in a proof that needs a vertex of small degree; the choice of the deleted vertex is a part of the proof.
