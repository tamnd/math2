## Summary

A formal proof is a finite sequence of formulas of a [[formal language|5MFZ4VNY6D]] in which each formula is an axiom, an assumption, or follows from earlier formulas by a stated [[rule of inference|QPD3X471Q1]]; its last formula is the statement proved.
An informal proof is an argument in mathematical language, a mix of words and symbols, written for a human reader who can fill in the routine steps.
The proofs in books and papers are informal, and the common view is that each correct informal proof can, in principle, be expanded into a formal proof.
The distinction matters because it explains what a proof in practice claims, and why a computer can check a proof only after it is formalized.

## Motivation

A [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] in a paper may say "by symmetry", "a routine computation shows", or "the other case is similar".
A reader trusts these phrases because she can supply the missing work.
This raises two questions.
The first asks what the missing work looks like when we write all of it.
The second asks whether a machine, which supplies nothing on its own, can check the argument.

Logic answers the first question with the notion of a formal proof, in which no step is left to the reader.
Proof assistants answer the second question: they check formal proofs, and they help a person to write them.
The informal proof stays the main form of communication, because a formal proof of a real theorem is long and hard to read.

## Notation

In logic, $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ means that the formula $\varphi$ has a formal proof from the set of formulas $\Gamma$ in a fixed proof system.
The word "derivation" is a common synonym of "formal proof".
Different proof systems give different formats: a [[Hilbert system|1MGMQX43B8]] uses many axioms and few rules, and [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]] uses few axioms and many rules.
In a proof assistant, a formal proof is a term or a script that the program checks; the page [[a proof checked by the Lean proof assistant|QF0Q7B5Q73]] shows two.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[A formal derivation of the transitivity of implication|DNRZJKRDZC]] in natural deduction. Each of its eight lines names an assumption or a rule.
- [[The informal proof that divisibility is transitive|RWQ7M25Z5Z]] has three lines. The example lists the logical steps that the three lines leave out.
- The Lean line `example : 2 + 2 = 4 := rfl` is a formal proof that a program checks, as [[this example|QF0Q7B5Q73]] shows.
- In *Principia Mathematica* (1910 to 1913), Whitehead and Russell derived arithmetic from logic in a formal style. The proposition from which $1 + 1 = 2$ follows appears in volume I, and the derivation is completed in volume II.

### Non-examples

- [[A Lean proof that uses `sorry`|BC3VRFZ7NC]] is not a formal proof. The condition that fails: one step is a placeholder, not an axiom or a rule.
- A proof sketch, such as "use induction on $n$; the step is similar to the base case", is not a formal proof. The condition that fails: the steps are described, not given.
- A check of many cases of a general claim is neither a formal nor an informal proof. The condition that fails: no chain of steps leads to the general claim.

### Basic properties

- A formal proof can be checked by an algorithm, because each step must match an axiom or a rule. Finding a formal proof is much harder than checking one.
- An informal proof is checked by readers. Its gaps are acceptable when the intended reader can fill them with routine work, as the page [[mathematical rigour|E1PE9R8DBB]] explains.
- A formal proof can be correct and still prove the wrong statement, when the formal statement does not express what the author meant.
- By [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]], a statement of first-order logic is true in all models of a set of axioms exactly when it has a formal proof from them. So formal provability matches truth in all models.
- By [[Gödel's incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]], no consistent formal system with an algorithmic list of axioms that contains enough arithmetic proves all true statements of arithmetic.

### History

Gottlob Frege published a formal language for logic, the Begriffsschrift, in 1879.
Whitehead and Russell published the three volumes of *Principia Mathematica* in 1910, 1912 and 1913.
In the 20th century Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory became the usual base: most mathematicians accept that their theorems could be derived in it.
From 2005 on, proof assistants checked formal proofs of the [[four colour theorem|0M406JC8J1]] (Coq, 2005) and the [[Kepler conjecture|AFMBQT06ZV]] (HOL Light and Isabelle, 2014).

### Common errors

- To think that "formal" means "written with many symbols". A page of symbols with gaps is informal, and a formal proof is defined by its rules, not by its look.
- To think that an informal proof is less correct than a formal proof. A correct informal proof has no errors; it leaves out steps that its reader can supply.
- To think that a proof assistant checks the meaning of a theorem. It checks that the proof proves the formal statement, and a person must check that this statement says what was meant.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When we explain a proof to a friend, we skip the steps that the friend already knows.
This is an informal proof, and it is how mathematicians write.
A formal proof skips nothing.
Each line follows from earlier lines by a rule from a fixed list, like moves in a board game.
A computer can check such a proof, because it only has to check each move against the list.
For example, from "if it rains, the street is wet" and "it rains", the rule called modus ponens gives "the street is wet".
A formal proof is a long chain of such moves.

### L2 human

We expand one informal sentence into formal steps.
The informal sentence: "Let $x$ be a real number with $x > 2$; then $x^2 > 4$, since $x^2 > 2x > 4$."

1. Assumption: $x > 2$.
2. From step 1 and $2 > 0$: $x > 0$, by the transitivity of $>$.
3. From steps 1 and 2: $x \cdot x > 2 \cdot x$, by the rule "if $a > b$ and $c > 0$, then $ac > bc$".
4. From step 1 and $2 > 0$: $2 \cdot x > 2 \cdot 2$, by the same rule.
5. From steps 3 and 4: $x \cdot x > 2 \cdot 2$, by the transitivity of $>$.
6. Arithmetic: $2 \cdot 2 = 4$, and $x \cdot x = x^2$ by the definition of the square. So $x^2 > 4$.

The informal sentence has two inequalities, and the expansion has six steps.
A full formal proof would also derive each rule above from the axioms of an ordered field.
The typical use of such an expansion is to find a gap: if a step has no rule, the informal proof has a gap there.
A trap is step 2: the informal sentence uses $x > 0$ without saying so.

### L3 human

The claim that every correct informal proof can be formalized is not a theorem, since "correct informal proof" has no formal definition.
It is a working belief, supported by a century of experience and by large libraries of formal proofs, such as Mathlib for Lean.
Formalization projects sometimes find errors, and they sometimes find that an informal proof uses a hidden hypothesis, but they have not found an accepted central theorem that cannot be formalized.

The formal side belongs to [[proof theory|N4EDJNMTEF]] and the [[logic topic|DYJ0KGTPMR]]: proof systems, their [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]] and completeness, and the length of proofs.
The software side belongs to [[mathematical software and formalization|F3F5GGYHMM]] and to [[proof assistants|YM6PRDE88V]].
The [[Curry-Howard correspondence|A77FER1Q5H]] links the two: in many proof assistants a formal proof is a program, and checking the proof is checking the type of the program.
The questions of who accepts a proof, and on what grounds, are treated in [[acceptance of proofs|MJ3EHQJJHX]] and [[computer-assisted proof|MC18C7BMFT]].
