## Summary

The proofs in books and papers are written for people, and they leave out steps that the reader can supply.
A formal proof leaves out nothing, so a machine can check it, and the gap between the two kinds explains both what a proof in practice claims and what a computer can verify.

Here is a proof as a textbook writes it.
If $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, write $b = ak$ and $c = bm$; then $c = (ak)m = a(km)$, so $a \mid c$.
Those two lines hide nine logical steps, among them the choice of the new names $k$ and $m$, the regrouping of a product, and the fact that $km$ is an integer, as [[the divisibility example|RWQ7M25Z5Z]] lists.

An informal proof is an argument in mathematical language, a mix of words and symbols, written for a reader who can fill in the routine steps.
A formal proof is a finite sequence of formulas of a [[formal language|5MFZ4VNY6D]] in which each formula is an axiom, an assumption, or follows from earlier formulas by a stated [[rule of inference|QPD3X471Q1]], and whose last formula is the statement proved.
The common view is that every correct informal proof can in principle be expanded into a formal one.

## Motivation

A [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] in a paper may say "by symmetry", "a routine computation shows" or "the other case is similar".
A reader trusts these phrases because she can do the missing work herself.
Two questions follow.
What does the missing work look like when we write all of it down?
And can a machine, which supplies nothing on its own, check the argument?

Logic answers the first question with the notion of a formal proof, in which no step is left to the reader.
Proof assistants answer the second: they check formal proofs, and they help a person to write them.
The informal proof remains the main way mathematicians communicate, because a formal proof of a real theorem is long and hard to read.
[[Computer-assisted proof|MC18C7BMFT]] shows where the two meet, in the formal checks of the four color theorem and the Kepler conjecture.

## Notation

In logic, $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ means that the formula $\varphi$ has a formal proof from the set of formulas $\Gamma$ in a fixed proof system.
"Derivation" is a common synonym of "formal proof".
Proof systems differ in format: a [[Hilbert system|1MGMQX43B8]] uses many axioms and few rules, and [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]] uses few axioms and many rules.
In a proof assistant, a formal proof is a term or a script that the program checks, as [[the Lean example|QF0Q7B5Q73]] shows.

## Remarks

### One sentence, six steps

Take the informal sentence "if $x > 2$, then $x^2 > 4$, since $x^2 > 2x > 4$".
We expand it, naming one rule for each step.

1. Assume $x > 2$.
2. Since $2 > 0$, transitivity of $>$ gives $x > 0$.
3. The rule "if $a > b$ and $c > 0$, then $ac > bc$", with $c = x$, turns step 1 into $x \cdot x > 2 \cdot x$.
4. The same rule with $c = 2$ turns step 1 into $2 \cdot x > 2 \cdot 2$.
5. Transitivity of $>$ joins steps 3 and 4 into $x \cdot x > 2 \cdot 2$, which is $x^2 > 4$.

The sentence had two inequalities, and the expansion has five steps.
Step 2 is one that the sentence never mentions, yet step 3 needs it.
A full formal proof would go further and derive each rule from the axioms of an ordered field.

### Two more formal proofs

[[A formal derivation of the transitivity of implication|DNRZJKRDZC]] proves $(p \to q) \to ((q \to r) \to (p \to r))$ in eight lines of natural deduction, each line with an assumption or a rule.
In Lean, the line `example : 2 + 2 = 4 := rfl` is a formal proof that the program checks by computing both sides, and [[the Lean example|QF0Q7B5Q73]] shows this and a longer proof.

At the far end of the scale, Alfred North Whitehead and Bertrand Russell derived arithmetic from logic in *Principia Mathematica*, published in three volumes in 1910, 1912 and 1913.
The proposition from which $1 + 1 = 2$ follows appears in volume I, and the derivation is completed in volume II.

### What is not a formal proof

[[A Lean proof that uses `sorry`|BC3VRFZ7NC]] is not a formal proof, because one step is a placeholder and not an axiom or a rule.
A sketch such as "use induction on $n$; the step is like the base case" is not a formal proof either, because it describes steps instead of giving them.
A check of many cases of a general claim is neither a formal nor an informal proof, because no chain of steps leads to the general claim.

### What logic says about formal proofs

A formal proof can be checked by an algorithm, because each step must match an axiom or a rule.
Finding a formal proof is much harder than checking one.

By [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]], a sentence of first-order logic is true in all models of a set of axioms exactly when it has a formal proof from them.
So in first-order logic, formal provability matches truth in all models.

By [[Gödel's incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]], no consistent formal system with an algorithmic list of axioms that contains enough arithmetic proves every true statement of arithmetic.
So formal proof cannot capture all arithmetic truth, even though it captures all that follows from a fixed set of axioms.

### Watch out

"Formal" does not mean "written with many symbols".
A page of symbols with gaps is informal, and a formal proof is defined by its rules, not by its look.

An informal proof is not less correct than a formal one.
A correct informal proof has no errors; it leaves out steps that its reader can supply, as [[mathematical rigor|E1PE9R8DBB]] explains.

A proof assistant does not check the meaning of a theorem.
It checks that the proof proves the formal statement, and a person must still check that the formal statement says what was meant.
For example, if an author states a claim "for all $n$" but lets $n$ range over a type with one element, the program accepts a true theorem that says almost nothing.

### History

Gottlob Frege published his formal language for logic, the *Begriffsschrift*, in 1879.
After *Principia Mathematica*, Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory became the usual base during the 20th century, and most mathematicians accept that their theorems could be derived in it.
Proof assistants then made formal proofs of famous theorems practical.
Georges Gonthier completed a formal proof of the [[four color theorem|0M406JC8J1]] in Coq in 2005.
The Flyspeck project completed a formal proof of the [[Kepler conjecture|AFMBQT06ZV]] in HOL Light and Isabelle in 2014.

### Try it

First, the informal step "$n^2 + n$ is even, since it is $n(n + 1)$" leaves something out.
What is it?
It uses the fact that one of two consecutive integers is even, and so their product is even.

Second, why can a program check a formal proof of a thousand lines faster than it can find a proof of one line?
Checking compares each line with a finite list of axioms and rules, while finding a proof means searching through a space of candidate proofs that has no bound in general.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When we explain a proof to a friend, we skip the steps that the friend already knows.
That is an informal proof, and it is how mathematicians write.
A formal proof skips nothing.
Each line follows from earlier lines by a rule from a fixed list, like moves in a board game.
A computer can check such a proof, because it only has to compare each move with the list.
For example, from "if it rains, the street is wet" and "it rains", the rule called modus ponens gives "the street is wet".
A formal proof is a long chain of such moves.

### L2 human

We find the hidden step in an informal proof by expanding it.
The claim is that the product of two odd integers is odd, and the informal proof reads "$(2a + 1)(2b + 1) = 4ab + 2a + 2b + 1$, which is odd".
Expand the product by the distributive law:
$$(2a + 1)(2b + 1) = 4ab + 2a + 2b + 1 = 2(2ab + a + b) + 1.$$
The informal proof did not write the second equality, and it did not say that $2ab + a + b$ is an integer.
Both steps are needed to match the definition of an odd integer, $2j + 1$ with $j$ an integer.
Check with $a = 1$, $b = 2$: $3 \cdot 5 = 15 = 2 \cdot 7 + 1$, and $2ab + a + b = 4 + 1 + 2 = 7$.
The typical use of such an expansion is to locate a gap: a step with no rule marks the place to look.
Watch out for steps that seem too small to write, such as closure of the integers under multiplication, since a formal proof needs each of them.

### L3 human

The claim that every correct informal proof can be formalized is not a theorem, because "correct informal proof" has no formal definition.
It is a working belief, supported by a century of experience and by large libraries of formal proofs such as Mathlib for Lean.
Formalization projects sometimes find errors or hidden hypotheses in informal proofs.

The formal side belongs to [[proof theory|N4EDJNMTEF]] and to [[logic|DYJ0KGTPMR]]: proof systems, their [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]] and completeness, and the length of proofs.
The software side belongs to [[mathematical software and formalization|F3F5GGYHMM]] and to [[proof assistants|YM6PRDE88V]].
The [[Curry-Howard correspondence|A77FER1Q5H]] links the two: in many proof assistants a formal proof is a program, and checking the proof is checking the type of the program.
Who accepts a proof, and on what grounds, is the subject of [[acceptance of proofs|MJ3EHQJJHX]].
