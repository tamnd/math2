## Summary

A class $\mathcal{K}$ of [[structures|983GZBQYT4]] in a [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]] $L$ has the *joint embedding property* (JEP) when any two members $A, B \in \mathcal{K}$ [[embed|SCRB74SWQN]] into a common member $C \in \mathcal{K}$.
Nothing is asked about how the two images meet.
Every [[age|5FKW715RGJ]] has JEP, and JEP is one of the three conditions of [[Fraïssé's theorem|V5CKNFP3HC]].
For a [[first-order theory|FZRMYTQFRR]] $T$, JEP of the class of models of $T$ says that $T$ cannot be split into two incompatible universal parts.

## Motivation

The members of an age all live inside one structure $M$, so any two of them sit together in a finitely generated piece of $M$.
JEP is the abstract form of this fact.
Without it, a class can split into two families that never meet, such as finite fields of characteristic $2$ and of characteristic $3$.
Such a class is not the age of any single structure.

## Notation

There is no standard symbol; the property is abbreviated JEP.
The formal definition is [[this definition|KA80AZB1CX]].
For a theory $T$, "$T$ has JEP" means that the class of models of $T$ has JEP.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The class of finite graphs has JEP, by the disjoint union; see [[this example|S7AMR4PSH0]].
- The class of models of a [[complete theory|655K8Y7VS8]] has JEP; see [[this example|NY92M56QR1]].
- The age of any structure has JEP.
- The class of finite linear orders has JEP: put one chain above the other.

### Non-examples

- The class of finite fields fails JEP, since no field contains both $\mathbb{F}_2$ and $\mathbb{F}_3$; see [[this non-example|8DQKEDXM8S]].
- The class of finite graphs that are complete or edgeless fails JEP: $K_2$ and its complement have no common extension in the class.

### Basic properties

- If $\mathcal{K}$ has the [[amalgamation property|XSCSX39GEZ]] and a member that embeds into every member, then $\mathcal{K}$ has JEP.
- AP does not imply JEP in general: finite fields have AP within each characteristic, but fail JEP.
- A class of models of $T$ has JEP if and only if, for universal sentences $\varphi, \psi$, $T \vdash \varphi \lor \psi$ implies $T \vdash \varphi$ or $T \vdash \psi$.

### History

The property is implicit in Fraïssé's characterization of ages from the early 1950s.

### Common errors

- To confuse JEP with AP. JEP is amalgamation over nothing, and it does not ask that common parts be identified.
- To assume JEP for a class with AP. Without a smallest member, AP over different bases does not join unrelated members.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two structures from the class must fit together inside a third one from the class.
They may overlap in any way, or not at all.
Finite graphs pass the test: draw the two graphs side by side.
Finite fields fail it: a field cannot have both $1 + 1 = 0$ and $1 + 1 + 1 = 0$.

### L2 human

We prove the universal sentence criterion in one direction.

1. Suppose $T$ has JEP and $T \vdash \varphi \lor \psi$, with $\varphi, \psi$ universal.
2. If neither is provable, take $M \models T + \neg \varphi$ and $N \models T + \neg \psi$.
3. Embed both into some $K \models T$; existential sentences go up along embeddings.
4. So $K \models \neg \varphi \land \neg \psi$, which contradicts $T \vdash \varphi \lor \psi$.

The converse uses diagrams and the compactness theorem.
A trap is to forget that $\neg \varphi$ is existential only because $\varphi$ is universal.

### L3 human

JEP for $T$ says that a disjunction of two universal sentences follows from $T$ only when one of them does.
Every complete theory has JEP, by [[elementary amalgamation|P2VMSM4H4Y]] over the empty set.
In Fraïssé theory, JEP is needed only to get one limit instead of several: a hereditary class with AP but without JEP splits into subclasses with JEP, and each has its own limit.
