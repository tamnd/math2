## Summary

A hypersequent puts several sequents side by side and claims that at least one of them holds.
This lets a proof keep two alternatives open, such as $p \Rightarrow q \mid q \Rightarrow p$, and that is what some logics need for cut-free proofs.
Exactly, a *hypersequent* is a finite nonempty [[multiset|MYJ8Y050J0]] of sequents, written as follows:
$$\Gamma_1 \Rightarrow \Delta_1 \mid \cdots \mid \Gamma_n \Rightarrow \Delta_n.$$
The sequents are its *components*, and the hypersequent is read as the disjunction of its components.
A *hypersequent calculus* is a proof system whose lines are hypersequents.
Its rules are the rules of a [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]], each applied to one component with the other components unchanged, together with external weakening, external contraction and extra structural rules that act on several components.
The main example is Avron's calculus HLC for Gödel-Dummett logic LC, whose extra rule is the communication rule; the [[definition|3PR9PEMXJ4]] states it in full.

## Motivation

Gödel-Dummett logic LC is [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]] with the extra axiom $(A \to B) \vee (B \to A)$.
In the calculus [[LJ|PZX9J3VEMT]], a cut-free derivation of $\Rightarrow A \vee B$ ends with ${\vee}$R, so it contains a derivation of $\Rightarrow A$ or of $\Rightarrow B$.
LC does not have this disjunction property.
The formula $(p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$ is in LC, but neither disjunct is: a one-world model with $p$ true and $q$ false refutes $p \to q$, and swapping the values refutes $q \to p$.
So no calculus in which every cut-free proof of a disjunction ends with ${\vee}$R can be complete for LC.

A hypersequent solves this by keeping both disjuncts alive at the same time, one in each component.
The communication rule then lets the two components exchange formulas, and that exchange is exactly the step that LC adds to intuitionistic logic.
The modal logic S5 has the same problem, and hypersequents solve it the same way.
Hypersequent calculi exist for fuzzy logics such as Gödel logic and for many substructural logics as well.

## Notation

Components are separated by a vertical bar $\mid$, and the letters $G$, $H$ or $\mathcal{G}$ stand for the rest of the hypersequent, the *side hypersequent*.
For intermediate logics the components are single-conclusion sequents $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Pi$, with $\Pi$ empty or one formula.
For classical, modal and substructural logics they are often multi-conclusion sequents $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta$.
The meaning of the bar depends on the logic.
For LC a hypersequent means the disjunction of the implications $\bigwedge \Gamma_i \to \bigvee \Delta_i$, and for S5 it means the disjunction of the formulas $\Box(\bigwedge \Gamma_i \to \bigvee \Delta_i)$.

## Remarks

### A first derivation

Start from the two axioms $q \Rightarrow q$ and $p \Rightarrow p$.
The communication rule moves the $p$ of the second axiom into the first component and the $q$ of the first axiom into the second:
$$\dfrac{q \Rightarrow q \qquad p \Rightarrow p}{p \Rightarrow q \mid q \Rightarrow p} \ (\mathsf{com})$$
Now ${\to}$R in each component gives $\Rightarrow p \to q \mid \Rightarrow q \to p$.
Then ${\vee}$R in each component gives two equal components $\Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$, and external contraction merges them.
The result is the [[prelinearity axiom|P4T18C5736]] with no cut, and the [[full derivation|BNCS8H51B2]] checks every step.
Notice that neither component of $p \Rightarrow q \mid q \Rightarrow p$ is valid alone; only the pair is.

### A second derivation

The weak excluded middle $\neg p \vee \neg\neg p$ is also in LC but not in intuitionistic logic.
Its derivation starts from $\neg p, p \Rightarrow$, which ${\to}$L builds from $p \Rightarrow p$ and $\bot \Rightarrow$.
Communication on two copies of this sequent sends both copies of $p$ to one component and both copies of $\neg p$ to the other:
$$\dfrac{p, \neg p \Rightarrow \qquad p, \neg p \Rightarrow}{p, p \Rightarrow \; \mid \; \neg p, \neg p \Rightarrow} \ (\mathsf{com})$$
Internal contraction, weakening by $\bot$ and ${\to}$R then give $\Rightarrow \neg p \mid \Rightarrow \neg\neg p$, and ${\vee}$R with external contraction finish.
The [[worked derivation|Q50NC2F1Y1]] gives each step and a Kripke model that shows the rule is needed.

### What it is not

The calculus HLJ is HLC without the communication rule.
Each of its rules copies the side hypersequent unchanged, so its components never talk to each other.
As a result, a hypersequent is derivable in HLJ only if one of its components is derivable in LJ, and HLJ is only LJ written with bars; the [[non-example of HLJ|805SVAZ350]] proves this.

The list of premises of a rule is also not a hypersequent.
The premises are all asserted, so the list reads as a conjunction.
For instance, $p \Rightarrow q$ and $q \Rightarrow p$ together are not valid in LC, while the hypersequent $p \Rightarrow q \mid q \Rightarrow p$ is.

A nested sequent is not a hypersequent either.
Its sequents can contain other sequents, so it is a tree, while the components of a hypersequent contain formulas only.

### The same idea for S5

Avron's calculus for S5 has multi-conclusion components, the rules of [[LK|PZX9J3VEMT]] in each component, and three modal rules.
The rule $\Box$L infers $G \mid \Gamma, \Box A \Rightarrow \Delta$ from $G \mid \Gamma, A \Rightarrow \Delta$.
The rule $\Box$R infers $G \mid \Box\Gamma \Rightarrow \Box A$ from $G \mid \Box\Gamma \Rightarrow A$.
The modal splitting rule infers $G \mid \Box\Gamma \Rightarrow \Box\Delta \mid \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$ from $G \mid \Box\Gamma, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Box\Delta, \Pi$.

Here is a cut-free derivation of $\Rightarrow p, \Box\neg\Box p$, the S5 law $\Diamond \Box p \to p$ in disguise.
From $p \Rightarrow p$, the rule $\Box$L gives $\Box p \Rightarrow p$.
Splitting gives $\Box p \Rightarrow \; \mid \; \Rightarrow p$, and then ${\neg}$R and $\Box$R give $\Rightarrow \Box\neg\Box p \mid \Rightarrow p$.
Internal weakening in both components and external contraction give $\Rightarrow p, \Box\neg\Box p$.

### The rules you can rely on

A hypersequent with one component is an ordinary sequent, so every LJ derivation is an HLC derivation in which every line has one component.
HLC is sound and complete for LC: the hypersequent $\Rightarrow A$ is derivable exactly when $A$ is a theorem of LC.
HLC has cut elimination, and a cut-free derivation has the [[subformula property|Z2NXKDK06G]], so backward proof search for LC stays inside the subformulas of the goal.
The bar touches the logical rules only through the structural rules, so one set of logical rules serves many logics, and only the structural rules change.

### Watch out

The bar is a disjunction, not a conjunction.
So a hypersequent is weaker than each of its components: $p \Rightarrow q \mid q \Rightarrow p$ holds in LC although neither $p \Rightarrow q$ nor $q \Rightarrow p$ does.

External contraction removes one of two identical components and nothing else.
You cannot contract $\Rightarrow p \to q$ and $\Rightarrow q \to p$ into one component, since they differ.

The communication rule is not an intuitionistic rule.
It is sound on Kripke models whose worlds form a chain, and it fails on a model with two incomparable worlds, which is why HLC proves more than LJ.

### History

Pottinger used structures of this kind in 1983 for the modal logics T, S4 and S5.
Avron introduced hypersequents for the relevance logic RM in 1987.
His 1991 paper gave the calculus for Gödel-Dummett logic with the communication rule, and his 1996 survey treated modal, intermediate and substructural logics in one framework.
Dummett introduced the logic LC in 1959.

### Try it

Is $p \Rightarrow q \mid q \Rightarrow p$ derivable in HLJ?
No: neither $p \Rightarrow q$ nor $q \Rightarrow p$ is derivable in LJ, and in HLJ some component must be.

Is $\Rightarrow p \mid p \Rightarrow$ derivable in HLC?
Its formula reading is $p \vee \neg p$, which is not a theorem of LC (a two-world chain with $p$ true only at the top refutes it), so by soundness it is not derivable.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An ordinary sequent is one claim of the form "these assumptions give this conclusion".
A hypersequent is a list of such claims, and it says that at least one of them holds.
For example, "$p$ gives $q$, or $q$ gives $p$" is a hypersequent with two claims.
The proof rules change one claim at a time, as in an ordinary proof.
One special rule, the communication rule, lets two claims exchange assumptions.
With it we can prove facts that hold when all situations are arranged in one line, like the days of a calendar.

### L2 human

How would you find the derivation of $(p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$ if nobody showed it to you?
Read the rules upward from the goal.
A single sequent $\Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$ forces ${\vee}$R to pick one disjunct, and both choices fail.
So first undo external contraction and keep two copies:
$$\Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p) \mid \Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p).$$
Now pick the left disjunct in the first copy and the right one in the second, and undo ${\to}$R in each:
$$p \Rightarrow q \mid q \Rightarrow p.$$
Neither component is an axiom, but the communication rule read upward splits the antecedents and gives the axioms $q \Rightarrow q$ and $p \Rightarrow p$.
The search shows the typical use of the calculus: because it is cut-free, every line contains only subformulas of the goal, so the search space is finite.

### L3 human

Hypersequents add one structural layer above the sequent, a disjunction of sequents at the meta level.
A nested sequent is a tree of sequents, and a [[labeled sequent calculus|F46GV42XCE]] names the worlds of a Kripke model explicitly.
[[Display calculi|2Q62W689SC]] and [[deep inference|4ZW0SHW2RG]] take other routes to the same goal of analytic calculi.
The bar can be seen as a structural disjunction one level up, just as the comma on the right of an LK sequent is a structural disjunction of formulas.
Rules on the bar such as communication correspond to conditions on Kripke frames, here linearity, and this link between structural rules and frame conditions returns in the labeled calculi.
