## Statement

Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a complex Hilbert space.
Let $s \mapsto H(s)$, $s \in [0, 1]$, be a map into the bounded [[self-adjoint operators|0NEGN5CRSC]] on $\mathcal{H}$ that is twice continuously differentiable in operator norm.
Assume that for each $s$ the [[spectrum|BMKATJ802A]] of $H(s)$ is the disjoint union of two closed sets $\sigma_1(s)$ and $\sigma_2(s)$.
Assume that $\operatorname{dist}(\sigma_1(s), \sigma_2(s)) \ge g$ for a fixed $g > 0$, and that $\sigma_1(s)$ depends continuously on $s$.
Let $P(s)$ be the spectral projection of $H(s)$ onto $\sigma_1(s)$.
For $\varepsilon > 0$ let $U_\varepsilon(s)$ be the [[unitary|MM0EJ2GDE4]] solution of
$$i \varepsilon \frac{d}{ds} U_\varepsilon(s) = H(s) U_\varepsilon(s), \qquad U_\varepsilon(0) = I.$$
Then there is a constant $C$, independent of $\varepsilon$, such that
$$\sup_{s \in [0, 1]} \norm{\big(I - P(s)\big) U_\varepsilon(s) P(0)} \le C \varepsilon.$$

## Remarks

Here $\varepsilon = 1/T$, where $T$ is the total time of the evolution in physical time $t = sT$ (with $\hbar = 1$), so $\varepsilon \to 0$ means a slow change of the Hamiltonian.
The proof compares $U_\varepsilon$ with the adiabatic evolution, which maps $\operatorname{Ran} P(0)$ exactly onto $\operatorname{Ran} P(s)$.
The difference is an oscillatory integral; one integration by parts, using the gap $g$ to invert the commutator with $H(s)$, gains the factor $\varepsilon$.

Example: let $\sigma_1(s) = \set{E(s)}$ be a simple eigenvalue, and prepare the system in its eigenstate at $s = 0$.
At time $s$ it is found in the corresponding eigenstate of $H(s)$, up to a phase and an error $O(\varepsilon)$.
The phase is the sum of a dynamical phase $-\varepsilon^{-1}\int_0^s E(u)\, du$ and a [[geometric phase|7BJM2TRPK2]].

The gap condition is necessary: when two eigenvalues cross or come close, the system can jump between them, as in Landau–Zener transitions.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If you change a quantum system slowly enough, and its energy levels never touch, the system stays in the level where it started.
A particle in the ground state of a slowly deformed trap stays in the ground state of the trap at each moment.
The slower the change, the smaller the chance of a jump to another level.

### L2 human

The theorem is due in its first form to Born and Fock (1928), and in this operator form to Kato (1950).
It is the quantum analogue of the classical [[adiabatic invariants|PY6R2DQN0W]].
It underlies the [[Born–Oppenheimer approximation|E6ZF252YH2]], where the slow nuclei play the role of the slowly varying parameter, and it is the source of the [[geometric phase|7BJM2TRPK2]].
Adiabatic quantum computation also relies on it: one deforms a simple Hamiltonian into one whose ground state encodes the answer, and the running time is governed by the gap.
