## Summary

Research on student difficulties with proof describes the obstacles that students meet when they construct, read and judge [[proofs|0B94TKFQJX]].
The obstacles fall into five groups.
The first group is the view of proof: a student may accept examples as proof, or see no need for a proof.
The second group is logic and language, for example [[quantifiers|EGTZPA6QH3]] and conditional statements.
The third group is concepts: a student may reason from a mental picture and not from the [[definition|8ZCZ3HW91Z]].
The fourth group is strategy: a student may know the facts but not know how to begin or which fact to use.
The fifth group is the writing of the proof.
The research matters because a teacher can respond to each group with different tasks.

## Motivation

Proof is the part of mathematics in which students fail most often, at school and at university.
A teacher who sees only the wrong final text cannot tell why the student failed.
The same blank page can come from a student who does not know the definition, from one who cannot unpack a quantifier, and from one who sees no need to prove an obvious claim.
Research separates these causes by interviews, tasks and analysis of written work.
The result is a map of difficulties that teachers and course designers use.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. View of proof: a student checks a claim for five values and writes "true for all $n$". The page on [[proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]] calls this an empirical proof scheme.
2. Logic: a student proves the [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] of the required statement, or uses the invalid step of [[affirming the consequent|2XXTDXK1ZC]].
3. Quantifiers: asked to prove that a function is not continuous at a point, a student negates the definition and gets the order of "for all" and "there exists" wrong. The page on [[negating statements|K930V33BSC]] treats the correct method.
4. Concepts: a student "proves" that a sequence has no limit because its terms never equal the limit. The student's picture of a limit as a value that is reached conflicts with the definition.
5. Strategy: a student knows the definition of a group homomorphism and the needed theorems, but does not see which theorem to use.
6. Induction: a student proves the base case and the step, but in the step assumes the statement for all $n$. The page on [[errors in induction proofs|V52HDTMPV5]] lists such errors.

### Non-examples

1. A false proof built on purpose, such as a "proof" that $1 = 2$ with a hidden division by zero, is a puzzle, not a student difficulty. The topic [[fallacies and errors in proofs|T6QQ749DM5]] collects such puzzles.
2. An error in arithmetic inside a correct proof plan is a slip. Research on proof difficulties studies errors in reasoning, not slips.

### Research findings

1. Gary Martin and Guershon Harel (1989) found that many preservice elementary teachers accepted inductive arguments as proofs of general statements.
2. Daniel Chazan (1993) found that some high school geometry students treated measured examples as proof, and that some thought a deductive proof could still have counterexamples.
3. Robert Moore (1994) observed a transition-to-proof course and listed seven sources of difficulty, among them the use of definitions, notation, and how to begin a proof.
4. Annie Selden and John Selden (1995) found that undergraduates often could not unpack the logical structure of informally stated theorems, a skill needed to begin and to check a proof.
5. Lulu Healy and Celia Hoyles (2000) studied high-attaining students of age 14 to 15 in England. The students were better at choosing correct arguments than at constructing them, and they expected algebraic arguments to receive the best marks.
6. Keith Weber (2001) compared undergraduates and doctoral students on proofs in abstract algebra. The undergraduates often knew the needed facts and theorems, but lacked strategic knowledge of which facts to use.
7. Weber and Lara Alcock (2004) distinguished syntactic proof production, which works with definitions and formulas only, from semantic proof production, which uses examples and pictures to guide the proof.
8. Tommy Dreyfus (1999) argued that students are rarely told what counts as an explanation, an argument or a proof, and that the expectations stay implicit.

### History

Early studies, from the 1970s and 1980s, looked at proof in school geometry and at the van Hiele levels of [[geometry education|1D7PZ2TF5Y]].
In the 1990s, research moved to the transition to proof at university, with the studies of Moore and of the Seldens.
Research in the 2000s looked at strategy, at the reading of proofs, and at the reasons for the difficulties in the logic of quantifiers.

### Common errors

1. A teacher explains each difficulty as a lack of effort. The research shows specific causes, and each cause needs a specific response.
2. A teacher gives more practice of the same kind. If the cause is an empirical proof scheme, more practice with algebra does not change the scheme.
3. A course teaches logic in isolation, and expects the students to transfer it to proofs. The unpacking of statements is a part of each proof, so students must practise it on the statements of real theorems.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Many students find proofs hard.
Some think that a few examples are enough.
Some do not understand words like "for all" and "there exists".
Some know the definitions, but do not know where to start.
Each problem needs a different kind of help.
For example, a student who thinks examples are enough needs a task where examples mislead: $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for $n = 0$ to $39$, but $40^2 + 40 + 41 = 41^2$.

### L2 human

We diagnose the work of one student on the task "prove that if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd".
The student writes the following text.

> Let $n$ be odd, so $n = 2k + 1$.
> Then $n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$, which is odd.


1. Read the text: each line is correct.
2. Compare with the task: the text proves "if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd". This is the converse of the task.
3. Classify: the error is in logic, not in algebra or concepts.
4. Respond: ask the student to write the hypothesis and the conclusion of the task, and those of the proof, side by side.
5. Repair: the task follows by [[contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]]. If $n$ is even, $n = 2k$, then $n^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2)$ is even.

The typical use of the research is this kind of diagnosis.
A trap is step 1: a teacher who checks only the lines does not find the error, because each line is true.
