## Summary

Two students get the same task: prove that if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd.
The first student leaves the page blank and says, "I know what odd means, but I don't know where to start."
The second writes $n = 2k + 1$, so $n^2 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$ is odd, and every line is correct, but the argument proves the converse.
The two failures look different on paper, and they have different causes.

Research on student difficulties with proof sorts such failures by their cause, so that a teacher can answer each cause with the right task.
The studies use interviews, written tasks and observation of courses, and they find five broad groups of obstacles.
A student may hold a view of [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] in which examples are enough.
A student may misread the logic, for example the order of [[quantifiers|EGTZPA6QH3]] or the direction of a conditional.
A student may reason from a mental picture instead of the [[definition|8ZCZ3HW91Z]].
A student may know the facts but lack a strategy for which fact to use.
And a student may have the argument but fail to write it as a proof.

## Motivation

Proof is where many students first fail in mathematics, at school and again at university.
A teacher who sees only the wrong final text cannot tell why it went wrong.
The blank page of the first student above could come from not knowing the definition, from not seeing the logical form of the goal, or from not seeing why an obvious claim needs a proof at all.
Each cause needs a different response, and more practice of the same kind helps with none of them.
So the research gives teachers a map: a list of the common causes, with evidence on how often they occur and at what stage.

## Notation

The research has no fixed symbols, but some terms recur.
A "proof scheme" is what a student accepts as convincing, in the sense of Harel and Sowder.
"Unpacking" a statement means writing out its logical form, with every quantifier and conditional explicit.
"Syntactic" and "semantic" proof production are two styles of work that Weber and Alcock named, described below.

## Remarks

### Five kinds of failure

A student who checks a claim for five values of $n$ and writes "true for all $n$" has a view of proof problem; the theory of [[proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]] calls this an empirical proof scheme.
A student who proves the [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]], as the second student above did, or who uses the invalid step of [[affirming the consequent|2XXTDXK1ZC]], has a problem with logic.
A student who must show that a function is not continuous at a point, and who swaps "for all" and "there exists" in the negation, has a problem with quantifiers; [[negating statements|K930V33BSC]] gives the correct method.

A student who argues that a constant sequence has no limit, because the terms of a sequence approach the limit but never reach it, has a concept problem.
The picture of a limit as a value that is never reached conflicts with the definition, which the constant sequence $1, 1, 1, \dots$ satisfies with limit $1$.
A student who knows the definition of a group homomorphism and the relevant theorems, but cannot choose which theorem to use, has a strategy problem.
And a student who, in an induction step, assumes the statement for all $n$ has a problem with the form of the proof; [[errors in induction proofs|V52HDTMPV5]] lists such errors.

### What is not a proof difficulty

A false proof built on purpose, such as a "proof" that $1 = 2$ with a hidden division by zero, is a puzzle, not a difficulty of the student who reads it.
The topic [[fallacies and errors in proofs|T6QQ749DM5]] collects such puzzles.
An error of arithmetic inside a correct plan is a slip, and a slip says little about how the student reasons.
The research studies errors in reasoning, not slips.

### Evidence from school and teacher education

Gary Martin and Guershon Harel (1989) gave preservice elementary teachers arguments for familiar statements.
Many of the teachers rated inductive arguments, based on checked examples, as valid proofs of general statements.
Daniel Chazan (1993) interviewed high school geometry students.
Some treated measured examples as proof, and some thought that a deductive proof could still have counterexamples.

Lulu Healy and Celia Hoyles (2000) surveyed high-attaining students of age 14 to 15 in England and Wales on proof in algebra.
The students were better at choosing correct arguments than at constructing them.
They also expected algebraic arguments to receive the best marks from a teacher.
These studies show that the view of proof is formed long before university.

### Evidence from university

The education researcher Robert Moore (1994) observed a transition-to-proof course and listed seven sources of difficulty.
Among them are not knowing the definitions, not knowing how to use a definition to give a proof its structure, trouble with notation, and not knowing how to begin.
Annie Selden and John Selden (1995) found that undergraduates often could not unpack the logical form of informally stated theorems, a skill that they need to begin a proof and to check one.

Keith Weber (2001) compared undergraduates and doctoral students on proofs in abstract algebra.
The undergraduates often knew the facts and theorems they needed, but lacked the strategic knowledge of which ones to use.
Weber and Lara Alcock (2004) separated syntactic proof production, which works only with definitions and formulas, from semantic proof production, which uses examples and pictures to guide the proof.
Tommy Dreyfus (1999) argued that students are rarely told what counts as an explanation, an argument or a proof, so the expectations stay implicit.
Most of these studies are small and qualitative, with a few to a few dozen students, so they name causes well but do not measure how common each cause is.

### History

Early studies, in the 1970s and 1980s, looked mostly at proof in school geometry, often with the van Hiele levels of [[geometry education|1D7PZ2TF5Y]].
In the 1990s the focus moved to the transition to proof at university, with the studies of Moore and of the Seldens.
Research in the 2000s turned to strategy, to the reading of proofs, and to the logic of quantifiers.

### Watch out

A common response to a weak proof is more practice of the same kind.
If the cause is an empirical proof scheme, more algebra drill leaves the scheme in place, and the student keeps checking cases.
A better response is a task where cases mislead: $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for $n = 0$ to $39$, but at $n = 40$ it equals $41^2$.

Another common response is a separate unit on logic, with the hope that students will transfer it to proofs.
The research on unpacking suggests that students must practice the logic on the statements of real theorems, inside the proofs where it is used.

### Try it

A student proves "if $ab = 0$ then $a = 0$ or $b = 0$" for real numbers by writing "if $a = 0$, then $ab = 0 \cdot b = 0$".
Which kind of difficulty is this?
The student proved the converse of one case, so it is a logic difficulty: the direction of the conditional is reversed.

A student says "I can't prove that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational because I can't check all fractions."
Which kind of difficulty is this?
It is a view of proof difficulty: the student thinks a proof means checking cases, and needs to see a proof by [[contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]] as a way to cover all cases at once.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Many students find proofs hard, but not all for the same reason.
Some think that a few examples are enough.
Some do not understand words like "for all" and "there exists".
Some know the definitions but do not know where to start.
Each problem needs a different kind of help.
For example, a student who thinks examples are enough needs a case where examples mislead: $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for $n = 0$ to $39$, but $40^2 + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41^2$.

### L2 human

We diagnose the work of the second student from the Summary on the task "if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd".
The student writes the following text.

> Let $n$ be odd, so $n = 2k + 1$.
> Then $n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$, which is odd.

First read the text line by line, and you find that each line is correct.
Then compare it with the task: the text proves "if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd", the converse of what was asked.
So the error is in logic, not in algebra or in concepts.
A good response is to ask the student to write the hypothesis and conclusion of the task and those of the text side by side.
The repair uses the [[contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]]: if $n$ is even, then $n = 2k$ and
$$n^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2),$$
which is even.
The lesson for diagnosis is that checking lines is not enough, because each line here is true and the proof still fails.

### L3 human

The research on difficulties ties together several strands of research on teaching proof.
The view of proof is the subject of [[proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]], and the role of mental pictures is the subject of [[concept image and concept definition|C5S2X0WDJ8]].
The logic of statements is the subject of [[teaching logic|K1Y2MAQQ9E]], and the reading of proofs is the subject of [[proof comprehension and validation|52Y2MW27J0]].
Responses to the difficulties include [[transition-to-proof courses|HQDCSJG379]], [[generic proofs|SG9XA69J64]] and the [[Moore method|WKM6535VHG]].
The survey by Stylianides, Stylianides and Weber in the NCTM Compendium (2017) brings these strands together.
