## Statement

Consider a finite-horizon [[stochastic dynamic program|SDW79PBNWM]] with stages $t = 0, \dots, T$, finite state sets $S_t$ and finite nonempty action sets $A_t(s)$.
It has transition laws $P_t(\cdot \mid s, a)$ on $S_{t+1}$, stage costs $c_t(s, a)$ and terminal cost $c_T$.
For a policy $\pi$, a stage $t$ and a state $s \in S_t$, the tail problem starts in $s$ at stage $t$.
Its expected cost under $\pi$ is $J_t^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}^\pi\big[\sum_{k=t}^{T-1} c_k(s_k, a_k) + c_T(s_T) \mid s_t = s\big]$.
Let $J_t^*(s)$ be the infimum of $J_t^\pi(s)$ over all policies.
Define $f_T = c_T$ and, for $t < T$,
$$f_t(s) = \min_{a \in A_t(s)} \Big( c_t(s, a) + \sum_{s' \in S_{t+1}} P_t(s' \mid s, a) \, f_{t+1}(s') \Big).$$
Then:

1. $J_t^* = f_t$ for every $t$.
2. Let a Markov policy choose, at each stage $t$ and state $s$, an action attaining the minimum in the definition of $f_t(s)$.
Then it is optimal for the whole problem and for every tail problem.
3. Let a policy be optimal from the initial state $s_0$, and let $s$ be reached at stage $t$ with positive probability.
Then the same policy is optimal for the tail problem from $s$ at stage $t$.

## Remarks

The proof is a backward induction on $t$.
Fix a first action $a$ at stage $t$.
The best achievable expected cost is $c_t(s, a)$ plus the expected optimal tail cost from stage $t + 1$, which is $\sum_{s'} P_t(s' \mid s, a) f_{t+1}(s')$ by induction.
Minimizing over $a$ gives $f_t(s)$.
Part 3 holds because, if the tail of an optimal policy could be improved on a reachable state, replacing it there would lower the total expected cost.
Example: in a weighted acyclic graph, let $P$ be a shortest path from $u$ to $v$ and let $w$ be a vertex of $P$.
Then the part of $P$ from $w$ to $v$ is a shortest path from $w$ to $v$.
Part 3 needs the qualifier "with positive probability": on states that are never reached, an optimal policy may act arbitrarily.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Richard Bellman put it in words like these: whatever the first decision was, the rest of an optimal plan must be optimal.
It must be optimal for the situation that the first decision produced.
So a best plan is made of best sub-plans.
This is what makes it possible to solve a long problem by working backwards one stage at a time instead of trying every plan.

### L2 human

The principle is the reason the [[Bellman equation|Z94Z6BY8JG]] holds, and it justifies every [[dynamic programming|R3FXF08WBH]] recursion for optimization problems.
For infinite-horizon discounted [[Markov decision processes|WCNHWTJXYD]] the same argument gives the stationary Bellman optimality equation, whose solution is found by value iteration or policy iteration.
It fails for objectives that are not additive over stages in a suitable way, for instance a variance of the total cost.
There the tail of an optimal plan need not be optimal for the tail objective.
