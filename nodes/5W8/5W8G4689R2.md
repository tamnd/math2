## Example

Let $S(n) = 1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n - 1)$ be the sum of the first $n$ odd numbers, for each integer $n \geq 1$.
We find and prove a formula for $S(n)$, and we name the kind of reasoning in each step.

1. Computation of cases: $S(1) = 1$, $S(2) = 4$, $S(3) = 9$, $S(4) = 16$ and $S(5) = 25$.
2. Inductive reasoning: the five values are $1^2, 2^2, 3^2, 4^2, 5^2$. We guess that $S(n) = n^2$ for each $n \geq 1$. This guess is likely, but not yet certain.
3. Abductive reasoning: we look for an explanation. A square of $n \times n$ dots splits into $n$ L-shaped layers, with $1, 3, 5, \ldots, 2n - 1$ dots. If this picture is right for each $n$, it explains the guess.
4. We check the picture in algebra. The layer between the $(k - 1) \times (k - 1)$ square and the $k \times k$ square has $k^2 - (k - 1)^2$ dots.
5. Deductive reasoning: for each $k \geq 1$, we have $k^2 - (k - 1)^2 = k^2 - (k^2 - 2k + 1) = 2k - 1$. This is the $k$-th odd number.
6. We add the equations of step 5 for $k = 1, 2, \ldots, n$. On the right we get $S(n)$.
7. On the left, each square $k^2$ with $1 \leq k \leq n - 1$ appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign. The sum is $n^2 - 0^2 = n^2$.
8. So $S(n) = n^2$ for each $n \geq 1$. Steps 5 to 7 are a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]], and the guess of step 2 is now a theorem.

## Remarks

The example shows the three kinds of [[reasoning|5V35SGEN1T]] in their usual order.
Step 2 gives a conjecture, step 3 gives a reason to believe it, and only steps 5 to 7 give certainty.
The evidence of step 1 would be the same if the formula failed at $n = 100$, and so it cannot replace the proof.

A change of the sequence changes the outcome.
For the sums of the first $n$ even numbers, the values $2, 6, 12, 20$ suggest $n(n + 1)$.
The same telescoping proof, with $k(k + 1) - (k - 1)k = 2k$, proves this formula.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Add the odd numbers one at a time: $1$, then $1 + 3 = 4$, then $1 + 3 + 5 = 9$, then $16$, then $25$.
These are the square numbers, and this pattern leads to a guess.
A picture explains the guess: a square of dots can be peeled into L-shaped layers of $1$, $3$, $5$, and so on, dots.
The picture makes the guess believable.
A short calculation then proves that each layer has the next odd number of dots, and this proof makes the guess certain.

### L2 human

We check the deduction for $n = 4$.

1. Step 5 gives four equations: $1^2 - 0^2 = 1$, $2^2 - 1^2 = 3$, $3^2 - 2^2 = 5$ and $4^2 - 3^2 = 7$.
2. The sum of the right sides is $1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16$.
3. The sum of the left sides is $4^2 - 0^2 = 16$, because $1^2$, $2^2$ and $3^2$ cancel.
4. Both sums agree, as the proof says.

The typical use of the pattern is a telescoping sum: we write each term as a difference, and most terms cancel.
A trap is to present steps 1 and 2 as the proof.
They give strong evidence, and the proof needs steps 5 to 7.
