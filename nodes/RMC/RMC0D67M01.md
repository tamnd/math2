## Example

The squaring map fixes the origin, and still it fails both conditions of linearity.
Let $f \colon \R \to \R$ be $f(x) = x^2$, where $\R$ is a vector space over itself.
We check the conditions of a [[linear map|0RJF668P1T]] one at a time.

1. Zero: $f(0) = 0$, so the quickest test does not rule out linearity.
2. Additivity: take $u = v = 1$; then $f(u + v) = f(2) = 4$, but $f(u) + f(v) = 1 + 1 = 2$.
3. Homogeneity: take $a = 2$ and $v = 1$; then $f(2 \cdot 1) = 4$, but $2 \cdot f(1) = 2$.

So $f$ is not a linear map, although it sends $0$ to $0$.

## Remarks

The failure is not an accident of the chosen numbers.
Expanding the square gives
$$f(u + v) - f(u) - f(v) = (u + v)^2 - u^2 - v^2 = 2uv,$$
so additivity fails for every pair with $u \ne 0$ and $v \ne 0$.
In the same way $f(av) = a^2 f(v)$, so homogeneity holds only when $a^2 = a$, that is $a \in \{0, 1\}$, or when $v = 0$.

The linear maps $\R \to \R$ are exactly the maps $x \mapsto cx$ for a constant $c$, by the [[theorem on matrix transformations|W6KB0J3SZZ]] with $n = m = 1$.
Their graphs are lines through the origin, and the graph of $x^2$ is a parabola.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Squaring does not respect sums: $(1 + 2)^2 = 9$, but $1^2 + 2^2 = 5$.
So squaring is not linear.
This is the same slip as writing $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$ in school algebra.

### L2 human

To disprove linearity, one failing instance is enough, and here $u = v = 1$ works.
The general identity $f(u + v) = f(u) + f(v) + 2uv$ shows that the cross term $2uv$ is what breaks additivity.
Watch out: the test $f(0) = 0$ passes here, so it can never prove that a map is linear.
