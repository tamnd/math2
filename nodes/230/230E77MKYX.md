## Example

Let $V = \{e, a, b, c\}$ be the [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]], with $a^2 = b^2 = c^2 = e$, $ab = ba = c$, $bc = cb = a$ and $ca = ac = b$.
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the set of [[cyclic subgroups|08ABT85339]] of $V$, ordered by inclusion.
A [[lattice|WH4SFAACAS]] is a partially ordered set in which each two elements have a least upper bound and a greatest lower bound.
We show that $\mathcal{C}$ is not a lattice.

1. The cyclic subgroups: $\langle e \rangle = \{e\}$, $\langle a \rangle = \{e, a\}$, $\langle b \rangle = \{e, b\}$ and $\langle c \rangle = \{e, c\}$. So $\mathcal{C}$ has $4$ elements.
2. $V$ itself is not in $\mathcal{C}$: each element of $V$ has order at most $2$, so a cyclic subgroup has at most $2$ elements, but $\abs{V} = 4$. This is the fact that [[the Klein four-group is not cyclic|DX6X1D21FE]].
3. Upper bounds: an upper bound of $\langle a \rangle$ and $\langle b \rangle$ in $\mathcal{C}$ is a cyclic subgroup that contains $a$ and $b$. It contains the set $\{e, a, b\}$ with $3$ elements, but each element of $\mathcal{C}$ has at most $2$ elements.
4. So $\langle a \rangle$ and $\langle b \rangle$ have no upper bound in $\mathcal{C}$, and so no least upper bound.
5. So $\mathcal{C}$ is not a lattice.

## Remarks

The example shows why the lattice of subgroups must contain all subgroups.
In the full [[lattice of subgroups|0B8BW4YWTF]] of $V$, the join of $\langle a \rangle$ and $\langle b \rangle$ is $V$, which is a subgroup but not a cyclic subgroup.
The full lattice has $5$ elements: $\{e\}$, the three subgroups of order $2$, and $V$.

Meets do exist in $\mathcal{C}$: the meet of two different subgroups of order $2$ is $\{e\}$.
So $\mathcal{C}$ is a partially ordered set with all meets of pairs, but not all joins.

If we replace $V$ by a cyclic group, the problem disappears.
Each subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic, so the cyclic subgroups of a cyclic group are all its subgroups, and they form a lattice.
The same problem appears in each group that is not locally cyclic.
In such a group, there are elements $x$ and $y$ such that $\langle x, y \rangle$ is not cyclic.
The reason: suppose that every subgroup $\langle x, y \rangle$ is cyclic.
Then for elements $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ with $n \geq 2$, we have $\langle x_1, x_2 \rangle = \langle z \rangle$ for some $z$, so $\langle x_1, \ldots, x_n \rangle = \langle z, x_3, \ldots, x_n \rangle$.
By induction on $n$, every finitely generated subgroup is cyclic, so the group is locally cyclic, which is false.
Now a cyclic subgroup that contains $x$ and $y$ contains $\langle x, y \rangle$, and a subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.
So $\langle x \rangle$ and $\langle y \rangle$ have no upper bound among the cyclic subgroups, and hence no join.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Klein four-group has four elements: "do nothing" and three moves $a$, $b$, $c$, each of which undoes itself.
Each move, with "do nothing", forms a small group of $2$ elements.
To hold both $a$ and $b$, a group needs at least $3$ elements, and in fact it needs all $4$.
The group of all $4$ elements cannot be made from one move.
So among the groups made from one move, none holds both $a$ and $b$.

### L2 human

We compare $\mathcal{C}$ with the lattice of all subgroups of $V$.

1. The subgroups of $V$ have order $1$, $2$ or $4$, by Lagrange's theorem.
2. The subgroups of order $1$ and $2$ are cyclic: $\{e\}$, $\langle a \rangle$, $\langle b \rangle$ and $\langle c \rangle$.
3. The only subgroup of order $4$ is $V$, and it is not cyclic.
4. So the lattice of all subgroups is $\mathcal{C} \cup \{V\}$, and $V$ is the join of each two different subgroups of order $2$.

The typical use of this example is a warning: a natural family of subgroups need not be closed under joins.
Before we call a family of subgroups a lattice, we must check that each two members have a join and a meet in the family.

A trap: the join in $\mathcal{C}$ is not the same as the join in the lattice of all subgroups.
An upper bound must lie in the family, and here the only common upper bound in the larger lattice, $V$, is not in $\mathcal{C}$.
