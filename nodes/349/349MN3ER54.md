## Example

Let $r > 0$ be the distance between two neutral atoms.
The *Lennard-Jones potential* is the pair potential
$$V(r) = 4 \varepsilon \left[ \left( \frac{\sigma}{r} \right)^{12} - \left( \frac{\sigma}{r} \right)^{6} \right],$$
where $\varepsilon > 0$ is the depth of the well and $\sigma > 0$ is the distance at which $V$ vanishes.
The $r^{-12}$ term models short-range repulsion, and the $r^{-6}$ term the van der Waals attraction.

Check of the shape: $V(\sigma) = 4\varepsilon (1 - 1) = 0$.
The derivative is $V'(r) = 4\varepsilon \left( -12 \sigma^{12} r^{-13} + 6 \sigma^6 r^{-7} \right)$, which vanishes when $r^6 = 2 \sigma^6$, that is at $r_m = 2^{1/6} \sigma$.
There $(\sigma/r_m)^6 = 1/2$, so $V(r_m) = 4\varepsilon (\frac14 - \frac12) = -\varepsilon$, the minimum.
As $r \to 0$, $V(r) \to +\infty$, and as $r \to \infty$, $V(r) \to 0$ from below.

## Remarks

A classical fluid of particles with this pair potential is the standard test system for simulations by molecular dynamics and Monte Carlo methods.
Its [[Mayer f-function|T4FZCAQC0D]] $f(r) = e^{-\beta V(r)} - 1$ is close to $-1$ for $r$ well below $\sigma$, and positive near the well, so the second virial coefficient changes sign with temperature.
Unlike [[hard spheres|39NMDCPCCV]], the Lennard-Jones fluid has an attractive part and so shows a liquid–gas transition with a critical point.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two atoms far apart attract each other weakly; pushed close together they repel very strongly.
The Lennard-Jones formula captures both effects with two powers of the distance.
The atoms are happiest at the distance $2^{1/6}\sigma$, where the energy reaches its lowest value $-\varepsilon$.
For argon, a good fit uses $\sigma$ of about a third of a nanometre.
