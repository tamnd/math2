## Statement

Row operations can be undone, they are matrix products, and they never change the solutions of a system.
Let $F$ be a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]], let $M$ be an $m \times k$ matrix over $F$, and let $\rho$ be an [[elementary row operation|04XWH28452]] on $m$ rows.
Write $\rho(M)$ for the result of applying $\rho$ to $M$.

1. (Reversibility.) There is an elementary row operation $\rho'$ of the same type with $\rho'(\rho(M)) = M$ for every $m \times k$ matrix $M$.
2. (Matrix form.) Let $E = \rho(I_m)$. Then $\rho(M) = EM$ for every $m \times k$ matrix $M$, and $E$ is invertible with $E^{-1} = \rho'(I_m)$.
3. (Solution sets.) Let $A$ be $m \times n$, let $b \in F^m$, and let $[A' \mid b'] = \rho([A \mid b])$. Then the systems $Ax = b$ and $A'x = b'$ have the same solution set in $F^n$.

## Remarks

### One step on a system

Take $x + 2y = 5$, $3x + 4y = 6$ and the step $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 3R_1$.
The new second equation is $(3x + 4y) - 3(x + 2y) = 6 - 15$, that is, $-2y = -9$.
Any solution of the old system satisfies both old equations, so it satisfies this combination.
The inverse step $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 + 3R_1$ gives back $3x + 4y = 6$, so any solution of the new system solves the old one.
Both systems have the solution $(-4, \tfrac92)$, and indeed $-4 + 9 = 5$, $-12 + 18 = 6$ and $-2 \cdot \tfrac92 = -9$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Scaling needs $c \ne 0$.
Scaling the equation $x - y = 1$ by $0$ gives $0 = 0$, and the system $x + y = 3$, $x - y = 1$ gains the solution $(3, 0)$, as [[the non-examples|EBR5JMWFAE]] show.

Row addition needs $i \ne j$.
The step $R_1 \leftarrow R_1 + (-1) R_1$ turns $R_1$ into a zero row and can gain solutions.

Row operations must be applied one at a time.
Replacing $R_1$ by $R_1 + R_2$ and $R_2$ by $R_2 + R_1$ at the same time is not a sequence of elementary operations, and it can gain solutions, as the same non-examples show.

The scalars must form a field, so that every $c \ne 0$ has an inverse.
Over the ring $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, scaling $x = 1$ by $2$ gives $2x = 2$, which has the extra solution $x = 3$, since $2 \cdot 3 = 6 = 2$ there.

### What follows

Row equivalent systems have the same solution set, as the pages on [[row equivalence|006BHFWMAP]] and [[its properties|E4GKK727ZM]] explain.
If $B$ comes from $A$ by a sequence of row operations, then $B = PA$ with $P$ an invertible product of [[elementary matrices|01REXW3WWE]].
Row operations on $[A \mid B]$ act on both blocks at once: $E[A \mid B] = [EA \mid EB]$.

### The converse fails for inconsistent systems

Two systems with the same solution set need not have row equivalent augmented matrices.
The systems $x = 0$, $x = 1$ and $0x = 1$, $0x = 0$ both have no solution.
But their reduced forms $\left[\begin{array}{c|c} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{array}\right]$ and $\left[\begin{array}{c|c} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{array}\right]$ differ.
For consistent systems with the same number of equations and unknowns, the converse is true, and it follows from the [[uniqueness of the RREF|BF9SMZMJZE]].

### Watch out

"Multiply an equation by $x$" or "divide by an expression that can be $0$" is not an elementary row operation, and it can change the solution set.
Dividing $x(x - 1) = 0$ by $x$, for instance, loses the solution $x = 0$.

## History

Elimination by adding multiples of one equation to another is the method of the "Fangcheng" chapter of the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*.
The theorem says, in modern terms, why that method neither loses nor invents solutions.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When we solve equations by hand, we swap equations, multiply an equation by a nonzero number, or add a multiple of one equation to another.
Each of these moves can be undone by a move of the same kind.
So the new equations are true exactly when the old ones are.
This is why elimination never loses a solution and never invents one.

### L2 human

The typical use is to justify every step of [[Gaussian elimination|0JVJ2WCXZH]].
After any number of steps, the reduced system has exactly the solutions of the original one.
So you may read the solutions off the final table without checking each step again.
A check in the original equations is still a good habit, because it catches arithmetic slips.

### L3 human

Part 2 says that row operations are left multiplications by invertible matrices.
Since $EAx = Eb$ and $Ax = b$ are equivalent for any invertible $E$, part 3 holds for every invertible $E$, not only for elementary ones.
Over a field, every invertible matrix is a product of elementary matrices, so row equivalence means $B = PA$ with $P$ in the general linear group.
Over the integers, scaling is allowed only by $\pm 1$, and the orbits of $\mathrm{GL}_m(\mathbb{Z})$ on integer matrices lead to the Hermite normal form.
