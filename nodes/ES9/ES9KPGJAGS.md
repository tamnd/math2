## Summary

Keller's geometrical theory of diffraction is a high-frequency method that adds diffracted rays to the rays of [[geometrical optics|90F3NWRHVD]].
Diffracted rays start at edges, corners and grazing points of obstacles, and they obey a generalised form of [[Fermat's principle|RKX9XJ0RVW]].
The field carried by each diffracted ray is fixed by a diffraction coefficient taken from an exactly solvable model problem, such as [[Sommerfeld's half-plane problem|SRGSR8X5SF]].
It explains how light reaches the geometrical shadow, and its failure at shadow boundaries led to the uniform theory of diffraction.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Geometrical optics says that the shadow of an obstacle is perfectly dark, which is not quite true.
Keller's idea is that sharp edges act like new sources of rays, which spread into the shadow.
A ray that hits an edge at some angle produces a whole cone of diffracted rays around the edge, all making the same angle with it.
These extra rays are weak when the wavelength is small, but they explain the faint light seen behind sharp edges.
