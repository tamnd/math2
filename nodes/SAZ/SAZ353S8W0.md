## Statement

The length of a cross product is an area, and it vanishes exactly for parallel vectors.
Let $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^3$ be nonzero vectors, and let $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ be the [[angle|0ZEHZY85E6]] between them.
Then the [[length|0XKBKGTCPC]] of the cross product is
$$\|a \times b\| = \|a\| \, \|b\| \sin \theta.$$
This number is the area of the parallelogram with sides $a$ and $b$ drawn from one point.
For all $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^3$, zero or not, $a \times b = 0$ if and only if $a$ and $b$ are parallel, that is, one of them is a multiple of the other.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The vectors must be nonzero for the angle to exist.
If $a = 0$, the product is $0$ and the parallelogram collapses to a segment or a point of area $0$, so nothing is lost; the angle is simply undefined.

The angle must be taken in $[0, \pi]$.
There $\sin \theta \geq 0$, so the right side is never negative, as a length must be.

### What it gives you

The first gift is the area of a triangle in space from its corners alone.
The triangle with vertices $P$, $Q$ and $R$ is half a parallelogram, so its area is
$$\tfrac{1}{2} \|(Q - P) \times (R - P)\|.$$
The [[area example|Q6QW96GXK3]] uses this to find the area $\tfrac{7}{2}$.

The second gift is a test for parallel vectors.
Two vectors are parallel exactly when their cross product is $0$, so three points $P, Q, R$ lie on one line exactly when $(Q - P) \times (R - P) = 0$.

The formula also says when the product is largest.
For given lengths, $\|a \times b\|$ is greatest when $a \perp b$, and then $\|a \times b\| = \|a\| \, \|b\|$.

Finally, the area is base times height, so the height is area divided by base.
The distance from a point $P$ to the line through $A$ with direction $d$ is $\|(P - A) \times d\| / \|d\|$, as the page on [[distances|C6GAS92QHX]] explains.

### The converse

Suppose $a$ and $b$ are not parallel, and $n$ is a vector perpendicular to both, of length $\|a\| \, \|b\| \sin \theta$, with $(a, b, n)$ right-handed.
Then $n = a \times b$.
Without the orientation condition, both $a \times b$ and $-(a \times b)$ qualify.

### Beyond three dimensions

For $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^n$ the area of the parallelogram is $\sqrt{\|a\|^2 \|b\|^2 - (a \cdot b)^2} = \|a\| \, \|b\| \sin \theta$ in every dimension, by Lagrange's identity in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
Only the vector $a \times b$ is special to $\mathbb{R}^3$, not the area.
One dimension up, the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by three vectors is the absolute value of the [[scalar triple product|34VJYG6GVD]].

## History

Reading the length of the cross product as an area belongs to the vector analysis of Gibbs and Heaviside in the 1880s.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two arrows from one point span a parallelogram.
The cross product of the arrows is a new arrow whose length is the area of that parallelogram.
For $(2, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 3, 0)$ the parallelogram is a $2$ by $3$ rectangle with area $6$.
The cross product is $(0, 0, 6)$, and its length is $6$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical problem: find the area of the parallelogram spanned by $a = (1, 1, 0)$ and $b = (1, 0, 1)$.
The cross product is
$$a \times b = (1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0, \; 0 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 1, \; 1 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1) = (1, -1, -1),$$
so the area is $\|a \times b\| = \sqrt{3}$.
Check by the angle: $\|a\| = \|b\| = \sqrt{2}$ and $a \cdot b = 1$, so $\cos \theta = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $\theta = 60^\circ$.
Then $\|a\| \, \|b\| \sin \theta = 2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \sqrt{3}$, as before.
Watch out: a triangle has half the area of the parallelogram, so do not forget the factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

### L3 human

The length of $a \times b$ is the $2$-dimensional volume of the parallelogram, which is $\sqrt{\det G}$ for the Gram matrix $G$ of $a$ and $b$.
The same formula gives the $k$-dimensional volume of a parallelotope in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
It is also the source of the surface area formula $\iint \|r_u \times r_v\| \, du \, dv$ of calculus.
The link between determinants and volume is the topic of the [[determinant as signed volume|04X3PR72VJ]].
