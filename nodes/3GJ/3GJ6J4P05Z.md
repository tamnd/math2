## Example

Let $\mathbb{Q}^\times = \mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\}$ be the set of nonzero [[rational numbers|0NCJ5WXN6Z]], with the usual multiplication.
We check that $(\mathbb{Q}^\times, \cdot)$ is a [[group|G5S1M9]].

1. Closure. Let $x, y \in \mathbb{Q}^\times$. The product of two rational numbers is rational. If $xy = 0$, then $x = 0$ or $y = 0$, which is false. So $xy \in \mathbb{Q}^\times$.
2. Associativity. Multiplication of rational numbers is associative, so $(xy)z = x(yz)$ for all $x, y, z \in \mathbb{Q}^\times$.
3. Identity. The number $1$ is in $\mathbb{Q}^\times$, and $1 \cdot x = x = x \cdot 1$ for each $x$.
4. Inverses. Let $x = a/b$ with integers $a, b$, $b \neq 0$. Since $x \neq 0$, also $a \neq 0$. Then $y = b/a$ is a nonzero rational number, and $xy = ab/(ba) = 1 = yx$.

Thus $\mathbb{Q}^\times$ is a group.
Since $xy = yx$ for all $x, y$, it is an [[abelian group|082GHNPJ53]].

## Remarks

The example shows why the number $0$ must be removed.
In $(\mathbb{Q}, \cdot)$, the number $0$ has no inverse, because $0 \cdot y = 0 \neq 1$ for each $y$.
In the same way, the nonzero real numbers $\mathbb{R}^\times$ and the nonzero complex numbers $\mathbb{C}^\times$ are groups under multiplication.
When we change $\mathbb{Q}$ to $\mathbb{Z}$, the result is not a group: [[the integers under multiplication are not a group|NH7XE98CMF]], and $\mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}$ is not a group either, because $2$ has no inverse in it.
The positive rational numbers form a group inside $\mathbb{Q}^\times$, because a product and an inverse of positive numbers are positive.
The negative rational numbers do not, because $(-1)(-1) = 1$ is positive.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take all fractions except zero, and combine two of them by multiplication.
The product of two nonzero fractions is a nonzero fraction.
Multiplying by $1$ changes nothing.
Each fraction can be undone: multiply $\tfrac{2}{3}$ by $\tfrac{3}{2}$ and you get $1$.
Zero is left out, because no fraction times zero gives $1$.

### L2 human

We find the inverse of a given element and check it.

1. Take $x = -\tfrac{4}{7}$. Write it as $a/b$ with $a = -4$ and $b = 7$.
2. The candidate inverse is $b/a = 7/(-4) = -\tfrac{7}{4}$.
3. The product is $\left(-\tfrac{4}{7}\right)\left(-\tfrac{7}{4}\right) = \tfrac{28}{28} = 1$.
4. So $x^{-1} = -\tfrac{7}{4}$.

The typical use of this group is in algebra with fractions: dividing by $x$ is the same as multiplying by $x^{-1}$.
A trap is to think that the inverse of $x$ is $-x$; that is the inverse for addition, and for multiplication $(-x) \cdot x = -x^2 \neq 1$ when $x = 2$.
