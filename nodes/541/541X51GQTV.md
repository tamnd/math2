## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $1 \le k < n$, and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$.
For a set $S \subseteq \set{1, \dots, n}$, write $\Sigma S$ for the sum of its elements and $S^c$ for its complement.
Fix a set $I$ of $k$ row indices.
Then
$$\det A = \sum_{J} (-1)^{\Sigma I + \Sigma J} \det A_{I,J} \det A_{I^c, J^c},$$
where $J$ runs over the $k$-element sets of column indices, and $\det A_{I,J}$ is the [[minor|0SD528P0EK]] with rows $I$ and columns $J$.
The same holds with the roles of rows and columns exchanged.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The rows of $A_{I^c, J^c}$ and its columns must be the complements of $I$ and $J$; other pairs of minors give a different sum.
The sign $(-1)^{\Sigma I + \Sigma J}$ is needed, as in the ordinary expansion.

### Consequences

- For $k = 1$ and $I = \set{i}$, the sum is the [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]] along row $i$.
- Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} P & Q \\ 0 & R \end{pmatrix}$ be block triangular, with $R$ of size $m$, and let $I$ be the set of the last $m$ rows.
  Every $J \neq I$ contains a column of the zero block, so $\det A_{I,J} = 0$.
  Only $J = I$ remains, and this gives $\det A = \det R \det P$, the [[block triangular rule|0J9TVTNB4K]].

### Converse

The minors $\det A_{I,J}$ for fixed $I$ and the complementary minors determine $\det A$, but not $A$.

### Generalizations

The expansion is the case of top degree of the multiplication $\Lambda^k K^n \otimes \Lambda^{n-k} K^n \to \Lambda^n K^n$ in the exterior algebra.
The [[Cauchy-Binet formula|08WE2W7DYS]] is a related sum over $k$-element sets of products of minors.

## History

Laplace gave this expansion in terms of complementary minors in 1772.
Vandermonde had already given a special case.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The usual expansion takes one row at a time.
The general expansion takes several rows at once.
Each term multiplies a small determinant cut from these rows by the determinant of the part that is left over.

### L2 human

Let $M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $I = \set{1, 2}$, so $\Sigma I = 3$.

1. $J = \set{1, 2}$: sign $+$, minors $3$ and $\det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 3$, term $9$.
2. $J = \set{1, 3}$: sign $-$, minors $-1$ and $-1$, term $-1$.
3. $J = \set{1, 4}$: sign $+$, minors $5$ and $-5$, term $-25$.
4. $J = \set{2, 3}$: sign $+$, minors $-5$ and $5$, term $-25$.
5. $J = \set{2, 4}$: sign $-$, minors $1$ and $1$, term $-1$.
6. $J = \set{3, 4}$: sign $+$, minors $8$ and $8$, term $64$.
7. So $\det M = 9 - 1 - 25 - 25 - 1 + 64 = 21$.
8. This agrees with the value $21$ found by [[Dodgson condensation|FZJMAYK2DN]] and by row reduction.

The typical use is a matrix with a block of zeros.
A trap: the sign uses the sums of the indices, not the number of indices.

### L3 human

Choose a basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ and write $e_I = e_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge e_{i_k}$.
Then $e_I \wedge e_{I^c} = (-1)^{\Sigma I - k(k+1)/2} e_1 \wedge \dots \wedge e_n$, which is the source of the sign.
