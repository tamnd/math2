## Statement

The general Laplace expansion takes several rows at once and writes the determinant as a sum of products of a minor from those rows and the minor that is left over.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $1 \le k < n$, and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$.
For a set $S \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$, write $\Sigma S$ for the sum of its elements and $S^c$ for its complement.
For sets $I$ of rows and $J$ of columns with the same number of elements, let $A_{I,J}$ be the submatrix of $A$ with rows in $I$ and columns in $J$, so that $\det A_{I,J}$ is a [[minor|0SD528P0EK]].
Fix a set $I$ of $k$ row indices.
Then
$$\det A = \sum_{J} (-1)^{\Sigma I + \Sigma J} \det A_{I,J} \det A_{I^c, J^c},$$
where $J$ runs over the $k$-element subsets of $\{1, \dots, n\}$.
The same holds with the roles of rows and columns exchanged: for a fixed set $J$ of $k$ columns, the sum runs over the $k$-element sets $I$ of rows.

## Remarks

### Two rows at a time

Take
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 & 1 \\ 4 & 0 & 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$$
and the rows $I = \{1, 3\}$.
These two rows vanish outside columns $1$ and $3$, so every $2 \times 2$ minor on rows $I$ is $0$ except the one with $J = \{1, 3\}$.
That minor and its complementary minor on rows $\{2, 4\}$ and columns $\{2, 4\}$ are
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = -3, \qquad \det \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix} = 16.$$
The sign is $(-1)^{(1+3) + (1+3)} = +1$, so $\det A = -3 \cdot 16 = -48$.
The ordinary expansion along one row would have needed two $3 \times 3$ determinants.

### The ordinary expansion and block matrices

For $k = 1$ and $I = \{i\}$, the sets $J$ are single columns $\{j\}$, the minor $\det A_{I,J}$ is the entry $a_{ij}$, and the complementary minor is $M_{ij}$.
So the formula becomes the [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]] along row $i$.

The other standard use is a block triangular matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} P & Q \\ 0 & R \end{pmatrix}$, with $R$ of size $m < n$.
Let $I$ be the set of the last $m$ rows.
Every set $J$ other than the last $m$ columns contains a column of the zero block, so $\det A_{I,J}$ has a zero column and vanishes.
Only $J = I$ remains, with sign $(-1)^{2 \Sigma I} = +1$, and the expansion gives $\det A = \det R \det P$, the [[block triangular rule|0J9TVTNB4K]].

### Why the complements and the signs matter

The second factor must use exactly the rows $I^c$ and the columns $J^c$, so that each term of the Leibniz formula is counted once.
The sign depends on the sums $\Sigma I$ and $\Sigma J$ of the indices, not on the number $k$ of indices.
For $k = 1$ this is the checkerboard sign $(-1)^{i+j}$.

### Generalizations

The expansion is the top degree part of the product $\Lambda^k K^n \otimes \Lambda^{n-k} K^n \to \Lambda^n K^n$ in the [[exterior algebra|0MDMZ965VE]].
The [[Cauchy-Binet formula|08WE2W7DYS]] is a related sum over $k$-element sets, with products of minors of two different matrices.

## History

The expansion carries the name of Pierre-Simon Laplace, like the expansion along one row.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The usual expansion takes one row at a time and multiplies each entry by a smaller determinant.
The general expansion takes several rows at once.
Each term multiplies a small determinant cut out of the chosen rows by the determinant of the part that is left when those rows and columns are removed.
It pays off when the chosen rows have many zeros, because then most of the small determinants are $0$.

### L2 human

Here is the full expansion of a matrix without zeros.
Let
$$M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad I = \{1, 2\}, \qquad \Sigma I = 3.$$
There are six sets $J$ of two columns.
For each, we list the sign $(-1)^{3 + \Sigma J}$, the minor on rows $1, 2$, the complementary minor on rows $3, 4$, and the product:

1. $J = \{1, 2\}$: sign $+$, minors $3$ and $3$, term $9$.
2. $J = \{1, 3\}$: sign $-$, minors $-1$ and $-1$, term $-1$.
3. $J = \{1, 4\}$: sign $+$, minors $5$ and $-5$, term $-25$.
4. $J = \{2, 3\}$: sign $+$, minors $-5$ and $5$, term $-25$.
5. $J = \{2, 4\}$: sign $-$, minors $1$ and $1$, term $-1$.
6. $J = \{3, 4\}$: sign $+$, minors $8$ and $8$, term $64$.

For example, in line 3 the minors are $\det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = 5$ and $\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = -5$.
The sum is $9 - 1 - 25 - 25 - 1 + 64 = 21$.
Check: [[Dodgson condensation|FZJMAYK2DN]] gives the same value $21$.
Without zeros, the method costs twelve $2 \times 2$ determinants, so its main use is for matrices with blocks of zeros.

### L3 human

Choose the standard basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ and write $e_I = e_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge e_{i_k}$ for $I = \{i_1 < \dots < i_k\}$.
Then $e_I \wedge e_{I^c} = (-1)^{\Sigma I - k(k+1)/2} e_1 \wedge \dots \wedge e_n$, and the two such factors for $I$ and $J$ combine to the sign $(-1)^{\Sigma I + \Sigma J}$.
In this language the expansion says that the action of $A$ on $\Lambda^n$ is the pairing of its actions on $\Lambda^k$ and on $\Lambda^{n-k}$.
