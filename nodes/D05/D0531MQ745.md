## Example

Claim: for all integers $a$ and $b$, if $4 \mid ab$, then $4 \mid a$ or $4 \mid b$.
A student writes: "We prove the contrapositive; assume $4 \nmid ab$; then $4$ divides neither $a$ nor $b$, because a multiple of $4$ times any integer is a multiple of $4$."
This is not a [[proof by contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]], and we find the condition that fails.

1. The claim has the form $P \Rightarrow Q$, with $P$ the statement "$4 \mid ab$" and $Q$ the statement "$4 \mid a$ or $4 \mid b$".
2. The contrapositive is $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$: if $4 \nmid a$ and $4 \nmid b$, then $4 \nmid ab$.
3. The student assumed $\neg P$ and concluded $\neg Q$.
   That is the inverse $\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q$, not the contrapositive.
4. The inverse is the contrapositive of the [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] $Q \Rightarrow P$, so it is equivalent to the converse, not to the claim.
5. The student's argument is in fact a correct proof of the converse: if $4 \mid a$ or $4 \mid b$, then $4 \mid ab$.
6. The claim itself is false.
   For $a = b = 2$ we get $ab = 4$, so $4 \mid ab$, but $4 \nmid 2$.

## Remarks

The example shows why the order of the negations matters.
The contrapositive negates both parts and swaps them, while the inverse negates both parts and keeps their order.
A proof of the inverse can be perfectly valid and still prove the wrong statement.

A true claim of the same shape uses a prime in place of $4$.
For a prime $p$, if $p \mid ab$, then $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$, which is [[Euclid's lemma|051VYKA1EX]].
Its correct contrapositive is "if $p \nmid a$ and $p \nmid b$, then $p \nmid ab$".
The number $4$ is not prime, and the pair $a = b = 2$ shows that the claim fails for it.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take the sentence "if an animal is a dog, then it has four legs".
Its contrapositive is "if an animal does not have four legs, then it is not a dog", and this says the same thing.
Its inverse is "if an animal is not a dog, then it does not have four legs".
The inverse is a different sentence, and it is false, because a cat has four legs.
A proof of the inverse does not prove the original sentence.

### L2 human

Sort four statements about an integer $n$ into equivalent pairs.
The original "if $6 \mid n$, then $n$ is even" is true, and so is its contrapositive "if $n$ is odd, then $6 \nmid n$".
The converse "if $n$ is even, then $6 \mid n$" is false, since $n = 2$ fails.
The inverse "if $6 \nmid n$, then $n$ is odd" is false for the same $n = 2$, and it stands or falls with the converse.
So the four statements form two pairs, original with contrapositive and converse with inverse.
The typical use of this check comes before writing "we prove the contrapositive": write out $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ and compare it with the claim.
Watch out: when the converse happens to be true, a proof of the inverse looks correct, and the error stays hidden until a case where the converse fails.
