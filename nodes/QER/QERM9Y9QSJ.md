## Summary

A Kelvin wave is a wave in a rotating fluid that is trapped against a boundary, such as a coastline.
It can also be trapped against the equator, where the Coriolis parameter changes sign.
The velocity across the boundary is zero.
Along the boundary the wave moves like a non-rotating gravity wave, at speed $\sqrt{gH}$ in a layer of depth $H$.
Its amplitude decays away from the boundary over a [[Rossby radius of deformation|MT3A0DDAA8]].
Kelvin waves carry tides along coasts and play a central role in the dynamics of El Niño.

## Explanations

### L1 human

On a rotating planet, a wave running along a coast is pushed against the coast by the rotation.
The water piles up at the coast, and this pile-up pushes back just enough to keep the wave going straight.
The result is a wave that hugs the coast and dies away within some tens to thousands of kilometres offshore.
In the northern hemisphere such a wave always keeps the coast on its right.
At the equator the deflection changes direction, so the rotation pushes the fluid towards the equator from both sides, and an equatorial Kelvin wave travels eastward along it.
