## Statement

Let $L$ be a signature, let $\mathcal{N}$ be an $L$-[[structure|983GZBQYT4]] with domain $N$, and let $\mathcal{M}$ be a [[substructure|676H7EGTKJ]] of $\mathcal{N}$ with domain $M \subseteq N$.
Then the following are equivalent.

1. $\mathcal{M}$ is an [[elementary substructure|8WZF9SBP39]] of $\mathcal{N}$.
2. For every $L$-[[formula|HYJ10AF6RT]] $\varphi(x_1, \dots, x_n, y)$ and all $a_1, \dots, a_n \in M$: if $\mathcal{N} \models \exists y \, \varphi(a_1, \dots, a_n, y)$, then there is $b \in M$ with $\mathcal{N} \models \varphi(a_1, \dots, a_n, b)$.

Condition 2 mentions truth only in $\mathcal{N}$.
It says that every formula with parameters in $M$ which has a witness in $\mathcal{N}$ has a witness in $M$.
A proof is in [[the proof of the Tarski-Vaught test|T0GQFH30JS]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The test must range over all formulas, not only over the quantifier-free ones.
Let $\mathcal{N}$ be $\Q$ with one new element $p$ placed above all rationals, in the signature $\set{<}$, and let $\mathcal{M} = (\Q, <)$.
Every quantifier-free $\varphi(\bar a, y)$ satisfied by $p$ is also satisfied by $\max(\bar a) + 1$, because both lie above all $a_i$ and differ from them.
But $\mathcal{N} \models \exists y \, \forall z \, \neg(y < z)$, and no rational is a greatest element of $\mathcal{N}$; so condition 2 fails, and $\mathcal{M} \not\preceq \mathcal{N}$.
The witness $b$ must be found in $M$, not in $N$: every substructure of $\mathcal{N}$ has witnesses in $N$.
The parameters must range over all finite tuples of $M$: with no parameters, condition 2 for $(2\Z, <) \subseteq (\Z, <)$ holds, since the translations of $\Z$ are automorphisms, although the substructure is not elementary: with the parameters $0$ and $2$, the formula $0 < y \land y < 2$ has the witness $1$ in $\Z$ and none in $2\Z$. For $(\N_{>0}, <) \subseteq (\N, <)$, a formula without parameters already fails; see [[this non-example|SW2EYVJJGF]].

### Consequences

- The automorphism criterion: suppose that for every finite tuple $\bar a$ of $M$ and every $b \in N$ there is an automorphism $\sigma$ of $\mathcal{N}$ with $\sigma(\bar a) = \bar a$ and $\sigma(b) \in M$. Then $\mathcal{M} \preceq \mathcal{N}$, because $\sigma$ moves each witness $b$ into $M$ and preserves $\varphi(\bar a, y)$. This gives $(\Q, <) \preceq (\R, <)$; see [[this example|AY1QQFQZ41]].
- The downward [[Löwenheim-Skolem theorem|484M0TZ1S7]] and the elementarity of [[Skolem hulls|NKQY9NB955]] follow by closing a set under witnesses.
- A nonempty subset $A \subseteq N$ that satisfies condition 2 is automatically the domain of a substructure: the formulas $y = c$ and $y = f(\bar x)$ have their unique witnesses in $A$.

### Converse

The theorem is an equivalence, so both directions hold.
The direction from 1 to 2 is the easy one.
The value of the test is the direction from 2 to 1: condition 2 is checked in $\mathcal{N}$ alone, while the definition of $\preceq$ compares truth in two structures.

### Generalizations

An embedding $h \colon \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{N}$ is elementary if and only if, for all $\varphi(\bar x, y)$ and $\bar a$ in $M$ with $\mathcal{N} \models \exists y \, \varphi(h(\bar a), y)$, some $b \in M$ has $\mathcal{N} \models \varphi(h(\bar a), h(b))$.
The same proof works for any set of formulas closed under subformulas, so the test also holds for fragments of infinitary logic.

## History

Alfred Tarski and Robert Vaught proved the test in their 1957 paper "Arithmetical extensions of relational systems", which also introduced elementary extensions.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose a small structure sits inside a big one.
The test says: the small one is an elementary part of the big one when every object that the big structure can describe, using names of small elements, can also be found among the small elements.
For example, the positive integers inside the natural numbers fail the test.
The description "a number below $1$" has the answer $0$ in the natural numbers, but no positive integer fits it.

### L2 human

We use the test to show that the even integers $(2\Z, <)$ are not an elementary substructure of $(\Z, <)$.

1. Take $\varphi(x_1, x_2, y) = x_1 < y \land y < x_2$ and the parameters $a_1 = 0$ and $a_2 = 2$ in $2\Z$.
2. In $\Z$ the element $1$ is a witness, so $\Z \models \exists y \, \varphi(0, 2, y)$.
3. Every witness $b$ satisfies $0 < b < 2$, so $b = 1$, which is not even.
4. So condition 2 fails, and $(2\Z, <) \not\preceq (\Z, <)$.

The typical use of the test is the other direction: to build an elementary substructure by adding witnesses, or to verify one by moving witnesses with automorphisms.
A trap is to ask for witnesses that satisfy $\varphi$ in $\mathcal{M}$; the test asks for truth in $\mathcal{N}$, and that is what makes the induction in the proof work.

### L3 human

The test is a closure condition: $M$ is elementary in $\mathcal{N}$ exactly when $M$ is closed under all the partial "witness functions" of $\mathcal{N}$.
Skolem functions make these witness functions total, so elementary substructures become the substructures of a Skolem expansion, as in [[Skolem functions and Skolem hulls|NKQY9NB955]].
The same pattern appears in set theory, where the Lévy reflection principle and the construction of countable elementary submodels of $V_\theta$ close a set under witnesses.
The induction of the proof also gives the [[elementary chain theorem|NHCRZ74JHH]], and a game form of the condition appears in the [[back-and-forth methods|N64R1MGDBN]].
