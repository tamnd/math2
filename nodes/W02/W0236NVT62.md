## Summary

The theory of computation studies which problems can be solved by an algorithm and how many resources a solution needs.
It makes the idea of an algorithm precise through a [[model of computation|YFHEG4GYHW]], such as the [[Turing machine|758EYCN27B]].
Its three main parts are [[automata theory|2ZSYVRGQ05]], [[computability theory|X14Y880PN5]] and [[computational complexity theory|2FXXY2HSS7]].
It gives the mathematical limits of what computers can do, independent of any real hardware.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Before building a program, one can ask whether any program at all can solve the task.
The theory of computation answers such questions with proofs instead of experiments.
It replaces real computers by simple idealised machines that read and write symbols by fixed rules.
For example, it proves that no program can decide, for every program and input, whether that program eventually stops.
It also sorts solvable problems by how much time or memory they need.
