## Statement

Let $A$ and $B$ be [[finite sets|0BBTH3YSZA]].
Then $|A| = |B|$ if and only if there is a [[bijection|0SNQEF7Z53]] $f : A \to B$.
Here $|A|$ is the [[cardinality|05099E1NMJ]] of $A$, the number of its elements.

The direction "a bijection gives equal sizes" is the one used in counting.
To count a set $A$, we find a bijection from $A$ to a set $B$ whose size we already know, and then $|A| = |B|$.
A proof of this kind is a bijective proof, one of the two forms of a [[combinatorial proof|~a16dfc7e]].
The [[proof|VD65RRN8P2]] proves both directions.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- A bijection is needed, and an injection is not enough. The inclusion map of $\set{1}$ into $\set{1, 2}$ is injective, but the sizes are $1$ and $2$. An injection $A \to B$ gives only $|A| \le |B|$.
- A surjection is not enough either. The map $\set{1, 2} \to \set{1}$ is surjective, and it gives only $|A| \geq |B|$.
- The sets must be finite for $|A|$ to be a natural number. For infinite sets, a bijection still gives "the same size", but this is the definition of [[equinumerous sets|0B0KR7FH2Q]], and it has new features. For example, $n \mapsto n + 1$ is a bijection from $\N$ to the proper subset $\N \setminus \set{0}$, as in [[Hilbert's hotel|0N3Y8G340X]].

### Consequences

- The set of subsets of $\set{1, \dots, n}$ is in bijection with the words of length $n$ in the letters $0$ and $1$, so there are $2^n$ [[subsets|0C5RDJE73X]].
- The map $S \mapsto X \setminus S$ is a bijection from the $k$-subsets to the $(n - k)$-subsets of an $n$-set $X$. This gives the [[symmetry of binomial coefficients|0R7JS3DP3W]].
- [[Stars and bars|009TEMTQKH]]: the solutions of $x_1 + \cdots + x_k = n$ in natural numbers are in bijection with the words of $n$ stars and $k - 1$ bars.
- [[Cayley's formula|0M2SSGSYPE]]: for $n \geq 2$, the [[Prüfer code|900KK0PPYM]] is a bijection from the trees on $\set{1, \dots, n}$ to the sequences of length $n - 2$ in $\set{1, \dots, n}$, so there are $n^{n-2}$ trees.
- [[Dyck words|0R9JR0465V]] of length $2n$ and [[binary trees|02A8EMC83C]] with $n$ internal nodes are in bijection, so both are counted by the [[Catalan numbers|092DRBFKMG]].
- The relation "there is a bijection between $A$ and $B$" is [[symmetric|BJA08CW1XK]], so a bijection in either direction gives $|A| = |B|$.

### Converse

The statement is an "if and only if", so the converse of each direction is part of it.
The direction "equal sizes give a bijection" is true for finite sets: number both sets with $1, \dots, n$ and match equal numbers.
For finite sets of the same size, an injection $A \to B$ is already a bijection, and so is a surjection $A \to B$.
These facts fail for infinite sets: the map $n \mapsto 2n$ from $\N$ to $\N$ is injective but not surjective.

### Generalizations

- Georg Cantor took the existence of a bijection as the definition of "the same size" for all sets. This gives the theory of cardinal numbers.
- The [[Schröder-Bernstein theorem|0A9V6RKQG6]] says: injections $A \to B$ and $B \to A$ give a bijection $A \to B$. For finite sets, the [[finite version|X5R6PJQQVD]] follows from the bijection principle and the injection bound $|A| \le |B|$.
- A weighted form: a bijection $f : A \to B$ with $w(f(a)) = v(a)$ for all $a$ gives $\sum_{a \in A} v(a) = \sum_{b \in B} w(b)$. Bijections that keep a statistic, such as the size of a part, prove identities between generating functions.
- The [[pigeonhole principle|0HGVCWQGY3]] is the injection bound in contrapositive form: when $|A| > |B|$, no map $A \to B$ is injective.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To show that two collections have the same size, we can pair each thing of the first collection with exactly one thing of the second, with nothing left over on either side.
For example, in a dance class each boy dances with exactly one girl, and nobody stands alone.
Then there are as many boys as girls, and we did not need to count either group.
In the same way, the subsets of $\set{1, 2, 3}$ pair with the words of length $3$ in the letters $0$ and $1$: a subset gives the word with $1$ in the positions of its elements.
There are $8$ such words, so there are $8$ subsets.

### L2 human

We count the subsets of $\set{1, \dots, 10}$ with no two consecutive numbers and exactly $3$ elements.

1. Let $A$ be the set of such subsets $\set{a < b < c}$, with $b \geq a + 2$ and $c \geq b + 2$.
2. Define $f(\set{a, b, c}) = \set{a, b - 1, c - 2}$. Then $a < b - 1 < c - 2 \le 8$, so $f$ maps $A$ to the set $B$ of $3$-subsets of $\set{1, \dots, 8}$.
3. Define $g(\set{x < y < z}) = \set{x, y + 1, z + 2}$ for $\set{x, y, z} \in B$. Then $y + 1 \geq x + 2$, $z + 2 \geq y + 3$ and $z + 2 \le 10$, so $g$ maps $B$ to $A$.
4. The maps $f$ and $g$ are inverse to each other: $g(f(\set{a, b, c})) = \set{a, b, c}$ and $f(g(\set{x, y, z})) = \set{x, y, z}$. So $f$ is a bijection.
5. By the bijection principle, $|A| = |B| = \binom{8}{3} = 56$.

The typical use is to replace a set with a condition by a set with no condition, as in step 2.
A trap is to give only the map $f$ and to forget the proof that it is a bijection.
The inverse map $g$ of step 3, with the checks of step 4, is the simplest way to give this proof.

### L3 human

In category language, the cardinality of finite sets is the invariant that classifies the objects of the category of finite sets up to isomorphism, and the bijection principle says exactly this.
So a bijective proof of $|A| = |B|$ is an isomorphism in this category, and it often explains more than a numerical identity: it can keep extra structure, such as a statistic or a group action.
In Mathlib the direction "a bijection gives equal sizes" is `Fintype.card_congr`, which takes an equivalence `α ≃ β`.

Bijective combinatorics looks for explicit bijections behind identities that have algebraic proofs, for example the [[partition identities|EZF8KV8YM6]] of Euler and Glaisher.
Some identities have a [[double counting|0F8YBHF2W3]] proof but no known simple bijective proof, and finding such a bijection is a research problem.
For infinite sets the same definition of equal size leads to Cantor's theory of cardinals and to the [[diagonalization technique|~7da89d71]].
