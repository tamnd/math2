## Example

Let
$$S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
over $\mathbb{R}$.
It is not in the domain of the [[Pfaffian|NH5HWBPWS9]], and no real number can play its role.

1. The [[definition|C9JJD2AVJR]] asks for an alternating matrix, so it needs $a_{21} = -a_{12}$.
2. Here $a_{21} = 1$ and $-a_{12} = -1$, so $S$ is not alternating over $\mathbb{R}$, and the definition does not apply.
3. Could some other number $p$ serve as a Pfaffian, with $p^2 = \det S$?
   The determinant is
$$\det S = 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1 = -1,$$
   and $-1$ is not the square of a real number, so no such $p$ exists.
4. Over the field $\mathbb{F}_2$ the answer changes.
   There $-1 = 1$, so $a_{21} = -a_{12}$ holds and the diagonal is $0$, and $S$ is alternating.
   Then $\operatorname{pf}(S) = a_{12} = 1$, and $\det S = -1 = 1 = 1^2$, as the square rule requires.

## Remarks

The condition that fails over $\mathbb{R}$ is $a_{ji} = -a_{ij}$.
The formula $a_{12}$ for a $2 \times 2$ Pfaffian could be applied to any matrix, but then it loses its link to the determinant.
For $S$ it gives $1$, while $\det S = -1 \ne 1^2$.
So the definition requires the alternating property precisely so that $\operatorname{pf}(A)^2 = \det A$ can hold.

If we change the lower left entry from $1$ to $-1$, we get $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is alternating.
Its Pfaffian is $1$ and its determinant is $0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot (-1) = 1 = 1^2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Pfaffian is defined only for matrices with zeros on the diagonal and with each entry below the diagonal equal to minus its mirror entry.
This matrix has the same entry $1$ on both sides of the diagonal, so it has no Pfaffian.
Its determinant is $-1$, a negative number, so it is not the square of any real number anyway.

### L2 human

Step 4 shows that the answer depends on the ring of scalars: in characteristic $2$ the words symmetric and skew-symmetric mean the same thing.
That is why the definition asks for $a_{ii} = 0$ as a separate condition, since in characteristic $2$ it does not follow from $a_{ji} = -a_{ij}$.
Watch out: do not apply the matching formula to a matrix that is not alternating, because the result has no meaning.
