## Example

Let LK$^-$ be LK of the [[definition|PZX9J3VEMT]] without cut and without the contraction rules CL and CR.
In LK$^-$ the sequent $\Rightarrow p \vee \neg p, p \vee \neg p$ is derivable.

1. $p \Rightarrow p$ is an axiom.
2. ${\neg}$R gives $\Rightarrow p, \neg p$.
3. ${\vee}$R, with $\neg p$ as the right disjunct, gives $\Rightarrow p, p \vee \neg p$.
4. XR gives $\Rightarrow p \vee \neg p, p$.
5. ${\vee}$R, with $p$ as the left disjunct, gives $\Rightarrow p \vee \neg p, p \vee \neg p$.

But $\Rightarrow p \vee \neg p$ is not derivable in LK$^-$.

6. The antecedent is empty, so the last step is not a left rule. Exchange needs two formulas.
7. A weakening would come from the empty sequent, and no rule of LK$^-$ has the empty sequent as its conclusion.
8. So the last step is ${\vee}$R, from $\Rightarrow p$ or from $\Rightarrow \neg p$.
9. Neither is valid, so neither is derivable, since LK$^-$ is sound.

So contraction is not admissible in LK$^-$, and LK$^-$ is not a contraction-free calculus.

## Remarks

The [[law of excluded middle|0RYYK8PG4S]] is valid, so LK$^-$ is incomplete.
Its derivation in LK uses CR after step 5.
In G3cp the same sequent has a derivation without contraction: R${\vee}$ keeps both disjuncts in one premise, $\Rightarrow p, \neg p$, which comes from the axiom $p \Rightarrow p, \bot$ by R${\to}$.
So the difference is in the rules: G3cp copies the formulas that a later step needs, and LK$^-$ does not.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Removing the copying rule from Gentzen's calculus is not enough.
Without it, the calculus can prove "$p$ or not $p$" twice in one sequent but not once.
A contraction-free calculus must change the other rules so that copying is never needed.

### L2 human

The test for a contraction-free calculus is whether contraction is admissible, so we look for a sequent with two copies of a formula.
Here the sequent with two copies of $p \vee \neg p$ is derivable and the one with one copy is not.
A trap is to think that any calculus without a contraction rule is contraction-free: the name refers to calculi in which contraction is admissible.
