## Example

Consider the argument:

- Premise 1: all squares are rhombuses.
- Premise 2: all rectangles are parallelograms.
- Conclusion: all squares are parallelograms.

Each sentence is a true A proposition.
We show that the argument is not a categorical syllogism and has no figure, although its conclusion is true.

1. The terms are "squares", "rhombuses", "rectangles" and "parallelograms". There are four of them, and a categorical syllogism has exactly three [[terms|KSJ9W29KXQ]].
2. Premise 1 uses "squares" and "rhombuses", and premise 2 uses "rectangles" and "parallelograms". No term occurs in both premises, so there is no middle term.
3. The [[figure|S4BTTGFR6J]] of a syllogism is the position of its middle term in the premises. Without a middle term the argument has no figure.
4. The form of the argument is "all $A$ are $B$, all $C$ are $D$, so all $A$ are $D$".
5. A second instance of this form: all squares are rhombuses, all triangles are polygons with three sides, so all squares are polygons with three sides.
6. In step 5 both premises are true and the conclusion is false, because a square has four sides. So the form is not valid.
7. The conclusion of the first instance is true for another reason (every square is a rectangle and every rectangle is a parallelogram), but that reason is not among the premises.

## Remarks

The example shows that a true conclusion and true premises do not make an argument valid.
The premises must be linked by a shared term, and that term is what carries the information from one premise to the other.
If premise 1 is replaced by "all squares are rectangles", the term "rectangles" becomes a middle term, and the argument becomes [[Barbara|AC6XQ4KRNT]], which is valid.
The fallacy of four terms often hides in an ambiguous word: when one word is used in two senses, the argument has four terms in fact although it shows three words.
For example, "every square is a rectangle, $9$ is a square, so $9$ is a rectangle" uses "square" for a figure and for a square number.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The two premises talk about different things: one about squares and rhombuses, the other about rectangles and parallelograms.
Nothing connects them, so they cannot lead to a conclusion together.
The conclusion "all squares are parallelograms" happens to be true, but not because of these premises.
If we keep the same pattern and change the words, we can get true premises and a false conclusion.
A correct argument of this kind needs one shared word that links the two premises.

### L2 human

We look at a four-term argument hidden by an ambiguous word.

1. Premise 1: every square is a rectangle.
2. Premise 2: $9$ is a square.
3. Conclusion: $9$ is a rectangle.
4. In premise 1, "square" means a quadrilateral with four equal sides and four right angles.
5. In premise 2, "square" means a number of the form $n^2$, here $9 = 3^2$.
6. So the word "square" stands for two different classes, and the argument has four terms: square figures, rectangles, square numbers and the number $9$.
7. The conclusion is false, or rather meaningless: a number is not a quadrilateral.

The typical use of the four-term test is to check that each term keeps one meaning in the whole argument.
The trap is to count words instead of classes: three words can name four classes.
