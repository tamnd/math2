## Example

Let $T = \set{r, a, b}$ with root $r$ and two leaves $a$, $b$ above it.
Let $V(p) = \set{a}$ and $V(q) = \set{b}$.
We check that this is a [[Beth model|~b3c503cf]] in which $r$ forces $p \vee q$ but neither $p$ nor $q$.

1. The paths are $\set{r, a}$ and $\set{r, b}$.
2. $V(p)$ is an up-set, since $a$ is a leaf.
3. $V(p)$ is barred: a bar for $r$ must meet the path $\set{r, b}$, so it is not inside $\set{a}$. Bars for $a$ inside $V(p)$ contain $a$. The same holds for $V(q)$.
4. The set $\set{a, b}$ is a bar for $r$, since it meets both paths.
5. $a \Vdash p$ and $b \Vdash q$. So every node of the bar forces $p$ or $q$, and $r \Vdash p \vee q$.
6. $r \notin V(p)$ and $r \notin V(q)$, so $r$ forces neither disjunct.

## Remarks

The example shows the new clause for disjunction: the choice of disjunct can depend on the path.
On the same frame, the Kripke clause gives $r \nVdash p \vee q$.
The tree is finite, so its truth values form the Boolean algebra of sets of leaves.
For instance, $r$ also forces $p \vee \neg p$, through the bar $\set{a, b}$, since $b \Vdash \neg p$.
Refuting excluded middle needs an infinite tree; see [[the Beth countermodel to excluded middle|~fe0b6a36]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Tomorrow it will either rain or be dry, and today we do not know which.
On each branch of the future, we will learn one of the two.
So today we know "rain or dry", although we know neither part.
Beth semantics counts this as knowing the disjunction.

### L2 human

1. Add a third leaf $c$ above $r$, with $p$ and $q$ both false at $c$.
2. Now the path $\set{r, c}$ meets no node that forces $p$ or $q$.
3. So no bar for $r$ forces $p$ or $q$ at each node, and $r \nVdash p \vee q$.

The typical use of this example is to compare Beth and Kripke semantics on one frame.
A trap: the bar $\set{a, b}$ is not the same as "some later node forces $p \vee q$"; every path must reach such a node.
