## Example

For a [[polyhedron|0325SGSJAR]], let $V$, $E$ and $F$ be the numbers of vertices, edges and faces.
We collect data and weigh it with the patterns of [[plausible reasoning|~430ce26d]].

1. The data. We count $V$, $E$, $F$ for several polyhedra and compute $V - E + F$.

| Polyhedron | $V$ | $E$ | $F$ | $V - E + F$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| tetrahedron | $4$ | $6$ | $4$ | $2$ |
| cube | $8$ | $12$ | $6$ | $2$ |
| octahedron | $6$ | $12$ | $8$ | $2$ |
| square pyramid | $5$ | $8$ | $5$ | $2$ |
| triangular prism | $6$ | $9$ | $5$ | $2$ |
| pentagonal prism | $10$ | $15$ | $7$ | $2$ |
| dodecahedron | $20$ | $30$ | $12$ | $2$ |
| icosahedron | $12$ | $30$ | $20$ | $2$ |

2. The conjecture $A$: "$V - E + F = 2$ for every polyhedron". Each row is a consequence of $A$ that is true, so $A$ becomes more credible.
3. The weight of the rows. The prisms and the pyramid are new kinds of shape after the five regular solids. Consequences of a new kind add more credibility than more regular solids would add.
4. A test of a new kind. Take a picture frame: a square slab with a square hole, built from four trapezoidal prisms. It has $V = 16$ (eight corners on top, eight below), $E = 32$ and $F = 16$.
5. The count of step 4 gives $V - E + F = 16 - 32 + 16 = 0$. So $A$ is false as stated, by deduction.
6. The repair. Every polyhedron in the table of step 1 is convex, and the frame is not. The conjecture "$V - E + F = 2$ for every convex polyhedron" is consistent with all the data, and it is [[Euler's polyhedron formula|09HT3P35YP]], a theorem.

## Remarks

The example shows the two directions of evidence.
Steps 2 and 3 are plausible inferences: they raise confidence and prove nothing.
Step 5 is a deduction: one false consequence ends the conjecture as stated.

The edge count of the frame in step 4 is $12$ on the top face (four outer, four inner, four between them), $12$ on the bottom face, and $8$ vertical edges.
A frame with $g$ holes gives $V - E + F = 2 - 2g$, which is the [[Euler characteristic|RNDDWDAFJ5]] of a surface of genus $g$.
Imre Lakatos built his book "Proofs and Refutations" on the history of this conjecture and its counterexamples.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Count the corners, edges and faces of a cube: $8$, $12$ and $6$.
Corners minus edges plus faces gives $2$.
A pyramid, a prism and other solids give $2$ as well.
After many solids, a person starts to believe that the answer is always $2$.
A picture frame gives $0$, so the rule needs a condition: it works for solids without holes.

### L2 human

We check the triangular prism row of step 1.

1. Vertices: the two triangles have $3$ corners each, so $V = 6$.
2. Edges: each triangle has $3$ edges, and $3$ vertical edges join them, so $E = 9$.
3. Faces: $2$ triangles and $3$ rectangles, so $F = 5$.
4. So $V - E + F = 6 - 9 + 5 = 2$.

A prism over an $n$-gon has $V = 2n$, $E = 3n$ and $F = n + 2$, so $V - E + F = 2$ for every $n$.
This is a whole family of verified consequences, but they all have the same kind, so together they add less credibility than their number suggests.
A trap: a table built only from convex shapes cannot detect that convexity is needed.
