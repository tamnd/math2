## Example

Problem: in a triangle $ABC$ whose angles at $B$ and $C$ are acute, construct a square $PQRS$ with $P$ and $Q$ on side $BC$, $R$ on side $CA$ and $S$ on side $AB$.
The four conditions together are hard to meet at once.
We [[vary the problem|~9b655a34]]: drop the condition that $R$ lies on $CA$, and keep the other three.

1. Take any point $S_0$ on $AB$, let $P_0$ be its foot on $BC$, and complete the square $P_0 Q_0 R_0 S_0$ on the side of $P_0$ towards $C$.
2. This square meets three of the four conditions. Every square that meets these three conditions is the image of $P_0 Q_0 R_0 S_0$ under a [[homothety|0ZG1PNEP35]] with centre $B$.
3. So the free vertex $R$ of these squares runs along the ray from $B$ through $R_0$.
4. Restore the dropped condition: $R$ must lie on $CA$. So $R$ is the intersection of the ray $B R_0$ with $CA$.
5. From $R$, drop the perpendicular to $BC$ to get $Q$, and complete the square $PQRS$.

A numeric case: $B = (0, 0)$, $C = (6, 0)$, $A = (2, 4)$.
The line $AB$ is $y = 2x$, and the line $CA$ is $y = 6 - x$.

1. Take $S_0 = (1, 2)$ on $AB$. Then $P_0 = (1, 0)$, $Q_0 = (3, 0)$ and $R_0 = (3, 2)$.
2. The ray $B R_0$ is $y = \frac{2}{3} x$. It meets $y = 6 - x$ where $\frac{5}{3} x = 6$, so $R = (3.6, 2.4)$.
3. The square has side $2.4$: $Q = (3.6, 0)$, $P = (1.2, 0)$ and $S = (1.2, 2.4)$.
4. Check: $S$ lies on $y = 2x$, since $2 \cdot 1.2 = 2.4$, and $R$ lies on $y = 6 - x$, since $6 - 3.6 = 2.4$.

The ratio of the homothety is $\frac{2.4}{2} = 1.2$.

## Remarks

Dropping a condition replaced one unknown square by a whole family of squares.
The family varies in a simple way, so the locus of the free vertex is a line, and the dropped condition then picks one member.
Pólya used this problem in How to Solve It to show how keeping only a part of the condition leads to a locus.

The side of the square is $\frac{a h}{a + h}$, where $a = BC$ and $h$ is the height from $A$.
Here $a = 6$ and $h = 4$, so the side is $\frac{24}{10} = 2.4$, which agrees.
The acute angles at $B$ and $C$ make $P$ and $Q$ lie on the segment $BC$ and not on its extension.

## Explanations

### L1 human

We want a square that touches all three sides of a triangle, with one side of the square on the base.
That is hard, so we first forget one demand: the top right corner may be anywhere.
Then it is easy to draw many such squares, small and large, all leaning on the left side of the triangle.
Their top right corners lie on one straight line from the bottom left corner of the triangle.
Where this line meets the right side of the triangle is the corner we need.

### L2 human

We prove that the free vertex runs along a ray, as used in step 3.

1. Let $PQRS$ be any square with $P, Q$ on line $BC$, $S$ on line $AB$, and $Q$ on the side of $P$ towards $C$.
2. Let $k = \frac{BS}{BS_0}$. The homothety $H$ with centre $B$ and ratio $k$ maps $S_0$ to $S$, since both lie on the same ray from $B$.
3. $H$ maps line $BC$ to itself, maps perpendiculars to perpendiculars, and multiplies lengths by $k$. So it maps $P_0$, the foot of $S_0$, to $P$, the foot of $S$.
4. The square on $P_0 S_0$ goes to the square on $PS$ on the same side, so $H(Q_0) = Q$ and $H(R_0) = R$.
5. Hence $R = H(R_0)$ lies on the ray from $B$ through $R_0$.

The typical use: drop one condition, find the family of solutions of the weaker problem, then restore the condition.

A trap: the locus must be proved, not read off a figure; here the homothety gives the proof.
