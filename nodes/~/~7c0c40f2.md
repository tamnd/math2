## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. By the [[definition|0W809K6AW0]], $(A^{\mathsf{T}})_{ij} = a_{ji}$, so $A^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, a $3 \times 2$ matrix.
2. Row 2 of $A$ is $(4, 5, 6)$, and it is column 2 of $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
3. Transposing again returns the rows to their places: $(A^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}} = A$.
4. $A + B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 & 3 \\ 6 & 5 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, so $(A + B)^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 6 \\ 3 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
5. Also $A^{\mathsf{T}} + B^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 6 \\ 3 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, the same matrix.
6. $(2A)^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 8 \\ 4 & 10 \\ 6 & 12 \end{pmatrix} = 2A^{\mathsf{T}}$.

## Remarks

The product $AB$ is not defined, but $AB^{\mathsf{T}}$ is, and $(AB^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}} = BA^{\mathsf{T}}$ by the [[product rule for the transpose|09GK5PS824]].
Here $AB^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $BA^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The rows of $A$ become the columns of $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
Adding first and then transposing gives the same result as transposing first and then adding.

### L2 human

Steps 4 and 5 show that the transpose is a linear map from $M_{2 \times 3}$ to $M_{3 \times 2}$.
It is a bijection, because it is its own inverse up to the change of size.
