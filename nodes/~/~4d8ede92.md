## Example

We give a cut-free derivation of $\Rightarrow \neg p \vee \neg\neg p$ in Avron's calculus HLC from the [[definition of hypersequent calculus|~b56e1b30]].
Here $\neg A$ abbreviates $A \to \bot$.
The communication rule is
$$\dfrac{G \mid \Gamma_1, \Delta_1 \Rightarrow \Pi_1 \qquad G \mid \Gamma_2, \Delta_2 \Rightarrow \Pi_2}{G \mid \Gamma_1, \Gamma_2 \Rightarrow \Pi_1 \mid \Delta_1, \Delta_2 \Rightarrow \Pi_2} \ (\mathsf{com})$$

1. $p \Rightarrow p$ is an axiom.
2. $\bot \Rightarrow$ is an axiom.
3. Apply ${\to}$L to steps 1 and 2, with $\Gamma = p$, $A = p$, $B = \bot$ and $\Delta$ empty. This gives $p \to \bot, p \Rightarrow$, that is, $\neg p, p \Rightarrow$.
4. Apply $\mathsf{com}$ with both premises equal to step 3 and $G$ empty. Take $\Gamma_1 = \Gamma_2 = p$, $\Delta_1 = \Delta_2 = \neg p$ and $\Pi_1 = \Pi_2$ empty.
5. Each premise $\Gamma_i, \Delta_i \Rightarrow \Pi_i$ is $p, \neg p \Rightarrow$, which is step 3.
6. The conclusion is $p, p \Rightarrow \; \mid \; \neg p, \neg p \Rightarrow$.
7. Internal contraction on $p$ in the first component gives $p \Rightarrow \; \mid \; \neg p, \neg p \Rightarrow$.
8. Internal contraction on $\neg p$ in the second component gives $p \Rightarrow \; \mid \; \neg p \Rightarrow$.
9. Internal weakening on the right of both components gives $p \Rightarrow \bot \mid \neg p \Rightarrow \bot$.
10. ${\to}$R on the first component gives $\Rightarrow p \to \bot \mid \neg p \Rightarrow \bot$, that is, $\Rightarrow \neg p \mid \neg p \Rightarrow \bot$.
11. ${\to}$R on the second component gives $\Rightarrow \neg p \mid \Rightarrow \neg p \to \bot$, that is, $\Rightarrow \neg p \mid \Rightarrow \neg\neg p$.
12. ${\vee}$R on each component gives $\Rightarrow \neg p \vee \neg\neg p \mid \Rightarrow \neg p \vee \neg\neg p$.
13. External contraction gives $\Rightarrow \neg p \vee \neg\neg p$.

No step uses cut.

We also check that the formula is not intuitionistically valid, so the communication rule is necessary.

14. Take the Kripke model with a root $r$ and two incomparable worlds $a$ and $b$ above $r$, with $p$ true at $a$ only.
15. At $r$, $\neg p$ fails, because $a \ge r$ forces $p$.
16. At $r$, $\neg\neg p$ fails, because $b \ge r$ forces $\neg p$: no world above $b$ forces $p$.
17. So $r$ forces neither disjunct, and $\neg p \vee \neg\neg p$ fails at $r$.

## Remarks

The communication step uses the general form of the rule, where both components keep part of each premise.
The special form that only swaps succedents gives $p, \neg p \Rightarrow \; \mid \; p, \neg p \Rightarrow$ from two copies of step 3, and that does not help.
The derivation separates $p$ from $\neg p$: one component keeps both copies of $p$, the other keeps both copies of $\neg p$.

In a Kripke model whose worlds form a chain, the formula holds at every world $w$.
If no world above $w$ forces $p$, then $w$ forces $\neg p$.
If some $v \ge w$ forces $p$, then every $u \ge w$ is comparable with $v$, so some world above $u$ forces $p$.
Then no world above $w$ forces $\neg p$, and $w$ forces $\neg\neg p$.
The [[Kripke semantics|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]] check in steps 14 to 17 uses a model that is not a chain, and it shows that intuitionistic logic does not prove the formula.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The weak excluded middle says: either $p$ is impossible, or the impossibility of $p$ is impossible.
Intuitionistic logic cannot prove it, because two future situations can disagree about $p$.
The communication rule assumes that any two situations are comparable.
With that rule we start from the fact "$p$ and not $p$ give a contradiction" and split it into two alternatives.
One alternative says "$p$ gives a contradiction", and the other says "not $p$ gives a contradiction".

### L2 human

The derivation has four phases.

1. Build $\neg p, p \Rightarrow$ from two axioms with ${\to}$L.
2. Apply communication to two copies, and send both copies of $p$ to one component and both copies of $\neg p$ to the other.
3. Contract, weaken with $\bot$, and apply ${\to}$R in each component to get $\Rightarrow \neg p \mid \Rightarrow \neg\neg p$.
4. Apply ${\vee}$R in each component and merge the two equal components.

The typical use is to show that HLC derives formulas of LC that do not have the form of the prelinearity axiom.
A trap is to use only the special form of communication, which swaps succedents; that form cannot separate $p$ from $\neg p$ in step 2.
