## Example

The object language $L$ is the language of propositional logic.
Its symbols are the variables $p, q, r, \dots$, the connectives $\neg$ and $\to$, and the two parentheses.
The formal system is the [[Hilbert system|1MGMQX43B8]] $H$ with the axiom schemas A1 to A3 and the rule modus ponens.

- A1: $\varphi \to (\psi \to \varphi)$.
- A2: $(\varphi \to (\psi \to \chi)) \to ((\varphi \to \psi) \to (\varphi \to \chi))$.
- A3: $(\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi) \to ((\neg \psi \to \varphi) \to \psi)$.

The metalanguage is mathematical English with the metavariables $\varphi, \psi, \chi, \Gamma$ and the turnstile $\vdash$.
We sort a theorem and a metatheorem into the two levels of [[object language and metalanguage|D9G8M42GKC]].

A theorem.

1. The string $p \to p$ consists of three symbols of $L$: the variable $p$, the connective $\to$ and the variable $p$. So it is a formula of $L$.
2. It has a derivation in $H$ of five lines: an instance of A2, an instance of A1, modus ponens, an instance of A1, and modus ponens. The page on [[formal systems|~113060c3]] gives the lines.
3. So $p \to p$ is a theorem of $H$, and it is an object of the object language.
4. The sentence "$\vdash p \to p$" is not a formula of $L$, because $\vdash$ is not a symbol of $L$. It is a true statement of the metalanguage about $H$.

A metatheorem.

5. The [[deduction theorem|9X08XK5RV2]] says: for every set $\Gamma$ of formulas and all formulas $\varphi, \psi$, if $\Gamma \cup \set{\varphi} \vdash \psi$, then $\Gamma \vdash \varphi \to \psi$.
6. The words "for every", "if" and "then", the letters $\Gamma, \varphi, \psi$, the set symbols $\cup$ and $\set{\ }$, and the turnstile all belong to the metalanguage.
7. The arrow in "$\varphi \to \psi$" is the symbol $\to$ of $L$. The expression denotes the formula $\ulcorner \varphi \to \psi \urcorner$ built from the values of $\varphi$ and $\psi$.
8. The proof of the deduction theorem is an induction on the length of derivations. It is an argument in the metalanguage, not a derivation in $H$.

The metatheorem gives a second proof that $p \to p$ is a theorem.

9. The one-line sequence $p$ is a derivation of $p$ from the hypothesis set $\set{p}$, so $\set{p} \vdash p$.
10. Apply the deduction theorem with $\Gamma = \emptyset$, $\varphi = p$ and $\psi = p$. It gives $\emptyset \vdash p \to p$, that is, $\vdash p \to p$.

## Remarks

The two proofs of $\vdash p \to p$ live on different levels.
The first exhibits a derivation in $H$; the second is a proof in the metalanguage that such a derivation exists.
The proof of the deduction theorem is constructive, so it turns the one-line derivation of step 9 into an explicit derivation of $p \to p$ in $H$.
If we replace $H$ by a system with the rule of generalization, such as a Hilbert system for first-order logic, the deduction theorem needs an extra hypothesis on free variables.
This change happens in the metatheorem, while the object language only gains quantifiers.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of the rules of chess and a book about chess.
A move such as "pawn from e2 to e4" is part of the game.
A sentence in the book such as "a king and a rook can always force checkmate against a lone king" is a fact about the game, proved by reasoning about all possible games.
In logic, a theorem such as "p implies p" is like a move that we can actually make.
A metatheorem such as the deduction theorem is like the fact in the book.

### L2 human

We sort the symbols of the statement "$\set{p, p \to q} \vdash q$".

1. $p$, $q$ and $p \to q$ are formulas of $L$, so they belong to the object language.
2. The braces, the comma and $\vdash$ belong to the metalanguage: they form a set of formulas and state derivability.
3. The statement is true: the lines $p$, $p \to q$, $q$ form a derivation from the hypotheses, with the last line by modus ponens.
4. By the deduction theorem with $\Gamma = \set{p}$, we get $\set{p} \vdash (p \to q) \to q$.

The typical use of metatheorems is to shorten work: we prove facts about derivations instead of writing the derivations.
A trap is to write the turnstile inside a formula, as in $p \to (\vdash q)$, which is not an expression of either level.
