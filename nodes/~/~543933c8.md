## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $C = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $AC = \begin{pmatrix} 6 - 5 & -2 + 2 \\ 15 - 15 & -5 + 6 \end{pmatrix} = I$ and $CA = \begin{pmatrix} 6 - 5 & 3 - 3 \\ -10 + 10 & -5 + 6 \end{pmatrix} = I$. So $A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] and $A^{-1} = C$.
2. The [[transposes|0W809K6AW0]] are $A^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $C^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -5 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. $A^{\mathsf{T}} C^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 - 5 & -10 + 10 \\ 3 - 3 & -5 + 6 \end{pmatrix} = I$.
4. So $(A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1} = C^{\mathsf{T}} = (A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}}$.
5. The general reason: by the [[transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]], $A^{\mathsf{T}} (A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} = (A^{-1} A)^{\mathsf{T}} = I^{\mathsf{T}} = I$, and in the same way $(A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} = (A A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} = I$.

## Remarks

Step 5 proves $(A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} = (A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}$ for every invertible matrix over a commutative ring.
So the notation $A^{-\mathsf{T}}$ for both is unambiguous.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To find the inverse of the transpose, we can transpose the inverse.
The numbers check out: the product in step 3 is the identity.

### L2 human

The reversal $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$ turns the equation $A^{-1} A = I$ into $A^{\mathsf{T}} (A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} = I$.
Transposition preserves the identity, so it preserves inverses.
