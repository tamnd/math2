## Description

The slow-growing hierarchy is a family of functions $g_\alpha : \N \to \N$, one for each ordinal $\alpha$ up to a fixed countable ordinal.
Below and at $\varepsilon_0$ it uses the standard fundamental sequences $\lambda[x]$ of the page [[fast-growing hierarchy|~24c8db71]].

- $g_0(x) = 0$.
- $g_{\alpha + 1}(x) = g_\alpha(x) + 1$.
- $g_\lambda(x) = g_{\lambda[x]}(x)$ for a limit ordinal $\lambda$.

So $g_\alpha(x)$ counts the successor steps in the descent from $\alpha$ to $0$, where each limit $\lambda$ is replaced by $\lambda[x]$.

## Remarks

### First values

Write $x \uparrow\uparrow 0 = 1$ and $x \uparrow\uparrow (k+1) = x^{x \uparrow\uparrow k}$, and $0^0 = 1$.

- $g_n(x) = n$ for $n < \omega$, and $g_\omega(x) = g_x(x) = x$.
- $g_{\omega + m}(x) = x + m$ and $g_{\omega \cdot m}(x) = m x$.
- $g_{\omega^2}(x) = g_{\omega \cdot x}(x) = x^2$.
- $g_{\omega^\omega}(x) = g_{\omega^x}(x) = x^x$. So $g_{\omega^\omega}(0), \dots, g_{\omega^\omega}(4)$ are $1, 1, 4, 27, 256$.
- $g_{\varepsilon_0}(0) = g_0(0) = 0$, $g_{\varepsilon_0}(1) = g_1(1) = 1$, $g_{\varepsilon_0}(2) = g_\omega(2) = 2$ and $g_{\varepsilon_0}(3) = g_{\omega^\omega}(3) = 27$.

### The substitution rule

**Lemma.** For $\alpha < \varepsilon_0$, $g_\alpha(x)$ is the number obtained from the [[Cantor normal form|6GD30FGYDZ]] of $\alpha$ by replacing every $\omega$ with $x$.

Proof, by [[transfinite induction|0ZHQMQ2C1N]] in two parts.

1. First, $g_{\gamma + \delta} = g_\gamma + g_\delta$ when the last exponent of $\gamma$ is at least the first exponent of $\delta$.
2. For $\delta = 0$ this is clear, and for $\delta = \delta' + 1$ it follows from $g_{\gamma + \delta' + 1} = g_{\gamma + \delta'} + 1$.
3. For a limit $\delta$ we have $(\gamma + \delta)[x] = \gamma + \delta[x]$, as in the composition law of the [[Hardy hierarchy|~ec594377]]. Induction on $\delta$ gives the claim.
4. Second, $g_{\omega^\alpha}(x) = x^{g_\alpha(x)}$, by induction on $\alpha$. For $\alpha = 0$: $g_1(x) = 1 = x^0$.
5. For $\alpha = \beta + 1$: $g_{\omega^{\beta+1}}(x) = g_{\omega^\beta \cdot x}(x) = x \cdot g_{\omega^\beta}(x) = x \cdot x^{g_\beta(x)} = x^{g_\alpha(x)}$, by step 1.
6. For a limit $\alpha$: $g_{\omega^\alpha}(x) = g_{\omega^{\alpha[x]}}(x) = x^{g_{\alpha[x]}(x)} = x^{g_\alpha(x)}$.
7. Steps 1 and 4 together turn the normal form $\omega^{\alpha_1} + \cdots + \omega^{\alpha_k}$ into $x^{g_{\alpha_1}(x)} + \cdots + x^{g_{\alpha_k}(x)}$, which is the substitution.

For $x \ge 1$, $\varepsilon_0[x]$ is a tower of $x - 1$ copies of $\omega$, so the rule gives $g_{\varepsilon_0}(x) = x \uparrow\uparrow (x - 1)$.

### Relation to the fast-growing hierarchy

The Wikipedia article reports these results.

- The hierarchy grows much more slowly than the fast-growing one: $g_{\varepsilon_0}$ is only of the order of $f_3$.
- $g_\alpha$ reaches the growth rate of $f_{\varepsilon_0}$ only when $\alpha$ is the Bachmann-Howard ordinal. This is attributed to Girard (1981) and to Cichon and Wainer (1983).
- Girard proved that the slow-growing hierarchy eventually catches up with the fast-growing one, with suitable fundamental sequences far beyond $\varepsilon_0$.

### Dependence on the fundamental sequences

The slow-growing hierarchy is very sensitive to the choice of the fundamental sequences, as Weiermann showed.
A small example: with $\varepsilon_0[0] = 1$ in place of $\varepsilon_0[0] = 0$, the tower $\varepsilon_0[x]$ has $x$ copies of $\omega$.
Then the substitution rule gives $g_{\varepsilon_0}(x) = x \uparrow\uparrow x$, which is the value listed on Wikipedia.
For example $g_{\varepsilon_0}(3)$ becomes $3^{27}$ in place of $27$.

### The theory and the stronger theory

For $\alpha \le \varepsilon_0$ the functions $g_\alpha$ are [[primitive recursive|5NVA2JRHPV]], by the substitution rule.
So the slow-growing functions up to $\varepsilon_0$ are far inside the [[provably total functions|~25aedb3b]] of [[Peano arithmetic|MPSCREYC3M]].
To reach the growth of $f_{\varepsilon_0}$, which $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove total, the index must go up to the Bachmann-Howard ordinal, far beyond $\varepsilon_0$.
$\mathsf{ZFC}$ proves that every $g_\alpha$ is total, by transfinite recursion on $\alpha$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The slow-growing hierarchy turns an ordinal into a formula in $x$ by a very lazy rule: replace every $\omega$ by $x$.
For example $\omega^2 + 3$ becomes $x^2 + 3$, so at $x = 5$ it gives $28$.
Even the huge ordinal $\varepsilon_0$, an endless tower of $\omega$, only gives a finite tower of $x$.
So the functions grow slowly compared with the fast-growing hierarchy.

### L2 human

A worked calculation of $g_{\omega^\omega}(3)$.

1. $\omega^\omega$ is a limit with a limit exponent, so $\omega^\omega[3] = \omega^{\omega[3]} = \omega^3$.
2. $\omega^3[3] = \omega^2 \cdot 3$, and by the additive rule $g_{\omega^2 \cdot 3}(3) = 3 \cdot g_{\omega^2}(3)$.
3. $\omega^2[3] = \omega \cdot 3$, so $g_{\omega^2}(3) = 3 \cdot g_\omega(3) = 3 \cdot 3 = 9$.
4. So $g_{\omega^\omega}(3) = 3 \cdot 9 = 27 = 3^3$, as the substitution rule predicts.

The typical use is as a contrast: the same ordinal and the same fundamental sequences give very different growth in the three hierarchies.
The trap: unlike the fast-growing hierarchy, the slow-growing hierarchy can change its growth rate a lot under a small change of the fundamental sequences.

### L3 human

The slow-growing hierarchy reads an ordinal notation as a polynomial-like expression and evaluates it at $x$.
So it measures the size of the notation system rather than the length of a descent.
Girard's theorem that it eventually matches the fast-growing hierarchy links [[ordinal analysis|C5E5TG8PGP]] with the study of dilators.
For independence results it is a warning: growth rates depend on the notation, and the [[proof-theoretic ordinal|C72RBH0CDR]] of a theory is linked to provable totality through the fast-growing hierarchy, not through this one.
