## Example

The best-known [[mathematical fallacy|~734fe138]] "proves" that $2 = 1$.
We give the argument, and then we check each step.

The argument:

1. Let $a$ and $b$ be real numbers with $a = b$, and assume $b \neq 0$.
2. Multiply both sides by $a$: $a^2 = ab$.
3. Subtract $b^2$ from both sides: $a^2 - b^2 = ab - b^2$.
4. Factor both sides: $(a + b)(a - b) = b(a - b)$.
5. Divide both sides by $a - b$: $a + b = b$.
6. Replace $a$ by $b$, since $a = b$: $2b = b$.
7. Divide both sides by $b$, which is not zero: $2 = 1$.

The check:

1. Step 2 is valid: if two numbers are equal, their products with the same number $a$ are equal.
2. Step 3 is valid for the same reason, with subtraction of $b^2$.
3. Step 4 is valid: $(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2$ and $b(a - b) = ab - b^2$ by the distributive law.
4. Step 5 is invalid. From $a = b$ we get $a - b = 0$, so the step divides by $0$.
5. The rule behind step 5 is [[cancellation|0GJM24EXP9]]: if $xc = yc$ and $c \neq 0$, then $x = y$. Here $c = a - b = 0$, so the condition $c \neq 0$ fails.
6. Without that condition the rule is false: $1 \cdot 0 = 2 \cdot 0$, but $1 \neq 2$.
7. Step 7 is valid, because $b \neq 0$ by step 1. The error is only in step 5.

The correct version of step 5 is the following statement.
For real numbers, $xc = yc$ holds exactly when $(x - y)c = 0$, so exactly when $x = y$ or $c = 0$.
In step 4 the second case $c = a - b = 0$ holds, and the equation gives no information about $a + b$ and $b$.

## Remarks

### Cancelling a factor that may be zero

The same error occurs in the solution of equations, where it loses solutions instead of proving false statements.
We solve $x^2 = 3x$ over $\mathbb{R}$.

1. Dividing both sides by $x$ gives $x = 3$. This step needs $x \neq 0$, which we do not know.
2. The correct method moves all terms to one side: $x^2 - 3x = 0$, so $x(x - 3) = 0$.
3. A product of two real numbers is $0$ exactly when one of the factors is $0$. So $x = 0$ or $x = 3$.
4. Both values satisfy the equation: $0^2 = 3 \cdot 0$ and $3^2 = 3 \cdot 3$.

So the division in step 1 lost the solution $x = 0$.
The safe rule is: before cancelling a factor $c$, either prove $c \neq 0$, or treat the case $c = 0$ separately.
The page on [[non-reversible steps|~8decb58a]] treats the opposite error, where a step adds solutions.

### Hidden divisions

A division by zero is often hidden in a variable or in a formula.

1. The factor $a - b$ above is zero because of a hypothesis stated several steps before.
2. A step "$\frac{x}{x} = 1$" hides the condition $x \neq 0$.
3. Cancelling $\sin x$ in $\sin x \cos x = \sin x$ loses all the solutions $x = k\pi$ with $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

### What the example shows

The argument needs the hypothesis $a = b$ twice: in step 2, to get $a^2 = ab$, and in step 6.
The same hypothesis makes the divisor of step 5 equal to $0$.
So no choice of $a$ and $b$ repairs the argument: when $a \neq b$, step 2 does not follow from step 1, and when $a = b$, step 5 divides by $0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Here is a famous trick.
Start with two equal numbers, say both equal to $3$.
After some correct algebra the trick reaches $(3 + 3) \times 0 = 3 \times 0$, which is true, because both sides are $0$.
Then it "cancels" the $0$ and gets $3 + 3 = 3$, which is false.
The error is the cancelling: from $6 \times 0 = 3 \times 0$ we cannot conclude $6 = 3$, because anything times $0$ is $0$.

### L2 human

We find the hidden division by zero in the following argument.

1. Let $x$ be a solution of $x - 2 = 0$.
2. Multiply both sides by $x + 2$: $x^2 - 4 = 0$.
3. Write this as $x^2 - 4 = x - 2$, since both sides are $0$.
4. Factor: $(x - 2)(x + 2) = x - 2$.
5. Divide by $x - 2$: $x + 2 = 1$, so $x = -1$.
6. But $x = 2$ by step 1, so "$2 = -1$".

Step 5 divides by $x - 2$, which is $0$ by step 1, so step 5 is invalid.
The correct conclusion of step 4 is "$x - 2 = 0$ or $x + 2 = 1$", and the first case is the hypothesis.

The typical use of this check is in solving equations: each division by an expression must come with a proof that the expression is not zero.
A trap is a division by a symbol that is zero only because of an earlier line, as in steps 1 and 5 here.
