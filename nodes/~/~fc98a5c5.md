## Example

Let $U$ be the plane $x + y = 0$ and $W$ the plane $y + z = 0$ in $\R^3$, as in [[the two-plane example|~d2e159b0]].
Their [[principal angles|~179768d8]] are $0$ and $\pi/3$.

1. The vectors $e = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2} (1, -1, 0) \in U$ and $f = (1, 0, 0) \in W$ are unit vectors, and they are the first vectors of the [[orthonormal bases|0S9GGTR0EX]] used there.
2. $e \cdot f = 1/\sqrt 2$, so the [[angle|0ZEHZY85E6]] between them is $\pi/4$.
3. $\pi/4$ is neither $0$ nor $\pi/3$. So it is not a principal angle.
4. The reason: $\theta_1$ is the smallest angle over all unit pairs, and the pair $u_1 = w_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt 3}(1, -1, 1)$ gives $0 < \pi/4$. So $(e, f)$ is not a maximizing pair for $k = 1$.
5. The other entries of $Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2$ in these bases are $-1/2$, $0$ and $-1/\sqrt 2$, which give the angles $2\pi/3$, $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/4$. None of them is a principal angle either.

## Remarks

Every pair of unit vectors $u \in U$ and $w \in W$ has $u \cdot w \le \cos \theta_1$.
So the angle between any two such vectors is at least $\theta_1$, but it need not be equal to any principal angle.
The principal angles come from the singular values of $Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2$, not from its entries.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Pick one direction in each of two planes, and measure the angle between them.
In general, the answer depends on the directions you picked, so it says little about the planes.
The principal angles are the special angles that do not depend on such a choice.

### L2 human

A common error is to take the angle between the first basis vectors of two subspaces as "the" angle between them.
This example shows that this gives $\pi/4$ for two planes whose principal angles are $0$ and $\pi/3$.
