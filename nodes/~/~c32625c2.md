## Proof

We differentiate the Leibniz formula term by term, and we recognize Laplace expansions.

1. Write $A(t) = (a_{ij}(t))$. Each entry $a_{ij}$ is differentiable, since $A$ is differentiable.
2. For $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$, the product rule below holds for complex values with the same proof.
3. By the [[Leibniz formula|016PE4VZ8Q]], $\det A(t) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i \sigma(i)}(t)$.
4. By the [[product rule|0V9A43H7WV]] and induction on the number of factors, each product is differentiable.
5. Its derivative is $\sum_{k=1}^{n} a'_{k \sigma(k)}(t) \prod_{i \ne k} a_{i \sigma(i)}(t)$.
6. A finite sum of differentiable functions is differentiable, so $\det A(t)$ is differentiable.
7. Exchange the sums over $\sigma$ and $k$: $\frac{d}{dt} \det A = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \sum_{\sigma} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, a'_{k \sigma(k)} \prod_{i \ne k} a_{i \sigma(i)}$.
8. Let $A_k$ be $A$ with row $k$ replaced by row $k$ of $A'$. By the Leibniz formula, the inner sum is $\det A_k$.
9. Let $C_{kj}$ be the [[cofactors|0W2QAVJQ1W]] of $A$. The cofactors of $A_k$ along row $k$ delete row $k$, so they equal $C_{kj}$.
10. By the [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]] of $A_k$ along row $k$, $\det A_k = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a'_{kj} C_{kj}$.
11. By steps 7 to 10, $\frac{d}{dt} \det A = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} C_{kj}\, a'_{kj}$.
12. By the definition of the [[adjugate|0GMPYM0NSR]], $\operatorname{adj}(A)_{jk} = C_{kj}$.
13. So $(\operatorname{adj}(A) A')_{jj} = \sum_{k} \operatorname{adj}(A)_{jk}\, a'_{kj} = \sum_{k} C_{kj}\, a'_{kj}$.
14. Summing over $j$ gives $\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}(A) A') = \sum_{j} \sum_{k} C_{kj}\, a'_{kj}$, which is the sum of step 11.
15. If $A(t)$ is invertible, then $\operatorname{adj}(A) = \det(A)\, A^{-1}$ by the [[adjugate identity|0528HKWZ1Z]].
16. The trace is linear, so $\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}(A) A') = \det A \cdot \operatorname{tr}(A^{-1} A')$.

## Idea

The determinant is a sum of products of entries, one from each row.
The product rule differentiates one row at a time, and each such term is the determinant with one row replaced by its derivative.
Laplace expansion along that row turns these determinants into cofactors, and the cofactors assemble into the adjugate.

## Variants

A second proof starts at the identity.
For a matrix $X$, $\det(I + \varepsilon X) = 1 + \varepsilon \operatorname{tr} X + O(\varepsilon^2)$, by the Leibniz formula.
For invertible $A$, $\det(A + \varepsilon X) = \det A \cdot \det(I + \varepsilon A^{-1} X)$, which gives the derivative $\det A \operatorname{tr}(A^{-1} X)$.
Both sides of the adjugate form are polynomials in the entries, and the invertible matrices are dense, so the adjugate form holds for all $A$.
For $n = 1$, the formula reads $(a)' = \operatorname{tr}((1)(a'))$, by the convention $\operatorname{adj}(a) = (1)$.
