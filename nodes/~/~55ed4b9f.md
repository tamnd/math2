## Example

A literal is a variable $p$ or its negation $\lnot p$, and the complement of a literal $\ell$ is written $\bar\ell$.
A [[clause|T3SXVH3ZZC]] is a finite set of literals, read as their disjunction; the empty clause $\Box$ is false under every valuation.
The resolution rule derives the clause $C \cup D$ from the two clauses $C \cup \set{\ell}$ and $D \cup \set{\bar\ell}$.
We show that this rule is [[sound|4A84VHDTHV]] for truth tables, and then use it once.

1. Let $v$ be a valuation that makes $C \cup \set{\ell}$ and $D \cup \set{\bar\ell}$ true.
2. Case $v(\ell) = \mathsf{T}$. Then $v(\bar\ell) = \mathsf{F}$, so the clause $D \cup \set{\bar\ell}$ is true because some literal of $D$ is true. So $C \cup D$ is true.
3. Case $v(\ell) = \mathsf{F}$. Then the clause $C \cup \set{\ell}$ is true because some literal of $C$ is true. So $C \cup D$ is true.
4. In both cases $v$ makes $C \cup D$ true, so the rule keeps truth. By induction on the length of a resolution derivation, every derived clause is true under each valuation that makes the given clauses true.
5. Take the clauses $\set{p, q}$, $\set{\lnot p, q}$, $\set{\lnot q, r}$, $\set{\lnot r}$.
6. Resolution on $p$ gives $\set{q}$ from the first two clauses. Resolution on $q$ gives $\set{r}$ from $\set{q}$ and $\set{\lnot q, r}$. Resolution on $r$ gives $\Box$ from $\set{r}$ and $\set{\lnot r}$.
7. By step 4, a valuation that makes the four clauses true would make $\Box$ true, which is impossible. So the four clauses are not [[satisfiable|QN7M1JCGT9]].
8. A direct check agrees: $\set{\lnot r}$ forces $r = \mathsf{F}$, then $\set{\lnot q, r}$ forces $q = \mathsf{F}$, and then $\set{p, q}$ and $\set{\lnot p, q}$ demand $p = \mathsf{T}$ and $p = \mathsf{F}$.

## Remarks

Soundness is what makes a resolution refutation a proof of unsatisfiability, and this is the basis of SAT solvers and of automated theorem provers.
Resolution on two literals at once is not sound: from $\set{p, q}$ and $\set{\lnot p, \lnot q}$ it would give $\Box$, but $p = \mathsf{T}$, $q = \mathsf{F}$ makes both clauses true.
Resolution is sound but not complete as a way of deriving consequences: $\set{p}$ entails $\set{p, q}$, and no resolution step applies to the single clause $\set{p}$.
It is complete only for refutation: every unsatisfiable finite set of clauses derives $\Box$.
Any set of formulas can be put in [[conjunctive normal form|07C4A8Y18K]] first, so resolution tests unsatisfiability for all of propositional logic.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose we know "it is sunny or it rains" and "it is not sunny or I take an umbrella".
If it is sunny, the second fact says that I take an umbrella.
If it is not sunny, the first fact says that it rains.
So in every case "it rains or I take an umbrella" is true.
Resolution is this step, and because it never turns true facts into a false one, it is sound.

### L2 human

We show that the clauses $\set{p}$, $\set{\lnot p, q}$, $\set{\lnot q}$ cannot all be true.

1. Resolution on $p$ of $\set{p}$ and $\set{\lnot p, q}$ gives $\set{q}$.
2. Resolution on $q$ of $\set{q}$ and $\set{\lnot q}$ gives $\Box$.
3. By soundness, a valuation that satisfies the three clauses would satisfy $\Box$, and no valuation does.

The typical use is a refutation: to show $\Gamma \models \varphi$, add the clauses of $\lnot\varphi$ to those of $\Gamma$ and derive $\Box$.
A trap is to cancel two complementary pairs in one step, which is the unsound rule shown in the remarks.
