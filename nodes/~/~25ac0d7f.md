## Example

Let $P(x, y)$ be the predicate "$x + y = 5$ and $x - y = 1$" on $\mathbb{Z}$.
We show that $\exists x \, \exists y \, P(x, y)$ and $\exists y \, \exists x \, P(x, y)$ are both true, and that the two orders are equivalent for every predicate.
So this is a case where the order of quantifiers does not change the meaning.

1. Solve the system. Adding the two equations gives $2x = 6$, so $x = 3$, and then $y = 5 - 3 = 2$.
2. $P(3, 2)$ is true: $3 + 2 = 5$ and $3 - 2 = 1$.
3. So $\exists y \, P(3, y)$ holds with $y = 2$, and $\exists x \, \exists y \, P(x, y)$ holds with $x = 3$.
4. In the same way, $\exists x \, P(x, 2)$ holds with $x = 3$, and $\exists y \, \exists x \, P(x, y)$ holds with $y = 2$.
5. In general, both orders are true exactly when some pair $(a, b) \in D \times D$ satisfies $P(a, b)$. Reason: each order asks for one $x$ and one $y$, and neither choice must work for more than one value of the other variable.
6. So $\exists x \, \exists y \, P(x, y) \equiv \exists y \, \exists x \, P(x, y)$ for every predicate $P$ on every domain $D$.

## Remarks

The example shows that the [[existential quantifier|0MSRQHCNN9]] commutes with itself.
The universal quantifier also commutes with itself: both $\forall x \, \forall y \, P(x, y)$ and $\forall y \, \forall x \, P(x, y)$ say that every pair satisfies $P$.
The order matters only between quantifiers of different kinds, as on the page [[order of quantifiers|0FYQMKFE77]].
If we replace $\exists y$ by $\forall y$, the statement $\exists x \, \forall y \, P(x, y)$ is false, because $P(3, y)$ fails for $y = 0$, and the order then matters.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"There is a boy and there is a girl who are friends" and "there is a girl and there is a boy who are friends" say the same thing.
Both claim that at least one friendly pair exists.
The order in which we name the two people does not matter.
With "every" and "some" mixed, the order does matter.

### L2 human

To check whether two quantifiers can be exchanged, look at their kinds.

1. Both $\exists$: the statement asks for one pair, so the order is free.
2. Both $\forall$: the statement claims something for all pairs, so the order is free.
3. One $\forall$ and one $\exists$: the order changes the dependence of the witness, and the statements can differ.

A trap is to group variables after a mixed prefix: $\forall x \, \exists y \, \exists z$ allows exchange of $y$ and $z$, but not of $x$ with $y$ or $z$.
