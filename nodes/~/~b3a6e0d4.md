## Statement

Let $\mathbb{K} = \R$ or $\C$, and give $\mathbb{K}^n$ the standard [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] $\ip{x}{y} = \sum_{j} x_j \overline{y_j}$.
Over $\R$ this is the dot product.
Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix over $\mathbb{K}$, and let $A^*$ be its [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]].
Write $\operatorname{Col}$, $\operatorname{Nul}$ and $\operatorname{Row}$ for the [[column space|0F0RPPJ2TT]], the [[null space|0PQ2GGY1QV]] and the [[row space|0EP73HK4C4]], and $\perp$ for the [[orthogonal complement|079D68S3AW]].
Then:

1. $\operatorname{Nul}(A^*) = \operatorname{Col}(A)^\perp$ and $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{Nul}(A^*)^\perp$ in $\mathbb{K}^m$.
2. $\operatorname{Nul}(A) = \operatorname{Col}(A^*)^\perp$ and $\operatorname{Col}(A^*) = \operatorname{Nul}(A)^\perp$ in $\mathbb{K}^n$.
3. Over $\R$, $A^* = A^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]] and $\operatorname{Col}(A^{\mathsf{T}}) = \operatorname{Row}(A)$. So $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{Nul}(A)^\perp$, $\operatorname{Nul}(A) = \operatorname{Row}(A)^\perp$, and $\operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{Nul}(A^{\mathsf{T}})^\perp$.
4. The system $Ax = b$ has a solution if and only if $b$ is orthogonal to every $y$ with $A^* y = 0$.
5. If $Ax = b$ has a solution, then it has exactly one solution $x_r$ in $\operatorname{Col}(A^*)$, and the set of all solutions is $x_r + \operatorname{Nul}(A)$.

The [[proof|~6cef713f]] rests on the identity $\ip{Ax}{y} = \ip{x}{A^* y}$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The conjugate in $A^*$ is needed over $\C$. For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \end{pmatrix}$, the null space is $\C(-i, 1)$ and $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \C(1, i)$. But $\ip{(-i, 1)}{(1, i)} = -i + \overline{i} = -2i \ne 0$, so $\operatorname{Nul}(A) \ne \operatorname{Row}(A)^\perp$. With $\operatorname{Col}(A^*) = \C(1, -i)$ we get $\ip{(-i, 1)}{(1, -i)} = -i + i = 0$, as part 2 says.
- The inner product must be the standard one. For another inner product on $\mathbb{K}^n$, the matrix $A^*$ must be replaced by the matrix of the [[adjoint|0R9NMSM1ZP]] for that inner product.

### Consequences

- Each of the four [[fundamental subspaces|73DVECPAT6]] determines the dimension of the other one on the same side: $\dim \operatorname{Row}(A) + \dim \operatorname{Nul}(A) = n$, by the [[orthogonal decomposition|0SP7P0G7KQ]]. This is rank-nullity again.
- Part 1 for real matrices is the separate node [[the complement of the column space|J4SZVFBHS5]].
- Part 4 is a form of the [[Fredholm alternative|4HH8C9V8JQ]]: either $Ax = b$ has a solution, or some $y$ has $A^* y = 0$ and $\ip{b}{y} \ne 0$.
- The solution $x_r$ of part 5 is the [[minimum-norm solution|~6f828050]] of $Ax = b$.

### Converse

Part 4 is an equivalence, so it has no separate converse.
In part 5 the converse also holds: every vector of $x_r + \operatorname{Nul}(A)$ solves $Ax = b$.

### Generalizations

For a linear map $T$ between finite-dimensional inner product spaces, the same statements read $\ker T^* = (\im T)^\perp$ and $\im T^* = (\ker T)^\perp$.
That is a row of the sub-area on adjoints, and the [[adjoint|0R9NMSM1ZP]] node links it.
For a bounded operator between Hilbert spaces, $\ker T^* = (\im T)^\perp$ still holds, but the image must be replaced by its closure in the second formula.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A matrix $A$ sends the row space onto the column space, and it sends the null space to zero.
The row space and the null space are at right angles, and together they fill the input space.
The column space and the left null space are at right angles, and together they fill the output space.

### L2 human

We test solvability with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $A^{\mathsf{T}} y = 0$ means $y_1 + 2 y_2 = 0$, so $\operatorname{Nul}(A^{\mathsf{T}}) = \R(2, -1)$.
2. For $b = (1, 2)$, $b \cdot (2, -1) = 0$, so $Ax = b$ has a solution, for example $x = (1, 0)$.
3. For $b = (1, 0)$, $b \cdot (2, -1) = 2 \ne 0$, so $Ax = b$ has no solution.
4. $\operatorname{Nul}(A) = \R(2, -1)$ and $\operatorname{Row}(A) = \R(1, 2)$, and $(2, -1) \cdot (1, 2) = 0$.
5. For $b = (1, 2)$, the solution in the row space is $x_r = t(1, 2)$ with $5t = 1$, so $x_r = (1/5, 2/5)$. All solutions are $x_r + s(2, -1)$.

The typical use is to decide whether a system has a solution without solving it.
The trap is to forget the conjugate over $\C$: the null space is orthogonal to the conjugated rows, not to the rows.

### L3 human

The four subspaces and their orthogonality give a complete picture of a matrix as a map, as in Strang's textbook.
With the singular value decomposition, the first $r$ right singular vectors span $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ and the others span $\operatorname{Nul}(A)$, and the same holds for the left singular vectors with $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Nul}(A^*)$; see the [[singular value decomposition|000NPTMX6J]].
In homological language, part 1 says that the cokernel of $A$ is identified with the kernel of $A^*$.
