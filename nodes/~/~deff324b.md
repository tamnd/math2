## Example

Let $p$ be a [[prime|01826KMG2H]], and let $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty) = \{m/p^k + \mathbb{Z} : m \in \mathbb{Z},\ k \geq 0\}$ be the [[Prüfer group|F0XAMM2PPS]], a subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$.
For $k \geq 0$, let $C_k = \langle 1/p^k + \mathbb{Z} \rangle$.
We show that $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ is [[locally cyclic|RXWA8TF2RM]] but not cyclic.

1. Each $C_k$ is cyclic, and $C_k \subseteq C_{k+1}$, because $1/p^k = p \cdot (1/p^{k+1})$.
2. Each $C_k$ is finite. Its elements are the classes $j/p^k + \mathbb{Z}$, and $j/p^k + \mathbb{Z}$ depends only on $j$ modulo $p^k$, so $C_k$ has at most $p^k$ elements.
3. Let $x_1, \ldots, x_r \in \mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$, with $x_i = m_i / p^{k_i} + \mathbb{Z}$. Put $k = \max(k_1, \ldots, k_r)$, or $k = 0$ if $r = 0$.
4. Then $x_i = m_i p^{k - k_i} / p^k + \mathbb{Z} = m_i p^{k - k_i} \cdot (1/p^k + \mathbb{Z})$, so $x_i \in C_k$ for each $i$.
5. So $\langle x_1, \ldots, x_r \rangle \subseteq C_k$. A [[subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic|0KB1ND7XDS]], so $\langle x_1, \ldots, x_r \rangle$ is cyclic. Thus $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ is locally cyclic.
6. The group $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ is infinite. The elements $1/p^k + \mathbb{Z}$ for $k \geq 1$ are pairwise distinct, because $1/p^k - 1/p^l$ lies strictly between $-1$ and $1$ and is not $0$ for $k \neq l$.
7. By steps 2 to 5, every finitely generated subgroup lies in a finite $C_k$. By step 6, no such subgroup is the whole group. So $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ is not finitely generated, and it is not [[cyclic|0KVGH0J5B3]].

## Remarks

The Prüfer group is the union of the chain $C_0 \subsetneq C_1 \subsetneq C_2 \subsetneq \cdots$ of finite cyclic groups.
By the [[remarks on the Prüfer group|F0XAMM2PPS]], its proper subgroups are exactly the groups $C_k$, so its lattice of subgroups is a chain.
A chain is a distributive lattice, which agrees with Ore's theorem that a group is locally cyclic exactly when its subgroup lattice is distributive.
The Prüfer group has no [[maximal subgroup|~1e0593e6]], because the chain of the $C_k$ has no largest member below the whole group.
It also [[has no finite exponent|~3e73c12d]], because $C_k$ has elements of order $p^k$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For $p = 2$, think of the rotations of a circle by a half turn, a quarter turn, an eighth of a turn, and so on, and all their combinations.
Any finite set of these rotations is made of turns by some fixed small fraction, such as $1/2^k$ of a turn.
So any finite set of these rotations lies inside the powers of one rotation.
But no single rotation generates all of them, because there is always a finer fraction.

### L2 human

We find a generator of $\langle 3/8 + \mathbb{Z},\ 5/32 + \mathbb{Z} \rangle$ in $\mathbb{Z}(2^\infty)$.

1. The largest denominator is $32 = 2^5$, so both elements lie in $C_5 = \langle 1/32 + \mathbb{Z} \rangle$, a cyclic group of order $32$.
2. In $C_5$, we have $3/8 = 12/32$ and $5/32$. So the subgroup is generated by the classes of $12$ and $5$ in $\mathbb{Z}/32\mathbb{Z}$.
3. Since $\gcd(5, 32) = 1$, the class of $5$ alone generates $\mathbb{Z}/32\mathbb{Z}$. So the subgroup is all of $C_5$, and $1/32 + \mathbb{Z}$ generates it.

The typical use of this example is as a model for a group that is locally cyclic, torsion and infinite.
A trap: every proper subgroup is finite and cyclic, and still the whole group is not cyclic.
