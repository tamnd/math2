## Description

The Hardy functions are functions $H_\alpha : \N \to \N$, one for each ordinal $\alpha \le \varepsilon_0$.
They use the standard fundamental sequences $\lambda[x]$ defined on the page [[fast-growing hierarchy|~24c8db71]].

- $H_0(x) = x$.
- $H_{\alpha + 1}(x) = H_\alpha(x + 1)$.
- $H_\lambda(x) = H_{\lambda[x]}(x)$ for a limit ordinal $\lambda$.

So $H_\alpha(x)$ is computed by a counting process on a pair $(\alpha, x)$.
A successor $\alpha + 1$ becomes $\alpha$ and $x$ grows by $1$.
A limit $\lambda$ becomes $\lambda[x]$ and $x$ stays the same.
The process stops at the ordinal $0$, and the output is the final value of $x$.

## Remarks

### First values

- $H_n(x) = x + n$ for $n < \omega$, by $n$ successor steps.
- $H_\omega(x) = H_x(x) = 2x$.
- $H_{\omega \cdot 2}(x) = H_{\omega + x}(x) = H_\omega(2x) = 4x$.
- $H_{\omega^2}(x) = H_{\omega \cdot x}(x) = 2^x \cdot x$, by the composition law below. So $H_{\omega^2}(0), \dots, H_{\omega^2}(4)$ are $0, 2, 8, 24, 64$.
- $H_{\omega^\omega}(2) = H_{\omega^2}(2) = 8$.
- $H_{\varepsilon_0}(0) = H_0(0) = 0$, $H_{\varepsilon_0}(1) = H_1(1) = 2$ and $H_{\varepsilon_0}(2) = H_\omega(2) = 4$.

### The composition law

**Lemma 1.** Let $\gamma, \delta < \varepsilon_0$, and suppose that the last exponent in the [[Cantor normal form|6GD30FGYDZ]] of $\gamma$ is at least the first exponent of $\delta$.
Then $H_{\gamma + \delta}(x) = H_\gamma(H_\delta(x))$ for all $x$.
The condition always holds when $\gamma = 0$ or $\delta = 0$.

Proof by [[transfinite induction|0ZHQMQ2C1N]] on $\delta$.

1. If $\delta = 0$, then $H_{\gamma}(x) = H_\gamma(H_0(x))$.
2. Let $\delta = \delta' + 1$. The normal form of $\delta'$ drops a final term $\omega^0$, so $\delta'$ satisfies the condition.
3. Then $H_{\gamma + \delta' + 1}(x) = H_{\gamma + \delta'}(x + 1) = H_\gamma(H_{\delta'}(x+1)) = H_\gamma(H_\delta(x))$.
4. Let $\delta$ be a limit. The condition makes the normal form of $\gamma + \delta$ the normal form of $\gamma$ followed by that of $\delta$.
5. The rules for $\lambda[x]$ change only the last term. So $(\gamma + \delta)[x] = \gamma + \delta[x]$.
6. The first exponent of $\delta[x]$ is at most the first exponent of $\delta$, so $\delta[x]$ satisfies the condition. Also $\delta[x] < \delta$.
7. So $H_{\gamma + \delta}(x) = H_{\gamma + \delta[x]}(x) = H_\gamma(H_{\delta[x]}(x)) = H_\gamma(H_\delta(x))$.

Check: $H_{\omega^2 + 3}(5) = H_{\omega^2}(H_3(5)) = H_{\omega^2}(8) = 2^8 \cdot 8 = 2048$.
Applied $x - 1$ times, the lemma gives $H_{\omega^\beta \cdot x} = (H_{\omega^\beta})^x$, the $x$-fold iterate.

### Relation to the fast-growing hierarchy

**Lemma 2.** For every $\alpha < \varepsilon_0$, $H_{\omega^\alpha} = f_\alpha$.

Proof by transfinite induction on $\alpha$.

1. For $\alpha = 0$: $H_{\omega^0}(x) = H_1(x) = H_0(x + 1) = x + 1 = f_0(x)$.
2. For $\alpha = \beta + 1$: rule 1 of the fundamental sequences gives $\omega^{\beta+1}[x] = \omega^\beta \cdot x$.
3. So $H_{\omega^{\beta+1}}(x) = H_{\omega^\beta \cdot x}(x) = (H_{\omega^\beta})^x(x)$, by Lemma 1.
4. By the induction hypothesis this is $f_\beta^x(x) = f_{\beta+1}(x)$. For $x = 0$ both sides are $0$.
5. For a limit $\alpha$: rule 2 gives $\omega^\alpha[x] = \omega^{\alpha[x]}$, and $\alpha[x] < \alpha$.
6. So $H_{\omega^\alpha}(x) = H_{\omega^{\alpha[x]}}(x) = f_{\alpha[x]}(x) = f_\alpha(x)$.

Small cases agree with the values above: $H_\omega = f_1$ is $x \mapsto 2x$, $H_{\omega^2} = f_2$ is $x \mapsto 2^x \cdot x$, and $H_{\omega^\omega}(2) = f_\omega(2) = 8$.
Also $H_{\omega^3}(2) = f_3(2) = 2048$.

The lemma fails at $\alpha = \varepsilon_0$, although $\omega^{\varepsilon_0} = \varepsilon_0$.
For $x \ge 1$, $H_{\varepsilon_0}(x) = H_{\omega^{\varepsilon_0[x-1]}}(x) = f_{\varepsilon_0[x-1]}(x)$, while $f_{\varepsilon_0}(x) = f_{\varepsilon_0[x]}(x)$.
For example $H_{\varepsilon_0}(2) = f_1(2) = 4$ and $f_{\varepsilon_0}(2) = f_\omega(2) = 8$.
The Wikipedia article states, after Gallier, that $f_{\varepsilon_0}(n - 1) \le H_{\varepsilon_0}(n) \le f_{\varepsilon_0}(n + 1)$ for all $n \ge 1$.

### Goodstein sequences and hydras

- The length function $L$ of [[Goodstein's theorem|XQMNJF241J]] satisfies $L(m) = H_{o_2(m)}(3) - 2$, with $L$ and $o_2(m)$ as defined there. The [[proof|~9843c4f2]] derives this with $P_x$, the predecessor along fundamental sequences.
- The standard battle of the [[Kirby-Paris hydra|~360e5faf]] lowers the ordinal of the hydra along fundamental sequences in the same way.
- So the lengths of these processes are values of Hardy functions near $\varepsilon_0$, and they outgrow the [[provably total functions|~25aedb3b]] of $\mathsf{PA}$.

### The theory and the stronger theory

For a fixed $\alpha < \varepsilon_0$, [[Peano arithmetic|MPSCREYC3M]] $\mathsf{PA}$ proves that $H_\alpha$ is total, because it proves transfinite induction up to each such $\alpha$.
$\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove that $H_{\varepsilon_0}$ is total, because $H_{\varepsilon_0}(n) \ge f_{\varepsilon_0}(n-1)$ and $f_{\varepsilon_0}$ dominates every provably total function of $\mathsf{PA}$.
$\mathsf{ZFC}$ proves that every $H_\alpha$ with $\alpha \le \varepsilon_0$ is total, by transfinite recursion.

### History

The Wikipedia article states that Stanley Wainer introduced the hierarchy in 1972.
The name comes from Godfrey Harold Hardy, whose paper of 1904 on a set of reals of cardinality $\aleph_1$ contains the idea of the definition.
Some authors use $\lambda[x+1]$ in place of $\lambda[x]$ in the limit clause, which changes the values.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A Hardy function is a countdown game with an ordinal and a counter.
Each time the ordinal goes down by one, the counter goes up by one.
When the ordinal is a limit, like $\omega$, it is replaced by a smaller ordinal chosen by the counter.
For example, start with $\omega$ and counter $3$: the ordinal becomes $3$, then three steps add $3$, and the result is $6$.
The game always stops, and the final counter is the value.

### L2 human

A worked calculation of $H_{\omega \cdot 2}(3)$.

1. $\omega \cdot 2$ is a limit, and $(\omega \cdot 2)[3] = \omega + 3$. The pair is now $(\omega + 3, 3)$.
2. Three successor steps give $(\omega, 6)$.
3. $\omega[6] = 6$, so the pair is $(6, 6)$.
4. Six successor steps give $(0, 12)$. So $H_{\omega \cdot 2}(3) = 12 = 4 \cdot 3$.

The typical use is to measure the length of a process that lowers an ordinal along fundamental sequences, as for Goodstein sequences.
The trap: $H_\alpha$ is much slower than $f_\alpha$, since $H_{\omega^\alpha} = f_\alpha$, and the two are compared only through this lemma.

### L3 human

The Hardy hierarchy is the "unfolded" form of the fast-growing hierarchy: $H_\alpha$ runs a single descent, while $f_\alpha$ iterates functions.
Its composition law $H_{\gamma + \delta} = H_\gamma \circ H_\delta$ turns ordinal addition into composition of functions.
This makes it the natural tool for the lengths of [[Goodstein sequences|XQMNJF241J]] and of hydra battles.
At $\varepsilon_0$ it catches up with the fast-growing hierarchy, so both give the same boundary for $\mathsf{PA}$.
