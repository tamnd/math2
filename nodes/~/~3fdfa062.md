## Proof

We use the positions $(\psi, s', \rho)$ of the [[evaluation game|D45Z7HETDA]], with $\rho = +$ when Eloise is the current verifier and $\rho = -$ when Abelard is.
Call a position good for Eloise when either $\rho = +$ and $\mathcal{M} \models \psi[s']$, or $\rho = -$ and $\mathcal{M} \not\models \psi[s']$.
Otherwise call it good for Abelard.
Here $\models$ is [[satisfaction|~cf18e0d7]].

Claim: at a position that is good for Eloise and not final, every move of Abelard leads to a position good for Eloise, and some move of Eloise does.
We prove the claim by [[cases|0WCP0PVET6]] on $\psi$, for $\rho = +$; the case $\rho = -$ is the same with "satisfied" and "not satisfied" exchanged.

1. $\psi = \neg \chi$: the next position is $(\chi, s', -)$. Since $\mathcal{M} \models \neg \chi[s']$, we have $\mathcal{M} \not\models \chi[s']$, so it is good for Eloise.
2. $\psi = \chi_1 \vee \chi_2$: Eloise moves. Some $\chi_i$ is satisfied under $s'$, by the clause for $\vee$, and the move to $(\chi_i, s', +)$ is good for her.
3. $\psi = \chi_1 \wedge \chi_2$: Abelard moves. Both $\chi_i$ are satisfied, by the clause for $\wedge$, so both moves give good positions.
4. $\psi = \exists x \, \chi$: Eloise moves. Some $a \in M$ gives $\mathcal{M} \models \chi[s'[x \mapsto a]]$, and the move $a$ is good for her.
5. $\psi = \forall x \, \chi$: Abelard moves. Every $a \in M$ gives $\mathcal{M} \models \chi[s'[x \mapsto a]]$, so every move is good for Eloise.
6. For $\rho = -$, a position is good for Eloise when $\psi$ fails under $s'$. Then Abelard is the verifier, and the roles in steps 2 to 5 exchange. For example, at $\chi_1 \vee \chi_2$ Abelard moves, and both disjuncts fail.

Now we build the strategy and prove part 1.

7. By the [[axiom of choice|0HTGB13FE2]], fix a function $C$ that picks an element from each nonempty subset of $M$.
8. Define Eloise's strategy $\sigma$ at a position good for her. At a connective, she chooses the first subformula that gives a good position.
9. At a quantifier, she chooses $C$ of the set of elements that give a good position. At other positions, she chooses any legal move.
10. Suppose $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi[s]$. Then the start position $(\varphi, s, +)$ is good for Eloise.
11. By the claim and [[induction|0ASJVEFA9J]] on the number of moves, every position of a play in which Eloise follows $\sigma$ is good for her.
12. Every play is finite, because each move passes to a proper subformula. So the play reaches an atomic position $(\alpha, s'', \rho)$ that is good for Eloise.
13. By the winning rule, Eloise wins at such a position: either $\rho = +$ and $\alpha$ is satisfied, or $\rho = -$ and $\alpha$ fails. So $\sigma$ is a winning strategy.
14. In the same way, if $\mathcal{M} \not\models \varphi[s]$, then the start position is good for Abelard, and Abelard has a winning strategy that keeps the positions good for him.
15. The two players cannot both have winning strategies: if each follows one, the resulting single play would be won by both, which the rules exclude.
16. Suppose Eloise has a winning strategy. By step 15, Abelard has none, so by step 14, $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi[s]$. With step 13, this proves part 1.
17. Part 2 follows in the same way, with the players exchanged, and it shows that the game is determined.

## Idea

Tarski's clauses say exactly that, at a true position, the verifier has a move to a true position and every move of the falsifier keeps the truth.
So the verifier can play to preserve truth, and truth at the end means winning.
Choice enters only in step 7, to turn "a good move exists" into a single strategy that picks one for every position.

## Variants

Another proof goes by induction on $\varphi$: winning strategies for the subformulas are combined into a strategy for $\varphi$.
At $\forall x \, \chi$, this needs a winning strategy for each $a \in M$, chosen at once, which again uses the axiom of choice.
For a finite structure, the existence of winning strategies can also be computed by backward induction on the game tree.
