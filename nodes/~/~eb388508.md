## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, so $A^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $A^{\mathsf{T}} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 9 & 2 + 12 \\ 2 + 12 & 4 + 16 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 14 \\ 14 & 20 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. By the [[definition|07PTPSZPME]], $\operatorname{tr}(A^{\mathsf{T}} A) = 10 + 20 = 30$.
3. The sum of the squares of the entries of $A$ is $1 + 4 + 9 + 16 = 30$, the same number.
4. In general, $(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)_{jj} = \sum_i a_{ij}^2$ is the squared length of column $j$. Summing over $j$ gives $\operatorname{tr}(A^{\mathsf{T}} A) = \sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2$.
5. Also $A A^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 11 \\ 11 & 25 \end{pmatrix}$ has trace $30$, as the cyclic property $\operatorname{tr}(A A^{\mathsf{T}}) = \operatorname{tr}(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)$ predicts.

## Remarks

The number $\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)} = \sqrt{30}$ is the [[Frobenius norm|JT8Q204E4R]] of $A$.
For real $A$, $\operatorname{tr}(A^{\mathsf{T}} A) = 0$ only when $A = 0$.
For complex $A$, the right form is $\operatorname{tr}(A^* A) = \sum_{i,j} |a_{ij}|^2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Multiply a matrix by its transpose and add the diagonal.
The result is the sum of the squares of all the entries.

### L2 human

The diagonal of $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ holds the squared lengths of the columns.
So its trace is the squared length of $A$ seen as a vector with $n^2$ coordinates.
