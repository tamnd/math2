## Example

Let $F = \mathbb{F}_2(s)$ be the field of rational functions in one variable $s$ over the field with two elements.
It is not a [[perfect field|VWBVKT794W]], since $s$ is not a square in $F$.
Let
$$C = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & s \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
the [[companion matrix|0ZCWPNZTQ3]] of $x^2 + s$ over $F$.

1. $C^2 = \begin{pmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & s \end{pmatrix} = s I$, and $C$ is not a scalar matrix. So the minimal polynomial of $C$ is $x^2 - s = x^2 + s$.
2. $x^2 + s$ has no root in $F$, since $s$ is not a square. A polynomial of degree $2$ without roots is [[irreducible|03FKX7Z5DC]].
3. So $C$ has no eigenvector in $F^2$, and the only $C$-invariant subspaces of $F^2$ are $0$ and $F^2$.
4. Each of these has the other as an invariant complement. So $C$ is [[semisimple|08VRX3Q4PB]] over $F$.
5. Let $\bar F$ be an [[algebraic closure|0X6AB5P3G1]] of $F$, and let $a \in \bar F$ with $a^2 = s$. In characteristic $2$, $x^2 + s = (x + a)^2 = (x - a)^2$.
6. So $a$ is the only eigenvalue of $C$ over $\bar F$. Since $C \ne a I$, $C$ is not [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] over $\bar F$.
7. $C - a I = \begin{pmatrix} a & s \\ 1 & a \end{pmatrix}$, and $(C - a I)^2 = \begin{pmatrix} a^2 + s & 2 a s \\ 2 a & s + a^2 \end{pmatrix} = 0$, since $a^2 = s$ and $2 = 0$.
8. So over $\bar F$, the decomposition $C = a I + (C - a I)$ has a nonzero nilpotent part, which is not defined over $F$.

## Remarks

This is the trap of the word "semisimple".
In characteristic $0$, and more generally over a perfect field, a map is semisimple exactly when it is diagonalizable over the algebraic closure.
Over the imperfect field $F$, the irreducible polynomial $x^2 + s$ becomes a square over $\bar F$, and this equivalence fails.
The same works for any prime $p$, with $F = \mathbb{F}_p(s)$ and the companion matrix of $x^p - s$.

Over $F$, the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]] can fail to exist.
Let $T$ be the companion matrix of $(x^2 + s)^2 = x^4 + s^2$ over $F$, acting on $F[x]/(x^4 + s^2)$ as multiplication by $x$.
Its minimal polynomial equals its characteristic polynomial, so every map that commutes with $T$ is a polynomial in $T$.
Let $T = S + N$ with $S N = N S$ and $N$ nilpotent.
Then $N$ commutes with $T = S + N$, so $N = h(T)$ for a polynomial $h$.
Since $N$ is nilpotent, $f = x^2 + s$ divides $h$.
Then $S^2 = T^2 + N^2 = T^2$, since $2 = 0$ and $f^2 = 0$ in this ring.
So $S^2 + s = f(T) \ne 0$, while $(S^2 + s)^2 = 0$.
So the minimal polynomial of $S$ is $(x^2 + s)^2$, which has a repeated irreducible factor, and $S$ is not semisimple.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Over most familiar fields, such as the rational, real or complex numbers, semisimple maps become diagonal once we allow complex or more general numbers.
This example uses a strange field where that fails.
The matrix has no invariant line, so it looks as simple as possible.
But in a bigger field, its only eigenvalue is repeated, and it is not diagonal in any basis.

### L2 human

Check step 1.

1. $C e_1 = e_2$ and $C e_2 = s e_1$.
2. So $C^2 e_1 = C e_2 = s e_1$ and $C^2 e_2 = s C e_1 = s e_2$.
3. So $C^2 = s I$, and $C^2 + s I = 2 s I = 0$ in characteristic $2$.

The typical use is to remember that the perfect field hypothesis in the Jordan-Chevalley decomposition is needed.
The trap is to identify "semisimple" with "diagonalizable over the algebraic closure" in characteristic $p$ without checking that the field is perfect.

### L3 human

The map $C$ makes $F^2$ into the field $F(a) = F[x]/(x^2 + s)$, which is a purely inseparable extension of $F$.
After extending scalars, $F(a) \otimes_F \bar F$ is not reduced, and this gives the nilpotent part.
Over a perfect field all finite extensions are separable, so the tensor product stays reduced and semisimplicity is preserved.
