## Example

Let $L$ be the propositional language with $\neg$ and $\lor$.
A *bivalent valuation* is any map $v : L \to \set{\mathsf{T}, \mathsf{F}}$; it is *Boolean* if it follows the [[truth tables|05RSK4Q657]] of $\neg$ and $\lor$.
Let $\vDash$ be classical consequence, defined by the Boolean valuations, in its [[multiple-conclusion|3AJTDAP93H]] form.
A valuation $v$ *respects* $\vDash$ if, whenever $\Gamma \vDash \Delta$ and $v$ makes all of $\Gamma$ true, $v$ makes some element of $\Delta$ true.

Claim 1: a bivalent valuation respects the multiple-conclusion relation $\vDash$ if and only if it is Boolean.

1. A Boolean valuation respects $\vDash$ by the definition of $\vDash$.
2. Conversely, let $v$ respect $\vDash$. These five facts hold by the truth tables: $A, \neg A \vDash \emptyset$; $\emptyset \vDash A, \neg A$; $A \lor B \vDash A, B$; $A \vDash A \lor B$; $B \vDash A \lor B$.
3. From $A, \neg A \vDash \emptyset$, the valuation $v$ does not make both $A$ and $\neg A$ true.
4. From $\emptyset \vDash A, \neg A$, the valuation $v$ makes $A$ or $\neg A$ true.
5. By 3 and 4, $v(\neg A) = \mathsf{T}$ if and only if $v(A) = \mathsf{F}$.
6. From $A \lor B \vDash A, B$: if $v(A \lor B) = \mathsf{T}$, then $v(A) = \mathsf{T}$ or $v(B) = \mathsf{T}$.
7. From $A \vDash A \lor B$ and $B \vDash A \lor B$: if $v(A) = \mathsf{T}$ or $v(B) = \mathsf{T}$, then $v(A \lor B) = \mathsf{T}$.
8. By 5, 6 and 7, $v$ follows the truth tables, so $v$ is Boolean.

Claim 2: some valuations that are not Boolean respect every single-conclusion inference $\Gamma \vDash A$.

9. Let $v^*(A) = \mathsf{T}$ if and only if $A$ is a [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]].
10. Let $\Gamma \vDash A$ and let $v^*$ make all of $\Gamma$ true. Then every element of $\Gamma$ is a tautology.
11. So every Boolean valuation makes all of $\Gamma$ true, and so makes $A$ true. Hence $A$ is a tautology and $v^*(A) = \mathsf{T}$.
12. But $v^*(p) = v^*(\neg p) = \mathsf{F}$, while $v^*(p \lor \neg p) = \mathsf{T}$. So $v^*$ is not Boolean.
13. The valuation $v^{\top}$ that makes every formula true also respects every single-conclusion inference, and $v^{\top}(p) = v^{\top}(\neg p) = \mathsf{T}$.

## Remarks

The valuation $v^*$ violates the multiple-conclusion fact $\emptyset \vDash p, \neg p$, and $v^{\top}$ violates $p, \neg p \vDash \emptyset$.
So the multiple-conclusion relation excludes both, as Claim 1 requires.
This is a precise form of the observation that the classical inferences fix the meaning of $\neg$ and $\lor$ only with multiple conclusions.
Claim 1 assumes the [[principle of bivalence|5DYNDW9526]]: it is about maps into $\set{\mathsf{T}, \mathsf{F}}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Ordinary valid arguments do not tell us exactly how "not" and "or" behave.
A strange assignment that calls only tautologies true is consistent with all of them.
Arguments with several conclusions rule out such strange assignments.

### L2 human

The inferences $\emptyset \vDash A, \neg A$ and $A \lor B \vDash A, B$ have no single-conclusion form that a valuation must respect.
They force excluded middle and the disjunction property at the level of each valuation.
A trap is to think that the single-conclusion tautology $p \lor \neg p$ forces $p$ or $\neg p$ to be true; the valuation $v^*$ shows that it does not.
