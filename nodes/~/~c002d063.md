## Statement

Let $\alpha$ be an ordinal, and let $\mathbf{CA}_\alpha$ be the class of [[cylindric algebras|RKCSWWVYTC]] of dimension $\alpha$.
A cylindric set algebra of dimension $\alpha$ is a subalgebra of the full set algebra $\mathcal{P}(U^\alpha)$ for a nonempty set $U$.
$\mathbf{RCA}_\alpha$ is the class of algebras isomorphic to subdirect products of cylindric set algebras.
An algebra is locally finite if each element $x$ has a finite dimension set $\Delta x = \set{\kappa : c_\kappa x \ne x}$.

**Theorem.**

1. $\mathbf{RCA}_\alpha$ is a [[variety|AJNZJD8442]].
2. If $\alpha \ge \omega$, every locally finite algebra in $\mathbf{CA}_\alpha$ is representable.
3. If $3 \le \alpha < \omega$, then $\mathbf{RCA}_\alpha$ is not axiomatizable by any finite set of first-order sentences.
4. In particular, if $3 \le \alpha < \omega$, then $\mathbf{RCA}_\alpha \ne \mathbf{CA}_\alpha$.

Part 1 is due to Tarski, part 3 to Monk; part 2 is proved in the monograph of Henkin, Monk and Tarski.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. The bound $\alpha \ge \omega$ in part 2 is needed. For finite $\alpha$, every algebra in $\mathbf{CA}_\alpha$ is locally finite, since $\Delta x \subseteq \alpha$. So part 2 for $\alpha = 3$ would give $\mathbf{CA}_3 = \mathbf{RCA}_3$, against part 4.
2. Local finiteness in part 2 cannot be dropped: for infinite $\alpha$ there are non-representable algebras in $\mathbf{CA}_\alpha$, and by part 2 none of them is locally finite.
3. The bound $\alpha \ge 3$ in part 3 is needed for the method: in dimension $1$, every algebra is representable.

### Consequences

1. Apply part 2 to [[the algebra of a first-order theory|~4b38e814]]: this gives [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]] for first-order logic with equality.
2. $\mathbf{CA}_\alpha$ for finite $\alpha$ is defined by finitely many equations. Part 3 shows that no finite set of extra equations defines the representable algebras.
3. The equations valid in $\mathbf{RCA}_\alpha$ for finite $\alpha$ are the equational form of the valid formulas of first-order logic in $\alpha$ variables. Part 3 is related to the fact that proofs of such formulas may need more than $\alpha$ variables.

### Converse

The converse of part 2 fails: a representable algebra need not be locally finite.
In $\mathcal{P}(U^\omega)$ with $|U| \ge 2$, the set $X$ of constant sequences has $c_\kappa X \ne X$ for every $\kappa$, so $\Delta X = \omega$.

### Generalizations

1. Part 2 extends to the dimension-complemented algebras, in which $\alpha \setminus \Delta x$ is infinite for every $x$.
2. For infinite $\alpha \ge \omega$, Monk's result has a form for finite schemas of equations; see Henkin, Monk and Tarski, Part II.
3. Andréka proved stronger forms of Monk's result.
4. The same questions for relation algebras have similar answers; see [[this theorem|~122d50e8]].

## History

Tarski introduced cylindric algebras as the algebraic form of first-order logic with equality, and he proved that the representable algebras form a variety.
Monk proved the non-finite axiomatizability of $\mathbf{RCA}_\alpha$ for finite $\alpha \ge 3$ in 1969.
Henkin, Monk and Tarski collected the theory in their monograph, Part I (1971) and Part II (1985).

## Explanations

### L1 human

A cylindric algebra is an abstract algebra with the laws of "or", "not", "there exists" and "equals".
Representable means that it is really an algebra of sets of points, with the usual meanings of these operations.
The theorem says two things.
If each element depends on only finitely many variables, the abstract algebra is always representable.
But with a fixed finite number $3$ or more of variables, no finite list of laws captures exactly the representable algebras.

### L2 human

1. Take the theory $T$ of dense linear orders without endpoints, and its cylindric algebra $A$ of dimension $\omega$.
2. Each formula has finitely many free variables, so $A$ is locally finite.
3. Part 2 gives a homomorphism of $A$ into a set algebra that keeps a chosen nonzero element nonzero.
4. Such a homomorphism is the same as a model of $T$ in which the chosen formula is satisfiable.

The typical use: part 2 gives algebraic proofs of the completeness theorem and of its variants.
The trap: part 3 does not say that $\mathbf{RCA}_\alpha$ is not a variety; it is a variety, but it needs infinitely many equations.

### L3 human

The representable algebras are $\mathbf{SP}$ of the set algebras, and Tarski's theorem says that this class is closed under homomorphic images too.
Henkin's neat embedding theorem describes them differently: an algebra of dimension $\alpha$ is representable iff it embeds into the $\alpha$-dimensional part of an algebra of dimension $\alpha + \omega$.
Monk's method, a sequence of non-representable algebras with a representable [[ultraproduct|AX9Q86GB8M]], is the standard tool for non-finite axiomatizability in algebraic logic.
The same method works for relation algebras and for many-dimensional modal logics.
