## Statement

Let $I$ be an open interval and let $f \colon I \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous and strictly monotone.
Assume that $f$ is differentiable at $a \in I$ with $f'(a) \ne 0$, and let $b = f(a)$.
Then $f^{-1}$ is differentiable at $b$, and
$$(f^{-1})'(b) = \frac{1}{f'(a)} = \frac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(b))}.$$

## Remarks

The inverse is [[continuous|~3bd5368b]], so $f^{-1}(b + k) \to a$ as $k \to 0$. The difference quotient of $f^{-1}$ at $b$ is the reciprocal of a difference quotient of $f$ at $a$.
If $f'(a) = 0$, then $f^{-1}$ is not differentiable at $b$. An example is $f(x) = x^3$ at $a = 0$.
For example, it gives the [[derivative of the logarithm|~275c907b]] from the derivative of the exponential function.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The graph of $f^{-1}$ is the graph of $f$ reflected in the line $y = x$.
The reflection turns a slope $m$ into the slope $1/m$.
