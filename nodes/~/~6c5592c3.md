## Example

Let $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = \{0, 1\}$ be the [[integers modulo $2$|03VFZ2WETT]], with $1 + 1 = 0$.
The [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})$ is the set of [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] $2 \times 2$ matrices with entries in $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, with matrix multiplication.
We compute its [[order|0B85S2V2DR]].

1. A $2 \times 2$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ has $4$ entries, and each entry is $0$ or $1$. So there are $2^4 = 16$ matrices in all.
2. Over a field, a square matrix is invertible if and only if its [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] is nonzero. The integers modulo the prime $2$ form a field, and its only nonzero element is $1$.
3. Modulo $2$ we have $-1 = 1$, so the determinant is $ad - bc = ad + bc$. Each of the products $ad$ and $bc$ is $0$ or $1$, so $ad + bc = 1$ exactly when one of the two products is $1$ and the other is $0$.
4. The case $ad = 1$ and $bc = 0$. The product $ad$ is $1$ only for $a = d = 1$. The product $bc$ is $0$ for $3$ of the $4$ pairs $(b, c)$, namely $(0, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$. This case gives $1 \cdot 3 = 3$ matrices.
5. The case $ad = 0$ and $bc = 1$. In the same way, this case gives $3 \cdot 1 = 3$ matrices.
6. The two cases do not overlap, so exactly $3 + 3 = 6$ matrices have determinant $1$.

Thus $|\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})| = 6$.
The six elements are
$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

## Remarks

The group is not [[abelian|082GHNPJ53]].
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ we get $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so $AB \neq BA$.
The [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$ also has order $6$ and is not abelian.
The six matrices permute the three nonzero column vectors of $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^2$, and only the identity matrix fixes all three.
Thus the action gives an injective [[homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]] into $S_3$, and since both groups have order $6$, it is an [[isomorphism|08MG7H8JW5]] $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}) \cong S_3$.
The general count is in [[the order of the general linear group over a finite field|0JQ1KNQW2K]]: for $q$ elements, $|\mathrm{GL}_2| = (q^2 - 1)(q^2 - q)$, and $q = 2$ gives $3 \cdot 2 = 6$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take the square tables of four entries, where each entry is $0$ or $1$.
There are $16$ such tables.
With the rule $1 + 1 = 0$, only $6$ of them can be undone by multiplication with another table.
These $6$ tables form a group, so the group has order $6$.

### L2 human

The count also follows from the columns of the matrix.

1. A matrix is invertible if and only if its two columns are linearly independent.
2. The first column can be any of the $2^2 - 1 = 3$ nonzero vectors.
3. Over $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, the multiples of the first column $v$ are $0$ and $v$. The second column must avoid these $2$ vectors, so it has $4 - 2 = 2$ choices.
4. Thus there are $3 \cdot 2 = 6$ invertible matrices.

The same method over $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ gives $(9 - 1)(9 - 3) = 48$.
A trap is to count the matrices with nonzero entries; the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has no zero entry, but its determinant is $1 - 1 = 0$.
