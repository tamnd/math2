## Summary

An *elementary proof* is a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] that uses only basic tools, and no advanced theory.
The meaning of "basic" depends on the field, and there is no general agreement on it.
In number theory, the word has a fixed use: an elementary proof uses the integers, the real numbers and inequalities, but no [[complex analysis|28T3Z4VEHH]].
An elementary proof can use real logarithms and limits of real sequences.
"Elementary" describes the tools of a proof, not its length or its difficulty.
Some elementary proofs are long and hard, and the elementary proofs of the [[prime number theorem|0EHBY5Y84N]] are an example.

## Motivation

A theorem about whole numbers can have a proof that leaves the whole numbers.
The first proofs of the prime number theorem study the [[Riemann zeta function|1XWBY4N0VT]] as a function of a complex variable.
So mathematicians asked whether the theorem needs complex analysis, or only the proof that they knew.
An elementary proof answers this kind of question: it shows that the theorem does not need the advanced tool.
Elementary proofs also give new methods, and they can be easier to check in a weak formal system.

## Notation

There is no special symbol.
Authors write "an elementary proof", "by elementary means", "elementary methods" or "an elementary argument".
The adjective "elementary" has other, unrelated uses in mathematics, such as elementary function, elementary matrix and elementary embedding.
The topic [[elementary number theory|7XZ94HTPQT]] is a field of study, not a kind of proof.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many primes is elementary|~001d6a9f]]. It uses only products, the number $1$ and the existence of a prime factor.
2. [[Erdős's proof of Bertrand's postulate is elementary|~6f616dbe]]. It uses binomial coefficients and the factorization of $n!$, and no complex numbers.
3. In 1948, Selberg found new methods, and these led him and Erdős to elementary proofs of the prime number theorem.
4. [[Mertens' theorems|7J3P1W042Y]] on sums over primes have elementary proofs that use [[Legendre's formula|01WPV9PHN7]] and bounds of Chebyshev.
5. The cases $n = 4$ and $n = 3$ of [[Fermat's last theorem|0Y5X3SDBCZ]] have elementary proofs.

### Non-examples

1. [[The 1896 proofs of the prime number theorem use complex analysis|~c84d5258]]. They need the zeta function as a function of a complex variable.
2. The proof of Fermat's last theorem by Wiles is not elementary. It uses elliptic curves and modular forms.
3. Dirichlet's 1837 proof of [[Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions|0GNVN3CA2P]] is not elementary. It uses Dirichlet $L$-functions and analysis.

### Basic properties

1. The word is relative. A proof is elementary relative to a list of allowed tools, and different fields use different lists.
2. Elementary is not easy. The elementary proofs of the prime number theorem are longer and harder than the shortest analytic proofs.
3. A new elementary proof can change the judgment of [[depth|~1ac18cfd]]. The prime number theorem was once called deep because no elementary proof was known.
4. Elementary proofs relate to logic. A proof that uses only induction and arithmetic of integers can be formalized in a weak system, such as [[elementary function arithmetic|2JD80TE553]].

### History

Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin proved the prime number theorem in 1896 with complex analysis.
In a 1921 lecture, Hardy said that an elementary proof of it seemed "extraordinarily unlikely", because some theorems "lie deep".
In 1948, new methods of Selberg led him and Erdős to elementary proofs.
After these proofs, the theorem was no longer seen as deep in Hardy's sense.
Harvey Friedman conjectured that every theorem published in the *Annals of Mathematics* whose statement is arithmetical can be proved in elementary arithmetic.

### Common errors

1. A student reads "elementary" as "easy" or "short". The word only restricts the tools; an elementary proof can be the hardest proof of a theorem.
2. A student thinks that an elementary proof uses no analysis at all. In number theory, the usual convention allows real logarithms, real inequalities and limits, and excludes complex analysis.
3. A student confuses the topic of elementary number theory with elementary proofs. A theorem of analytic number theory can have an elementary proof.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A proof is elementary when it uses only simple tools.
For whole numbers, the simple tools are adding, multiplying, dividing and comparing.
A proof that uses complex numbers or advanced calculus is not elementary.
An elementary proof can still be very long and hard.
"Elementary" tells you which tools the proof uses, not how easy it is.

### L2 human

Consider Euclid's theorem: there are infinitely many primes.

1. Let $p_1, \dots, p_k$ be any finite list of primes, and let $N = p_1 \cdots p_k + 1$.
2. Each $p_i$ divides $N - 1$, so no $p_i$ divides $N$.
3. Since $N > 1$, $N$ has a prime factor, and this prime is not in the list.

The proof uses only multiplication and division of integers, so it is elementary.
Euler gave another proof of the same theorem: the sum of $1/p$ over all primes diverges.
That proof uses infinite series and logarithms, so it is less elementary, but it gives more information.
A trap: "elementary" is not a property of the theorem; one theorem can have elementary and non-elementary proofs.

### L3 human

In analytic number theory, the prime number theorem is equivalent to the statement that $\zeta(s)$ has no zeros on the line $\operatorname{Re} s = 1$.
Hardy expected that a theorem about the zeros of an analytic function needs the theory of analytic functions.
Selberg's symmetry formula, an identity for sums of $\log^2 p$ and $\log p \log q$, gave a route that stays inside real analysis.
In logic, the distinction becomes precise: a proof is elementary in Friedman's sense if it can be formalized in elementary function arithmetic, a system with exponentiation and bounded induction.
Avigad (2003) discusses how much of number theory can be formalized in such systems.
The distinction also connects with depth: a theorem is often called deep when every known proof needs tools beyond those in the statement.
