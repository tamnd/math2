## Summary

Let $L$ be a relational [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]], and let $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ be $L$-[[structures|983GZBQYT4]] with domains $A$ and $B$.
A *partial isomorphism* from $\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{B}$ is an injective map $p$ from a subset of $A$ into $B$ such that $R^{\mathcal{A}}(a_1, \dots, a_k) \iff R^{\mathcal{B}}(p(a_1), \dots, p(a_k))$ for each $k$-ary relation symbol $R$ and all $a_i$ in the domain of $p$.
A *back-and-forth system* from $\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{B}$ is a nonempty set $I$ of partial isomorphisms with two properties.
Forth: for all $p \in I$ and $a \in A$ there is $q \in I$ with $p \subseteq q$ and $a \in \operatorname{dom} q$.
Back: for all $p \in I$ and $b \in B$ there is $q \in I$ with $p \subseteq q$ and $b \in \operatorname{ran} q$.
The structures are *partially isomorphic*, or *potentially isomorphic*, written $\mathcal{A} \cong_p \mathcal{B}$, when such a set exists.
The formal definition is [[this definition|~9384b148]].
Partial isomorphism is the exact notion of sameness for the infinitary logic $L_{\infty\omega}$, by [[Karp's theorem|~ca02717b]], and it is what the [[back-and-forth method|~61b06888]] turns into an isomorphism for countable structures.

## Motivation

In the proof of Cantor's theorem on dense orders, only one fact about the orders is used: each finite order-preserving map extends by one element in either direction.
This fact makes sense for structures of any size, countable or not.
A back-and-forth system isolates this fact as an object.
It then becomes a question which structures have such a system, and what it says about them when they are not countable.
The answer is Karp's theorem: a back-and-forth system exists exactly when no sentence of $L_{\infty\omega}$ tells the structures apart.

## Notation

We write $I \colon \mathcal{A} \cong_p \mathcal{B}$ when $I$ is a back-and-forth system from $\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{B}$.
The bounded form is a sequence $(I_m)_{m \le n}$ of nonempty sets of partial isomorphisms such that each $p \in I_{m+1}$ extends, in both directions, to a member of $I_m$; one writes $\mathcal{A} \cong_n \mathcal{B}$.
Hodges speaks of back-and-forth equivalent structures.
The letters $\operatorname{dom}$ and $\operatorname{ran}$ denote the domain and the range of a map.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The order-preserving finite maps from $(\Q, <)$ to $(\R, <)$ form a back-and-forth system, although the orders are not isomorphic; see [[this example|~9ed0f11a]].
- Any two infinite sets in the empty signature are partially isomorphic, whatever their cardinalities; see [[this example|~8f3c4221]].
- For an isomorphism $f \colon \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{B}$, the set of restrictions of $f$ to finite subsets is a back-and-forth system.
- The finite partial isomorphisms between two graphs with the extension property form a back-and-forth system; see [[this example|~e23dc699]].

### Non-examples

- $(\Z, <)$ and $(\Z + \Z, <)$ are elementarily equivalent, but no back-and-forth system exists between them; see [[this non-example|~c299ea70]].
- The finite order-preserving maps from $(\Z, <)$ to $(\Q, <)$ do not form a back-and-forth system: back fails for $p = \set{(0, 0), (1, 1)}$ and $b = \frac12$, since no integer lies strictly between $0$ and $1$.
- The empty set of maps has the forth and back properties vacuously, but it is not a back-and-forth system, because the definition asks for a nonempty set.

### Basic properties

- $\cong_p$ is an equivalence relation: the inverses of the members of $I$ form a system from $\mathcal{B}$ to $\mathcal{A}$, and the composites $q \circ p$ of members of two systems form a system.
- If $I$ is a back-and-forth system, the finite restrictions of its members also form one. Given a finite $p' \subseteq p \in I$ and $a \in A$, take $q \supseteq p$ in $I$ with $a \in \operatorname{dom} q$; then $p' \cup \set{(a, q(a))}$ is a finite restriction of $q$.
- If $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ are countable and $\mathcal{A} \cong_p \mathcal{B}$, then $\mathcal{A} \cong \mathcal{B}$, by the back-and-forth method.
- If $\mathcal{A}$ is finite and $\mathcal{A} \cong_p \mathcal{B}$, then $\mathcal{A} \cong \mathcal{B}$: a member $q$ of $I$ with domain $A$ has no proper extension, so back puts every $b \in B$ into the range of $q$.
- $\mathcal{A} \cong_p \mathcal{B}$ if and only if Duplicator has a winning strategy in the infinite [[Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game|2RE7YAZK8R]].
- $\mathcal{A} \cong_p \mathcal{B}$ implies that $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ are [[elementarily equivalent|P3B1V182N4]], and by Karp's theorem it is equivalent to agreement on all sentences of $L_{\infty\omega}$.

### History

Roland Fraïssé used systems of partial isomorphisms in his thesis of the early 1950s to describe elementary equivalence.
The characterization of partial isomorphism by the logic $L_{\infty\omega}$ is named after Carol Karp.

### Common errors

- To read $\cong_p$ as isomorphism. $(\Q, <) \cong_p (\R, <)$, and they are not isomorphic.
- To forget nonemptiness. Without it every pair of structures would have a system, the empty one.
- To ask only for forth. Forth alone gives embeddings of finite parts, and $(\N, <)$ and $(\Q, <)$ have such a system without back.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A back-and-forth system is a supply of small matchings between two structures.
Each matching pairs a few elements of one structure with a few elements of the other and keeps all relations between them.
The supply is large enough that any matching can be extended by one more element, chosen on either side.
For example, between two infinite sets with no structure at all, every one-to-one matching of finitely many elements can be extended, so the matchings of finite sets form such a supply.
Two structures with such a supply look alike to every test that inspects finitely many elements at a time, even when they have different sizes.

### L2 human

We check that the finite restrictions of the translations of $(\Z, <)$ form a back-and-forth system from $(\Z, <)$ to itself.

1. Let $I$ be the set of maps $x \mapsto x + t$ restricted to finite subsets of $\Z$, for all $t \in \Z$.
2. Each member is injective and preserves and reflects $<$, so it is a partial isomorphism.
3. Forth: $p$ is a restriction of $x \mapsto x + t$ to a finite set $F$, and $a \in \Z$. The restriction to $F \cup \set{a}$ extends $p$ and lies in $I$.
4. Back: for $b \in \Z$, the restriction to $F \cup \set{b - t}$ has $b$ in its range.
5. So $I$ is a back-and-forth system, and every member of $I$ extends to an automorphism.

The typical use of a system is to prove that two structures cannot be told apart by a logic, through Karp's theorem or the game.
A trap is the map $\set{(0, 0), (1, 2)}$: it is a partial isomorphism, but no back-and-forth system on $(\Z, <)$ contains it, because back fails for $b = 1$.
