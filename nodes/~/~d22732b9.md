## Example

Let HLJ be the calculus HLC of the [[definition of hypersequent calculus|~b56e1b30]] without the communication rule $\mathsf{com}$.
Let LJm be the sequent calculus with the same axioms and the same internal, logical and cut rules, applied to single sequents with no side hypersequent.
LJm is a multiset form of [[LJ|PZX9J3VEMT]], so it derives exactly the intuitionistically valid sequents.
We show that HLJ does not derive $\Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$, although HLJ has hypersequents, external weakening and external contraction.

**Claim.** If a hypersequent $H$ is derivable in HLJ, then some component of $H$ is derivable in LJm.

We prove the claim by induction on the derivation of $H$.

1. Axiom. $H$ is $A \Rightarrow A$ or $\bot \Rightarrow$, which has one component, and that component is an axiom of LJm.
2. External weakening. $H$ is $G \mid S$ and $G$ is derivable. By induction a component of $G$ is derivable in LJm, and it is also a component of $H$.
3. External contraction. $H$ is $G \mid S$ and $G \mid S \mid S$ is derivable. By induction a component of $G$ or the component $S$ is derivable in LJm. Both are components of $H$.
4. A rule with one premise $G \mid S$ and conclusion $G \mid S'$. This covers internal weakening and contraction, ${\wedge}$L, ${\vee}$R and ${\to}$R.
5. In case 4, by induction a component of $G$ is derivable, or $S$ is derivable. In the first case that component is in $H$. In the second case the same rule of LJm derives $S'$ from $S$.
6. A rule with two premises $G \mid S_1$ and $G \mid S_2$ and conclusion $G \mid S'$. This covers ${\wedge}$R, ${\vee}$L, ${\to}$L and cut.
7. In case 6, if a component of $G$ is derivable, it is in $H$. Otherwise the induction hypothesis gives derivations of $S_1$ and of $S_2$ in LJm.
8. Then the same rule of LJm derives $S'$ from $S_1$ and $S_2$.
9. These cases cover every rule of HLJ, so the claim holds.

**Consequence.** Suppose that HLJ derives $\Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$.

10. This hypersequent has one component, so by the claim LJm derives the sequent $\Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$.
11. Then $(p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$ is valid in every intuitionistic Kripke model, by soundness of LJ.
12. Take the Kripke model with root $r$ and two incomparable worlds $a, b$ above $r$. Let $p$ be true at $a$ only and $q$ at $b$ only.
13. At $r$, $p \to q$ fails, because $a \ge r$ forces $p$ and does not force $q$.
14. At $r$, $q \to p$ fails, because $b \ge r$ forces $q$ and does not force $p$.
15. So the disjunction fails at $r$. This contradicts step 11, so HLJ does not derive the hypersequent.

## Remarks

The proof of the claim uses only one feature of the rules: every rule except $\mathsf{com}$ has the same side hypersequent $G$ in all premises and in the conclusion.
So the condition that fails is the presence of a rule that makes two components interact.
The [[example of the prelinearity axiom|~01080d03]] shows that adding $\mathsf{com}$ is enough to derive the formula.

The argument works for any extension of HLJ by rules that act on one component.
External structural rules alone never make a calculus stronger than its sequent part.

The [[Kripke semantics|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]] model in step 12 has two incomparable worlds.
On a model whose worlds form a chain, the formula is valid, and that is why $\mathsf{com}$ is sound for LC.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A hypersequent says "one of these claims holds".
If the rules only ever work on one claim at a time, the bars do nothing useful.
Each proof then shows one of the claims on its own.
So the calculus without the communication rule proves only what ordinary intuitionistic logic proves.
Ordinary intuitionistic logic cannot prove "$p$ gives $q$, or $q$ gives $p$", so this calculus cannot prove it either.

### L2 human

The argument has two parts.

1. Show by induction on derivations that some component of each derivable hypersequent is derivable alone.
2. Show with a Kripke model of three worlds that $(p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$ is not intuitionistically valid.

The typical use of this argument is to check that a new structural rule really adds strength to a hypersequent calculus.
A trap is to think that external contraction lets components interact: it only removes a copy of a component that is already there.
