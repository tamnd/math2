## Example

Let $P$ and $Q$ be unary relation symbols, and let $\varphi = \exists x \, P(x) \wedge \exists y \, Q(y)$.
The [[formula|HYJ10AF6RT]] $\varphi$ has two quantifier symbols.
Its [[quantifier rank|~e5a7f04b]] is $1$.

1. $P(x)$ and $Q(y)$ are atomic, of rank $0$.
2. $\exists x \, P(x)$ and $\exists y \, Q(y)$ have rank $1$.
3. The conjunction takes the maximum, so $\operatorname{qr}(\varphi) = \max(1, 1) = 1$.

So the map "number of quantifier symbols" is not the quantifier rank.
More generally, $\bigwedge_{i=1}^{m} \exists x_i \, P_i(x_i)$ has $m$ quantifiers and rank $1$.

## Remarks

The [[prenex normal form|CS82V969DE]] $\exists x \, \exists y \, (P(x) \wedge Q(y))$ of $\varphi$ is equivalent to $\varphi$ and has rank $2$.
So the rank is a property of the formula as written, and equivalent formulas can have different ranks.
The rank of a property is the least rank of a formula that expresses it; for the property of $\varphi$ it is $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The formula asks two separate questions: is there a $P$, and is there a $Q$.
Neither question depends on the answer to the other, so a game needs only one round to test either of them.
Counting the quantifier symbols treats the two questions as one long chain, and that overcounts.

### L2 human

We see the difference in a game on two structures with one element each.
Let $\mathcal{A}$ have one element in $P$ and in $Q$, and let $\mathcal{B}$ have one element in $P$ only.

1. $\varphi$ holds in $\mathcal{A}$ and fails in $\mathcal{B}$.
2. Spoiler wins in one round: Spoiler picks the element of $\mathcal{A}$, and its only possible answer in $\mathcal{B}$ is not in $Q$.
3. One round matches rank $1$, not the two quantifiers of $\varphi$.

A trap is to count quantifiers after moving to prenex form; the rank then counts every quantifier of the prenex prefix.
