## Proof

We build $S$ from the projections onto the generalized eigenspaces, then prove uniqueness with simultaneous diagonalization.

1. Write $p_T = (t - \lambda_1)^{m_1} \cdots (t - \lambda_k)^{m_k}$ with distinct $\lambda_i$. This is possible since $p_T$ splits.
2. By the [[decomposition into generalized eigenspaces|0Z3GDY89T5]], part 3, the projections $\pi_i$ onto the $V_{\lambda_i}$ are polynomials in $T$.
3. Let $S = \sum_i \lambda_i \pi_i$ and $N = T - S$. By part 4 of the same theorem, $S$ is diagonalizable and $N$ is nilpotent.
4. $S$ is a polynomial in $T$, so $N = T - S$ is one too. Polynomials in $T$ commute, so $S N = N S$.
5. This proves existence, and that $S$ and $N$ are polynomials in $T$.
6. For uniqueness, let $T = S' + N'$ with $S'$ diagonalizable, $N'$ nilpotent and $S' N' = N' S'$.
7. $S'$ commutes with $S'$ and with $N'$, so it commutes with $T = S' + N'$. In the same way, $N'$ commutes with $T$.
8. $S$ and $N$ are polynomials in $T$, by step 5. So $S'$ and $N'$ commute with $S$ and with $N$.
9. $S$ and $S'$ are commuting diagonalizable maps. By [[simultaneous diagonalization|0FK17N4DKA]], they are diagonal in one basis.
10. In that basis $S - S'$ is a difference of [[diagonal matrices|0Q9PGXD9E4]], so it is diagonal. Thus $S - S'$ is diagonalizable.
11. By the [[nilpotent characterizations|~662223db]], $N^n = 0$ and $N'^n = 0$.
12. $N$ and $N'$ commute, so the [[binomial theorem|DS9TS6AFMW]] expands $(N' - N)^{2n}$ as a sum of multiples of $N'^a N^b$ with $a + b = 2n$.
13. In each term, $a \ge n$ or $b \ge n$. So each term is $0$, and $N' - N$ is nilpotent.
14. $S - S' = N' - N$, since both sides come from $T = S + N = S' + N'$.
15. So $D = S - S'$ is diagonalizable and nilpotent. Let $\operatorname{diag}(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n)$ be its diagonal form.
16. $D^{2n} = 0$, so $\mu_j^{2n} = 0$, and each $\mu_j = 0$ since $F$ is a field.
17. So the diagonal form is $0$, and $D = 0$. Hence $S' = S$ and $N' = N$.

## Idea

The projections onto the generalized eigenspaces are polynomials in $T$, so $S = \sum_i \lambda_i \pi_i$ is one too.
Any other decomposition commutes with $T$, hence with $S$ and $N$.
Then $S - S'$ is both diagonalizable and nilpotent, which forces it to be $0$.

## Variants

One can construct $S = g(T)$ directly, with $g \equiv \lambda_i \bmod (t - \lambda_i)^{m_i}$ for each $i$, by the Chinese remainder theorem.
Over a perfect field, the same uniqueness argument works with "semisimple" in place of "diagonalizable", since commuting semisimple maps have a semisimple sum there.
Existence over a perfect field follows by passing to the algebraic closure and showing that $S$ is fixed by the Galois group, or by a Newton iteration.
