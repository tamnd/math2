## Example

Problem: prove that $a^4 + b^4 = c^4$ has no solution in positive integers.
We show that the [[inventor's paradox|~aaa3fffe]] is a bad guide here, if the more ambitious problem is taken to be the theorem for every exponent.

1. The more ambitious problem. [[Fermat's Last Theorem|0Y5X3SDBCZ]] states that $a^n + b^n = c^n$ has no solution in positive integers for any $n \ge 3$. It contains the given problem as the case $n = 4$.
2. The cost of the ambitious problem. Fermat stated it around 1637, and Andrew Wiles proved it in 1994, with a step completed jointly with Richard Taylor. The proof uses elliptic curves and modular forms, and it is long and difficult.
3. The cost of the special problem. The case $n = 4$ has a short proof by [[infinite descent|0EFYCFHYFG]], which goes back to Fermat; see [[Fermat's Last Theorem for n = 4|0E741HSMJ1]].
4. The structure of that proof. A solution of $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$ in positive integers is shown to give a smaller one, through the classification of Pythagorean triples. Since positive integers cannot decrease forever, there is no solution.
5. The given problem follows: a solution of $a^4 + b^4 = c^4$ gives the solution $x = a$, $y = b$, $z = c^2$ of $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$.

So the general problem was not easier.
A solver who had followed the inventor's paradox in the form "prove it for every $n$" would have been stuck for three centuries.

## Remarks

The paradox is a suggestion, not a law, and this is the standard warning.
The general problem was harder because the method of step 4 depends on the exponent $4$: it uses that $a^4$ is a square, and no single descent works for every $n$.

The example still contains the paradox in a smaller form.
Step 4 proves the stronger statement about $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$, because the descent produces equations of that form and not of the form $a^4 + b^4 = c^4$.
So the right strengthening was a small one, chosen to make the descent close, and not the most ambitious one.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Fermat claimed that $a^n + b^n = c^n$ has no solutions in whole numbers for any power $n$ above $2$.
For the power $4$ there is a short proof: from one solution one could always build a smaller one, which is impossible.
For all powers at once, the proof took more than $350$ years and needed whole new areas of mathematics.
So here the small question was far easier than the big one.

### L2 human

We check how far the case $n = 4$ reaches: it also settles $n = 8$ and $n = 12$.

1. A solution of $a^8 + b^8 = c^8$ gives $(a^2)^4 + (b^2)^4 = (c^2)^4$, a solution for $n = 4$.
2. In the same way a solution for $n = 12$ gives one for $n = 4$, with $a^3$, $b^3$, $c^3$.
3. So every multiple of $4$ is settled by the case $n = 4$.

The typical lesson: before choosing the general problem, ask whether the method for the special case has any chance of working in general.

A trap: the reduction of step 1 goes from large exponents to their divisors, so the case $n = 4$ settles the multiples of $4$, but it says nothing about $n = 3$ or $n = 5$.
