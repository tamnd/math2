## Example

Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
over $\R$, with $p_A = (t - 1)(t - 2)^2$, as in the [[generalized eigenspace example|~47d94525]].
We compute its [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]] $A = S + N$.

1. [[Bézout's identity|0VY3WPP9Y3]] for the coprime factors: $(t - 2)^2 - (t - 1)(t - 3) = (t^2 - 4t + 4) - (t^2 - 4t + 3) = 1$.
2. By the [[kernels lemma|~4b2aa8fd]], the projection onto $V_1 = \ker (A - I)$ is $\pi_1 = (A - 2I)^2$.
3. With $M = A - 2I$, $\pi_1 = M^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $\pi_2 = I - \pi_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. $S = 1 \cdot \pi_1 + 2 \pi_2 = 2 I - M^2$. Since $M^2 = A^2 - 4A + 4I$, $S = -A^2 + 4A - 2I$.
5. $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 4 & 3 \\ -4 & 8 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so $S = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
6. $N = A - S = A^2 - 3A + 2I = (A - I)(A - 2I) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
7. $N^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - 1 & -1 + 1 & 0 \\ 1 - 1 & -1 + 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = 0$, so $N$ is [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]].
8. Let $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$. Then $Q^2 = I$ and $S = Q \operatorname{diag}(2, 2, 1) Q^{-1}$, so $S$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]].
9. $S N = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 2 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = N S$. So the parts commute.

## Remarks

The columns of $Q$ are eigenvectors of $S$: $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$ for $2$, and $(1, 1, -1)$ for $1$.
Step 8 can be checked as $S Q = Q \operatorname{diag}(2, 2, 1)$: the first two columns of $S Q$ are $2 e_1$ and $2 e_2$, and the third is $(1, 1, -1)$.
$N$ is a product of the factors $A - I$ and $A - 2I$, so $N$ is $0$ on $V_1$, and on $V_2$ it equals $A - 2I$.
The diagonal part $\operatorname{diag}(1, 3, 1)$ of $A$ is not $S$.
With $R = A - \operatorname{diag}(1, 3, 1)$, the products of $\operatorname{diag}(1, 3, 1)$ and $R$ in the two orders differ.
$A$ itself is not diagonalizable, since $N \ne 0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

We split the matrix $A$ into two parts.
The first part $S$ only stretches vectors along three directions, by $2$, $2$ and $1$.
The second part $N$ dies after two steps.
The two parts commute, and both are built from $A$ by sums and products.

### L2 human

Check that $S$ and $A$ have the same characteristic polynomial.

1. $S$ is block upper triangular with diagonal entries $2, 2, 1$.
2. So $p_S = (t - 2)^2 (t - 1)$, by the triangular determinant.
3. This equals $p_A$, as it must, since $N$ is nilpotent and commutes with $S$.

The typical use is to compute $A^k = \sum_j \binom{k}{j} S^{k-j} N^j = S^k + k S^{k-1} N$, since $N^2 = 0$.
The trap is to take $S$ as the diagonal of $A$, which gives a decomposition with non-commuting parts.

### L3 human

The polynomial $g(t) = -t^2 + 4t - 2$ satisfies $g \equiv 1 \bmod (t - 1)$ and $g \equiv 2 \bmod (t - 2)^2$.
These congruences determine $g$ modulo $m_A = (t - 1)(t - 2)^2$, by the Chinese remainder theorem.
