## Proof

We write each projection with an orthonormal eigenbasis and check all identities on the basis vectors.

1. Let $e_1, \dots, e_n$ be an orthonormal basis with $T e_k = \mu_k e_k$. It exists by the hypothesis. For normal $T$ over $\mathbb{C}$ it exists by [[the complex spectral theorem|04CW1P44VD]], and for self-adjoint $T$ over $\mathbb{R}$ by [[the real spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]].
2. For $i = 1, \dots, m$, let $J_i$ be the set of $k$ with $\mu_k = \lambda_i$. Each $k$ lies in exactly one $J_i$, because each $\mu_k$ is one of the distinct eigenvalues.
3. By [[coordinates in an orthonormal basis|09DZ8CD1AK]], $v = \sum_k \ip{v}{e_k} e_k$ for every $v \in V$. So $(T - \lambda_i I) v = \sum_k (\mu_k - \lambda_i) \ip{v}{e_k} e_k$.
4. Let $v \in E_i$, the [[eigenspace|0AYEPBQMAW]] of $\lambda_i$. Then the sum in step 3 is $0$. The $e_k$ are [[linearly independent|0JWM6GJNQW]], so $\ip{v}{e_k} = 0$ for every $k \notin J_i$.
5. Conversely, each $e_k$ with $k \in J_i$ lies in $E_i$. With step 4, the vectors $e_k$ with $k \in J_i$ are an orthonormal basis of $E_i$.
6. Define $Q_i v = \sum_{k \in J_i} \ip{v}{e_k} e_k$. Then $Q_i v \in E_i$, and $v - Q_i v = \sum_{k \notin J_i} \ip{v}{e_k} e_k$ is orthogonal to every $e_k$ with $k \in J_i$. So $v - Q_i v \in E_i^\perp$.
7. The decomposition $v = w + w'$ with $w \in E_i$ and $w' \in E_i^\perp$ is unique, by the [[orthogonal decomposition|0SP7P0G7KQ]]. So $Q_i v$ is the [[orthogonal projection|0Q8HZTJC7G]] $P_i v$, and $P_i e_k = e_k$ for $k \in J_i$ and $P_i e_k = 0$ otherwise.
8. Two linear maps that agree on a basis are equal. So we check identities on each $e_k$. Let $k \in J_l$.
9. Part 1. $P_i P_j e_k$ is $e_k$ if $i = j = l$, and $0$ otherwise. So $P_i^2 = P_i$, and $P_i P_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$. Also $\sum_i P_i e_k = P_l e_k = e_k$, so $\sum_i P_i = I$.
10. For $v, w \in V$, step 6 gives $\ip{P_i v}{w} = \sum_{k \in J_i} \ip{v}{e_k} \ip{e_k}{w}$. The same sum equals $\ip{v}{P_i w} = \sum_{k \in J_i} \overline{\ip{w}{e_k}} \ip{v}{e_k}$. So $P_i$ is self-adjoint.
11. Part 2. $\sum_i \lambda_i P_i e_k = \lambda_l e_k = \mu_k e_k = T e_k$. So $T = \sum_i \lambda_i P_i$.
12. Part 3. For $r \geq 0$, $T^r e_k = \mu_k^r e_k = \lambda_l^r e_k$, and $\sum_i \lambda_i^r P_i e_k = \lambda_l^r e_k$. So $T^r = \sum_i \lambda_i^r P_i$. A linear combination of these identities gives $p(T) = \sum_i p(\lambda_i) P_i$.
13. Part 4. Let $p_i(t) = \prod_{j \neq i} (t - \lambda_j) / (\lambda_i - \lambda_j)$. The denominators are not $0$, because the $\lambda_j$ are distinct. Then $p_i(\lambda_i) = 1$, and $p_i(\lambda_l) = 0$ for $l \neq i$.
14. By part 3, $p_i(T) = \sum_l p_i(\lambda_l) P_l = P_i$. This is the product formula of part 4.
15. Part 5. By [[the algebraic properties of the adjoint|~2b450581]], $T^* = \sum_i \overline{\lambda_i} P_i^* = \sum_i \overline{\lambda_i} P_i$, since each $P_i$ is self-adjoint.
16. Let $q = \sum_i \overline{\lambda_i} p_i$, a polynomial. By part 3 and step 13, $q(T) = \sum_l q(\lambda_l) P_l = \sum_l \overline{\lambda_l} P_l = T^*$.

## Idea

In an orthonormal eigenbasis, $P_i$ keeps the basis vectors for $\lambda_i$ and kills the others.
Every identity is then a statement about diagonal matrices with entries $0$ and $1$.
Lagrange interpolation turns the projections, and hence $T^*$, into polynomials in $T$.

## Variants

- Part 4 can be proved without the eigenbasis. If $T$ is diagonalizable, then $p_i(T)$ is the projection onto $E_i$ along the other eigenspaces. Orthogonality of the eigenspaces, from [[orthogonality of eigenspaces|0JHGAV9H25]], makes this projection orthogonal.
- In matrix form, $P_i = \sum_{k \in J_i} u_k u_k^*$, where $u_k$ are the orthonormal eigenvector columns of $U$ in $A = U D U^*$.
