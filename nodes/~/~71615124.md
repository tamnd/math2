## Example

The [[Sylvester-Gallai theorem|T3GGJN70P2]] says: let $S$ be a finite set of points in the [[Euclidean plane|0XW7V9ZZ05]], not all on one line.
Then some line contains exactly two points of $S$.
Such a line is called an ordinary line.
Kelly's proof is a standard example of a [[proof from the Book|~3e9515d6]].
Let $\mathcal{L}$ be the set of lines that contain at least two points of $S$.

1. If $S$ has $n$ points, then $\mathcal{L}$ has at most $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ lines, so $\mathcal{L}$ is finite.
2. Consider the pairs $(p, \ell)$ with $p \in S$, $\ell \in \mathcal{L}$ and $p \notin \ell$.
3. Such a pair exists. Take two points $a, b$ of $S$; the points of $S$ are not all on the line $ab$.
4. There are finitely many pairs, and each has a positive distance from $p$ to $\ell$.
5. Choose a pair $(p, \ell)$ with the least distance, and call this distance $h > 0$.
6. Suppose $\ell$ contains at least three points of $S$. We derive a contradiction.
7. Choose coordinates with $\ell$ as the $x$-axis and $p = (0, h)$. The foot of the perpendicular from $p$ is $(0, 0)$.
8. Three points of $S$ lie on $\ell$. Two of them have $x$-coordinates of the same sign, counting $0$ as both signs.
9. Reflect in the $y$-axis if needed. Then these two points are $b = (\beta, 0)$ and $c = (\gamma, 0)$ with $0 \le \beta < \gamma$.
10. Let $m$ be the line through $p$ and $c$. Then $m \in \mathcal{L}$.
11. The line $m$ meets the $x$-axis only at $c$, and $b \ne c$. So $b \notin m$, and $(b, m)$ is a pair.
12. The line $m$ has the equation $h x + \gamma y - h \gamma = 0$. It passes through $(0, h)$ and $(\gamma, 0)$.
13. The distance from $b$ to $m$ is $\frac{|h \beta - h \gamma|}{\sqrt{h^2 + \gamma^2}} = \frac{h (\gamma - \beta)}{\sqrt{\gamma^2 + h^2}}$.
14. Since $\beta \ge 0$, we have $\gamma - \beta \le \gamma$. Since $h > 0$, we have $\gamma < \sqrt{\gamma^2 + h^2}$.
15. So the distance from $b$ to $m$ is less than $h$. This contradicts the choice of $(p, \ell)$ in step 5.
16. So $\ell$ contains exactly two points of $S$, and $\ell$ is an ordinary line.

Now we check the criteria of a Book proof.

- Correctness: each step is checked above, and step 8 is the [[pigeonhole principle|0HGVCWQGY3]].
- Brevity: the core of the proof is the choice of step 5 and the estimate of steps 13 to 15.
- One idea: the [[extremal choice|MD3MRG90W4]] of the closest pair does all the work.
- Few prerequisites: the proof needs only the distance from a point to a line.
- Explanation: a line with three points always yields a closer pair, so the closest pair must use an ordinary line.

## Remarks

The example shows the typical shape of an extremal proof.
We choose an object that is least in some measure, and we show that a bad property gives a smaller one.
The finiteness of $S$ in step 4 ensures that a least distance exists.
For the integer lattice $\mathbb{Z}^2$ each line through two points holds infinitely many, and no pair has a least distance.

Aigner and Ziegler give Kelly's proof in their chapter on lines in the plane, and they praise it as the best proof.
James Joseph Sylvester posed the problem in 1893, and Tibor Gallai published a proof in 1944.
Donald Coxeter objected that Kelly's proof uses distance, which is not part of the statement.
The statement uses only points, lines and the order of points on a line.
Coxeter gave a proof that uses only the axioms of order.
So the judgement of a Book proof can depend on what the reader takes as the natural setting of the theorem.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Put a few dots on paper, not all on one straight line.
The theorem says that some straight line passes through exactly two of the dots.
Here is the trick.
Among all pairs of a dot and a line through two other dots, pick the pair where the dot is closest to the line.
If that line held three dots, then two of them would sit on the same side of the point straight below our dot.
A line from our dot to the farther of those two would pass closer to the nearer one.
That gives a closer pair, which is impossible, so the line holds only two dots.

### L2 human

We run step 15 once, with $S = \{(0, 0), (1, 0), (3, 0), (0, 2)\}$.

1. Take $p = (0, 2)$ and $\ell$ the $x$-axis, which holds three points of $S$. The distance is $h = 2$.
2. The points $b = (1, 0)$ and $c = (3, 0)$ lie on the same side of the foot $(0, 0)$, with $\beta = 1$ and $\gamma = 3$.
3. The line $m$ through $(0, 2)$ and $(3, 0)$ has the equation $2x + 3y - 6 = 0$.
4. The distance from $b$ to $m$ is $\frac{|2 - 6|}{\sqrt{4 + 9}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{13}} \approx 1.11$, which is less than $2$.
5. The line $m$ holds no other point of $S$: $(0, 0)$ gives $-6$ and $(1, 0)$ gives $-4$. So $m$ is an ordinary line.

The typical use of the example is to show the [[extremal principle|MD3MRG90W4]] at work in geometry.
A trap is step 8: the two points must lie on the same side of the foot, or the new distance need not be smaller.
