## Definition

Let $L$ be a [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]] with relation symbols only, and let $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ be $L$-[[structures|983GZBQYT4]] with domains $A$ and $B$.

1. A *partial isomorphism* from $\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{B}$ is an injective map $p \colon D \to B$, with $D \subseteq A$, such that for each $k$-ary relation symbol $R$ of $L$ and all $a_1, \dots, a_k \in D$, $\mathcal{A} \models R(a_1, \dots, a_k)$ if and only if $\mathcal{B} \models R(p(a_1), \dots, p(a_k))$.
2. A *back-and-forth system* from $\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{B}$ is a set $I$ of partial isomorphisms from $\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{B}$ with three properties.
3. Nonempty: $I \ne \emptyset$.
4. Forth: for every $p \in I$ and every $a \in A$ there is $q \in I$ with $p \subseteq q$ and $a \in \operatorname{dom} q$.
5. Back: for every $p \in I$ and every $b \in B$ there is $q \in I$ with $p \subseteq q$ and $b \in \operatorname{ran} q$.
6. $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ are *partially isomorphic*, written $\mathcal{A} \cong_p \mathcal{B}$, when a back-and-forth system from $\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{B}$ exists.

## Notation

The maps are viewed as sets of pairs, so $p \subseteq q$ means that $q$ extends $p$.
Some books ask that the members of $I$ be finite; by a basic property of [[back-and-forth systems|~95b1c1d3]] this gives the same relation $\cong_p$.

## Remarks

When $L$ has constant symbols, a partial isomorphism must also map $c^{\mathcal{A}}$ to $c^{\mathcal{B}}$ for each constant $c$, and its domain must contain all $c^{\mathcal{A}}$.
When $L$ has function symbols, one replaces each $k$-ary function by its graph, a $(k+1)$-ary relation, or asks that $p$ be an isomorphism between substructures.

Example: in the empty signature, the set of all injective maps between finite subsets of two infinite sets is a back-and-forth system.

Non-example: for $\mathcal{A} = (\set{0, 1}, <)$ and $\mathcal{B} = (\set{0, 1, 2}, <)$, the set of all finite partial isomorphisms is not a back-and-forth system, because the map $\set{(0, 0), (1, 2)}$ has no extension with $1$ in its range.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A partial isomorphism matches some elements of one structure with some elements of the other and keeps every relation among them.
A back-and-forth system is a nonempty collection of such matchings in which every matching can grow by one element, taken from either side, and stay in the collection.

### L2 human

We check the non-example above.

1. The map $p = \set{(0, 0), (1, 2)}$ is injective and $0 < 1$, $0 < 2$, so $p$ is a partial isomorphism.
2. A partial isomorphism $q \supseteq p$ with $1 \in \operatorname{ran} q$ needs some $a \in \set{0, 1}$ with $q(a) = 1$.
3. Both $0$ and $1$ already have the images $0$ and $2$, so no such $a$ exists, and back fails.

A trap is to check forth and back only for the empty map: here the empty map extends in both directions, and the failure appears only at $p$.
