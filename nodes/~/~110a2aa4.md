## Example

For an integer $n$, let $p$ be "$4$ divides $n$", and let $q$ be "$n$ is even".
The implication $p \to q$ is "if $4$ divides $n$, then $n$ is even", and it is true.
Consider the statement $S$: "if $n$ is odd, then $4$ does not divide $n$".
We check that $S$ is neither the [[converse nor the inverse|09A3N7ZN5G]] of $p \to q$.

1. The statement $S$ has the form $\neg q \to \neg p$, because "$n$ is odd" is the negation of "$n$ is even".
2. The converse of $p \to q$ is $q \to p$. Its antecedent is $q$, and the antecedent of $S$ is $\neg q$. So $S$ is not the converse.
3. The inverse of $p \to q$ is $\neg p \to \neg q$. Its antecedent is $\neg p$, and the antecedent of $S$ is $\neg q$. So $S$ is not the inverse.
4. The truth values confirm this. The converse "if $n$ is even, then $4$ divides $n$" is false for $n = 2$.
5. The inverse "if $4$ does not divide $n$, then $n$ is odd" is false for $n = 2$ as well.
6. The statement $S$ is true: if $4$ divided an odd $n$, then $n$ would be even by $p \to q$.

So $S$ is the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] of $p \to q$, which is the one related implication that is equivalent to $p \to q$.

## Remarks

The example shows that the three related implications differ in two independent operations: swapping the parts and negating them.
The converse swaps, the inverse negates, and the contrapositive does both.
Two operations applied together give back a statement with the same truth value; one operation alone can change it.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Start from "if a number is a multiple of $4$, then it is even".
Swapping the two parts gives "if a number is even, then it is a multiple of $4$", and $2$ shows that this is false.
Swapping and adding "not" to both parts gives "if a number is not even, then it is not a multiple of $4$", and this is true.
The last sentence is the contrapositive, not the converse.

### L2 human

To name a related implication, we compare it with $p \to q$ in two steps.

1. Check the order: find whether the antecedent is built from $p$ or from $q$.
2. Check the negations: find whether the parts are negated.

Order from $q$ and no negations gives the converse; order from $p$ and negations gives the inverse; order from $q$ and negations gives the contrapositive.
A trap is to call every implication with "not" in it the contrapositive, which mixes up the inverse and the contrapositive.
