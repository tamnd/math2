## Summary

Philosophy and mathematics in education is the study of the views of mathematics that teachers and students hold, and of the effect of these views on teaching and learning.
A view of mathematics answers questions such as: what is mathematics, where does its certainty come from, and what is a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] for?
Paul Ernest (1989) described three views of teachers: the instrumentalist view, the Platonist view and the problem-solving view.
The idea matters because a teacher's view shapes what the teacher asks students to do, and a student's view shapes what the student counts as an answer or as a proof.

## Motivation

Two teachers can follow the same syllabus and teach different subjects.
One teacher presents each theorem with a finished proof and asks students to reproduce it.
Another teacher asks students to make conjectures, test them, and argue about them.
The first teacher sees mathematics as a fixed body of results, and the second sees it as a human activity of inquiry.
Students learn the view together with the content.
A student who has only seen proofs to copy may think that a proof is a ritual, not a reason.
So research on the teaching of proof must ask which views of mathematics and of proof the teachers and the students hold.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The instrumentalist view sees mathematics as a set of facts and rules that are useful for other purposes. A teacher with this view stresses procedures and their correct use.
2. The Platonist view sees mathematics as a fixed and unified body of knowledge that is discovered, not invented. A teacher with this view stresses the explanation of the structure of the subject.
3. The problem-solving view sees mathematics as a growing field of human inquiry, whose results stay open to revision. A teacher with this view stresses investigation, conjecture and argument.
4. Imre Lakatos, in "Proofs and Refutations" (published as a book in 1976), wrote a classroom dialogue in which a conjecture about polyhedra changes under counterexamples. Teachers have used the book as a model for a classroom of conjectures and refutations.
5. Gila Hanna (1990) separated proofs that only prove from proofs that also explain. She argued that in the classroom the explaining function of proof should come first.

### Non-examples

1. The question whether mathematical objects exist independently of the mind is a question of the [[philosophy of mathematics|NTJ5XW0PVD]] itself. It belongs here only when the subject is how such views act in teaching.
2. A study of the attitudes of students, such as anxiety or enjoyment, belongs to [[affect in mathematics learning|SAPATZCM0C]]. Attitudes are about feelings, and views of mathematics are about what mathematics is.

### Research findings

1. Ernest (1989) proposed the three views above as a model of the beliefs of teachers. It is a theoretical model, and later studies used it to classify teachers.
2. Alan Schoenfeld (1988) observed a geometry class that was taught well by the usual standards. Its students did not use the results that they had proved when they solved construction problems. He concluded that the students saw proof and construction as separate activities.
3. Eric Knuth (2002) interviewed secondary school teachers in the United States about proof. The teachers saw proof mainly as a way to establish truth, and few of them named the explanation of why a result is true.
4. Hanna (2000) reviewed the functions of proof in the classroom and argued that explanation and exploration matter as much as verification. This uses the list of the [[functions of proof|ENY0GKRJS8]] of Michael de Villiers.

### History

Philosophers of mathematics wrote little about teaching before the 1970s.
"Proofs and Refutations" by Lakatos, and later work of Reuben Hersh, described mathematics as a human practice that grows by argument.
Mathematics educators used this view to argue for classrooms with conjectures and refutations.
Ernest wrote on these questions in the late 1980s, and his book "The Philosophy of Mathematics Education" appeared in 1991.

### Common errors

1. A researcher assumes that a teacher's stated view and the teacher's practice agree. Studies often find differences between what teachers say and what they do in class.
2. A reader takes one of the three views of Ernest as the correct one. The model describes views, and it does not say which view is right.
3. A teacher assumes that students share the teacher's view of proof. A student may accept an argument because the teacher gave it, which is a different view of proof.

## Explanations

### L1 human

People have different ideas about what mathematics is.
Some people think that mathematics is a list of rules to follow, such as "to divide by a fraction, turn it upside down and multiply".
Other people think that mathematics is a set of truths that exist and wait to be found.
Others think that mathematics is something people make by asking questions and checking ideas.
A teacher with the first idea asks students to learn the rule.
A teacher with the third idea asks students to explain why the rule works, for example why $6 \div \frac{1}{2} = 12$: there are $12$ halves in $6$.
These ideas change what happens in a classroom.

### L2 human

We read one classroom task through the three views of Ernest.
The task is: "Prove that the sum of the angles of a triangle is $180^\circ$."

1. The instrumentalist view: the task is a rule to learn. The student memorizes the standard proof with the line parallel to one side.
2. The Platonist view: the task shows a fact of the structure of Euclidean geometry. The teacher explains how the result follows from the [[parallel postulate|2PC3XY0PYZ]].
3. The problem-solving view: the task is a question to explore. The students measure triangles, conjecture the sum, look for a reason, and ask what changes on a sphere.
4. The three views lead to three lessons with the same [[theorem|05MDKQBEAF]]. In the third lesson, students meet the [[functions of proof|ENY0GKRJS8]] of discovery and explanation, not only verification.

The typical use of these views is to describe a teacher or a curriculum, and to explain why a reform of the curriculum succeeds or fails in practice.
A trap is to read the views as levels of quality.
A good lesson can use the Platonist view, and a bad lesson can use the problem-solving view.

### L3 human

Research on views of mathematics joins the philosophy of mathematics, the psychology of beliefs and the study of teaching practice.
Views of proof are a special case: the [[proof schemes|~968d6007]] of Guershon Harel and Larry Sowder describe what convinces a student, and the views of teachers decide which proof schemes a classroom rewards.
The social view of proof in the philosophy of mathematics, in which a proof is an argument that convinces a qualified community, matches the classroom research on [[sociomathematical norms|~718ed082]].
The page on [[rigour and the acceptance of proofs|1BTHSHST4M]] treats the same question for research mathematics.
