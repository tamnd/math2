## Statement

Let $\mathcal{M}$ be an $L$-[[structure|983GZBQYT4]] with domain $M$.
Let $L_M$ be $L$ with a new constant symbol $c_m$ for each $m \in M$, and let $\mathcal{M}_M$ be the [[expansion|EY20BF96SJ]] of $\mathcal{M}$ that interprets $c_m$ as $m$.

- The atomic diagram $\mathrm{Diag}(\mathcal{M})$ is the set of [[atomic sentences|MCZRXR3SVB]] and negated atomic sentences of $L_M$ that are true in $\mathcal{M}_M$.
- The elementary diagram $\mathrm{ElDiag}(\mathcal{M})$ is the set of all $L_M$-[[sentences|DKFGQTRYZE]] true in $\mathcal{M}_M$.

Let $\mathcal{N}$ be an $L$-structure, and let $h \colon M \to N$ be a map.
Let $(\mathcal{N}, h)$ be the expansion of $\mathcal{N}$ to $L_M$ that interprets $c_m$ as $h(m)$.

1. $(\mathcal{N}, h) \models \mathrm{Diag}(\mathcal{M})$ if and only if $h$ is an [[embedding|SCRB74SWQN]] of $\mathcal{M}$ into $\mathcal{N}$.
2. $(\mathcal{N}, h) \models \mathrm{ElDiag}(\mathcal{M})$ if and only if $h$ is an [[elementary embedding|CGH8C8EA9N]] of $\mathcal{M}$ into $\mathcal{N}$.

Every $L_M$-structure has the form $(\mathcal{N}, h)$, with $h(m)$ the value of $c_m$.
So the models of $\mathrm{Diag}(\mathcal{M})$ are, up to renaming, the extensions of $\mathcal{M}$, and the models of $\mathrm{ElDiag}(\mathcal{M})$ are its elementary extensions.
A proof is in [[the proof of the diagram lemmas|~4dff371b]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The atomic diagram must contain the negated atomic sentences.
With the atomic sentences alone, the map from $\mathcal{M}$ to a one-element structure in which every relation holds satisfies them, and it is not injective.
So the positive diagram describes homomorphisms, not embeddings.
The elementary diagram cannot be replaced by the atomic diagram in part 2.
The inclusion $(2\Z, <) \subseteq (\Z, <)$ satisfies $\mathrm{Diag}(2\Z, <)$ and is not elementary; see [[this example|~65feae4d]].

### Consequences

- Robinson's method of diagrams: to embed $\mathcal{M}$ (elementarily) into a model of a theory $T$, it is enough to show that $T \cup \mathrm{Diag}(\mathcal{M})$ (or $T \cup \mathrm{ElDiag}(\mathcal{M})$) is consistent, often by the [[compactness theorem|1NR3HAWG9D]].
- The upward [[Löwenheim-Skolem theorem|484M0TZ1S7]]: $\mathrm{ElDiag}(\mathcal{M})$ together with many new distinct constants is finitely satisfiable when $\mathcal{M}$ is infinite.
- The [[elementary amalgamation theorem|~c9e11fa5]] and the [[Łoś-Tarski theorem|9RN0KYXNT1]] are proved by this method.
- For a finite structure in a finite signature, finitely many sentences of the diagram, with existential quantifiers in place of the constants and a bound on the size, give one sentence that describes $\mathcal{M}$ up to isomorphism; see [[this proof|TADK8J4X7Z]].

### Converse

Both parts are equivalences.
The direction "embedding gives a model of the diagram" is the easy one.
The other direction lets a syntactic fact (a model of a set of sentences) produce a map.

### Generalizations

The positive diagram, the set of true atomic $L_M$-sentences, characterizes homomorphisms in the same way.
For a subset $A \subseteq M$, the diagrams with constants only for $A$ characterize maps defined on the substructure generated by $A$, and the elementary diagram over $A$ characterizes [[partial elementary maps|~7e54c18a]].

## History

The name "diagram" is due to Abraham Robinson, who was the first model theorist to use diagrams in a systematic way.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The diagram of a structure is a complete list of the simple facts about named elements.
For the numbers $1, 2, 3$ with the order $<$, the list says "$1 < 2$", "$2 < 3$", "$1 < 3$", "not $2 < 1$", "$1 \ne 2$", and so on.
Any other ordered set in which three elements make all these facts true contains a copy of $1, 2, 3$.
So writing the list is the same as asking for a copy.

### L2 human

We use part 1 and compactness to prove: if every finitely generated subgroup of a group $G$ is orderable, then $G$ is orderable.

1. Let $L = \set{\cdot, {}^{-1}, e}$, and let $T$ be the theory of ordered groups in $L \cup \set{<}$: the group axioms, "$<$ is a linear order", and $x < y \to (z x < z y \land x z < y z)$.
2. We show that $T \cup \mathrm{Diag}(G)$ has a model. By compactness, it is enough to satisfy $T \cup \Delta$ for each finite $\Delta \subseteq \mathrm{Diag}(G)$.
3. The sentences of $\Delta$ mention finitely many constants $c_{g_1}, \dots, c_{g_n}$. Let $G_0$ be the subgroup generated by $g_1, \dots, g_n$, with an order from the hypothesis.
4. Interpret $c_g$ in $G_0$ as $g$ when $g \in G_0$ and as $e$ otherwise. Each sentence of $\Delta$ only computes in the subgroup $G_0$, so it is true there, and $G_0 \models T \cup \Delta$.
5. Let $(H, <, h)$ be a model of $T \cup \mathrm{Diag}(G)$. By part 1, $h \colon G \to H$ is an embedding of groups.
6. Put $g < g'$ in $G$ when $h(g) < h(g')$ in $H$. It is linear because $h$ is injective, and it is invariant because $h$ is a homomorphism.

The typical use is steps 2 to 5: an embedding problem becomes a consistency problem, and compactness reduces it to finite parts.
A trap is to drop the negated atomic sentences from the diagram.
Then $h$ is only a homomorphism, possibly onto the trivial group, and step 6 fails because $h$ need not be injective.

### L3 human

The diagram lemmas say that $L_M$-structures are the same as $L$-structures with a map from $M$.
In the language of categories, models of $\mathrm{Diag}(\mathcal{M})$ form the coslice category of embeddings under $\mathcal{M}$, and models of $\mathrm{ElDiag}(\mathcal{M})$ the category of elementary extensions.
This turns questions about maps into questions about theories, where compactness applies.
In algebra the positive diagram of a group is a presentation of it, with all elements as generators and the multiplication table as relations.
Robinson used diagrams to define model completeness and model companions, studied in the sub-area on [[quantifier elimination and model completeness|1A9JSC5YG8]].
Types over $M$, studied in the sub-area on [[types and saturated models|Z772KEC9BY]], describe one new element in an elementary extension, as the elementary diagram describes all of $\mathcal{M}$.
