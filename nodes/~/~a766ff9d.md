## Example

Let $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
It is real and symmetric, but it is not the [[Gram matrix|0HX8HZ1WTQ]] of any two vectors.
We give three tests, and each one shows this.

1. Suppose that $M = G(v_1, v_2)$ for vectors $v_1, v_2$ in a real inner product space. Then $\norm{v_1}^2 = \norm{v_2}^2 = 1$ and $\ip{v_1}{v_2} = 2$.
2. Test 1. The [[Cauchy-Schwarz inequality|0RFWW56Y22]] gives $|\ip{v_1}{v_2}| \leq \norm{v_1}\norm{v_2} = 1$. But $2 > 1$.
3. Test 2. Expand $\norm{v_1 - v_2}^2 = \norm{v_1}^2 - 2\ip{v_1}{v_2} + \norm{v_2}^2 = 1 - 4 + 1 = -2$. A squared norm is never negative.
4. Test 3. A Gram matrix of two vectors has $\det G = \norm{v_1}^2\norm{v_2}^2 - \ip{v_1}{v_2}^2 \geq 0$. But $\det M = 1 - 4 = -3$.
5. Each test gives a contradiction, so $M$ is not a Gram matrix.

## Remarks

The vector $c = (1, -1)$ gives $c^T M c = 1 - 2 - 2 + 1 = -2 < 0$, so $M$ is not positive semidefinite.
Its eigenvalues are $3$ and $-1$, with the eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \frac12 \\ \frac12 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is a Gram matrix: of $(1, 0)$ and $(\frac12, \frac{\sqrt3}{2})$, for example.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix says that two vectors of length $1$ have dot product $2$.
That is impossible, because two unit vectors have a dot product between $-1$ and $1$.
So no pair of vectors has this table of dot products.

### L2 human

Symmetry is necessary for a real Gram matrix, but it is not sufficient.
The matrix must also be positive semidefinite, and the quickest test here is the determinant of a $2 \times 2$ matrix.
A trap: a positive determinant is not enough either.
The matrix $-I_2$ has determinant $1$, but its diagonal entries are negative.
