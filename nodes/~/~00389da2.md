## Statement

Let $N$ be a set with an element $0$, a map $S \colon N \to N$ (the [[successor|0832XVR512]] map) and a relation $<$ on $N$.
Write $x \le y$ for "$x < y$ or $x = y$".
The base theory consists of the following four conditions.

- (B1) The relation $<$ is a strict linear order. No $x$ satisfies $x < x$, and $x < y < z$ gives $x < z$. For all $x, y$, exactly one of $x < y$, $x = y$, $y < x$ holds.
- (B2) No element $x$ satisfies $x < 0$.
- (B3) For each $n$, we have $n < S(n)$, and no $m$ satisfies $n < m < S(n)$.
- (B4) Each $n \neq 0$ equals $S(k)$ for some $k \in N$.

The three principles, each stated for all subsets $A \subseteq N$, are these.

- (I) Induction: if $0 \in A$, and $n \in A$ implies $S(n) \in A$ for each $n$, then $A = N$.
- (SI) Strong induction: if, for each $n$, the inclusion $\{k \in N : k < n\} \subseteq A$ implies $n \in A$, then $A = N$.
- (WO) Well-ordering: if $A \neq \emptyset$, then $A$ has a least element, that is, an $a \in A$ with $a \le x$ for every $x \in A$.

Theorem: if $N$ satisfies (B1) to (B4), then (I), (SI) and (WO) are equivalent.

## Remarks

### The base theory

The conditions (B1) to (B4) hold for the [[natural numbers|0MWKJ44V5A]] with $S(n) = n + 1$ and the usual order.
In the [[Peano axioms|0QFT7Z76N5]], the order is defined by $m < n$ when $m + c = n$ for some $c \neq 0$.
Then (B1) to (B4) are theorems, proved by induction: (B4) and (B2) are Lemmas A and B of the [[proof of strong induction|C54QYDXFP3]], (B3) is its Lemma C, and the totality in (B1) is Lemma T of the [[proof of the well-ordering principle|QPX5Z5YFH8]].
So, given the other Peano axioms, the induction axiom can be replaced by (SI) or by (WO) together with (B1) to (B4).
The statement speaks about all subsets of $N$, as in the second-order Peano axioms.

### The proof

The [[proof by a cycle of implications|~c55054a3]] shows (I) implies (SI), (SI) implies (WO), and (WO) implies (I).
Each implication uses only some of the conditions (B1) to (B4), and the proof marks where each one is used.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. Condition (B4) is needed for (WO) to imply (I). Let $N = \{0, 1\} \times \mathbb{N}$ with the lexicographic order, $0 = (0, 0)$ and $S(i, n) = (i, n + 1)$. Then (B1) to (B3), (WO) and (SI) hold, but (I) fails, as the L2 explanation shows.
2. Condition (B1) is used in the step from (SI) to (WO): a least element must be compared with every other element, and without linearity an element with nothing below it need not be least.
3. Conditions (B2) and (B3) are used in the step from (I) to (SI). They say that the elements below $S(n)$ are exactly the elements below $n$, and $n$ itself.

### Consequences

1. Any one of the three principles can serve as the axiom, and the others become theorems. Books differ on which one they take first.
2. Proofs by [[minimal counterexample|~f8c099da]] and by [[infinite descent|0EFYCFHYFG]] are valid exactly when induction is.
3. In the example of item 1 of the previous list, (SI) holds and (I) fails. So (SI) also implies (I) only with (B4).

### Converse

The theorem is already an equivalence.
The direction from (SI) to (I) also has a short direct proof, which uses only (B3) and (B4).
Let $A$ satisfy the hypotheses of (I), and let $\{k : k < n\} \subseteq A$.
If $n = 0$, then $n \in A$.
Otherwise $n = S(k)$ by (B4), and $k < n$ by (B3), so $k \in A$ and $n = S(k) \in A$.
So $A$ satisfies the hypotheses of (SI), and (SI) gives $A = N$.

### Generalizations

For any set $X$ with a relation $R$, the analogue of (WO) says that $R$ is well-founded, and the analogue of (SI) is well-founded induction.
These two are equivalent for every relation, with no analogue of (B1) to (B4), when the least element in (WO) is replaced by a minimal element.
That general form, and transfinite induction, belong to set theory.
The first-order versions of the three principles, as schemes for formulas of arithmetic, are compared in mathematical logic.

## Explanations

### L1 human

There are three ways to say that the counting numbers have no hidden parts.
Induction says: if a property holds for $0$ and passes from each number to the next, it holds for all numbers.
Strong induction says: if a property passes to each number from all the numbers below it, it holds for all numbers.
Well-ordering says: each collection of counting numbers that is not empty has a smallest member.
The theorem says that these three rules say the same thing, once we know the basic facts about the order, such as "nothing lies between $3$ and $4$".

### L2 human

We check the counterexample of the remarks in detail, to see why (B4) matters.

1. The set $N$ is $\mathbb{N}$ followed by a second copy of $\mathbb{N}$: first $(0, 0) < (0, 1) < \cdots$, then $(1, 0) < (1, 1) < \cdots$.
2. Every nonempty subset $A$ has a least element: if $A$ meets the first copy, take the least $n$ with $(0, n) \in A$; otherwise take the least $n$ with $(1, n) \in A$. So (WO) holds.
3. The element $(1, 0)$ is not $S$ of anything, because $S(i, n) = (i, n + 1)$ has second entry at least $1$. So (B4) fails.
4. The first copy contains $0$ and is closed under $S$, but it misses $(1, 0)$. So (I) fails.

The typical use of the theorem is to choose the most convenient form in a proof: induction for a step from $n$ to $n + 1$, well-ordering for a smallest counterexample.
A trap: the theorem compares the principles over a base theory.
A claim such as "well-ordering implies induction" without (B4) is false.

### L3 human

In the second-order setting of this page, (I) says that no proper subset of $N$ contains $0$ and is closed under $S$.
So $N$ is generated by $0$ and $S$.
(WO) together with (B1) says that $<$ is a well-order.
Then (B2) to (B4) force the order type $\omega$, the order type of $\mathbb{N}$.
The counterexample has order type $\omega \cdot 2$, the least order type after $\omega$ of a well-order with no largest element.
In first-order arithmetic, the induction schema and the least number schema are compared formula class by formula class, in mathematical logic.
In type theory, induction for an inductive type is the primitive principle, and well-founded induction is derived from it.
