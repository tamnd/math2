## Example

Let $\mathfrak{N} = (\N, S, V)$ with $S = \set{(n, n+1) : n \in \N}$ and $V(p) = \emptyset$ for every variable $p$.
Let $\mathfrak{M}' = (\set{a}, \set{(a, a)}, V')$ with $V'(p) = \emptyset$ for every $p$.
Let $f \colon \N \to \set{a}$ be the constant map.
We show that $f$ is a bounded morphism, so that its graph $Z = \set{(n, a) : n \in \N}$ is a [[bisimulation|7T740SFHHV]].

1. Atoms: every variable is false at $n$ and at $a = f(n)$.
2. Forth: if $n S m$, then $f(n) = a$ and $f(m) = a$, and $a R' a$.
3. Back: if $f(n) R' v'$, then $v' = a$. The world $n + 1$ satisfies $n S (n+1)$ and $f(n+1) = a$.
4. Atom clause for $Z$: this is step 1.
5. Zig clause for $Z$ at $(n, a)$: the only move $n S (n+1)$ is answered by $a R' a$, and $(n+1, a) \in Z$.
6. Zag clause for $Z$ at $(n, a)$: the only move $a R' a$ is answered by $n S (n+1)$, and $(n+1, a) \in Z$.

So every $n \in \N$ is bisimilar to $a$.

## Remarks

The steps 4 to 6 repeat steps 1 to 3 in general: the graph of every bounded morphism is a bisimulation.
The example shows that bisimulation does not see well-foundedness or infinity: $S$ has no cycles, and $\mathfrak{M}'$ is one point with a loop.
The chain $\mathfrak{N}$ from $0$ is the tree unravelling of $\mathfrak{M}'$ from $a$, since the paths from $a$ are $a$, $aa$, $aaa$, and so on.
If $S$ is replaced by the finite chain $0 S 1 S \cdots S k$, the back clause fails at $k$, and $\square \bot$ is true at $k$ and false at $a$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Number the steps of an endless staircase $0, 1, 2, \ldots$.
From each step one can go up one step.
A single room with a door into itself offers the same choice: one can always go on.
Sending every step to that one room respects all moves, so the two pictures look the same from inside.

### L2 human

1. Take the formula $\square \lozenge \top$. At each $n$, the only successor $n+1$ has a successor, so $\square \lozenge \top$ is true at $n$.
2. At $a$, the only successor $a$ has a successor, so $\square \lozenge \top$ is true at $a$.
3. By induction on $k$, the formula $\square^k \lozenge \top$ is true at every $n$ and at $a$.

The typical use of bounded morphisms is to transfer validity: a formula valid on $(\N, S)$ is valid on the reflexive point, by the invariance theorem.
A trap: a bounded morphism need not be injective or onto, but by the back clause its image is closed under $R'$.
