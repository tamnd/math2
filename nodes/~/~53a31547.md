## Example

[[Goldbach's conjecture|063JNBSMJ0]] states that every even $n \geq 4$ is a sum of two [[primes|0SN0MAEKYX]].
Let $g(n)$ be the number of ways to write $n = p + q$ with primes $p \leq q$.
The conjecture says $g(n) \geq 1$.
We look at the size of $g(n)$, which gives stronger [[numerical evidence|~ab16d8a0]] than the bare statement.

1. The counts. A sieve of the primes up to $10^6$ gives:

| $n$ | $10$ | $10^2$ | $10^3$ | $10^4$ | $10^5$ | $10^6$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $g(n)$ | $2$ | $6$ | $28$ | $127$ | $810$ | $5402$ |

2. The trend. The counts grow roughly like $n/(\ln n)^2$. A failure at large $n$ would need $g(n)$ to drop from thousands of partitions to none.
3. The prediction. The Hardy and Littlewood heuristic predicts $g(n) \approx C_2 \prod_{p \mid n,\ p > 2} \dfrac{p-1}{p-2} \displaystyle\int_2^{n-2} \frac{dt}{\ln t \, \ln(n-t)}$, with $C_2 = 0.6601618\ldots$.
4. The test. For $n = 10^5$ the prediction is $810.3$, and $g(n) = 810$. For $n = 10^6$ the prediction is $5416.7$, and $g(n) = 5402$.
5. A finer test. The neighbour $n = 999998 = 2 \cdot 31 \cdot 127^2$ has a smaller product over its odd prime factors. The prediction is $4236.2$, and $g(999998) = 4206$.
6. The weight. Steps 4 and 5 test a quantitative prediction, including its dependence on the prime factors of $n$. That is evidence of a different kind from "the next case works".

## Remarks

Goldbach's conjecture has been verified for all even $n$ up to $4 \cdot 10^{18}$.
The plot of $g(n)$ against $n$ is [[Goldbach's comet|110ATC90X5]]; its separate bands come from the factor $\prod (p-1)/(p-2)$ of step 3.
The prediction of step 3 is itself a [[heuristic argument|~722bccf4]] and not a theorem.
So the agreement in steps 4 and 5 supports both the heuristic and the conjecture, and proves neither.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every even number we try can be written as a sum of two primes.
More than that: large even numbers can be written so in thousands of ways.
For example, $1{,}000{,}000$ has $5402$ such ways.
It would be very strange if, far out, the number of ways suddenly dropped to zero.
That is why mathematicians believe the conjecture, even without a proof.

### L2 human

We count $g(100)$ by hand.

1. For each prime $p \leq 50$ we test whether $100 - p$ is prime.
2. $p = 3$: $97$ is prime. $p = 11$: $89$ is prime. $p = 17$: $83$ is prime.
3. $p = 29$: $71$ is prime. $p = 41$: $59$ is prime. $p = 47$: $53$ is prime.
4. The other primes up to $50$ give composite partners, for example $100 - 7 = 93 = 3 \cdot 31$.
5. So $g(100) = 6$.

The typical use: counting the partitions, not only finding one, tests the conjecture against a precise prediction.
A trap: the agreement in steps 4 and 5 is with a heuristic formula, so it does not rule out rare exceptions.
