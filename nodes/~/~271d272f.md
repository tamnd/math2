## Statement

Let $V$ be an [[inner product space|01EK923SKP]] over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and let $v_1, \dots, v_k \in V$.
Let $G$ be their [[Gram matrix|0HX8HZ1WTQ]], with $G_{ij} = \ip{v_i}{v_j}$.

1. $G$ is [[Hermitian|0P17NGQQMQ]]: $G_{ji} = \overline{G_{ij}}$ for all $i, j$.
2. For every column $c \in \mathbb{K}^k$, $c^* G c = \norm{\sum_{i=1}^k \overline{c_i}\, v_i}^2 \geq 0$. So $G$ is [[positive semidefinite|01MMVW8CKC]].
3. For every $c \in \mathbb{K}^k$, $Gc = 0$ if and only if $\sum_{j=1}^k \overline{c_j}\, v_j = 0$.
4. $G$ is invertible if and only if $v_1, \dots, v_k$ are [[linearly independent|0EVHJFTG9Z]].
5. The rank of $G$ is the dimension of the span of $v_1, \dots, v_k$.

The [[proof|~bc2d1c65]] computes $Gc$ as a column of inner products.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Positive definiteness of the inner product is needed for part 4. For the semidefinite form $\beta(x, y) = x_1 y_1$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, the matrix of $\beta$ on the independent pair $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is singular.
- Positivity is needed for part 2. For the [[Minkowski form|TFGECQ5C32]] $\beta(x, y) = x_1 y_1 - x_2 y_2$ and the single vector $(0, 1)$, the matrix is $(-1)$.
- The conjugates in parts 2 and 3 are needed over $\mathbb{C}$. For $v_1 = (1, 0)$ and $v_2 = (i, 0)$ in $\mathbb{C}^2$, $G = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $c = (1, -i)$ give $Gc = 0$. Then $\overline{c_1} v_1 + \overline{c_2} v_2 = (1, 0) + i(i, 0) = 0$, but $c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 = (2, 0)$.

### Consequences

- $\det G \neq 0$ exactly for independent families. The [[squared volume theorem|~6efb00d4]] adds that $\det G \geq 0$.
- For an $n \times k$ real matrix $A$, $A^T A$ is invertible if and only if the columns of $A$ are independent. Then the normal equations $A^T A x = A^T b$ of least squares have a unique solution.
- Vectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ form a basis if and only if $\det(A^T A) \neq 0$, which is $(\det A)^2 \neq 0$.

### Converse

Part 4 is an equivalence.
A partial converse of part 2 also holds: each positive semidefinite matrix is a Gram matrix.
The vectors are the columns of a square root of its transpose.

### Generalizations

- In every inner product space, also of infinite dimension, the same proof works, since only $k$ vectors are involved.
- For a positive semidefinite Hermitian form instead of an inner product, parts 1 to 3 hold with "norm" replaced by the form. Part 4 then needs definiteness on the span.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Make the table of all inner products of a list of vectors.
The table can be inverted exactly when no vector in the list is a combination of the others.
So we can test independence with numbers that use only lengths and angles.

### L2 human

Worked example: test $v_1 = (1, 2)$ and $v_2 = (2, 4)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

1. The entries are $v_1 \cdot v_1 = 5$, $v_1 \cdot v_2 = 10$ and $v_2 \cdot v_2 = 20$.
2. So $G = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 10 \\ 10 & 20 \end{pmatrix}$, and $\det G = 100 - 100 = 0$.
3. The column $c = (2, -1)$ gives $Gc = (0, 0)$.
4. Part 3 then predicts $2v_1 - v_2 = 0$, and indeed $2(1, 2) - (2, 4) = 0$.

The typical use is to read off a linear relation from a vector in the null space of $G$.
A trap: over $\mathbb{C}$, the relation has the conjugate coefficients $\overline{c_j}$, not $c_j$.

### L3 human

The Gram matrix is the matrix of the inner product, restricted to the span, in the spanning family.
The map $c \mapsto \sum_j \overline{c_j} v_j$ is antilinear, and part 3 says that its kernel is the null space of $G$.
So the null space of $G$ is the space of linear relations among the $v_j$, up to conjugation.
