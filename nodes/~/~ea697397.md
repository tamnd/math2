## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the matrix of [[the $3 \times 2$ SVD example|~461482fa]].

1. $A^T A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $3$, so it is invertible, and $(A^T A)^{-1} = \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. So $A^+ = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T = \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. The SVD gives the same matrix. With $\sigma_1 = \sqrt3$, $v_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$, $u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}(2, 1, 1)$, $\sigma_2 = 1$, $v_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)$ and $u_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(0, 1, -1)$, we get $\frac{1}{\sqrt3} v_1 u_1^T = \frac16 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $v_2 u_2^T = \frac12 \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Their sum is the matrix of step 2.
4. $A^+ A = \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 2 & 1 - 1 \\ 1 - 1 & 1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$. It is symmetric, and then $A A^+ A = A$ and $A^+ A A^+ = A^+$ follow at once.
5. $A A^+ = \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is symmetric. So all four Penrose conditions hold.
6. $A A^+ = I_3 - u_3 u_3^T$ with $u_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}(1, -1, -1)$. So it is the projection onto the plane $x_1 - x_2 - x_3 = 0$, the column space of $A$.
7. For $b = (1, 2, 3)$, $A^+ b = \frac13 (1 + 4 - 3,\ 1 - 2 + 6) = \left(\frac23, \frac53\right)$. Check with the [[normal equations|4D7MRG4D91]]: $A^T A (\frac23, \frac53) = (3, 4) = A^T b$.

## Remarks

- With independent columns, $A^+ A = I_n$, so $A^+$ is a left inverse of $A$. It is not a right inverse: $A A^+ \ne I_3$, since a $3 \times 3$ matrix of rank $2$ is not invertible.
- Other left inverses exist, for example $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Among all left inverses, $A^+$ is the one whose product $A X$ is symmetric.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When the columns of $A$ are independent, there is a formula for the pseudoinverse with an ordinary inverse.
We check it two ways, by this formula and by the SVD, and we get the same matrix.

### L2 human

The formula $(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ is the matrix of the normal equations, so it gives the unique least-squares solution.
Step 3 shows that it agrees with $V \Sigma^+ U^T$.
A trap: the other left inverse in the remarks also gives $X A = I_2$, but $A X$ is not symmetric, so it fails condition 3.
