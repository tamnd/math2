## Summary

The matrix unit $E_{ij}$ is the matrix with entry $1$ in row $i$ and column $j$ and $0$ in every other place; the [[formal definition|~2c685e2c]] states it.
They multiply by the [[rule|~eda9ba19]] $E_{ij} E_{kl} = \delta_{jk} E_{il}$.
The $mn$ matrix units of size $m \times n$ form the [[standard basis|0V7ZD6P7RK]] of the space of $m \times n$ matrices.

## Motivation

A matrix unit is the simplest nonzero matrix.
Every matrix is a sum of multiples of matrix units, and the products of matrix units are known from one rule.
So many identities about matrices reduce to a check on matrix units.
This is the standard tool for the [[centre of the matrix ring|~c64c046f]] and for its [[two-sided ideals|~bf3478d5]].

## Notation

- Other notations are $e_{ij}$ and $E^{(ij)}$. Some books write $e_i e_j^{\mathsf{T}}$, the product of a column and a row of the identity matrix.
- In Mathlib, `Matrix.stdBasisMatrix i j a` is the matrix with entry `a` at `(i, j)` and `0` elsewhere. Recent versions call it `Matrix.single i j a`.
- Do not confuse $E_{ij}$ with the [[identity matrix|~495c2486]], which some books write $E$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The products of the 2 × 2 matrix units|~d358b7b5]].
- [[A 2 × 2 matrix written in the basis of matrix units|~0e0d5476]].

### Non-examples

- [[The identity matrix is a sum of matrix units, not a matrix unit|~b7b47fc6]].
- The zero matrix and $2E_{11}$ are not matrix units.

### Basic properties

Let $R$ be a ring with identity and let $E_{ij}$ be the $n \times n$ matrix units over $R$.

- Each matrix is $A = \sum_{i, j} a_{ij} E_{ij}$. Both sides have entry $a_{pq}$ at $(p, q)$, because only the term $(i, j) = (p, q)$ contributes.
- The sum is unique, so over a field the $E_{ij}$ form a basis of $M_{m \times n}(F)$, and $\dim M_{m \times n}(F) = mn$.
- $E_{ij} E_{kl} = \delta_{jk} E_{il}$, by [[this rule|~eda9ba19]]. In particular $E_{ii}$ is [[idempotent|0KCE8CG86P]] and $E_{ij}$ is [[nilpotent|07BSPZPSMF]] for $i \neq j$.
- $E_{11} + E_{22} + \dots + E_{nn} = I_n$, and $E_{ii} E_{jj} = 0$ for $i \neq j$.
- $E_{ij} A$ is the matrix with row $j$ of $A$ in row $i$ and $0$ elsewhere. $A E_{ij}$ is the matrix with column $i$ of $A$ in column $j$ and $0$ elsewhere.
- $E_{ij} A E_{kl} = a_{jk} E_{il}$. This picks out one entry of $A$ and moves it to the place $(i, l)$.
- $\operatorname{tr} E_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$ and $E_{ij}^{\mathsf{T}} = E_{ji}$.

### Use in proofs

- The [[centre|~c64c046f]] of $M_n(R)$: a matrix that commutes with every $E_{ij}$ is [[scalar|~30ae7883]].
- The [[ideals|~bf3478d5]] of $M_n(F)$: the identity $E_{ij} A E_{kl} = a_{jk} E_{il}$ moves one nonzero entry of $A$ to each place.
- The [[cyclic property of the trace|0Y4NF1DRKD]] and [[associativity|073E4XC016]] can be checked on matrix units and extended by linearity.

### Common errors

- Writing $E_{ij} E_{kl} = E_{il}$ without the condition $j = k$.
- Reading $E_{ij}$ as the [[elementary matrix|01REXW3WWE]] $I + E_{ij}$. The two are different.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A matrix unit is a matrix of zeros with a single $1$.
The two indices say where the $1$ is.
Every matrix is built from these by scaling and adding.

### L2 human

As a linear map, $E_{ij}$ sends the basis vector $e_j$ to $e_i$ and every other basis vector to $0$.
So $E_{ij} E_{kl}$ sends $e_l$ to $e_k$ and then $e_k$ to $e_i$ when $k = j$, and to $0$ otherwise.
This is the multiplication rule.

### L3 human

In a ring $S$, a family $e_{ij}$ with $e_{ij} e_{kl} = \delta_{jk} e_{il}$ and $\sum_i e_{ii} = 1$ is a *system of matrix units*.
Such a system forces $S \cong M_n(e_{11} S e_{11})$.
This is a key step in the theory of simple rings, which belongs to [[ring theory|0B1261B5ED]].
