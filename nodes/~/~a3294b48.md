## Example

*Statement.* For integers $a$, $b$ and $c$, if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$.
Here $a \mid b$ means that $b = ak$ for some integer $k$, by the definition of [[divisibility|0PG2H7WKGG]].

A student hands in this text as the proof:

$$a \mid b, \quad b \mid c$$
$$b = ak$$
$$c = bl$$
$$c = akl$$
$$a \mid c$$

Each formula is true under the right reading, but the text is not written in the [[language of proofs|~67d2371f]].
We check what the reader cannot know from the text.

1. The status of line 1 is not stated. It can be the hypothesis, or a claim that the student wants to prove.
2. The letter $k$ in line 2 is not introduced. The reader cannot tell whether $k$ is an integer, and whether it comes from the definition or is a new choice.
3. The same holds for $l$ in line 3.
4. The step from lines 2 and 3 to line 4 has no reason. The reader must find the substitution $c = bl = (ak)l$ alone.
5. Line 5 needs the fact that $kl$ is an integer, and the text does not say it.
6. The text does not say which line is the conclusion. A reader could read the column from the bottom up, as a list of things to check.

The layout also cannot separate a correct argument from a wrong one.
Here is a column of the same shape for the false claim "if $a \mid bc$, then $a \mid b$":

$$a \mid bc$$
$$bc = ak$$
$$b = a \cdot \tfrac{k}{c}$$
$$a \mid b$$

The last step needs $\frac{k}{c}$ to be an integer, and the column hides this gap.
The claim fails for $a = 6$, $b = 2$, $c = 3$: here $6 \mid 6 = bc$, but $6 \nmid 2$.

## Remarks

1. A correct version of the first column adds four phrases. They are "suppose that $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$", "so there are integers $k$ and $l$ with", "then", and "since $kl$ is an integer". The page [[the language of proofs|~67d2371f]] writes it out in its L2 explanation.
2. The example shows that correct formulas do not make a proof. A proof also states the role of each formula and the reason for each step.
3. A column of formulas is acceptable inside a proof for a chain of equalities, such as $c = bl = (ak)l = a(kl)$. There each sign "=" gives the link between two lines. It is not acceptable for steps that change the logical status, such as an assumption or a choice.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Imagine a recipe that lists only the ingredients and the result, with no instructions.
A cook cannot tell what to do first, or what to do with each item.
The column of formulas is like that recipe.
Each line is a true fact.
But the column does not say which facts are given, which numbers are new, and which fact is the answer.
A second column of the same shape "proves" a false claim, so the shape alone cannot be trusted.

### L2 human

We repair the second column and find where it breaks.

1. "Suppose that $a \mid bc$." This opens the hypothesis.
2. "Then there is an integer $k$ with $bc = ak$." This uses the definition of divisibility.
3. "Hence $b = a \cdot \frac{k}{c}$ when $c \ne 0$." This step is correct as an equation of rational numbers.
4. "Since $\frac{k}{c}$ is an integer, $a \mid b$." The words "since" force the writer to state a reason, and no reason exists.
5. For $a = 6$, $b = 2$, $c = 3$ we get $k = 1$ and $\frac{k}{c} = \frac{1}{3}$, which is not an integer.

The typical use of proof words is to make such a gap visible.
The word "since" demands a reason, and the missing reason shows the error.
A trap is to think that a short column is clearer than sentences.
It is shorter, but it moves the work of finding the logic from the writer to the reader.
