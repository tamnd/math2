## Example

On $\mathbb{C}^2$ let $\beta(x, y) = x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$.
This is the formula of the real dot product, applied to complex vectors without conjugates.
We check the three axioms of an [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]].

1. Linearity in the first argument holds: $\beta(ax + bz, y) = (ax_1 + bz_1)y_1 + (ax_2 + bz_2)y_2 = a\beta(x, y) + b\beta(z, y)$.
2. Conjugate symmetry fails. Let $x = (i, 0)$ and $y = (1, 0)$. Then $\beta(x, y) = i$ and $\beta(y, x) = i$, but $\overline{i} = -i \neq i$.
3. Positive definiteness fails in two ways. Let $x = (1, i)$. Then $\beta(x, x) = 1 + i^2 = 0$, but $x \neq 0$.
4. Let $x = (i, 0)$. Then $\beta(x, x) = i^2 = -1 < 0$.
5. So $\beta$ is not an inner product, although it is a symmetric [[bilinear form|0SQZMXB6GP]].

## Remarks

The example shows why a complex inner product needs a conjugate.
With conjugates on the second factor, the formula becomes $x_1 \overline{y_1} + x_2 \overline{y_2}$, the [[standard inner product on $\mathbb{C}^2$|~a4d9ba49]].
Then $(1, i)$ gets the value $1 \cdot 1 + i \cdot \overline{i} = 1 + 1 = 2$, which is its correct square length.
The same failure happens on $\mathbb{C}^n$ for every $n \geq 1$: already on $\mathbb{C}$, the number $i$ gives $i \cdot i = -1$.
A symmetric bilinear form over $\mathbb{C}$ is never positive definite on a nonzero space, because $\beta(ix, ix) = -\beta(x, x)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For real vectors, a vector times itself gives a sum of squares, which is never negative.
For complex numbers, a square can be negative: $i \cdot i = -1$.
So the plain formula $x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$ can give a negative "squared length", or $0$ for a nonzero vector such as $(1, i)$.
The cure is to conjugate the second vector, because $i$ times the conjugate of $i$ is $1$.

### L2 human

To show that a formula is not an inner product, it is enough to find one vector where it fails.

1. Try a basis vector times $i$, such as $x = (i, 0)$.
2. Compute $\beta(x, x) = i \cdot i = -1$.
3. A negative value violates positive definiteness, so we are done.

The typical use of this test is to catch a missing conjugate in a formula over $\mathbb{C}$.
A trap: the form is fine on real vectors, so tests with real entries only will not find the error.
