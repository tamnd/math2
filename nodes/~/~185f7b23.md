## Example

Let $x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{pmatrix}$ and $y = \begin{pmatrix} 3 + 4i \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{C}^2$.

1. By the [[definition|0EX045E2BP]], $x^* = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \end{pmatrix}$, a row vector.
2. $x^* x = 1 \cdot 1 + (-i) \cdot i = 1 + 1 = 2$, and $|1|^2 + |i|^2 = 2$.
3. $y^* = \begin{pmatrix} 3 - 4i & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $y^* y = (3 - 4i)(3 + 4i) + 2 \cdot 2 = 25 + 4 = 29$, which is $|3 + 4i|^2 + |2|^2$.
4. In general $x^* x = \sum_k \overline{x_k} x_k = \sum_k |x_k|^2$, a real number $\ge 0$, equal to $0$ only for $x = 0$.
5. $x^* y = 1 \cdot (3 + 4i) + (-i) \cdot 2 = 3 + 2i$, and $y^* x = (3 - 4i) + 2i = 3 - 2i = \overline{x^* y}$.

## Remarks

The number $y^* x$ is the standard inner product $\langle x, y \rangle$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$, linear in $x$.
Some books put the conjugate on the other factor, with $\langle x, y \rangle = x^* y$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Multiplying the conjugate transpose of a vector by the vector gives the square of its length.
Every term is a squared absolute value, so the result is never negative.

### L2 human

Step 5 shows that the complex inner product is conjugate-symmetric, not symmetric.
This is the reason for the conjugates in the conjugate transpose.
