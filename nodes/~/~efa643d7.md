## Example

Let $\mathcal{B}$ be the class of finite Boolean algebras, in the signature $\set{\wedge, \vee, \neg, 0, 1}$ with $0 \ne 1$.
We show that $\mathcal{B}$ is a Fraïssé class whose [[limit|HJVRBDQSGG]] is the countable [[atomless Boolean algebra|NFEEGWJFCK]].

1. Each finite Boolean algebra is isomorphic to a power set $\mathcal{P}(X)$ with $X$ finite, its set of atoms, so there are countably many types.
2. HP: a subalgebra of a finite Boolean algebra is a finite Boolean algebra. A Boolean algebra generated by $n$ elements has at most $2^{2^n}$ elements, so finitely generated means finite.
3. An embedding $\mathcal{P}(X) \to \mathcal{P}(Y)$ is $S \mapsto \beta^{-1}(S)$ for a unique surjection $\beta \colon Y \to X$.
4. [[AP|XSCSX39GEZ]]: given surjections $\beta \colon Y \to X$ and $\gamma \colon Z \to X$, let $W = \set{(y, z) : \beta(y) = \gamma(z)}$. The two projections from $W$ are surjective, and they give embeddings into $\mathcal{P}(W)$ that agree on $\mathcal{P}(X)$.
5. [[JEP|~3b0d5f2d]]: the two-element algebra embeds into every member, so JEP follows from AP.
6. Let $M$ be the limit. If $a$ were an atom of $M$, apply the extension property to the subalgebra $\set{0, a, \neg a, 1}$ and an extension splitting $a$ into two atoms. It gives $0 < b < a$, a contradiction.
7. So $M$ is countable and atomless, and such an algebra is unique up to isomorphism.

## Remarks

Under Stone duality, finite Boolean algebras correspond to finite sets, and embeddings correspond to surjections in the other direction.
So AP for Boolean algebras is a pullback of finite sets.
The Stone space of the limit is the Cantor space, and the automorphism group of the limit is the homeomorphism group of the Cantor space.
The limit is also the algebra of clopen subsets of the Cantor space.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Finite Boolean algebras are the algebras of all subsets of a finite set.
Gluing two of them over a common part works by taking pairs of points that lie over the same point.
The countable structure that contains every finite one and is as symmetric as possible has no smallest nonzero elements: every piece can be split again.

### L2 human

We amalgamate $\mathcal{P}(\set{1, 2})$ over $\mathcal{P}(\set{\ast})$, the two-element algebra.

1. Here $\beta$ and $\gamma$ both send $\set{1, 2}$ to the point $\ast$.
2. Then $W = \set{1, 2} \times \set{1, 2}$ has $4$ points, and $\mathcal{P}(W)$ has $16$ elements.
3. The first copy of $\mathcal{P}(\set{1, 2})$ sits in $\mathcal{P}(W)$ as unions of rows, and the second as unions of columns.

The typical use is to see that the atomless Boolean algebra is ultrahomogeneous.
A trap is to think of embeddings of finite algebras as injections of atoms; they correspond to surjections of atoms the other way.
