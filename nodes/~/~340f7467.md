## Statement

Let $A$ be a [[Hermitian|0EKZCX4K3P]] $n \times n$ complex matrix, and let $B$ be a [[positive definite|0QQCG3JWJ4]] $n \times n$ complex matrix.
Let $^*$ be the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]].

1. There are an invertible matrix $P$ and a real diagonal matrix $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ with
$$P^* B P = I \quad \text{and} \quad P^* A P = D.$$
2. The columns $p_1, \dots, p_n$ of $P$ satisfy $A p_i = \lambda_i B p_i$ and $p_i^* B p_j = \delta_{ij}$.
3. The numbers $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ are the roots of $\det(A - \lambda B) = 0$, repeated by multiplicity. They are the [[eigenvalues|0ZND7NQ970]] of $B^{-1} A$.
4. $A$ is positive semidefinite if and only if all $\lambda_i \ge 0$, and positive definite if and only if all $\lambda_i > 0$.
5. If $A$ and $B$ are real, then $P$ can be chosen real, and then $P^T B P = I$ and $P^T A P = D$.

The equation $A p = \lambda B p$ is the generalized eigenvalue problem for the [[matrix pencil|KQ7V0J5CGG]] $A - \lambda B$, and the $\lambda_i$ are its generalized eigenvalues.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $B$ must be positive definite, not only Hermitian. For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the polynomial $\det(A - \lambda B) = -1 - \lambda^2$ has no real root. By part 3, no $P$ as in part 1 exists.
- $A$ must be Hermitian. If $P^* A P = D$ is real diagonal, then $A = (P^*)^{-1} D P^{-1}$ is Hermitian.
- The result is about congruence $P^* X P$, not similarity $P^{-1} X P$. The matrix $P$ is in general not unitary.
- The result does not extend to three matrices: three Hermitian matrices need not be diagonal in one congruence, even if one of them is positive definite.

### Consequences

- The spectral theorem is the case $B = I$, where $P$ is unitary.
- The generalized eigenvalues of a Hermitian $A$ with respect to a positive definite $B$ are real, although $B^{-1} A$ need not be Hermitian.
- [[The $2 \times 2$ example|~c0549a97]] solves a generalized eigenvalue problem and checks both congruences.
- The quadratic form $x^* A x$ has its extreme values on the ellipsoid $x^* B x = 1$ at the vectors $p_i$, with values $\lambda_i$.

### Converse

If $P$ is invertible with $P^* B P = I$, then $B = (P^{-1})^* P^{-1}$ is positive definite, by [[the Gram matrix theorem|0AM050KCAW]].
So the positivity of $B$ is necessary for $P^* B P = I$.

### Generalizations

Two Hermitian matrices that commute are diagonal in a common unitary basis; this is [[simultaneous diagonalization|0FK17N4DKA]] by similarity.
For two Hermitian matrices with some combination $\alpha A + \beta B$ positive definite, the same result holds with that combination in place of $B$.
In mechanics, the problem $A p = \lambda B p$ appears in vibration theory, with a stiffness matrix $A$ and a mass matrix $B$.

## History

The Wikipedia article on definite matrices proves the result by the Cholesky decomposition of $B$ and describes it as a simultaneous diagonalization of two quadratic forms.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two quadratic forms can be simplified at the same time, if one of them is positive.
We choose new coordinates in which the positive form becomes $y_1^2 + \cdots + y_n^2$.
In these coordinates the other form becomes $\lambda_1 y_1^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n y_n^2$.

### L2 human

To compute $P$, solve $\det(A - \lambda B) = 0$, find a vector $p_i$ with $(A - \lambda_i B) p_i = 0$, and scale it so that $p_i^* B p_i = 1$.
For distinct $\lambda_i$ the vectors $p_i$ are automatically $B$-orthogonal.
A trap: the $p_i$ are orthogonal for the inner product $x^* B y$, not for the standard one, so $P$ is not unitary.

### L3 human

A positive definite $B$ defines the inner product $\ip{x}{y}_B = y^* B x$ on $\C^n$.
The matrix $B^{-1} A$ is self-adjoint for this inner product, and the theorem is the spectral theorem in the space $(\C^n, \ip{\cdot}{\cdot}_B)$.
In the language of forms, the pair of a Hermitian form and a positive definite form has a complete set of congruence invariants, the generalized eigenvalues.
In numerical linear algebra, the Cholesky reduction of the proof is a standard method for the symmetric definite generalized eigenvalue problem.
