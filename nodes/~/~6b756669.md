## Example

Claim: every [[tree|0MJ0JS72DZ]] with $n \ge 1$ vertices has exactly $n - 1$ edges.
Here a tree is a finite [[graph|0JD5C82QMB]] that is [[connected|0FFAYNMAJZ]] and [[acyclic|0XNN9Z8E5A]].
We prove the claim by [[induction on size|~ad849a99]], with the number of vertices as the size.
First we prove a lemma that gives the reduction.

Lemma: every tree $T$ with at least $2$ vertices has a [[leaf|EA8S0JHXMP]], that is, a vertex of [[degree|0KS0KRDV4Y]] $1$.

1. Since $T$ is connected and has two vertices, there is a [[path|0JDE56HWQ8]] between them, so $T$ has at least one edge.
2. The graph $T$ is finite, so it has finitely many paths. Let $P = v_0 v_1 \cdots v_k$ be a path with the largest number of edges. By step 1, $k \ge 1$.
3. The vertex $v_0$ is adjacent to $v_1$. Suppose that $v_0$ has another neighbour $w \neq v_1$.
4. If $w = v_i$ for some $i \ge 2$, then $v_0 v_1 \cdots v_i v_0$ is a cycle. This contradicts the hypothesis that $T$ is acyclic.
5. If $w$ is not on $P$, then $w v_0 v_1 \cdots v_k$ is a path with $k + 1$ edges. This contradicts the choice of $P$ in step 2.
6. So $v_1$ is the only neighbour of $v_0$, and $v_0$ is a leaf.

Now the induction on $n$.
Let $P(n)$ be the statement "every tree with $n$ vertices has $n - 1$ edges".

1. Base case $n = 1$: a graph with one vertex has no edge, because each edge joins two distinct vertices. So it has $0 = 1 - 1$ edges.
2. Induction step: let $n \ge 1$, assume $P(n)$, and let $T$ be an arbitrary tree with $n + 1$ vertices.
3. Since $n + 1 \ge 2$, the lemma gives a leaf $v$ of $T$. Let $e$ be the one edge at $v$.
4. Let $T'$ be the graph obtained from $T$ by deleting $v$ and $e$. It has $n$ vertices and one edge fewer than $T$.
5. $T'$ is acyclic, because each cycle of $T'$ would be a cycle of $T$.
6. $T'$ is connected. Let $x$ and $y$ be vertices of $T'$, and take a path from $x$ to $y$ in $T$. Each inner vertex of a path has two neighbours on the path, and $v$ has only one neighbour, so $v$ is not on this path. So the path lies in $T'$.
7. By steps 5 and 6, $T'$ is a tree with $n$ vertices. By $P(n)$, it has $n - 1$ edges.
8. So $T$ has $(n - 1) + 1 = n = (n + 1) - 1$ edges, and $P(n + 1)$ holds.
9. By induction, $P(n)$ holds for every $n \ge 1$.

## Remarks

1. The example shows the two parts of an induction on size: a lemma that a reduction exists (a leaf), and a check that the reduced object still satisfies the hypotheses (steps 5 and 6).
2. The step starts from an arbitrary tree with $n + 1$ vertices and removes a leaf. It does not start from a tree with $n$ vertices and add a leaf; that order would need a separate proof that every tree arises in this way.
3. Removing an arbitrary vertex does not work. In the path with $3$ vertices, removing the middle vertex leaves two isolated vertices, which is not a tree.
4. The proof of the lemma gives two leaves, $v_0$ and $v_k$, by the same argument at the other end of $P$. So every tree with at least $2$ vertices has at least $2$ leaves.
5. The claim fails for graphs that are not trees. A cycle with $n \ge 3$ vertices has $n$ edges, and two disjoint edges form an acyclic graph with $4$ vertices and $2$ edges.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A tree is a network of points and lines with no loops, in which every point can be reached from every other point.
A tree with $2$ points has $1$ line, and a tree with $3$ points has $2$ lines.
In general there is always one line fewer than points.
To see this, find a point at the end of a branch, a leaf, and cut it off with its line.
What remains is a smaller tree, with one point fewer and one line fewer.
Repeat until one point and no line remain.

### L2 human

We follow the proof on a tree $T$ with vertices $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ and edges $12$, $23$, $24$, $45$.

1. A longest path is $1\,2\,4\,5$, with $3$ edges. Its end vertex $1$ is a leaf.
2. Removing vertex $1$ and edge $12$ leaves vertices $2, 3, 4, 5$ and edges $23$, $24$, $45$. This is again a tree.
3. A longest path in it is $3\,2\,4\,5$, and $3$ is a leaf. Removing it leaves vertices $2, 4, 5$ and edges $24$, $45$.
4. Removing the leaf $2$ leaves vertices $4, 5$ and edge $45$, and removing the leaf $4$ leaves vertex $5$ alone.
5. We removed $4$ edges in $4$ steps and reached $1$ vertex with $0$ edges. So $T$ has $4 = 5 - 1$ edges.

The typical use of the result is the characterisation of trees: a connected graph with $n$ vertices is a tree exactly when it has $n - 1$ edges.
A trap is to apply the induction hypothesis to $T$ minus an arbitrary vertex: the result may be disconnected, and then it is not a tree.
