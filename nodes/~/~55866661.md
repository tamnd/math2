## Example

Starting problem, already solved in [[counting the squares on a chessboard by the four steps|~8867314c]]: how many squares of all sizes are there on an $8 \times 8$ chessboard?
A square of side $k$ can be placed in $(9 - k)^2$ positions, so the count is $8^2 + 7^2 + \dots + 1^2 = 204$.
We now pose new problems from it, as in [[problem posing|~71594150]].

1. Attributes of the problem: the board is $8 \times 8$; the board is square; the shapes counted are squares; the shapes follow the grid lines.
2. "What if not squares?" New problem: how many rectangles of all sizes are there on the chessboard?
3. Solution. A rectangle on the board is fixed by its left and right sides and its top and bottom sides. The board has $9$ vertical grid lines and $9$ horizontal grid lines.
4. So a rectangle is a choice of $2$ of the $9$ vertical lines and $2$ of the $9$ horizontal lines. The number of rectangles is $\binom{9}{2} \cdot \binom{9}{2} = 36 \cdot 36 = 1296$, by the [[binomial coefficient|0GBZM0MT0F]] $\binom{9}{2} = 36$.
5. Check by the method of the original problem: a rectangle of width $a$ and height $b$ can be placed in $(9 - a)(9 - b)$ positions, and $\sum_{a=1}^{8} \sum_{b=1}^{8} (9 - a)(9 - b) = 36 \cdot 36 = 1296$.
6. Look back. The new method, choosing grid lines, is shorter than the old one, and it gives a closed form at once for an $n \times n$ board: $\binom{n + 1}{2}^2$ rectangles.

The variant was easy to pose, it needed a new idea, and its answer led to further questions.

## Remarks

The two counts give a surprise: $1296 = 36^2 = 1^3 + 2^3 + \dots + 8^3$.
In general $\binom{n + 1}{2}^2 = \left(\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}\right)^2$, the [[squared triangular number|23WBA59VWP]], equals $1^3 + 2^3 + \dots + n^3$.
So counting rectangles in two ways poses a further problem: find a counting argument that splits the rectangles into groups of sizes $1^3, 2^3, \dots, n^3$.
Other variants come from the remaining attributes: an $m \times n$ board gives $\binom{m + 1}{2}\binom{n + 1}{2}$ rectangles, and a cube of side $n$ gives $\binom{n + 1}{2}^3$ boxes.
The fraction of rectangles that are squares is $\frac{204}{1296} = \frac{17}{108}$, about $16\%$.
Many related counting questions are collected under [[chessboard problems|WPGRNSZ1ZN]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A chessboard has $8$ rows and $8$ columns of small squares.
If we also count the bigger squares made of several small ones, there are $204$ squares in all.
Now change the question: how many rectangles are there, of any shape?
Each rectangle is fixed by two of the $9$ lines going across and two of the $9$ lines going down.
There are $36$ ways to choose two lines from nine, so there are $36 \times 36 = 1296$ rectangles.
Changing "square" to "rectangle" gave a new problem and a new way of counting.

### L2 human

We check the formula $\binom{n + 1}{2}^2$ on a $3 \times 3$ board.

1. Rectangles by size: $1 \times 1$ in $9$ positions, $1 \times 2$ and $2 \times 1$ in $6$ each, $1 \times 3$ and $3 \times 1$ in $3$ each.
2. Then $2 \times 2$ in $4$ positions, $2 \times 3$ and $3 \times 2$ in $2$ each, and $3 \times 3$ in $1$.
3. Total: $9 + 6 + 6 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 36$.
4. The formula gives $\binom{4}{2}^2 = 6^2 = 36$, and also $1^3 + 2^3 + 3^3 = 36$.
5. The squares among them: $9 + 4 + 1 = 14$.

The typical use: pose the variant, solve it by a new method, and check the new method against the old one on a small case.

A trap: posing "what if not $8 \times 8$" and changing only the number gives a routine variant; the change from squares to rectangles is the one that needs a new idea.
