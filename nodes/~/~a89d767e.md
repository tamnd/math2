## Proof

We use the notation of the [[statement|~5d6edbff]].
Part A proves positional determinacy of parity games on finite graphs in full, by Zielonka's argument.
Part B proves in full that emptiness of a parity tree automaton is decidable, and that a nonempty language contains a regular tree.
Part C gives the translation between S2S and automata in outline; complementation and the passage from Rabin to parity conditions are cited, not proved.

**Part A: Positional determinacy of finite parity games.**

A parity game $G = (V, V_0, V_1, E, \Omega)$ has a finite set $V$ of vertices, split into $V_0$ and $V_1$, edges $E \subseteq V \times V$, and priorities $\Omega \colon V \to \N$.
Every vertex has at least one successor.
A play is an infinite path $v_0 v_1 v_2 \cdots$ along $E$, where the owner of $v_k$ chooses $v_{k+1}$.
Player $0$ wins the play when the largest priority that occurs infinitely often is even; otherwise player $1$ wins.
A strategy of player $j$ maps each finite play ending in $V_j$ to a successor; it is positional when it depends only on the last vertex.
$W_j(G)$ is the set of vertices from which player $j$ has a winning strategy.

1. Prefix independence. Removing or adding a finite prefix does not change the set of priorities that occur infinitely often, so it does not change the winner.
2. The two players cannot both win from one vertex, because the play that follows both winning strategies would be won by both. So $W_0(G) \cap W_1(G) = \emptyset$.
3. Attractors. For $X \subseteq V$ and a player $i$, let $A_0 = X$ and let $A_{k+1}$ be $A_k$ together with the vertices of $V_i$ that have some successor in $A_k$ and the vertices of $V_{1-i}$ whose successors all lie in $A_k$. The union $\operatorname{Attr}_i(X)$ is reached after at most $|V|$ rounds.
4. The attractor strategy of player $i$ moves from each vertex of $V_i \cap (A_{k+1} \setminus A_k)$ to a successor in $A_k$. Along a play that follows it, the least $k$ with $v \in A_k$ drops at each step outside $X$. So the play reaches $X$ within $|V|$ steps from every vertex of $\operatorname{Attr}_i(X)$.
5. Traps. Let $U = V \setminus \operatorname{Attr}_i(X)$. A vertex of $V_i \cap U$ has all successors in $U$, and a vertex of $V_{1-i} \cap U$ has some successor in $U$; otherwise it would have joined the attractor. So player $i$ cannot leave $U$, player $1-i$ can stay in $U$, and $G[U]$ is again a game in which every vertex has a successor.
6. Lemma. Let $\sigma$ be a positional strategy of player $j$ that wins from every vertex of $W_j(G)$, and let $v \in W_j(G)$. If $v \in V_j$ then $\sigma(v) \in W_j(G)$. If $v \in V_{1-j}$ then every successor of $v$ lies in $W_j(G)$. Indeed, for such a successor $u$, every play from $u$ that follows $\sigma$ becomes, with $v$ in front, a play from $v$ that follows $\sigma$; it is won by $j$, and step 1 removes $v$ again.

**Claim.** $V = W_0(G) \cup W_1(G)$, and each player $j$ has one positional strategy that wins from every vertex of $W_j(G)$.

We prove the claim by induction on $|V|$; the empty game is the base case.

7. Let $d$ be the largest priority in $G$, let $i = d \bmod 2$, let $N = \set{v : \Omega(v) = d}$, and let $A = \operatorname{Attr}_i(N)$. By step 5, $G' = G[V \setminus A]$ is a game, and it has fewer vertices because $N \ne \emptyset$.
8. By induction $V \setminus A = W'_0 \cup W'_1$ with $W'_j = W_j(G')$, and there are positional strategies $\sigma'_0, \sigma'_1$ that win in $G'$ from every vertex of $W'_0$ and $W'_1$ respectively.
9. Case 1: $W'_{1-i} = \emptyset$. Let player $i$ follow $\sigma'_i$ on $V \setminus A$, the attractor strategy on $A \setminus N$, and any successor on $N$.
10. Take a play that follows this strategy. If it visits $A$ infinitely often, then by step 4 it visits $N$ infinitely often. So $d$ is the largest priority that occurs infinitely often, and player $i$ wins.
11. Otherwise the play stays in $V \setminus A$ from some point on. That tail is a play of $G'$ that follows $\sigma'_i$ from a vertex of $W'_i = V \setminus A$, so player $i$ wins it, and by step 1 the whole play. Hence $W_i(G) = V$, with a positional strategy.
12. Case 2: $W'_{1-i} \ne \emptyset$. Let $B = \operatorname{Attr}_{1-i}(W'_{1-i})$, computed in $G$.
13. A play of $G$ that starts in $W'_{1-i}$ and follows $\sigma'_{1-i}$ stays in $W'_{1-i}$. Player $1-i$ stays there by step 6 applied in $G'$. A vertex of $V_i \cap W'_{1-i}$ has all its $G$-successors in $V \setminus A$ by step 5, so they are $G'$-successors, and they lie in $W'_{1-i}$ by step 6.
14. So such a play is a play of $G'$ that follows $\sigma'_{1-i}$, and player $1-i$ wins it.
15. Let $\tau$ be the attractor strategy of player $1-i$ on $B \setminus W'_{1-i}$ and $\sigma'_{1-i}$ on $W'_{1-i}$. By steps 4, 14 and 1, $\tau$ wins from every vertex of $B$.
16. By step 5 with the roles exchanged, $G'' = G[V \setminus B]$ is a game in which player $1-i$ cannot leave $V \setminus B$. It has fewer vertices than $G$ because $W'_{1-i} \ne \emptyset$.
17. By induction $V \setminus B = W''_0 \cup W''_1$ with positional strategies $\sigma''_0, \sigma''_1$ that win in $G''$ from $W''_0$ and $W''_1$.
18. Player $i$ wins from $W''_i$ with $\sigma''_i$. Player $i$ never leaves $V \setminus B$ when following $\sigma''_i$, and player $1-i$ cannot leave it by step 16. So every such play is a play of $G''$ that follows $\sigma''_i$, and player $i$ wins it.
19. Player $1-i$ wins from $W''_{1-i} \cup B$ with the positional strategy that is $\tau$ on $B$ and $\sigma''_{1-i}$ on $V \setminus B$. A play that enters $B$ follows $\tau$ from then on and is won by step 15 and step 1. A play that never enters $B$ is a play of $G''$ that follows $\sigma''_{1-i}$ from a vertex of $W''_{1-i}$, and it is won too.
20. The sets $W''_i$ and $W''_{1-i} \cup B$ cover $V$. With step 2 they are exactly $W_i(G)$ and $W_{1-i}(G)$. This completes the induction and proves the claim.

**Part B: Emptiness of parity tree automata.**

Let $A = (Q, \Sigma, q_{\mathrm{in}}, \Delta, \Omega)$ be a parity tree automaton.
The emptiness game $G_A$ is played between Automaton (player $0$) and Pathfinder (player $1$).

21. The vertices of Automaton are the states $q \in Q$ and a sink $\bot$. The vertices of Pathfinder are the transitions $\delta = (q, a, q_0, q_1) \in \Delta$.
22. Edges: from $q$ to every transition whose first component is $q$; from $(q, a, q_0, q_1)$ to $q_0$ and to $q_1$; from a state without transitions to $\bot$; and from $\bot$ to itself.
23. Priorities: $\Omega(q)$ on a state $q$, $0$ on transitions, and $1$ on $\bot$. A play that never reaches $\bot$ visits states infinitely often, so its largest priority seen infinitely often is the largest $\Omega(q)$ over the states $q$ it visits infinitely often. A play that reaches $\bot$ is lost by Automaton.
24. Claim: $L(A) \ne \emptyset$ if and only if $q_{\mathrm{in}} \in W_0(G_A)$.
25. Suppose $t \in L(A)$ with an accepting run $\rho$. Automaton plays with a current node $w$, starting at $w = \varepsilon$, and keeps the current state equal to $\rho(w)$. At $\rho(w)$ he picks $(\rho(w), t(w), \rho(w0), \rho(w1)) \in \Delta$. If Pathfinder then picks $\rho(wc)$, the new current node is $wc$; if $\rho(w0) = \rho(w1)$ the node $w0$ is taken.
26. The nodes $w$ form a path of the tree, and the states of the play are the states of $\rho$ along it. The path is good, so Automaton wins. Hence $q_{\mathrm{in}} \in W_0(G_A)$.
27. Conversely let $q_{\mathrm{in}} \in W_0(G_A)$. By Part A there is a positional strategy $\sigma$ of Automaton that wins from every vertex of $W_0(G_A)$.
28. For $q \in W_0(G_A) \cap Q$, $\sigma(q) \in W_0(G_A)$ by step 6, so $\sigma(q) \ne \bot$; write $\sigma(q) = (q, a_q, q^0, q^1)$. By step 6 again, $q^0, q^1 \in W_0(G_A)$.
29. Define $\rho(\varepsilon) = q_{\mathrm{in}}$, and for each node $w$ with $\rho(w) = q$ put $t(w) = a_q$, $\rho(w0) = q^0$ and $\rho(w1) = q^1$. By step 28 and induction on $|w|$, every $\rho(w)$ lies in $W_0(G_A)$, so this defines a tree $t$ and a run $\rho$ of $A$ on $t$.
30. Along a path $\varepsilon, w_1, w_2, \ldots$, the sequence $\rho(\varepsilon), \sigma(\rho(\varepsilon)), \rho(w_1), \sigma(\rho(w_1)), \ldots$ is a play that follows $\sigma$ from $q_{\mathrm{in}}$. Automaton wins it, so by step 23 the path is good. Hence $\rho$ is accepting and $t \in L(A)$.
31. Regularity. In step 29 the subtree of $t$ at $w$ is determined by $\rho(w)$, since the same rules build it from that state. So $t$ has at most $|Q|$ distinct subtrees.
32. Decidability. There are finitely many positional strategies $\sigma$ of Automaton. For a fixed $\sigma$, delete the edges from Automaton's vertices that $\sigma$ does not use; the plays that follow $\sigma$ are the infinite paths of the remaining graph $H_\sigma$.
33. The strategy $\sigma$ loses from $q_{\mathrm{in}}$ exactly when $H_\sigma$ has a closed walk, reachable from $q_{\mathrm{in}}$, with odd largest priority. Such a walk, repeated forever after a path to it, is a losing play. Conversely, a losing play eventually visits only vertices seen infinitely often; between two later visits to a vertex of the top such priority lies such a walk.
34. This condition is checked by a finite graph search. By steps 24 and 27, $L(A) \ne \emptyset$ exactly when some positional $\sigma$ passes the test, so emptiness is decidable. The recursion of Part A is also an algorithm, since attractors are computed by finite iteration.

**Part C: From S2S to automata (outline; complementation is cited).**

35. Rabin conditions to parity conditions. Every Rabin tree automaton can be turned effectively into a parity tree automaton with the same language, by recording the order of latest visits to states. We cite this translation and do not prove it. With it, Part B proves part 3 of the theorem for Rabin automata.
36. Formulas to automata. First-order variables are replaced by set variables restricted to singletons, as in the [[proof of Büchi's theorem|~2b2fce64]]. The tracks of $X_1, \ldots, X_m$ form a $\set{0,1}^m$-tree.
37. Atomic formulas. "$X$ is a singleton" uses states $s$ (the element lies below) and $n$ (none below), with $\Omega(s) = 1$ and $\Omega(n) = 0$. In state $s$, label $1$ sends $n$ to both children, and label $0$ sends $s$ to one child and $n$ to the other. State $n$ needs label $0$ and sends $n$ to both. Priority $1$ forbids an infinite chain of $s$. Similar automata handle $X \subseteq Y$ and $s_c(x) = y$.
38. Disjunction is union of automata. An existential quantifier is projection: the automaton guesses the erased track, exactly as in Part A of the [[proof of Büchi's theorem|~2b2fce64]].
39. Negation needs complementation of parity tree automata. We cite it: Rabin proved it in 1969, Gurevich and Harrington gave a game proof in 1982, and Emerson and Jutla and Mostowski gave proofs from positional determinacy of parity games on infinite graphs in 1991. Part A covers only finite graphs, because the acceptance game of one automaton on one infinite tree is infinite.
40. Automata to formulas. Guess sets $X_{q_1}, \ldots, X_{q_n}$ for the run, as in the [[Büchi-Elgot-Trakhtenbrot theorem|~f98304ff]]. "Every path is good" quantifies over paths $P$ and says, for each odd $p$: if $p$ occurs infinitely often on $P$, so does a larger priority. "Infinitely often on $P$" is definable, since the prefix order of the tree is S2S-definable.
41. Decidability. For a sentence $\varphi$, build the automaton of $\varphi \wedge X_1 \subseteq X_1$ by steps 36 to 39. Its language is the set of all trees if $\varphi$ is true, and empty otherwise. Step 34 decides which case holds, which proves part 4.

So Part A and Part B are proved in full, step 35 and step 39 are cited, and steps 36 to 40 are an outline.

## Idea

Acceptance and emptiness of tree automata are games.
To decide emptiness, Automaton chooses transitions and Pathfinder chooses directions, and the game is finite because only states matter.
A winning strategy for Automaton becomes an accepting run, and positional determinacy makes it depend only on the state, so the tree it builds is regular.
Positional determinacy itself follows by induction on the size of the game: the player who likes the top priority attracts to it, and the rest of the game is smaller.

## Variants

Rabin's original proof of complementation was a direct and long induction on automata.
Gurevich and Harrington used games with a latest appearance record as finite memory, and the parity condition with positional strategies gives the shortest proof.
Muchnik's theorem, with a proof by Walukiewicz, extends the method: decidability of the MSO theory passes from a structure to its tree-like iteration.
