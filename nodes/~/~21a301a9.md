## Example

[[Varignon's theorem|B2ZJW0YBQ7]] says: the [[midpoints|0S706JBZBG]] of the four sides of a quadrilateral $ABCD$ are the vertices of a parallelogram.
A student who draws only convex quadrilaterals may state the theorem for convex quadrilaterals.
We give a proof and read it to see which hypotheses it uses.

1. Treat points as vectors in the plane. The midpoint of $AB$ is $K = \frac{1}{2}(A + B)$. Likewise $L = \frac{1}{2}(B + C)$, $M = \frac{1}{2}(C + D)$ and $N = \frac{1}{2}(D + A)$.
2. Then $L - K = \frac{1}{2}(B + C) - \frac{1}{2}(A + B) = \frac{1}{2}(C - A)$.
3. Also $M - N = \frac{1}{2}(C + D) - \frac{1}{2}(D + A) = \frac{1}{2}(C - A)$.
4. So $L - K = M - N$: the sides $KL$ and $NM$ are parallel to the diagonal $AC$, and each has half its length.
5. In the same way, $N - K = \frac{1}{2}(D - B) = M - L$, so the sides $KN$ and $LM$ are parallel to the diagonal $BD$.
6. A quadrilateral $KLMN$ with $L - K = M - N$ is a parallelogram, provided that its vertices are not all on one line.

Step 1 to step 6 use only the formula for a midpoint and vector arithmetic.
No step uses the fact that $ABCD$ is convex.
So the proof shows more than the student stated: the result holds for non-convex quadrilaterals too.

We check this on a non-convex quadrilateral.

7. Let $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (4, 0)$, $C = (1, 1)$ and $D = (0, 4)$. The angle at $C$ is greater than $180°$, so $ABCD$ is not convex.
8. The midpoints are $K = (2, 0)$, $L = (2.5, 0.5)$, $M = (0.5, 2.5)$ and $N = (0, 2)$.
9. $L - K = (0.5, 0.5)$ and $M - N = (0.5, 0.5)$. These are equal, and both equal $\frac{1}{2}(C - A) = \frac{1}{2}(1, 1)$.
10. $N - K = (-2, 2)$ and $M - L = (-2, 2)$. These are equal, and both equal $\frac{1}{2}(D - B) = \frac{1}{2}(-4, 4)$.
11. So $KLMN$ is a parallelogram, as the proof predicts.

## Remarks

The example shows the function of discovery: reading a proof for the hypotheses it really uses can give a more general theorem.
The figure suggested convexity, but the proof did not need it.
The proof also gives facts that were not asked for: the sides of $KLMN$ are parallel to the diagonals of $ABCD$ and half as long.
Step 6 shows one case where the conclusion needs care.
The four midpoints lie on one line when the diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ are parallel, and this can happen for a self-crossing quadrilateral.
For $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (0, 1)$, $C = (2, 0)$ and $D = (2, 1)$, the midpoints are $(0, 0.5)$, $(1, 0.5)$, $(2, 0.5)$ and $(1, 0.5)$, all on the line $y = 0.5$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Draw a four-sided shape and mark the middle of each side.
Join the four middle points in order, and you get a parallelogram.
Most people first try a nice shape, such as a kite or a rectangle.
The proof never uses the fact that the shape bulges outward.
So the same thing happens for a dented shape, like an arrowhead, and a quick drawing confirms it.

### L2 human

We test the proof on a second non-convex quadrilateral: $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (6, 0)$, $C = (2, 2)$, $D = (0, 6)$.

1. Midpoints: $K = (3, 0)$, $L = (4, 1)$, $M = (1, 4)$, $N = (0, 3)$.
2. $L - K = (1, 1)$ and $M - N = (1, 1)$, which is $\frac{1}{2}(C - A)$.
3. $N - K = (-3, 3)$ and $M - L = (-3, 3)$, which is $\frac{1}{2}(D - B)$.
4. Opposite sides are equal vectors, so $KLMN$ is a parallelogram.

The typical use of this reading of a proof is to strengthen a theorem: we list the hypotheses that the proof uses and drop the others.
A trap is to infer a general claim from the check in steps 1 to 4.
The checks only illustrate; the vector proof is what covers every quadrilateral.
