## Example

Let $V = M_2(\mathbb{R})$, the space of real $2 \times 2$ matrices, and let $T(X) = X^T$, the [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]].
Let $E_{ij}$ be the matrix with entry $1$ at position $(i, j)$ and $0$ elsewhere.

1. In the basis $E_{11}, E_{12}, E_{21}, E_{22}$: $T$ fixes $E_{11}$ and $E_{22}$, and it exchanges $E_{12}$ and $E_{21}$.
2. So $[T]$ is the permutation matrix of the transposition of positions $2$ and $3$.
3. Its determinant is the sign of a transposition, so the [[definition|03F5KP94K0]] gives $\det T = -1$.
4. Check in the basis $E_{11}, E_{22}, E_{12} + E_{21}, E_{12} - E_{21}$.
5. $T$ fixes the first three, and $T(E_{12} - E_{21}) = E_{21} - E_{12} = -(E_{12} - E_{21})$.
6. So the matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 1, -1)$, with determinant $-1$, as the [[invariance statement|0KMN32XGPF]] predicts.

## Remarks

The second basis splits $V$ into symmetric matrices, of dimension $3$, and skew-symmetric matrices, of dimension $1$.
On $M_n(\mathbb{R})$, the transpose fixes the symmetric matrices and negates the $n(n-1)/2$-dimensional space of skew-symmetric ones.
So its determinant is $(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}$, which is $1$ for $n = 4$.
The map $T$ is its own inverse, which agrees with $(\det T)^2 = 1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Transposing a $2 \times 2$ matrix keeps the diagonal entries and swaps the two off-diagonal entries.
A swap of two coordinates is a reflection, and a reflection has determinant $-1$.

### L2 human

The space here is $4$-dimensional, so the matrix of $T$ is $4 \times 4$, not $2 \times 2$.
The basis of symmetric and skew-symmetric matrices makes the matrix diagonal.
A trap: $\det T$ is about the map $X \mapsto X^T$; it is not $\det X^T$, which equals $\det X$.
