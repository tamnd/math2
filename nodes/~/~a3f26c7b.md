## Example

Let $W = \set{w, n}$, $N = \set{w}$, $R = \set{(w, w), (w, n), (n, n)}$, and let $V$ be any valuation.
This is a [[Kripke model|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]] with a non-normal world $n$, an [[impossible world|8PAJMAN8DM]] in Kripke's sense.
The relation $R$ is reflexive, so the model is one of Kripke's models for $\mathsf{S2}$.
We show that $\square(p \vee \neg p)$ is true at $w$, but $\square \square(p \vee \neg p)$ is false at $w$.

1. The connectives $\vee$ and $\neg$ are classical at every world, so $p \vee \neg p$ is true at $w$ and at $n$.
2. $w$ is normal, and both of its successors $w$ and $n$ make $p \vee \neg p$ true. So $w \models \square(p \vee \neg p)$.
3. $n$ is non-normal, so every formula $\square \varphi$ is false at $n$. In particular, $n \not\models \square(p \vee \neg p)$.
4. $w R n$, so $w \not\models \square \square(p \vee \neg p)$.
5. The same argument applies to every model with a normal world that sees a non-normal one. So $\square(p \vee \neg p)$ is valid, while $\square \square(p \vee \neg p)$ is not.

## Remarks

The example shows that the rule of [[necessitation|G989VP2NGP]] fails in Kripke's semantics with non-normal worlds: from the valid $\square(p \vee \neg p)$ it does not yield $\square \square(p \vee \neg p)$.
This matches the Lewis systems $\mathsf{S2}$ and $\mathsf{S3}$, which contain $\square A$ for every tautology $A$ but do not prove $\square \square(p \vee \neg p)$.
If $n$ is made normal, the model becomes an ordinary reflexive Kripke model, and $\square \square(p \vee \neg p)$ becomes true at $w$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A normal world checks "necessarily" by looking at the worlds it can see.
A non-normal world says "no" to every claim of necessity, even for laws of logic.
So a law of logic is necessary at a normal world, but "necessarily necessary" fails as soon as a non-normal world is visible.

### L2 human

1. Draw $w$ with a loop and an arrow to $n$, and give $n$ a loop.
2. At $w$, check $\square(p \vee \neg p)$: look at $w$ and $n$, where $p \vee \neg p$ is true. The box holds.
3. At $n$, the box is false by definition, without looking at successors.
4. At $w$, check $\square \square(p \vee \neg p)$: the successor $n$ fails $\square(p \vee \neg p)$. The double box fails.

A trap: validity is checked only at normal worlds; at $n$ even $\square(p \vee \neg p)$ fails.
