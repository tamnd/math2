## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $U$ and $W$ be [[subspaces|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$.
The following three conditions are equivalent.

1. The [[sum|0RMKABS2S5]] $U + W$ is [[direct|041JTZCK9Q]]: each vector of $U + W$ is $u + w$ with $u \in U$ and $w \in W$ in exactly one way.
2. If $u + w = 0$ with $u \in U$ and $w \in W$, then $u = 0$ and $w = 0$.
3. The [[intersection|0V33SPH7RR]] $U \cap W$ is $\{0\}$.

In particular, $V = U \oplus W$ if and only if $U + W = V$ and $U \cap W = \{0\}$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $U$ and $W$ must be subspaces. In $\R$, the subsets $U = \{0, 1, 2\}$ and $W = \{0, -1\}$ meet only in $0$, but $1 = 1 + 0 = 2 + (-1)$ has two expressions.
- The statement is about two subspaces. For three subspaces, the analogue of condition 3 with pairwise intersections is weaker than directness, as [[three lines in the plane|~7846d01c]] show. The correct test is the [[criterion for several subspaces|~550a8327]].

### Consequences

- To prove $V = U \oplus W$, it is enough to check that each vector is some $u + w$ and that $U \cap W = \{0\}$.
- In finite dimension, the [[dimension criterion|~daf08520]] replaces the check $U + W = V$ by a count of dimensions.
- If $U + W$ is direct, then a family that is independent in $U$ together with a family that is independent in $W$ is independent in $V$.

### Converse

Each condition implies the other two, so there is no separate converse.
The condition "$U + W = V$" is independent of the three: the sum of the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis in $\R^3$ is direct, but it is not $\R^3$, see [[the two axes in space|~d559e535]].

### Generalizations

- The same criteria hold for submodules of a module over any ring, with the same proof.
- In the lattice of subspaces, $U \cap W = \{0\}$ and $U + W = V$ say that $U$ and $W$ are complements, which Mathlib writes `IsCompl U W`.

## History

The test by the intersection is in the early abstract treatments of vector spaces, for example in Halmos's *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces* of 1942.
It is the first criterion in most modern textbooks, such as Axler's *Linear Algebra Done Right*.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two subspaces give unique splittings exactly when they share only the zero vector.
If they shared a nonzero vector $x$, then $x$ could be put in the first part or in the second part, and the splitting would not be unique.

### L2 human

We test whether $U = \{(x, y, z) : x + y + z = 0\}$ and $W = \R(1, 1, 1)$ give a direct sum.

1. A vector $t(1, 1, 1)$ of $W$ lies in $U$ when $3t = 0$, so $t = 0$.
2. So $U \cap W = \{0\}$, and the sum $U + W$ is direct by the criterion.
3. Each $(x, y, z)$ is $(x - m, y - m, z - m) + m(1, 1, 1)$ with $m = (x + y + z)/3$, so $U + W = \R^3$.

Hence $\R^3 = U \oplus W$.
The trap is to stop after step 2: it shows that the sum is direct, not that it is all of $\R^3$.

### L3 human

Condition 2 says that the addition map $U \times W \to V$, $(u, w) \mapsto u + w$, has kernel $\{0\}$.
Condition 3 describes the same kernel, since $(u, w)$ lies in it exactly when $u = -w \in U \cap W$.
So the criteria are the statement that a linear map is injective if and only if its kernel is zero.
