## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. Squaring each entry gives $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 9 & 16 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. By the [[product rule|076JGP6R3T]], $(A^2)_{11} = 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 3 = 7$ and $(A^2)_{12} = 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 4 = 10$.
3. Also $(A^2)_{21} = 3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 3 = 15$ and $(A^2)_{22} = 3 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 4 = 22$.
4. So $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 10 \\ 15 & 22 \end{pmatrix}$, which differs from step 1 in every entry.

## Remarks

The entrywise product is the [[Hadamard product|SQT6QCEXZ8]] $A \circ A$, a different operation.
It agrees with the matrix product for [[diagonal matrices|044JNC7ZAA]], which is one reason for the error.
In some software, `A^2` or `A**2` means the entrywise power, so check the convention.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Squaring a matrix does not mean squaring its numbers.
It means multiplying the matrix by itself with the row-times-column rule.

### L2 human

The matrix square mixes the entries: $(A^2)_{11} = a_{11}^2 + a_{12} a_{21}$.
The term $a_{12} a_{21}$ is missing in the entrywise square.
