## Example

The classical derivation of $p \vee \neg p$ in natural deduction has two parts.

1. Steps 1 to 8 of [[the double negation of excluded middle|~9ac335b9]] derive $\neg\neg(p \vee \neg p)$ without assumptions.
2. The last step applies reductio ad absurdum: from a derivation of $\bot$ from $\neg\varphi$, infer $\varphi$.
3. With $\varphi := p \vee \neg p$, it turns the derivation of $\bot$ from $\neg(p \vee \neg p)$ into a derivation of $p \vee \neg p$.

We check that this is not a derivation in [[intuitionistic logic|52XCYMK0WW]].

4. The rules of $\mathrm{NJ}$ are introduction and elimination for $\wedge$, $\vee$, $\to$, and "from $\bot$ infer $\varphi$".
5. Reductio ad absurdum discharges the assumption $\neg\varphi$ and concludes $\varphi$. No rule of step 4 does this.
6. The step can not be replaced by intuitionistic rules: the conclusion $p \vee \neg p$ fails in [[the two-point countermodel|~bc4c4586]], and $\mathrm{NJ}$ is sound for Kripke models.

## Remarks

The example shows where classical logic goes beyond intuitionistic logic: one rule, at one place in the derivation.
Adding reductio ad absurdum, or double negation elimination $\neg\neg\varphi \to \varphi$, to $\mathrm{NJ}$ gives the classical system $\mathrm{NK}$.
If the rule is used only on formulas $\varphi$ with $\vdash_{\mathsf{i}} \varphi \vee \neg\varphi$, the result is again intuitionistic.
For example, the rule is harmless for formulas without variables.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The classical proof of "$p$ or not $p$" first shows that denying it leads to a contradiction.
Then it concludes the statement itself.
This last move is the one that intuitionistic logic does not allow.
A refutation of a denial is not yet a proof that names one of the two cases.

### L2 human

1. Let $p$ be an open problem, for example "there are infinitely many twin primes".
2. The intuitionistic part of the derivation shows that "$p$ or not $p$" can not be refuted.
3. The final classical step claims "$p$ or not $p$", but the derivation does not tell which disjunct holds.
4. An intuitionistic proof of a disjunction always tells which disjunct holds, by the [[disjunction property|~59f11dc8]].

The typical use of this example is to locate the single non-constructive step in a classical proof.
A trap: it is not the use of $\neg$ that makes a proof classical, but the step that removes a double negation.
