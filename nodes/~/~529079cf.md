## Example

Let $Q$ be the property of a world in a Kripke model with one relation: "the world has at least two distinct successors".
We show that no formula $\varphi$ of the [[modal mu-calculus|MZ0M2KR9S0]] satisfies, for every model $\mathfrak{M}$ and world $w$, $\mathfrak{M}, w \models \varphi$ exactly when $w$ has property $Q$.

Let $\mathfrak{M}_1 = (W_1, R_1, V_1)$ with $W_1 = \{w, u_1, u_2\}$, $R_1 = \{(w, u_1), (w, u_2)\}$, and $V_1(p) = \emptyset$ for every $p$.
Let $\mathfrak{M}_2 = (W_2, R_2, V_2)$ with $W_2 = \{w', u'\}$, $R_2 = \{(w', u')\}$, and $V_2(p) = \emptyset$ for every $p$.
Let $Z = \{(w, w'), (u_1, u'), (u_2, u')\}$.

1. Atoms: every propositional variable is false at every world of both models, so linked worlds agree on all variables.
2. Forth for $(w, w')$: the successors of $w$ are $u_1$ and $u_2$. Both are linked to $u'$, and $w' R_2 u'$.
3. Back for $(w, w')$: the only successor of $w'$ is $u'$, and $w R_1 u_1$ with $u_1 Z u'$.
4. Forth and back for $(u_1, u')$ and $(u_2, u')$: none of these worlds has a successor, so there is nothing to check.
5. By steps 1 to 4, $Z$ is a [[bisimulation|X27BR4YP2Y]], and $w Z w'$.
6. By [[bisimulation invariance|~88334673]], extended to fixed points, $w$ and $w'$ satisfy the same formulas of $L_\mu$.
7. World $w$ has the two successors $u_1 \neq u_2$, so $w$ has property $Q$. World $w'$ has one successor, so $w'$ does not have property $Q$.
8. If $\varphi$ defined $Q$, then $\mathfrak{M}_1, w \models \varphi$ and $\mathfrak{M}_2, w' \not\models \varphi$, which contradicts step 6. So no formula defines $Q$.

## Remarks

The example shows that counting successors is outside the modal $\mu$-calculus, although it is easy in first-order logic: $\exists y \exists z (x R y \wedge x R z \wedge y \neq z)$.
So the modal $\mu$-calculus and first-order logic are incomparable: first-order logic counts, and $L_\mu$ expresses reachability, which first-order logic cannot.
Graded modal logic adds diamonds $\lozenge_{\geq k}$ for counting, and its fixed-point extension is the graded $\mu$-calculus.
If we change $V_1$ so that $p$ is true at $u_1$ only, the relation $Z$ is no longer a bisimulation, and $\lozenge p \wedge \lozenge \neg p$ then forces two successors.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A modal formula only looks at what the next worlds are like, not at how many copies of them there are.
In the first model the world $w$ has two identical next worlds, and in the second model $w'$ has one.
Seen from inside the logic, the two pictures look the same.
So the logic cannot say "there are two next worlds".

### L2 human

We check one formula on both models to see the invariance at work.

1. Take $\varphi = \lozenge \square \bot$, "some successor has no successor".
2. In $\mathfrak{M}_1$: $w R_1 u_1$ and $u_1$ has no successor, so $u_1 \models \square \bot$ and $w \models \varphi$.
3. In $\mathfrak{M}_2$: $w' R_2 u'$ and $u'$ has no successor, so $w' \models \varphi$.
4. The two worlds agree on $\varphi$, as the invariance predicts.

The typical use of this argument is to show that a property is not definable: find two bisimilar worlds that differ on it.
A trap: the argument needs the bisimulation to hold for every variable, so all propositional variables must agree on linked worlds.
