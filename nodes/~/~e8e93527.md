## Summary

The cut rule of a [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] infers $\Gamma, \Pi \Rightarrow \Delta, \Lambda$ from $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta, A$ and $A, \Pi \Rightarrow \Lambda$, for any formula $A$, the cut formula.
Its context-sharing form infers $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta$ from $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta, A$ and $A, \Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta$; the [[definition|~5bc7d378]] gives both forms.
The rule expresses the use of a lemma, and it is the only rule of Gentzen's calculi whose premises contain a formula that can be absent from the conclusion.

## Motivation

Mathematical proofs are built from lemmas: one proves $A$ once and then uses it as an assumption.
A calculus of proofs needs a rule for this step, and in the sequent calculus this rule is cut.
Gentzen observed that the other rules only build formulas up, so derivations without cut can be analysed formula by formula.
This led to the question whether cut is needed at all, which the [[cut-elimination theorem|AYRZ98Y0NW]] answers.

## Notation

The rule is written with the cut formula on the right of the left premise and on the left of the right premise.
Many books name it Cut or (cut), and Gentzen's German name is *Schnitt*.
In one-sided calculi, cut infers $\Rightarrow \Gamma, \Delta$ from $\Rightarrow \Gamma, A$ and $\Rightarrow \Delta, \neg A$.
In natural deduction, the corresponding pattern is an introduction rule followed by an elimination rule on the same formula.

## Remarks

### Examples

- A cut on $q$ chains $p \to q, p \Rightarrow q$ and $q, q \to r \Rightarrow r$ into $p \to q, p, q \to r \Rightarrow r$, see [[this example|~a8b06b41]].
- The context-sharing and context-splitting forms are interderivable in LK with structural rules, see [[this example|~236fb85c]].
- A cut of an axiom $A \Rightarrow A$ with any sequent $A, \Pi \Rightarrow \Lambda$ gives $A, \Pi \Rightarrow \Lambda$ back, since the contexts of the axiom are empty.
- Modus ponens is a cut: from $\Rightarrow A$ and $A \Rightarrow B$, a cut gives $\Rightarrow B$.

### Non-examples

- The rule from $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta, A$ and $\Pi \Rightarrow \Lambda, A$ to $\Gamma, \Pi \Rightarrow \Delta, \Lambda$ is not a cut: the formula is on the same side twice, and the rule is unsound, see [[this non-example|~d8af9341]].
- Contraction, from $A, A, \Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta$ to $A, \Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta$, removes a formula but has only one premise. It is a structural rule, and it is not a cut.
- The rule ${\to}$L has two premises and makes $A \to B$ from $A$ and $B$. The new formula contains both active formulas, so the rule is not a cut.

### Basic properties

- Cut is sound: it turns true premises into a true conclusion.
- In LK, cut is admissible: by the [[cut-elimination theorem|AYRZ98Y0NW]], every derivable sequent has a cut-free derivation.
- Cut is not derivable from the other rules of LK: with the extra initial sequents $\Rightarrow p$ and $p \Rightarrow q$, cut gives $\Rightarrow q$, and the other rules do not.
- Every rule except cut has the [[subformula property|~694ac127]], so cut-free derivations contain only subformulas of their end sequent.
- Removing cuts can make derivations much longer, as the [[complexity of cut elimination|~29155909]] shows.

### History

Gerhard Gentzen introduced the rule, under the name *Schnitt*, in his sequent calculi LK and LJ, published in 1934 and 1935.
He proved in the same work that the rule can be removed from derivations.
The name cut is the English translation of *Schnitt*.

### Common errors

- Placing the cut formula on the same side in both premises. The correct rule needs it on the right of one premise and on the left of the other.
- Thinking that the cut formula must be a subformula of the conclusion. It can be any formula.
- Thinking that cut elimination makes a derivation shorter. It usually makes it longer.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A lemma is a small result that we prove first and use later.
The cut rule is the step "we proved $A$, and from $A$ we get our goal, so we get our goal".
For example, from "if it rains, the street is wet" and "if the street is wet, it is slippery", we get "if it rains, it is slippery".
The middle claim, "the street is wet", is the cut formula, and it disappears in the result.

### L2 human

We check one instance of the context-splitting rule.

1. Take the left premise $p \Rightarrow p \vee q$. It is derivable from the axiom $p \Rightarrow p$ by ${\vee}$R.
2. Take the right premise $p \vee q \Rightarrow q \vee p$. It is derivable by ${\vee}$L from $p \Rightarrow q \vee p$ and $q \Rightarrow q \vee p$, each by ${\vee}$R.
3. The cut formula is $A = p \vee q$, with $\Gamma = p$ and $\Pi$, $\Delta$ empty and $\Lambda = q \vee p$.
4. The conclusion is $p \Rightarrow q \vee p$.

The typical use of cut is to reuse a derived sequent many times, which keeps derivations short.
A trap: after cut elimination, the derivation of step 4 is just ${\vee}$R from the axiom, and it never mentions $p \vee q$.

### L3 human

Cut is transitivity of the entailment relation, and in categorical semantics it is the composition of morphisms.
Under the [[Curry-Howard correspondence|A77FER1Q5H]], a cut corresponds to substituting one program into another, and cut elimination to running the program.
In substructural logics the two forms of cut differ, and the form chosen fixes how contexts are combined.
The problem of when two derivations with cuts are the same proof leads to the [[identity of proofs|~8cb6c275]].
