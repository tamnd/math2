## Summary

The monovariant method is a method of proof for processes that change a state by allowed moves.
A monovariant is a function $M$ on the states, with values in an ordered set, such that $M(t) < M(s)$ whenever a move changes $s$ into $t$.
If the values of $M$ are natural numbers, or more generally lie in a well-ordered set, then every sequence of moves is finite, so the process stops.
The same holds when each move increases an integer-valued $M$ and $M$ is bounded above.
The method matters because it proves termination, and the stop state then has the property that no move is possible, which often answers the problem.

## Motivation

An [[invariant|~6e8e1c09]] never changes, so it can show that a target is unreachable.
Many questions ask the opposite kind of thing: does a process always stop, or does it always reach a state of a given kind?
A process that goes on for a long time does not show that it never stops, and a computer run of a few cases proves nothing about all cases.
We need a quantity that moves in one direction at each step and cannot move in that direction for ever.
The natural numbers give such a quantity: a strictly decreasing sequence of natural numbers is finite, by the [[well-ordering principle|01BX15HSRR]].
For example, a sort that swaps two neighbouring entries in the wrong order lowers the number of pairs in the wrong order by $1$ at each swap, so it must stop.

## Notation

There is no standard symbol.
The words "monovariant" and "potential function" are used for the same idea.
In the theory of algorithms the quantity is a "variant" or a "ranking function", and in dynamical systems it is a [[Lyapunov function|01REYCJ371]].

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[Sorting by swaps of neighbours|~cbaec087]]: the number of inversions decreases by $1$ at each swap. So a list of $n$ numbers is sorted after at most $n(n - 1)/2$ swaps.
- [[Chip-firing on a path with two sinks|~12b87b80]]: a weight of the chips, zero at the sinks, decreases by $2$ at each firing. So the game stops.
- The [[Euclidean algorithm|0TRNT4X7J0]] replaces a pair $(a, b)$ with $b > 0$ by $(b, r)$, where $r$ is the remainder of $a$ on division by $b$. The second entry decreases, since $0 \le r < b$, so the algorithm stops.
- A table of real numbers allows a move that changes the sign of each entry of a row or a column whose sum is negative. Such a move with line sum $s < 0$ increases the total of the table by $-2s > 0$. The reachable tables differ only in signs, so there are finitely many, and the process stops with each row sum and each column sum not negative.
- [[Proof by infinite descent|0EFYCFHYFG]] is a monovariant argument on solutions: each solution gives a smaller one in the natural numbers, which cannot go on for ever.

### Non-examples

- The map $x \mapsto x/2$ on the positive real numbers decreases $x$ at each step, but it never stops, as [[this example|~4586bad7]] shows. The values do not lie in a well-ordered set.
- Chip-firing on a cycle of $3$ vertices with $2, 1, 1$ chips returns to the start after $3$ firings and goes on for ever. A strict monovariant cannot exist, because a state repeats.
- In the 15 puzzle, the number of tiles in their home cells goes up at some moves and down at others. It is neither an [[invariant|~6e8e1c09]] nor a monovariant.

### Basic properties

- If $M$ has values in the natural numbers and each move lowers $M$ by at least $1$, then each sequence of moves from $s$ has at most $M(s)$ moves. After $k$ moves the value is at most $M(s) - k$, and it is not negative.
- If $M$ has values in a [[well-ordered|01CGSJ0GNQ]] set, then every sequence of moves is finite. An infinite sequence would give an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of values, whose set has no least element.
- If the set of states is finite and $M$ strictly increases at each move, then no state repeats, so the process stops after fewer moves than the number of states.
- When the process stops, no move is possible from the final state. This is the extra fact that the method gives about the final state.
- A monovariant proves that every order of moves stops. It does not say that the final state is the same for each order; that needs a separate argument.

### When it applies

The method applies when a problem describes a process and asks whether it stops, or whether it reaches a state with a given property.
Typical signals are "prove that the process ends", "after finitely many steps" and "show that eventually".
It also proves the existence of an object: start anywhere, improve by moves, and stop at an object where no improvement is possible.

### The steps

1. Describe the states and the moves exactly.
2. Find a quantity that each move changes in one direction: a sum, a count of bad pairs, a sum of squares, a distance.
3. Prove that the change is strict at each move, for each kind of move.
4. Prove that the quantity cannot change for ever: it is a natural number, or it is bounded and changes by at least a fixed amount, or the states are finite.
5. Conclude that the process stops, and read off the property of the final state from "no move is possible".

### When it fails

The method fails when the quantity decreases but its values have no least element, as for the halving of a positive real number.
It also fails when some moves keep the quantity and these moves can repeat; then a second quantity is needed for those moves.
When a process can loop, as chip-firing on a cycle can, no monovariant exists at all.

### Related methods

- An [[invariant|~6e8e1c09]] stays fixed, and it proves impossibility. A monovariant moves one way, and it proves termination.
- The [[extremal principle|~21d1cff7]] starts from a smallest or largest object. A monovariant argument often turns into an extremal argument: take the state with the least value of $M$.
- [[Infinite descent|0EFYCFHYFG]] and the [[well-ordering principle|01BX15HSRR]] are the logical base of the method.

### History

The Euclidean algorithm, in Book VII of Euclid's "Elements", stops because the remainders decrease.
Pierre de Fermat used infinite descent, a monovariant argument on solutions of equations, in the seventeenth century.
Per Bak, Chao Tang and Kurt Wiesenfeld introduced the sandpile model in 1987, and Anders Björner, László Lovász and Peter Shor studied chip-firing games on graphs in 1991.

### Common errors

- To show that the quantity decreases, but not that it is bounded below by a value it cannot pass. Positive real numbers can decrease for ever.
- To check only some of the moves. A single kind of move that increases the quantity breaks the argument.
- To conclude that the final state is unique because the process stops. Termination and uniqueness of the result are different claims.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some processes look as if they could go on for ever.
To show that one must stop, find a number that goes down at every step and is always a whole number at least $0$.
A whole number that goes down by at least $1$ at each step cannot go below $0$, so the steps must end.
For example, take the list $3, 1, 2$ and swap two neighbours whenever they are in the wrong order.
The number of pairs in the wrong order is $2$ at the start, and each swap lowers it by $1$.
So after at most $2$ swaps the list is sorted.

### L2 human

We show that the sign-change process on a table stops, and we run it on one table.
A move picks a row or a column with a negative sum and changes the sign of each of its entries.

1. Let $S$ be the sum of all entries. If a move changes a line with sum $s < 0$, the sum of that line becomes $-s$, so $S$ changes by $-s - s = -2s > 0$.
2. Each move changes the signs only, so each reachable table has the same absolute values as the start. A table with $mn$ entries has at most $2^{mn}$ such sign patterns.
3. $S$ strictly increases, so no table repeats. By step 2, the process stops after fewer than $2^{mn}$ moves.
4. At the end no move is possible, so each row and each column has a sum that is not negative.
5. We run it on the table with rows $(1, -2, 3)$ and $(-4, 1, -1)$. The row sums are $2$ and $-4$, the column sums are $-3, -1, 2$, and $S = -2$.
6. We change the second row, with sum $-4$. The rows become $(1, -2, 3)$ and $(4, -1, 1)$, and $S = -2 + 8 = 6$.
7. The column sums are now $5, -3, 4$. We change the second column, with sum $-3$. The rows become $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(4, 1, 1)$, and $S = 6 + 6 = 12$.
8. The row sums are $6, 6$ and the column sums are $5, 3, 4$. No move is possible, and the process stops.

The typical use is the existence proof of step 4: the process proves that a table with all line sums not negative exists.
A trap is step 3 without step 2: $S$ is a real number, and an increasing real number can go on for ever, so the finiteness of the states is needed.

### L3 human

A monovariant is a map from the states to a well-founded order that strictly decreases along each move.
Then the move relation is a [[well-founded relation|0DQPNJNPYK]], and so every sequence of moves is finite.
Values in the ordinals give stronger tools: [[Goodstein's theorem|XQMNJF241J]] proves termination by a decreasing ordinal below $\varepsilon_0$, and this proof cannot be done in Peano arithmetic.
In the theory of programs, a ranking function plays the same role, and term rewriting systems are proved terminating by orders on terms.

In dynamical systems a [[Lyapunov function|01REYCJ371]] decreases along the trajectories and proves the stability of an equilibrium.
In optimization, the objective value is a monovariant of a local search, and the final state is a local optimum.
In algebraic combinatorics, the [[abelian sandpile model|N33S3095R5]] and chip-firing use monovariants to prove that stabilization ends, and an exchange argument proves that the final state does not depend on the order of the firings.
The [[invariant method|~6e8e1c09]] is the two-sided case, and many proofs combine both: an invariant fixes what stays, and a monovariant shows that the process ends.
