## Example

On the set $\Z^{\N}$ of integer sequences, put $a \approx b$ when $a(i) = b(i)$ for infinitely many $i$.
We check that $\approx$ is not an [[equivalence relation|0G8VEZ3WTK]], so it does not define a [[reduced product|F4WRD396PB]].

1. Let $a = (0, 0, 0, \dots)$, $b = (0, 1, 0, 1, \dots)$ and $c = (1, 1, 1, \dots)$.
2. $a(i) = b(i)$ for every even $i$, an infinite set, so $a \approx b$.
3. $b(i) = c(i)$ for every odd $i$, an infinite set, so $b \approx c$.
4. $a(i) = 0 \neq 1 = c(i)$ for every $i$, so $a \not\approx c$.
5. So $\approx$ is not transitive.

The reason is that the family of infinite subsets of $\N$ is not a [[filter|15PF73BKY6]]: the even and the odd numbers are infinite, and their intersection is empty.

## Remarks

The example shows which axiom of a filter makes $\sim_F$ transitive: closure under finite intersections.
Every filter on $\N$ that contains all cofinite sets, such as the Fréchet filter, gives a transitive relation.
If we choose a nonprincipal ultrafilter $\mathcal{U}$ on $\N$, then exactly one of the evens and the odds lies in $\mathcal{U}$, and $b$ becomes equal to $a$ or to $c$, but not to both.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose we call two lists "equal" when they agree at infinitely many places.
The all-zero list agrees with the list $0, 1, 0, 1, \dots$ at the even places.
That list agrees with the all-one list at the odd places.
But the all-zero list and the all-one list agree nowhere.
So this kind of "equality" does not behave like equality.

### L2 human

We find which axiom fails.

1. A filter must contain the intersection of two of its members.
2. The evens $E$ and the odds $O$ are infinite, but $E \cap O = \emptyset$ is finite.
3. In step 4 above, the agreement set of $a$ and $c$ would have to contain the intersection $E \cap O$ of the two agreement sets, and that intersection is empty.

The trap is to think that "large" may mean "infinite"; a notion of large sets must be closed under intersection.
