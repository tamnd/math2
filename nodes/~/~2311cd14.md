## Summary

The phrase "up to $\sim$" says that a statement treats objects related by an [[equivalence relation|0G8VEZ3WTK]] $\sim$ as the same.
"$x$ is unique up to $\sim$" means that some $x$ has the property, and that any two such objects $x$ and $y$ satisfy $x \sim y$.
Equivalently, the property singles out exactly one [[equivalence class|0W95W33CMP]].
Common forms are "up to order", "up to sign", "up to a constant", "up to isomorphism" and "modulo $n$".
The phrase matters because many uniqueness theorems are false for strict equality and true for a coarser relation.

## Motivation

The number $12$ factors into primes as $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3$, and also as $3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2$.
So the factorization is not unique as a list.
But the two lists differ only in the order of their entries.
The fundamental theorem of arithmetic says that the factorization is unique "up to order".
This phrase states the theorem exactly, and it shows which differences the theorem ignores.
Without it, the theorem would need a longer statement about multisets, or a convention that sorts the factors.

## Notation

1. "Unique up to $\sim$" means existence, and $x \sim y$ for any two solutions $x$ and $y$.
2. "Up to isomorphism" uses the relation "is isomorphic to", written $\cong$.
3. "Modulo $n$" or "mod $n$" means up to congruence modulo $n$. "Modulo $H$" means up to the relation $g \sim g'$ when $g^{-1}g' \in H$.
4. "Unique up to unique isomorphism" means that any two solutions are isomorphic by exactly one isomorphism that respects the defining data.

## Remarks

### The relation must be stated

The phrase "up to" is incomplete without the relation.
"There is one group of order $3$" is true up to isomorphism, and false as a statement about sets with operations.
A reader must identify the relation from the context, and an author should name it.
The relation must be an equivalence relation, or "the same" has no coherent meaning.

### Uniqueness up to and definite descriptions

When an object is unique up to $\sim$, "the" object can mean one of two things.

1. The equivalence class: "the isomorphism class of groups of order $3$".
2. Any representative, with the promise that later statements do not depend on the choice.

The second reading is the common one, and it hides an obligation.
Each later statement must be invariant under $\sim$, as in [[well-defined|~a33d83e5]] operations on classes.
Statements about the order of a group are invariant under isomorphism.
Statements about the elements of a group as sets, such as "$1 \in G$", are not.
See also [[definite descriptions in definitions|~5d2f5192]].

### Examples

1. [[Prime factorization is unique up to order and sign|~1ec9005b]] for nonzero integers other than $\pm 1$.
2. [[Antiderivatives on an interval are unique up to a constant|~55810c4d]].
3. A [[cyclic group|0KVGH0J5B3]] of order $n$ is unique up to isomorphism: each is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, see [[classification of cyclic groups|0736AXH2ME]].
4. A [[basis|0KCQ9Y3QKA]] of a vector space is not unique, but its number of elements is unique. So the [[dimension|03GSZ8RTZJ]] is well defined.
5. A complex square root of a nonzero number is unique up to sign.
6. The [[universal property|EN4DD2FAJH]] of a product determines the product up to unique isomorphism.

### Non-examples

1. [[Groups of order 4 are not unique up to isomorphism|~e338db2e]]: $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and the Klein four-group differ.
2. A basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not unique up to order and scaling: $\{(1, 0), (0, 1)\}$ and $\{(1, 0), (1, 1)\}$ are bases, and $(1, 1)$ is not a multiple of $(0, 1)$.
3. The determinant does not determine a real $2 \times 2$ matrix up to [[similarity|0E89HQATM8]]. The matrices $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ have determinant $1$, but $PIP^{-1} = I$ for each invertible $P$, so only $I$ is similar to $I$.

### Basic properties

1. Uniqueness up to equality is the strongest case. A coarser relation gives a weaker uniqueness statement.
2. If $x$ is unique up to $\sim$ and $\sim$ is coarser than $\approx$, then $x$ is also unique up to $\approx$.
3. A function $f$ of the solutions gives a unique value exactly when $f$ is constant on the classes of $\sim$. For example, all cyclic groups of order $n$ have the same number of generators, because isomorphisms map generators to generators.
4. "Unique up to $\sim$" for the solutions of a property $P$ is equivalent to "$\{x : P(x)\}$ is one equivalence class of $\sim$".

### History

Gauss introduced congruence with respect to a modulus, and the sign $\equiv$, in his *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* of 1801.
The phrase "up to isomorphism" spread with the abstract algebra of the early twentieth century.
Category theory, from 1945 onward, made "unique up to unique isomorphism" the standard form of uniqueness for universal constructions.

### Common errors

1. A student states "the factorization is unique" and then treats $2 \cdot 3$ and $3 \cdot 2$ as different factorizations.
2. A student uses a property of a representative that is not invariant under the relation, such as "the" element $1$ of a group defined up to isomorphism.
3. A student calls a relation an "up to" relation without checking that it is an equivalence relation.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"Up to" tells us which differences we choose to ignore.
For example, we may say that there is only one way to split $12$ into prime factors, up to order.
The splittings $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3$ and $3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2$ use the same primes in a different order.
By saying "up to order", we agree to count them as the same splitting.
Then there really is exactly one splitting of $12$.

### L2 human

We prove that the solutions of $x^2 = 4$ in $\mathbb{R}$ are unique up to sign.

1. Existence: $2^2 = 4$.
2. Let $x^2 = 4$ and $y^2 = 4$. Then $x^2 - y^2 = 0$, so $(x - y)(x + y) = 0$.
3. In $\mathbb{R}$, a product is $0$ only when a factor is $0$. So $x = y$ or $x = -y$.
4. So any two solutions are equal up to sign.

The relation here is $x \sim y$ when $x = \pm y$, which is an equivalence relation.
The typical use: once the solutions are known up to sign, a sign condition such as $x \geq 0$ picks one of them.
A trap is to forget the relation: "the solution of $x^2 = 4$" is not unique.

### L3 human

Category theory gives the standard form of "up to": an object with a universal property is unique up to a unique isomorphism that respects the structure maps.
This is stronger than "unique up to isomorphism", because it gives canonical identifications between any two choices.
Statements that are invariant under isomorphism can then use "the" object.
Homotopy type theory goes further: by univalence, isomorphic structures are equal, and each statement in the theory is invariant under isomorphism.
In Lean and Mathlib, constructions such as quotients and limits come with an explicit choice and with lemmas that the choice is unique up to isomorphism.
