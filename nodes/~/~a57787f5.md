## Example

Let $\R^\R$ be the set of all maps $\R \to \R$, with pointwise addition and scalar multiplication.
It is a vector space, by the [[function space example|0GZB2SN0PH]].
Take [[composition|05YBKMRVAR]] as multiplication, and let $f(x) = x^2$ and $g = h = \id_\R$.

1. $(f \circ (g + h))(x) = f(2x) = 4x^2$.
2. $(f \circ g + f \circ h)(x) = x^2 + x^2 = 2x^2$.
3. At $x = 1$, the two values are $4$ and $2$. So $f \circ (g + h) \ne f \circ g + f \circ h$.
4. Left distributivity fails, so $\R^\R$ with composition is not a ring, and not an [[associative algebra|1TZSV19D85]].
5. Right distributivity holds: $((g + h) \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) + h(f(x))$ for all maps.
6. Homogeneity also fails: $(f \circ (2\id))(1) = 4$, but $(2 f)(1) = 2$.

## Remarks

- The failure in step 3 comes from $f$, which is not additive. Restricting to the linear maps $\R \to \R$ gives the algebra $\End(\R) \cong \R$.
- Composition is still associative, and $\id_\R$ is still a unit. So $\R^\R$ is a monoid under composition.
- The additive maps form a ring under composition, because left distributivity is exactly additivity of the left factor.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Squaring the double of a number gives four times its square, not twice.
So composing with the squaring map does not respect sums.
This breaks a rule that every algebra must satisfy.

### L2 human

The example shows where linearity enters the algebra $\End(V)$.
Right distributivity holds for every map, by the definition of the sum.
Left distributivity needs the left factor to be additive.
The trap is to assume that every space of maps with composition is a ring.
