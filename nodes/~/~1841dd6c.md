## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring.
For $n \ge 1$ and $A = (a_{ij}) \in M_n(K)$, consider four functions.

1. The axiomatic [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]] $\det A$: the unique function that is multilinear and alternating in the rows, with value $1$ on $I_n$.
2. The Leibniz sum $L(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \sgn(\sigma)\, a_{1 \sigma(1)} \cdots a_{n \sigma(n)}$.
3. The Laplace recursion: $D_1(a) = a$ and, for $n \ge 2$,
$$D_n(A) = \sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{1+j} a_{1j}\, D_{n-1}(A_{1j}),$$
where $A_{1j}$ is the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ matrix obtained from $A$ by deleting row $1$ and column $j$.
4. The column version: the unique function $\det'$ that is multilinear and alternating in the columns, with value $1$ on $I_n$.

Then $\det A = L(A) = D_n(A) = \det'(A)$ for all $n \ge 1$ and all $A \in M_n(K)$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Commutativity of $K$ is needed.
Over a noncommutative ring the order of the factors in each Leibniz term matters, and the row and column versions differ.
For example, over the quaternions, the "Leibniz sum" of $\begin{pmatrix} i & j \\ i & j \end{pmatrix}$ with factors taken in row order is $ij - ji = 2k \neq 0$, although the two rows are equal.

### Consequences

- A textbook can start from any of the three definitions; the theorems of this sub-area hold for all of them.
- The Laplace recursion shows that $\det A$ is a polynomial in the entries with integer coefficients, homogeneous of degree $n$.
- The row and column versions agree, so all statements about rows hold for columns.

### Converse

The statement is a list of equalities, so it has no separate converse.
A function that agrees with $\det$ for one size $n$ need not satisfy the Laplace recursion for other sizes; the recursion ties the sizes together.

### Generalizations

The recursion can expand along any row or column, by the [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]].
A fourth definition uses the action of an endomorphism on the top exterior power; it agrees with these by the [[existence and uniqueness theorem|~1e90e396]].

## History

Laplace gave the general expansion of a determinant in terms of its complementary minors in 1772.

## Explanations

### L1 human

There are three common recipes for the determinant.
One adds signed products over all ways to choose one entry from each row and column.
One expands along the first row into smaller determinants.
One describes the determinant by three rules.
The theorem says that the three recipes always give the same number.

### L2 human

We compare the recipes on $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. Leibniz: the identity gives $1 \cdot 4$, and the swap gives $-2 \cdot 3$. So $L(A) = -2$.
2. Laplace recursion: $D_2(A) = 1 \cdot D_1(4) - 2 \cdot D_1(3) = 4 - 6 = -2$.
3. Axioms: write row $2$ as $(3, 4) = 3(1, 2) + (0, -2)$. Multilinearity and the equal-row rule give $\det A = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$, and the triangular rule gives $-2$.

The typical use is to choose the most convenient definition for a proof: the axioms for structural results, Leibniz for transposes, the recursion for induction.
A trap: the recursion with the signs $(-1)^{1+j}$ omitted gives the permanent, not the determinant.

### L3 human

The three descriptions are three views of one object: a basis vector of $\operatorname{Alt}^n(K^n)$, its expansion in the basis of monomials, and its compatibility with the decomposition $K^n = K \oplus K^{n-1}$.
The agreement of the row and column versions reflects the fact that $\Lambda^n$ commutes with duality for free modules of finite rank.
