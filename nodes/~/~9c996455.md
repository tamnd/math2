## Example

Let $p$, $q$ and $r$ be propositional variables.
We show $\vdash (p \to q) \to ((q \to r) \to (p \to r))$ in the [[Hilbert system|1MGMQX43B8]] L.
This formula is the law of [[hypothetical syllogism|7MT8CPCEM4]], written as one implication.
Let $\Gamma = \set{p \to q, \; q \to r, \; p}$.

1. The list $p$, $p \to q$, $q$, $q \to r$, $r$ is a derivation from $\Gamma$. Lines 1, 2 and 4 are hypotheses, line 3 is [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] from lines 1 and 2, and line 5 is modus ponens from lines 3 and 4. So $\Gamma \vdash r$.
2. The [[deduction theorem|9X08XK5RV2]] with the hypothesis $p$ removed gives $\set{p \to q, \; q \to r} \vdash p \to r$.
3. The deduction theorem with $q \to r$ removed gives $\set{p \to q} \vdash (q \to r) \to (p \to r)$.
4. The deduction theorem with $p \to q$ removed gives $\vdash (p \to q) \to ((q \to r) \to (p \to r))$.

## Remarks

The example shows the standard pattern: assume the antecedents, derive the consequent with modus ponens, and remove the hypotheses with the deduction theorem, the last antecedent first.
The same steps work with formulas $\varphi$, $\psi$, $\chi$ in place of $p$, $q$, $r$, and they give $\vdash (\varphi \to \psi) \to ((\psi \to \chi) \to (\varphi \to \chi))$.

The deduction theorem only shortens the writing, and its proof turns each use into an explicit derivation.
For step 2, a derivation of $p \to r$ from $\set{p \to q, \; q \to r}$ without the deduction theorem is the following.

1. $q \to r$, a hypothesis.
2. $(q \to r) \to (p \to (q \to r))$, by A1 with $\varphi := q \to r$ and $\psi := p$.
3. $p \to (q \to r)$, by modus ponens from lines 1 and 2.
4. $(p \to (q \to r)) \to ((p \to q) \to (p \to r))$, by A2 with $\varphi := p$, $\psi := q$, $\chi := r$.
5. $(p \to q) \to (p \to r)$, by modus ponens from lines 3 and 4.
6. $p \to q$, a hypothesis.
7. $p \to r$, by modus ponens from lines 6 and 5.

If the order of the hypotheses removed in steps 3 and 4 is changed, the result is $\vdash (q \to r) \to ((p \to q) \to (p \to r))$, which is also a theorem of L.
The same law appears as an axiom in some other Hilbert systems, for example in the Bernays-Tarski system for the [[implicational propositional calculus|ZMVBAY7ZZD]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The law says: if $p$ gives $q$, and $q$ gives $r$, then $p$ gives $r$.
In ordinary words: if rain makes the street wet, and a wet street makes it slippery, then rain makes it slippery.
A proof in L of this law from the axioms alone is long.
With the deduction theorem we may assume the three parts, use modus ponens twice, and then turn the assumptions back into "if ... then" parts.

### L2 human

We use the same pattern for $\vdash (p \to (q \to r)) \to (q \to (p \to r))$, the exchange of antecedents.

1. Let $\Delta = \set{p \to (q \to r), \; q, \; p}$.
2. From $p$ and $p \to (q \to r)$, modus ponens gives $q \to r$.
3. From $q$ and $q \to r$, modus ponens gives $r$. So $\Delta \vdash r$.
4. Removing $p$, then $q$, then $p \to (q \to r)$ with the deduction theorem gives the theorem.

The typical use of this method is to prove the laws of implication in L in a few lines.
A trap is the order of removal: the hypothesis removed first becomes the innermost antecedent, so removing $p \to (q \to r)$ first gives a different formula.
