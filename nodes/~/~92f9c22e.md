## Example

We show that [[elementary function arithmetic|2JD80TE553]] proves
$$\forall a \, \exists p \le a^a + 1 \, (a < p \land \mathrm{Prime}(p)),$$
where $\mathrm{Prime}(p)$ is the $\Delta_0$ formula $1 < p \land \forall d \le p \, (d \mid p \to d = 1 \lor d = p)$ and $d \mid n$ is $\exists q \le n \, (d \cdot q = n)$.
This is a formal version of [[Euclid's theorem|0292EPPPT0]] on [[prime numbers|01826KMG2H]].
We use the formulation with the symbol $x^y$ of the [[definition|~16cc5c8f]], and we work inside $\mathsf{EFA}$.

1. Let $\varphi(a)$ be $\exists m \le a^a \, (1 \le m \land \forall k \le a \, (1 \le k \to k \mid m))$. Its quantifiers are bounded, so $\varphi$ is $\Delta_0(\exp)$.
2. Base case. $0^0 = 1$ by the first exponentiation axiom, and $m = 1$ witnesses $\varphi(0)$, because no $k$ with $1 \le k \le 0$ exists.
3. Step. Suppose $m \le a^a$ witnesses $\varphi(a)$, and let $m' = m \cdot (a + 1)$. Every $k \le a + 1$ with $k \ge 1$ divides $m'$.
4. The bound. $\mathsf{EFA}$ proves $a^a \le (a + 1)^a$, by $\Delta_0(\exp)$ induction on the exponent. So $m' \le (a + 1)^a \cdot (a + 1) = (a + 1)^{a + 1}$, and $m'$ witnesses $\varphi(a + 1)$.
5. Induction for $\varphi$ gives $\forall a \, \varphi(a)$.
6. Fix $a \ge 1$, take $m$ from step 5, and let $N = m + 1$. Then $2 \le N \le a^a + 1$.
7. The least number principle for $\Delta_0$ formulas holds in $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$. It gives a least $p$ with $1 < p$ and $p \mid N$, since $N$ itself is such a number.
8. $p$ is prime: a divisor $d$ of $p$ with $1 < d < p$ would divide $N$ and contradict the minimality of $p$.
9. $p > a$: if $p \le a$, then $p \mid m$ by step 1 and $p \mid m + 1$, so $p \mid 1$, which is false for $p > 1$.
10. For $a = 0$ the prime $p = 2$ satisfies $0 < 2 \le 0^0 + 1$.

So $\mathsf{EFA}$ proves that above every number there is a prime, with an explicit bound.

## Remarks

The number $m$ of step 5 plays the role of $a!$ or of the least common multiple of $1, \ldots, a$; the proof only needs a common multiple below $a^a$.
The proof uses $\mathsf{exp}$ only to bound $m$ in step 1; without the bound $a^a$, the formula $\varphi$ would not be $\Delta_0$.
Whether $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$, without $\mathsf{exp}$, proves that there are infinitely many primes is a long-standing open problem.
The obstacle is the same: $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$ cannot show that a common multiple of $1, \ldots, a$ exists, since such numbers grow exponentially in $a$.
The usual informal proof with the [[factorial|0XWG0JGDFW]] $a!$ also works in $\mathsf{EFA}$, because $a! \le a^a$ and the factorial has a $\Delta_0(\exp)$ graph.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many primes multiplies the numbers $1, 2, \ldots, a$ and adds $1$.
For $a = 4$ this gives $1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 + 1 = 25$.
Any prime that divides this number, here $5$, is larger than $a$.
The proof uses a number of size about $a^a$, so it needs powers to exist, and elementary function arithmetic has them.

### L2 human

We follow the proof for $a = 3$ in $\mathfrak{N}$.

1. For $a = 0, 1, 2, 3$, steps 2 and 3 give $m = 1, 1, 2, 6$ in turn.
2. Each value is within its bound: $1 \le 1$, $1 \le 1$, $2 \le 4$ and $6 \le 27$.
3. $N = 7$, and its least divisor $p > 1$ is $7$.
4. $7$ is prime and $7 > 3$, and $7 \le 3^3 + 1 = 28$.

The typical use is as a test of how much of elementary number theory a weak theory carries.
A trap is to take the least common multiple without a bound: the formula "some $m$ is divisible by all $k \le a$" is $\Sigma_1$, not $\Delta_0$, and $\mathsf{EFA}$ has no induction for it.
