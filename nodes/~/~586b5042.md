## Definition

Let $F$ be a finite set of [[clauses|T3SXVH3ZZC]], a [[CNF|0B3D4C8YF2]].
A *resolution derivation* of a clause $C$ from $F$ is a sequence of clauses $\pi = (C_1, \dots, C_s)$ with $C_s = C$.
Each $C_i$ is a clause of $F$ (an *axiom*), or a resolvent of two earlier clauses $C_j, C_k$ with $j, k < i$, by the [[resolution rule|ZDW6N1PGJ7]]
$$\frac{A \cup \set{x} \qquad B \cup \set{\neg x}}{A \cup B}.$$
The variable $x$ is the *pivot* of the step.
A *resolution refutation* of $F$ is a derivation of the empty clause $\square$ from $F$.

The *graph* of $\pi$ has the indices $1, \dots, s$ as nodes, and an edge from $j$ to $i$ when $C_j$ is a premise of the step that gives $C_i$.

1. $\pi$ is *tree-like* if every derived clause $C_i$, other than $C_s$, is a premise of at most one step. Axioms may occur several times in the sequence.
2. $\pi$ is *dag-like*, or *general*, without this condition.
3. $\pi$ is *regular* if on every path of the graph from $C_s$ back to an axiom, each variable is the pivot of at most one step.

The measures of a refutation are these.

- The *size* (or *length*) $S(\pi) = s$ is the number of clauses.
- The *width* of a clause is its number of literals, $w(C) = \abs{C}$. The width of $\pi$ is $w(\pi) = \max_i w(C_i)$, and $w(F)$ is the largest width of a clause of $F$.
- $S(F \vdash \square)$, $S_T(F \vdash \square)$ and $w(F \vdash \square)$ are the least size of a refutation, the least size of a tree-like refutation, and the least width of a refutation of $F$.

Resolution is a refutation system in the sense of a [[propositional proof system|~8ae21f34]].
A string is a proof of $F$ if it is a refutation of $F$; checking this takes polynomial time.
Soundness and completeness are the [[soundness and completeness of resolution|DH1P95R390]].

## Notation

Many writers write $F \vdash_{\mathrm{Res}} \square$, and $\mathrm{Res}$, $\mathrm{Res}_T$ and $\mathrm{RegRes}$ for general, tree-like and regular resolution.
Some writers measure size by the number of literals, which is at most $2n$ times the number of clauses for $n$ variables.
Some writers allow the *weakening* rule "from $C$ infer $C \cup D$"; it can be removed without increasing size or width, so the measures do not change.
On the pages of this topic, size of a resolution refutation means the number of clauses.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The measures of a small refutation|~5d9a0e62]]: a refutation of four clauses with size $7$ and width $2$, which is tree-like and regular.
- [[A tree-like refutation from a DPLL run|~765fb3a9]]: every unsatisfiable run of the DPLL algorithm gives a tree-like refutation of at most the same size.
- A run of the Davis-Putnam procedure, as in [[this example|20PVEKMM24]], resolves out one variable at a time. The refutation it gives is regular.

### Non-examples

- [[An extended resolution refutation|~fa3abe36]] uses clauses that define new variables. They are neither axioms nor resolvents, so it is not a resolution refutation.
- [[Resolving two variables at once|C7ZTS8N0VB]] is not a resolution step, and it is unsound.

### Basic properties

- *Hierarchy.* Every tree-like refutation is dag-like. A tree-like refutation of least size is regular. Exponential separations are known in both steps: Bonet, Esteban, Galesi and Johannsen separated tree-like from regular resolution in 2000, and Alekhnovich, Johannsen, Pitassi and Urquhart separated regular from general resolution in 2002.
- *Restrictions.* A refutation of $F$ and a partial assignment $\rho$ give a refutation of $F|_\rho$ that is no larger; see the [[restriction lemma|~15e29404]].
- *DPLL.* An unsatisfiable run of the DPLL algorithm with $t$ nodes in its search tree gives a tree-like refutation with at most $t$ clauses. Conversely, a tree-like refutation gives a search tree of the same size that splits on the pivots. So the running time of DPLL on unsatisfiable formulas is bounded below by $S_T(F \vdash \square)$.
- *CDCL.* Each clause learned by a conflict-driven clause-learning solver is derivable by resolution from the current clauses, so an unsatisfiable run gives a resolution refutation of size polynomial in the run. Conversely, CDCL with restarts p-simulates general resolution: Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche, and Atserias, Fichte and Thurley, proved this in 2011.
- *Lower bounds.* Exponential lower bounds on $S(F \vdash \square)$ are known for the [[pigeonhole formulas|~7468ccc3]] ([[Haken's theorem|~dd16430c]]), the [[Tseitin formulas|~4386fc47]] on expanders, and random $3$-CNFs with $\Delta n$ clauses for a large constant $\Delta$. The [[size-width relation|~d2afe9c2]] reduces all three to width bounds.
- *Proof search.* Resolution is not automatable unless $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$; see [[automatizability|~64ace3b8]].
- *Interpolation.* Resolution has feasible interpolation, even by monotone circuits; see [[feasible interpolation|~afa24e9d]].

### History

Davis and Putnam used resolution on clauses in 1960, and Robinson made resolution the basis of automated theorem proving in 1965.
Tseitin proved the first lower bounds, for regular resolution, in 1968.
Haken proved the first exponential lower bound for general resolution in 1985.

### Common errors

- Size in clauses and size in literals differ. The difference is a factor of at most $2n$, so it does not matter for exponential bounds.
- A lower bound for resolution says nothing about stronger systems. The pigeonhole formulas are hard for resolution and easy for [[cutting planes|~f9bde1dc]].
- In a tree-like refutation, axioms may be repeated, but a derived clause used twice must be derived twice.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Resolution is the proof system behind modern SAT solvers.
It shows that a list of clauses has no solution by combining two clauses into a new one, step by step, until it reaches the empty clause.
Proof complexity asks how many steps such a proof needs.
For some families of lists, the least number of steps grows exponentially with the length of the list.

### L2 human

We compare dag-like and tree-like refutations of $F = \set{\set{a}, \set{\neg a, b}, \set{\neg b, c}, \set{\neg b, \neg c}}$.

1. Dag-like: $\set{a}$, $\set{\neg a, b}$, $\set{b}$, $\set{\neg b, c}$, $\set{c}$, $\set{\neg b, \neg c}$, $\set{\neg c}$, $\square$. Here $\set{b}$ is a premise of the steps for $\set{c}$ and $\set{\neg c}$. So the refutation has size $8$, and it is not tree-like.
2. Tree-like: derive $\set{b}$ twice. This adds the three clauses $\set{a}$, $\set{\neg a, b}$, $\set{b}$ once more, so the size is $11$.
3. Both refutations have width $2$, and both are regular, since each path resolves $c$, then $b$, then $a$.

The typical use of the definition is to fix the refinement and the measure, and then prove a lower bound for one family of formulas.
A trap: a derived clause that is used twice must be counted twice in the tree-like size.

### L3 human

Resolution is the weakest of the standard proof systems, and the one whose lower bounds are best understood.
Its lines are clauses, so it is the lowest level of [[bounded-depth Frege|~2cc592a7]], in the form of the [[sequent calculus with atomic cuts|~db40454c]].
Lower bounds for it are often proved with games on partial assignments, such as the Prover-Delayer game of Pudlák and Impagliazzo for tree-like resolution.
