## Example

For an integer $n$, let $p$ be "$6$ [[divides|096PW7Q014]] $n$", and let $q$ be "$3$ divides $n$".
We consider the implication $p \to q$ for every integer $n$, and we check the four related implications.

1. The implication $p \to q$ is true. If $n = 6k$ for an integer $k$, then $n = 3(2k)$, so $3$ divides $n$.
2. The [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] $q \to p$ reads "if $3$ divides $n$, then $6$ divides $n$". It is false: for $n = 3$, the antecedent is true, and $6$ does not divide $3$.
3. The inverse $\neg p \to \neg q$ reads "if $6$ does not divide $n$, then $3$ does not divide $n$". It is false: for $n = 3$, the antecedent is true, and $3$ divides $3$.
4. The [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] $\neg q \to \neg p$ reads "if $3$ does not divide $n$, then $6$ does not divide $n$". It is true by step 1 and the law of the contrapositive.
5. The single value $n = 3$ refutes both the converse and the inverse, as their logical equivalence predicts.

## Remarks

The example shows the typical pattern: a true implication, a true contrapositive, and a false converse and inverse.
Each counterexample to the converse is a number divisible by $3$ and not by $6$, that is, an odd multiple of $3$ such as $3$, $9$ or $15$.
If we change $6$ to $3$ in $p$, then all four implications become true, because $p$ and $q$ become the same statement.
If we add the hypothesis "$n$ is even" to the converse, it becomes true: an even multiple of $3$ is a multiple of $6$, because $2$ and $3$ are coprime.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A number that splits into $6$ equal whole parts also splits into $3$ equal whole parts.
The reverse is not true: $3$ splits into $3$ parts of size $1$, but not into $6$ whole parts.
So the sentence is true, and the sentence with the parts exchanged is false.

### L2 human

We test the converse on a list of numbers before we try to prove it.

1. The converse is "if $3$ divides $n$, then $6$ divides $n$".
2. Take the multiples of $3$ up to $15$: $3, 6, 9, 12, 15$.
3. Among them, $6$ and $12$ are divisible by $6$, and $3$, $9$ and $15$ are not.
4. So $n = 3$ is a counterexample, and the converse is false.

The typical use is to decide quickly whether a converse is worth trying to prove.
A trap: a check of $n = 6$ and $n = 12$ alone would make the converse look true.
