## Statement

Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $V^*$ be its [[dual space|0M856Q1AMZ]].
For a [[bilinear form|0P799D6AMN]] $B$ on $V$ define $L_B, R_B \colon V \to V^*$ by $L_B(u) = B(u, \cdot)$ and $R_B(v) = B(\cdot, v)$.

1. $L_B$ and $R_B$ are linear maps, and $B \mapsto L_B$ is a linear bijection from the bilinear forms on $V$ onto $\Hom(V, V^*)$.

Now let $V$ have a basis $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ with [[dual basis|0W6M3PSD08]] $(e_1^*, \dots, e_n^*)$, and let $A$ be the [[matrix|0P24YW565F]] of $B$.

2. If $x$ and $y$ are the coordinate vectors of $u$ and $v$, then $B(u, v) = x^T A y$.
3. The map $B \mapsto A$ is a linear bijection from the bilinear forms on $V$ onto $M_n(F)$.
4. In the bases $(e_i)$ and $(e_i^*)$, the [[matrix|0YYMY95FWS]] of $L_B$ is $A^T$ and the matrix of $R_B$ is $A$.

The [[proof|~d8bf4ec2]] evaluates both sides on basis vectors.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Part 1 needs no finite dimension. Parts 2 to 4 need a finite basis, because a matrix has finitely many rows.
- Part 4 needs the dual basis. In another basis of $V^*$ the matrix of $L_B$ is a different matrix.
- Bilinearity is needed: for the [[map $x_1 y_1 + x_2$|~af4ab818]], the value $B(u, \cdot)$ is not linear, so $L_B(u)$ is not in $V^*$.

### Consequences

- If $\dim V = n$, the bilinear forms on $V$ form a space of dimension $n^2$.
- $B$ is [[symmetric|0AXZNE5XNH]] exactly when $L_B = R_B$, and exactly when $A = A^T$.
- The maps $L_B$ and $R_B$ have the same rank, because $A$ and $A^T$ have the same [[rank|0ZC9YTXZRY]]. This common number is the rank of $B$.
- The [[nondegeneracy criteria|~acb831c2]] follow from part 4.

### Converse

Each linear map $T \colon V \to V^*$ comes from exactly one form, namely $B(u, v) = T(u)(v)$.
Each matrix $A \in M_n(F)$ comes from exactly one form, namely $B(u, v) = x^T A y$.

### Generalizations

For two spaces $V$ and $W$, the bilinear maps $V \times W \to F$ correspond to $\Hom(V, W^*)$ and to $\Hom(W, V^*)$.
If $\dim V = m$ and $\dim W = n$, their matrices are the $m \times n$ matrices.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A bilinear form on the plane is the same thing as a table of four numbers.
It is also the same thing as a rule that turns each vector into a linear function.
The vector $u$ goes to the function "pair with $u$".
All three descriptions carry the same information.

### L2 human

We take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ on $\Q^2$ and find $L_B$.

1. $L_B(e_1)(y) = B(e_1, y) = y_1 + 2y_2$, so $L_B(e_1) = e_1^* + 2e_2^*$.
2. $L_B(e_2)(y) = B(e_2, y) = 3y_2$, so $L_B(e_2) = 3e_2^*$.
3. The columns of the matrix of $L_B$ are $(1, 2)$ and $(0, 3)$, so the matrix is $A^T$.

The typical use is the transfer of questions about forms to questions about linear maps, such as kernels and ranks.
A trap: many books define the map $V \to V^*$ with the other slot, and then the matrix is $A$ instead of $A^T$.

### L3 human

Part 1 is the adjunction $\Hom(V \otimes V, F) \cong \Hom(V, \Hom(V, F))$ of the tensor product.
For finite-dimensional $V$ it gives $\operatorname{Bil}(V) \cong V^* \otimes V^*$.
The change of basis rule $P^T A P$ is the law of a map $V \to V^*$, and it differs from the law $P^{-1} A P$ of a map $V \to V$.
The same view works for modules over a commutative ring, where $L_B$ need not be bijective even when $\det A \neq 0$.
