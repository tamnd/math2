## Example

Let $S_3$ be the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] on $\{1, 2, 3\}$, with $6$ elements.
We find its [[lattice of subgroups|0B8BW4YWTF]] and check that it is modular and not distributive.

1. Orders: by [[Lagrange's theorem|3C53TD]], the order of a subgroup divides $6$, so it is $1$, $2$, $3$ or $6$.
2. Prime order: a subgroup $P$ of prime order $p$ is generated by each of its elements $x \neq e$, because the order of $x$ divides $p$ and is not $1$.
3. Order $2$: the elements of order $2$ are the transpositions $(1\,2)$, $(1\,3)$ and $(2\,3)$. By step 2, they give three subgroups $\langle (1\,2) \rangle$, $\langle (1\,3) \rangle$ and $\langle (2\,3) \rangle$, each with $2$ elements.
4. Order $3$: the elements of order $3$ are $(1\,2\,3)$ and $(1\,3\,2) = (1\,2\,3)^2$. By step 2, they give one subgroup $A_3 = \{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$.
5. So $S_3$ has exactly $6$ subgroups: $\{e\}$, the three subgroups of order $2$, $A_3$, and $S_3$.
6. Covering: a subgroup that lies strictly between $\{e\}$ and a subgroup $P$ of prime order would have order strictly between $1$ and $p$ and dividing $p$, which is impossible. A subgroup strictly between $P$ and $S_3$ would have order divisible by $p$, dividing $6$, and strictly between $p$ and $6$, which is also impossible.
7. So the Hasse diagram has $8$ lines: $\{e\} \prec P$ and $P \prec S_3$ for each of the four subgroups $P$ of prime order.
8. Meets and joins: two different subgroups $P$ and $Q$ of prime order have $P \cap Q = \{e\}$, because $P \cap Q$ is a subgroup of $P$ other than $P$. Their join contains $P$ strictly, so by step 6 it is $S_3$.
9. Modular: the modular law asks that $x \vee (y \wedge z) = (x \vee y) \wedge z$ when $x \leq z$. If $x = z$, both sides are $x$. If $x = \{e\}$, both sides are $y \wedge z$. If $z = S_3$, both sides are $x \vee y$. In this lattice, each pair $x < z$ has $x = \{e\}$ or $z = S_3$, so the law holds.
10. Not distributive: let $x = \langle (1\,2) \rangle$, $y = \langle (1\,3) \rangle$ and $w = \langle (2\,3) \rangle$. Then $x \wedge (y \vee w) = x \wedge S_3 = x$. But $(x \wedge y) \vee (x \wedge w) = \{e\} \vee \{e\} = \{e\} \neq x$.

## Remarks

The lattice has one bottom element, one top element, and four elements in the middle that are not comparable.
A lattice of this shape is often called $M_4$.

The example shows that a group can have a [[modular|QX7WZ92QAK]] lattice of subgroups although not all of its subgroups are normal.
The subgroup $\langle (1\,2) \rangle$ is not normal: $(1\,3)(1\,2)(1\,3) = (2\,3)$.
The normal subgroups of $S_3$ are $\{e\}$, $A_3$ and $S_3$, and they form a chain.

The lattice is not [[distributive|0HM0HQCK6N]], in agreement with Ore's theorem.
By that theorem, a group with a distributive lattice of subgroups is locally cyclic, and $S_3$ is finitely generated and not cyclic.

For $n \geq 4$, the lattice of subgroups of $S_n$ is not modular.
The group $S_n$ contains a copy of the dihedral group $D_4$, whose [[lattice of subgroups is not modular|~bd61a465]].
The subgroups of $D_4$ form an interval in the lattice of $S_n$, so the failure of the modular law in $D_4$ is also a failure in $S_n$.
For $n = 4$, the group $S_4$ has $30$ subgroups.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The group $S_3$ is the group of the six ways to arrange three cards.
Its subgroups are the "do nothing" group, three groups that each swap one pair of cards, one group of the three cyclic shifts, and the whole group.
In the picture, "do nothing" is at the bottom and the whole group is at the top.
The four middle groups sit in one row between them.
Any two different middle groups meet only in "do nothing", and together they give the whole group.

### L2 human

We check step 8 for $P = \langle (1\,2) \rangle$ and $Q = A_3$.

1. $P \cap Q = \{e\}$, because $(1\,2)$ is not a power of $(1\,2\,3)$.
2. The join $P \vee Q$ contains $Q$, so its order is a multiple of $3$. It also contains $P$, so its order is even.
3. So the order of $P \vee Q$ is a multiple of $6$, and $P \vee Q = S_3$.

The typical use of this example is the first non-abelian case of a lattice of subgroups that is not a chain and not a product of chains.
It shows that a lattice can be modular without being distributive.

A trap: the four middle subgroups are not all of the same order.
Three have order $2$ and one has order $3$, but in the Hasse diagram all four sit directly between $\{e\}$ and $S_3$.
