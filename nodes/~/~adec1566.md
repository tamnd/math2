## Example

Work in the first-order language with a binary function symbol $\cdot$ (written by juxtaposition) and a constant $e$.
Let $T$ be the [[theory|92SWXST1ZM]] with the following four sentences.

- G1. $\forall x \, \forall y \, \forall z \; ((x y) z = x (y z))$.
- G2. $\forall x \; (x e = x \land e x = x)$.
- G3. $\forall x \, \exists y \; (x y = e \land y x = e)$.
- N. $\exists x \, \exists y \; (x y \neq y x)$.

G1 to G3 are the axioms of a [[group|G5S1M9]], and N says that the group is not commutative.
We show that $T$ is [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]] by a [[model|MVCH6N9SNW]].

1. Take the [[symmetric group|0RJMNEN3BJ]] $S_3$ of all permutations of $\set{1, 2, 3}$. Interpret $x y$ as the composition $x \circ y$ (first $y$, then $x$), and $e$ as the identity permutation.
2. G1 holds, because composition of functions is associative: both sides send $k$ to $x(y(z(k)))$.
3. G2 holds, because composing with the identity changes nothing.
4. G3 holds, because each permutation is a bijection and its inverse function is again a permutation of $\set{1, 2, 3}$.
5. For N, take $x = (1\,2)$ and $y = (1\,3)$, the transpositions.
6. Compute $x y$ at $1$: $y$ sends $1$ to $3$, and $x$ sends $3$ to $3$. So $(x y)(1) = 3$.
7. Compute $y x$ at $1$: $x$ sends $1$ to $2$, and $y$ sends $2$ to $2$. So $(y x)(1) = 2$.
8. Since $3 \neq 2$, $x y \neq y x$, and N holds in $S_3$.
9. So $S_3$ is a model of $T$, and $T$ is satisfiable.
10. By [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]] of first-order logic, every sentence derivable from $T$ is true in $S_3$. A sentence and its negation cannot both be true in $S_3$, so $T$ is consistent.

## Remarks

The example shows that commutativity does not follow from the group axioms.
Indeed, $T$ is the theory $\set{G1, G2, G3}$ plus the negation of $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x y = y x)$, and a consistent extension by a negation means exactly that the statement is not derivable.
If N is replaced by its negation, the theory is again consistent, with the model $(\Z, +, 0)$.
So the group axioms neither derive commutativity nor its negation; in the sense of a theory that decides each sentence, the theory of groups is not complete.
A smaller model does not exist: every group with at most $5$ elements is commutative, so $S_3$, with $6$ elements, is a smallest model of $T$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The group rules say how to combine moves, such as shuffles of three cards.
We want to know whether the rules allow two moves whose order matters.
Swap the first two cards, or swap the first and the third card.
Doing these two swaps in one order puts a different card on top than doing them in the other order.
So a world that obeys all the rules and has such moves exists, and the rules with "order matters for some pair" cannot contradict each other.

### L2 human

We repeat the check of step 8 with the full permutations.

1. $x = (1\,2)$ sends $1 \mapsto 2$, $2 \mapsto 1$, $3 \mapsto 3$, and $y = (1\,3)$ sends $1 \mapsto 3$, $3 \mapsto 1$, $2 \mapsto 2$.
2. $x y$ sends $1 \mapsto x(3) = 3$, $2 \mapsto x(2) = 1$, $3 \mapsto x(1) = 2$. So $x y = (1\,3\,2)$.
3. $y x$ sends $1 \mapsto y(2) = 2$, $2 \mapsto y(1) = 3$, $3 \mapsto y(3) = 1$. So $y x = (1\,2\,3)$.
4. The two 3-cycles differ, so $x y \neq y x$.

The typical use of a model is to prove consistency and unprovability at the same time.
A trap is to check the axioms in a model that is not a group, for example the set of all functions from $\set{1,2,3}$ to itself; G3 fails there, because a constant function has no inverse.
