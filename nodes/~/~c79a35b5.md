## Statement

Let $T$ be a [[first-order theory|92SWXST1ZM]] in the language $\set{0, S, +, \cdot}$ that contains [[Peano arithmetic|MPSCREYC3M]] $\mathsf{PA}$.
Let the axioms of $T$ be given by a $\Sigma_1$ formula that defines a decidable set in $\N$.
Let $\mathrm{Prov}_T$ be the standard [[provability predicate|NB2BCZR40F]] built from it, and let $\mathrm{Con}(T) := \neg \mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner 0 = S0 \urcorner)$ be the [[consistency statement|~3f19fcb2]] of $T$.

If $T$ is [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]], then $T \nvdash \mathrm{Con}(T)$.
In addition, $T \vdash \mathrm{Con}(T) \leftrightarrow G_T$, where $G_T$ is the Gödel sentence of [[the first incompleteness theorem|X9WYNYE29C]].

In short: a consistent theory of this kind cannot prove its own consistency.
The proof is in [[the proof of the second incompleteness theorem|~f0ce5a68]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. Consistency: an inconsistent theory proves every sentence, including $\mathrm{Con}(T)$.
2. The standard predicate: for the [[Rosser provability predicate|~55c1b89b]], $T$ proves the Rosser consistency statement. So $\mathrm{Con}(T)$ must be built from a predicate that satisfies the [[derivability conditions|~abdd9e6a]].
3. A $\Sigma_1$ axiom formula: Feferman built a $\Pi_1$ formula $\alpha^*$ that defines the axioms of $\mathsf{PA}$ in $\N$, with $\mathsf{PA} \vdash \mathrm{Con}_{\alpha^*}$. So the theorem is about a formula, not only about a set of axioms.
4. Enough arithmetic: the proof needs D2 and D3 provable in $T$. These hold for extensions of $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$, so "contains $\mathsf{PA}$" can be weakened to "contains $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$".
5. Computable axioms: true arithmetic $\mathrm{Th}(\N)$ is consistent, but its axioms have no $\Sigma_1$ definition, so $\mathrm{Con}(T)$ is not defined as above.

### Consequences

1. If $T$ is consistent, then $\mathrm{Con}(T)$ is a true $\Pi_1$ sentence that $T$ does not prove.
2. The theory $T + \neg \mathrm{Con}(T)$ is consistent; [[for PA it is ω-inconsistent|~0977deea]].
3. Consistency proofs need stronger means: $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ proves $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, but it is [[not conservative over PA|EQH7XTZ29V]].
4. [[Hilbert's program|P1RW6JWX73]] asked for a finitary consistency proof of arithmetic. If finitary reasoning is formalized inside $\mathsf{PA}$, the theorem rules out such a proof of $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$.
5. [[Gentzen's consistency proof|QPV0HNP22S]] proves $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ in primitive recursive arithmetic with induction up to the ordinal $\varepsilon_0$, which $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove.
6. If $\mathsf{ZFC}$ is consistent, it does not prove $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{ZFC})$, and so it does not prove that a set model of $\mathsf{ZFC}$ exists.

### Converse

The converse holds and is immediate: if $T \nvdash \mathrm{Con}(T)$, then $T$ is consistent, since an inconsistent theory proves every sentence.
So for these theories, $T$ is consistent exactly when $T \nvdash \mathrm{Con}(T)$.
A different converse fails: $T \vdash \neg \mathrm{Con}(T)$ does not imply that $T$ is inconsistent, as $\mathsf{PA} + \neg \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ shows.

### Generalizations

1. The theorem holds for consistent theories in other languages that interpret $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ with a $\Sigma_1$ axiom formula, such as $\mathsf{ZFC}$.
2. [[Löb's theorem|3HD30YG3DG]] generalizes it: if $T \vdash \mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner \varphi \urcorner) \to \varphi$, then $T \vdash \varphi$. The case $\varphi := (0 = S0)$ is the second theorem.
3. The formalized version: $T \vdash \mathrm{Con}(T) \to \neg \mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner \mathrm{Con}(T) \urcorner)$.

## History

Gödel stated the second theorem in his 1931 paper, as a corollary of the first, and gave only an outline of its proof.
The first full published proof appeared in 1939 in the second volume of Hilbert and Bernays, Grundlagen der Mathematik.
Löb isolated the derivability conditions in their present form in 1955.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose a list of axioms for arithmetic, checkable by a computer, never proves a contradiction.
Then the axioms cannot prove the statement "these axioms never prove a contradiction".
To trust the axioms, one needs reasons from outside them.

### L2 human

1. Let $G$ say "$G$ is not provable"; part 1 of the first theorem gives "if $T$ is consistent, then $T \nvdash G$".
2. The proof of this implication uses only reasoning that $T$ can carry out, by the derivability conditions.
3. So $T \vdash \mathrm{Con}(T) \to G$.
4. If $T \vdash \mathrm{Con}(T)$, then $T \vdash G$, which part 1 forbids.

The typical use is to show that a consistency statement, or a statement that implies it, is independent of $T$.
A trap: the theorem does not say that $\mathrm{Con}(T)$ is doubtful; $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ is true and provable in $\mathsf{ZFC}$.

### L3 human

In [[provability logic|WJK0YA4GP2]] the theorem is the formula $\neg \square \bot \to \neg \square \neg \square \bot$, a theorem of $\mathsf{GL}$.
It depends on the choice of the formula that defines the axioms, unlike the first theorem, which is about the set of theorems.
It is the base of the ordering of theories by consistency strength, and of ordinal analysis, which started with [[Gentzen's consistency proof|QPV0HNP22S]].
