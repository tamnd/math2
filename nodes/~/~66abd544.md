## Example

Let $\mathbb{N}_{>0} = \{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$ be the positive [[natural numbers|0MWKJ44V5A]].
The rule $a \ast b = a^b$ is a [[binary operation|0M418FE7BB]] on $\mathbb{N}_{>0}$, because a positive integer to a positive integer power is a positive integer.
We show that it is not [[associative|0K8F7PVDMX]].

1. Take $a = 2$, $b = 3$, $c = 2$.
2. $(2 \ast 3) \ast 2 = (2^3)^2 = 8^2 = 64$.
3. $2 \ast (3 \ast 2) = 2^{(3^2)} = 2^9 = 512$.
4. Since $64 \neq 512$, the law fails for this triple, so exponentiation is not associative.

## Remarks

In general, $(a^b)^c = a^{bc}$, while $a^{(b^c)}$ has the exponent $b^c$.
The two exponents $bc$ and $b^c$ are different for most $b$ and $c$, for example $3 \cdot 2 = 6$ and $3^2 = 9$.
The law holds for some triples, for example when $a = 1$, or when $b = c = 2$, because $2 \cdot 2 = 2^2$.
The convention for a tower of powers is to group from the top: $a^{b^c}$ means $a^{(b^c)}$.
The reason is that $(a^b)^c$ can be written $a^{bc}$, so it needs no special notation.
Exponentiation is not [[commutative|099E72KTF8]] either: $2^3 = 8$, but $3^2 = 9$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Compute $2$ to the power $3$, and then square the result: $8 \cdot 8 = 64$.
Now first compute $3$ squared, which is $9$, and compute $2$ to the power $9$: $512$.
The answers are different, so the brackets matter for powers.

### L2 human

To show that the law fails, choose a triple and compute both groupings.
Here the rule $(a^b)^c = a^{bc}$ predicts the failure: the two groupings have the exponents $bc$ and $b^c$.
A trap is to test with $b = c = 2$, where $bc = b^c = 4$ and the two groupings agree.
The typical use of this non-example is to show why the reader must know the convention for $2^{3^2}$, which is $2^9 = 512$.
