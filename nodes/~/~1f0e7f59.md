## Example

[[Fermat's little theorem|0M25PRN3WQ]] says: if $p$ is prime and $a$ is an integer not divisible by $p$, then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$.
[[Euler's theorem|0VC4DVE7DQ]] says: if $n \geq 1$ and $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, then $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$, where $\varphi$ is [[Euler's totient function|0DAMHCT17R]].
We check that Euler's theorem is [[stronger|~f7d8f0b6]] than Fermat's theorem.

1. Suppose Euler's theorem, and let $p$ be prime and $a$ an integer not divisible by $p$.
2. The only positive divisors of $p$ are $1$ and $p$, and $p$ does not divide $a$. So $\gcd(a, p) = 1$.
3. Each of the numbers $1, 2, \ldots, p - 1$ is coprime to $p$, and $p$ is not. So $\varphi(p) = p - 1$.
4. Euler's theorem with $n = p$ gives $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$, which is Fermat's theorem.
5. Euler's theorem covers cases that Fermat's theorem does not cover. For $n = 8$, the numbers coprime to $8$ in $\{1, \ldots, 8\}$ are $1, 3, 5, 7$, so $\varphi(8) = 4$.
6. With $a = 3$, Euler's theorem gives $3^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$. We check: $3^4 = 81 = 10 \cdot 8 + 1$. Fermat's theorem says nothing here, because $8$ is not prime.

## Remarks

Steps 1 to 4 show that Euler's theorem implies Fermat's theorem by a short argument.
Step 5 shows that Euler's theorem has more cases, so it is stronger in the informal sense used for proved theorems.
As statements, both theorems are true, so each one formally implies the other; the comparison is about which one gives the other directly.
[[Lagrange's theorem|3C53TD]] on groups is stronger still: applied to the group of units modulo $n$, which has $\varphi(n)$ elements, it gives Euler's theorem.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Fermat's theorem is a rule for remainders when we divide by a prime, such as $7$.
Euler's theorem is a rule of the same kind for division by any number, such as $8$ or $10$.
When the number is prime, Euler's rule becomes Fermat's rule, so Euler's rule includes Fermat's rule and says more.

### L2 human

We check both theorems on small cases.

1. Fermat with $p = 5$ and $a = 2$: $2^4 = 16 = 3 \cdot 5 + 1$, so $2^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$.
2. Euler with $n = 10$ and $a = 3$: the numbers coprime to $10$ in $\{1, \ldots, 10\}$ are $1, 3, 7, 9$, so $\varphi(10) = 4$. Then $3^4 = 81 = 8 \cdot 10 + 1$, so $3^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{10}$.
3. The second case is not a case of Fermat's theorem, because $10$ is not prime.

The typical use of Euler's theorem is the computation of large powers modulo a composite number.
A trap: the condition $\gcd(a, n) = 1$ is needed; for $a = 2$ and $n = 10$ we get $2^4 = 16 \equiv 6 \pmod{10}$.
