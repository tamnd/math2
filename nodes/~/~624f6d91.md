## Example

The claim is: for every [[integer|0C4JS9RF5M]] $n$, the number $n^2 - n$ is even.
We write one proof for a first-year course and one proof for a reader who knows elementary number theory.
Both are instances of [[writing proofs|~7e59ebb2]], because each one fits a stated reader.

The proof for a first-year course:

> Let $n$ be an integer.
> We have $n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$.
> Every integer is even or odd, so we consider two cases.
> Case 1: $n$ is even.
> Then $n = 2k$ for an integer $k$, by the [[definition of an even integer|BV6E0ZG3YN]].
> So $n(n - 1) = 2 \cdot k(n - 1)$, and $k(n - 1)$ is an integer.
> Hence $n(n - 1)$ is even.
> Case 2: $n$ is odd.
> Then $n = 2k + 1$ for an integer $k$, so $n - 1 = 2k$.
> So $n(n - 1) = 2 \cdot k(2k + 1)$, and $k(2k + 1)$ is an integer.
> Hence $n(n - 1)$ is even.
> In both cases $n^2 - n$ is even.
> $\square$

The proof for a reader who knows elementary number theory:

> Since $n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$ is a product of two consecutive integers, one of its factors is even, and so is the product.
> $\square$

We check that each text fits its reader.

1. The first proof states each fact that a first-year student may not have. These are the factorization, the split into even and odd, and the definition in each case.
2. The first proof is a [[proof by cases|0WCP0PVET6]], and it says why the two cases cover all integers.
3. The second proof uses the fact "of two consecutive integers, one is even" without proof. For its reader this fact is standard, and the first proof contains its proof.
4. Both proofs are correct. They differ only in the steps that they leave to the reader.
5. A numerical check: for $n = 5$ we get $25 - 5 = 20 = 2 \cdot 10$, and for $n = -3$ we get $9 + 3 = 12 = 2 \cdot 6$.

## Remarks

1. The choice of the reader decides which steps are written. The page [[level of detail in proofs|~01d1ea56]] treats this choice in general.
2. The short proof would be a gap in a first-year exercise, where the task is to prove the fact about consecutive integers.
3. The long proof would be tiresome in a research paper, where a reader expects the short form.
4. For $n^3 - n = (n - 1)n(n + 1)$ the same two styles prove that $n^3 - n$ is divisible by $3$. The long proof then has three cases, by the remainder of $n$ on division by $3$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A teacher explains a fact one way to a beginner and another way to a colleague.
A proof is the same.
The fact here is that $n^2 - n$ is always even: for $n = 4$ it is $12$, and for $n = 7$ it is $42$.
For a beginner, we treat even and odd $n$ one at a time and show each calculation.
For an expert, one sentence is enough: $n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$, and of two neighbouring whole numbers one is even.

### L2 human

We write the two versions for the claim "$n^3 - n$ is divisible by $3$ for every integer $n$".

1. Factor: $n^3 - n = n(n^2 - 1) = (n - 1)n(n + 1)$.
2. Long version: by division with remainder, $n = 3q$, $n = 3q + 1$ or $n = 3q + 2$ for an integer $q$.
3. If $n = 3q$, the factor $n$ is divisible by $3$.
4. If $n = 3q + 1$, the factor $n - 1 = 3q$ is divisible by $3$.
5. If $n = 3q + 2$, the factor $n + 1 = 3(q + 1)$ is divisible by $3$.
6. In each case one factor is divisible by $3$, so the product is divisible by $3$.
7. Short version: "Of three consecutive integers, one is divisible by $3$."

The typical use is to decide, before writing, who the reader is.
A trap is to mix the two styles.
A proof that checks easy cases in full and skips the hard case does not fit any reader.
