## Statement

Let $f \colon G \to H$ be a [[group homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]].
Then the following are equivalent.

1. $f$ is an [[epimorphism|RVXDSY04KG]] in the [[category of groups|2CX7RPPXA9]]. That is, for each group $K$ and all homomorphisms $u, v \colon H \to K$ with $u \circ f = v \circ f$, we have $u = v$.
2. $f$ is [[surjective|01RVCMSN5N]].

The proof is in [[the proof that epimorphisms of groups are surjective|~9748a698]].

## Remarks

The direction from 2 to 1 is easy, and it holds in each category of sets with structure.
The direction from 1 to 2 is the content of the theorem.
The proof must find two different homomorphisms out of $H$ that agree on the image $f(G)$.
If $f(G)$ is a normal subgroup $N$ of $H$, then the projection $H \to H/N$ and the trivial homomorphism are such a pair, if $N \ne H$.
But $f(G)$ need not be normal, and the proof then uses a group of permutations.

The dual statement also holds: a homomorphism is a [[monomorphism|Y2NP1JFR86]] in the category of groups if and only if it is injective.
This direction is simpler, and the [[category of groups|2CX7RPPXA9]] gives a proof.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The theorem is a property of the category of groups, and it fails in other categories of algebras.

- In the category of rings with identity, the inclusion $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$ is an epimorphism, but it is not surjective ([[this example|ASXD4S15Z4]]).
- In the category of [[monoids|0N6R902FG1]], the inclusion of the natural numbers $(\mathbb{N}, +)$ in $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is an epimorphism, but it is not surjective. Two monoid homomorphisms $u, v$ that agree on $\mathbb{N}$ agree on $-n$, because $u(-n)$ and $v(-n)$ are both inverses of $u(n) = v(n)$, and inverses are unique.

The proof uses inverses in an essential way: the maps $x \mapsto hx$ on the cosets are bijections only because $h$ has an inverse.

### Consequences

- A homomorphism of groups is an isomorphism of the category if and only if it is both a monomorphism and an epimorphism, because a bijective homomorphism has an inverse homomorphism.
- The same proof works in the category of finite groups. If $H$ is finite, then the proof uses only a finite group of permutations as the target of the two homomorphisms.

### Converse

The converse is the direction from 2 to 1.
If $f$ is surjective and $u \circ f = v \circ f$, then each $h \in H$ is $f(g)$ for some $g \in G$, so $u(h) = u(f(g)) = v(f(g)) = v(h)$.

### Generalizations

In the [[category of abelian groups|99ZVSM254K]], the epimorphisms are also the surjective homomorphisms.
There the proof is short: if $f(G) \ne H$, then the projection $H \to H/f(G)$ and the zero map agree on $f(G)$ but are different.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In category theory, we cannot look at the elements of an object.
So we cannot say directly that a map is surjective.
In its place, there is the notion of an epimorphism: a map $f$ such that two maps out of the target are equal as soon as they agree after $f$.
For groups, the theorem says that this abstract notion is the same as surjectivity.
This is a real theorem, because for rings and for monoids the two notions are different.

### L2 human

We follow the proof for the inclusion $f \colon A_3 \to S_3$, which is not surjective.
Compose permutations from right to left.

1. The image is $K = A_3$, with the two left cosets $A_3$ and $(1\,2)A_3$. Add one more point $\infty$. This gives a set $Y$ with three points.
2. Each $h \in S_3$ acts on $Y$ by $\lambda_h(xA_3) = hxA_3$ and $\lambda_h(\infty) = \infty$. An even $h$ fixes the three points. An odd $h$ swaps the two cosets and fixes $\infty$.
3. Let $\sigma$ swap the point $A_3$ with $\infty$, and fix $(1\,2)A_3$. Define $\mu_h = \sigma \lambda_h \sigma^{-1}$.
4. For an even $h$, $\lambda_h$ is the identity, so $\mu_h$ is the identity too. Thus $\lambda$ and $\mu$ agree on $A_3$.
5. For an odd $h$, $\lambda_h$ swaps $A_3$ and $(1\,2)A_3$, but $\mu_h$ swaps $\infty$ and $(1\,2)A_3$. So $\lambda_{(1\,2)} \ne \mu_{(1\,2)}$.
6. Thus $\lambda \circ f = \mu \circ f$, but $\lambda \ne \mu$, and $f$ is not an epimorphism.

Here $A_3$ is normal in $S_3$, so the projection $S_3 \to S_3/A_3$ and the trivial map also work.
For the image $\{e, (1\,2)\}$ of an inclusion $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to S_3$, the quotient method fails.
The only normal subgroup of $S_3$ that contains $(1\,2)$ is $S_3$, so we must use the construction with $\sigma$.

A common error is to think that "epimorphism" and "surjective" are the same word in each category.
They are the same for groups, but not for rings.

### L3 human

The category of groups is balanced: a morphism that is both a monomorphism and an epimorphism is an isomorphism.
The theorem is not a formal consequence of the axioms of a category, and its proof uses a specific construction.
Another proof uses the [[amalgamated free product|M848AHBRCM]] $H *_K H$ with $K = f(G)$: its two canonical maps from $H$ agree on $K$, and they are different when $K \ne H$.
For topological groups the answer changes: in the category of Hausdorff topological groups, a homomorphism with a dense image is an epimorphism.
