## Example

Let $A$ be the set of functions $g \colon \R^2 \to \R$ of the form $g(x, y) = ax + by + c$ with $a, b, c \in \R$.
With pointwise operations, $A$ is a vector space.
We show that $A$ is not the [[dual space|0M856Q1AMZ]] $(\R^2)^*$.

1. The constant function $1$ lies in $A$, with $a = b = 0$ and $c = 1$.
2. Every [[linear form|0RT96J4D3H]] $f$ satisfies $f(0, 0) = f(0 \cdot (0, 0)) = 0 \cdot f(0, 0) = 0$.
3. The constant $1$ takes the value $1 \ne 0$ at $(0, 0)$. So $1$ is not a linear form, and $A \ne (\R^2)^*$.
4. The functions $1, x, y$ form a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] of $A$. They span $A$ by the definition of $A$.
5. They are independent. If $c + ax + by = 0$ for all $(x, y)$, then the point $(0, 0)$ gives $c = 0$, the point $(1, 0)$ gives $a = 0$, and the point $(0, 1)$ gives $b = 0$.
6. So $\dim A = 3$, while $\dim (\R^2)^* = 2$. The [[dimensions|05G2G0P224]] also show that the two spaces differ.
7. The linear forms are the elements of $A$ with $c = 0$. They form a [[subspace|00YC1D652K]] of $A$ of dimension $2$.

## Remarks

- In school, a function such as $x \mapsto 3x + 1$ is often called "linear". In linear algebra, it is affine, and only the case $c = 0$ is a linear form.
- An affine function $g$ is a linear form plus a constant: $g = f + g(0, 0)$ with $f$ linear. So $A \cong (\R^2)^* \oplus \R$.
- The level sets $\set{g = 0}$ of nonconstant affine functions are the lines of the plane, also those that miss the origin. The kernels of nonzero linear forms are only the lines through the origin.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The function $3x + 2y + 1$ on the plane is not a linear form, because it is not zero at the origin.
Linear forms are the functions $ax + by$ with no constant term.
Adding the constant term gives a larger space, of dimension $3$ instead of $2$.

### L2 human

To test whether a function belongs to the dual space, first test its value at $0$.

1. Compute $g(0, 0)$.
2. If $g(0, 0) \ne 0$, then $g$ is not a linear form.
3. If $g(0, 0) = 0$ and $g(x, y) = ax + by + c$, then $c = 0$ and $g$ is linear.

The typical use is to keep the words "linear" and "affine" apart.
A trap: the test $g(0) = 0$ is necessary but not enough for general functions; $(x, y) \mapsto xy$ is zero at $0$ and is not linear.
