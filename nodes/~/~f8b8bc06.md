## Summary

The forward-backward method is a way to find a proof of "if $A$, then $B$".
The backward process starts from the conclusion $B$ and asks "How can $B$ be shown?"; the answer is a statement $B_1$ such that $B_1$ implies $B$, and the question is repeated for $B_1$.
The forward process starts from the hypothesis $A$ and derives consequences $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ of it.
The search ends when a forward statement $A_k$ is a statement $B_j$ of the backward chain, and the proof is then written forward: $A \Rightarrow A_1 \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow A_k = B_j \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow B_1 \Rightarrow B$.
The method is a plan for finding a [[direct proof|0NTF6DMVAP]], and it explains where the first line of a proof comes from.

## Motivation

A finished proof starts from the hypothesis and moves to the conclusion, but the writer seldom found it in that order.
From the hypothesis alone, many consequences can be derived, and most of them lead nowhere.
The conclusion tells which consequences are useful: to prove that a triangle is isosceles, one looks for two equal sides, so the hypothesis should be turned into an equation between sides.
Working from both ends narrows the search.
The method also explains why published proofs often seem to start with an unmotivated step: the step was found by working backward.

## Notation

Daniel Solow, who named the method, calls the question asked in the backward process the key question.
Velleman organizes the same search as a list of givens and a goal: each step either adds a given or replaces the goal by a new goal, according to the logical form of the goal.
A common layout for scratch work has two columns, with the forward statements on the left and the backward statements on the right.
In Lean, the goal view shows the givens above and the goal below; the tactic `apply` works backward, replacing the goal, and `have` works forward, adding a given.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[A right triangle with area a quarter of the square of the hypotenuse is isosceles|~dc72e17e]]: backward from "two sides are equal" to "$(a - b)^2 = 0$", forward from the area and the [[Pythagorean theorem|0VP3A1290K]].
2. [[A composition of injective functions is injective|~18228221]]: the definition of injectivity tells how to start and how to finish.
3. To prove $a^2 + b^2 \geq 2ab$, the backward step "it suffices that $a^2 - 2ab + b^2 \geq 0$, that is, $(a - b)^2 \geq 0$" meets the forward fact that squares are at least $0$; see [[an argument that starts from the conclusion|~d73ceca2]] for how to write it up.
4. To prove that $f(x) = 3x + 2$ maps $\mathbb{R}$ onto $\mathbb{R}$: the goal is to find, for each $y$, an $x$ with $3x + 2 = y$. Working backward gives $x = \frac{y - 2}{3}$, and the proof then checks this $x$ forward; this is a [[constructive proof|01XM7JEKHY]].
5. In an $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ proof that $\lim_{x \to 1} 2x = 2$, the backward step "$|2x - 2| < \varepsilon$ follows from $|x - 1| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$" finds $\delta = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$, and the proof starts with "Let $\delta = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$".

### Non-examples

1. [[Squaring the goal produces a goal that does not imply it|~6b41c0de]]: the step from "$x + 1 \geq 0$" to "$(x + 1)^2 \geq 0$" goes the wrong way, since the new goal does not imply the old one.
2. Writing the backward chain as the proof. A text that starts "$a^2 + b^2 \geq 2ab$" and ends "$0 \leq (a - b)^2$, which is true" proves nothing unless each step is reversed, as [[the argument that starts from the conclusion|~d73ceca2]] shows.
3. A forward process that lists consequences of $A$ without looking at $B$. It is not wrong, but it is a search without direction, not the forward-backward method.

### Basic properties

1. Each backward step replaces the goal $B$ by a statement $B'$ with $B' \Rightarrow B$, a sufficient condition. A statement $B'$ with only $B \Rightarrow B'$ is a necessary condition and cannot replace the goal.
2. Each forward step adds a statement $A'$ with $A \Rightarrow A'$; by [[implication|0ZNX6DQ3EJ]] chains, all forward statements can be used together.
3. The logical form of the goal suggests the backward step: for "for all $x$" let $x$ be arbitrary; for "if $P$, then $Q$" assume $P$; for "there is an $x$" look for a witness; for "$P$ and $Q$" prove both; for "$P$ if and only if $Q$" prove two directions; for "not $P$" assume $P$ and look for a contradiction.
4. The final proof is written forward, in the order hypothesis, forward steps, then the backward steps in reverse order.

### History

Pappus of Alexandria, in Book 7 of his Collection, described analysis, which assumes what is sought and works back to something known, and synthesis, which reverses the analysis to give the proof.
George Pólya's How to Solve It (1945) treats working backwards and the method of Pappus as heuristics for problem solving.
Daniel Solow's book How to Read and Do Proofs (1982) introduced the name "forward-backward method" and the key question.

### Common errors

1. A backward step that uses a necessary condition instead of a sufficient one, as in non-example 1.
2. Writing up the scratch work, so that the proof starts with the conclusion.
3. A forward step that uses the conclusion, which makes the argument circular.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To plan a trip from home to a new place, it helps to look at the map from both ends.
From home, we know a few streets that we can reach.
From the new place, we see which streets lead to it.
When the two sets of streets meet, we have a route.
A proof can be planned in the same way: from what we know, and back from what we want, until the two meet in the middle.

### L2 human

We find a proof that if $n$ is an odd integer, then $8 \mid n^2 - 1$.

1. Backward: $8 \mid n^2 - 1$ means $n^2 - 1 = 8m$ for an integer $m$. Since $n^2 - 1 = (n - 1)(n + 1)$, it suffices to show that $(n - 1)(n + 1)$ is $8$ times an integer.
2. Forward: $n$ odd means $n = 2k + 1$ for an integer $k$. So $n - 1 = 2k$ and $n + 1 = 2k + 2 = 2(k + 1)$.
3. The two chains meet: $(n - 1)(n + 1) = 4k(k + 1)$.
4. One of $k$ and $k + 1$ is even, so $k(k + 1) = 2j$ for an integer $j$, and $(n - 1)(n + 1) = 8j$.
5. Written forward, the proof is: $n = 2k + 1$, so $n^2 - 1 = 4k(k + 1) = 8j$, so $8 \mid n^2 - 1$.

The typical use: the backward step decides which form of the hypothesis to use, here the factorization into $(n - 1)(n + 1)$.

A trap: step 4 is a new idea that neither chain gives by itself; the method narrows the search but does not replace thought.

### L3 human

In automated theorem proving, forward chaining derives consequences of the facts and backward chaining reduces goals to subgoals, and the same two directions appear in logic programming, where Prolog works backward from the query.
In interactive proof assistants, a proof state is a list of hypotheses and goals, and most tactics are backward steps; the proof term that results reads forward.
Pólya's heuristics and other strategies for finding proofs are treated in the topic [[heuristics of discovery|X1NSTVGDNQ]], and the layout of the final write-up in the topic [[writing and reading proofs|STSM6PR7TX]].
