## Statement

A *hydra* is a finite [[rooted tree|0C9J6W2A8A]].
Its *heads* are the leaves other than the root, and the hydra is *dead* when it is the root alone.
A *battle* consists of steps $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, and at step $n$ Hercules chooses a head $h$ and a number $N_n \ge 0$.
Let $p$ be the parent of $h$.

- If $p$ is the root, the head $h$ is removed.
- Otherwise let $g$ be the parent of $p$. The head $h$ is removed, and then $N_n$ copies of the subtree rooted at $p$ are attached to $g$ as new children.

A *strategy* is a rule that chooses $h$ and $N_n$ from the battle so far.
The *standard battle* uses $N_n = n$ and always cuts the rightmost head, where new copies are drawn to the right.
Let $\mathrm{Hydra}(k)$ be the number of steps of the standard battle that starts with a path of $k$ edges.

**Theorem.**

1. Every battle ends: for every hydra and every strategy, the hydra is dead after finitely many steps.
2. The function $\mathrm{Hydra}$ eventually dominates every function that [[Peano arithmetic|MPSCREYC3M]] $\mathsf{PA}$ proves total.
3. So $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove that the standard battle kills every hydra. Hence $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove that every recursive strategy kills every hydra.
4. The statement that the standard battle kills every hydra is provable in $\mathsf{PA}$ plus transfinite induction up to [[epsilon nought|KT50SMH3EC]] $\varepsilon_0$.

The [[proof|~100c3bb3]] proves parts 1 and 4 in full and derives part 3 from part 2, which it cites.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The theory in part 3 is $\mathsf{PA}$. In $\mathsf{PA}$ plus transfinite induction up to $\varepsilon_0$, the battle ends, by part 4.
- The copies grow on the grandparent $g$, one level nearer to the root. If they grew on $p$ itself with $N_n \ge 1$, the node $p$ of a path of two edges would never lose all its children, and the battle would never end.
- In part 3 the strategy is recursive. "Every strategy" quantifies over arbitrary functions and is not a sentence of first-order arithmetic.
- The hydra is finite. For an infinite tree, a head can be cut at each step for ever without the tree ever becoming a single root.

### Consequences

- The standard battle gives $\mathrm{Hydra}(0) = 0$, $\mathrm{Hydra}(1) = 1$, $\mathrm{Hydra}(2) = 3$ and $\mathrm{Hydra}(3) = 37$. The value $\mathrm{Hydra}(4)$ is much larger than Graham's number.
- Part 1 holds for every choice of the numbers $N_n$, however large. Only the shape of the tree, measured by an ordinal below $\varepsilon_0$, decides termination.
- Parts 2 and 3 give one more natural statement that is true but independent of $\mathsf{PA}$, like [[Goodstein's theorem|XQMNJF241J]].

### Converse

The converse question is whether Hercules can lose: there is no strategy that keeps a hydra alive for ever, by part 1.
So in this game "Hercules always wins" and "no strategy loses" mean the same thing.

### Generalizations

- The [[Buchholz hydra|~bf22387c]] adds labels to the nodes. Its battles also end, but the proof needs ordinals up to the [[Takeuti-Feferman-Buchholz ordinal|S5FTQGHVKC]].
- [[Beklemishev's worm principle|~9689a72a]] is a hydra on a path, and it is also unprovable in $\mathsf{PA}$.

## History

Laurence Kirby and Jeff Paris introduced the hydra game in 1982, in the paper "Accessible independence results for Peano arithmetic".
In the same paper they proved the unprovability of Goodstein's theorem in $\mathsf{PA}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A hydra is a tree drawn upside down, and its heads are the ends of the branches.
Hercules cuts one head at each step.
The hydra then grows new copies of a part of itself, more copies at each later step.
Even so, Hercules always wins: after enough steps only the root is left.
For a path with three edges, the standard battle takes $37$ steps.
For a path with four edges it takes so many steps that no computer could ever count them.

### L2 human

A worked check of $\mathrm{Hydra}(2) = 3$.

1. The hydra is a path: root $r$, a middle node $p$ and a head $h$ on top of $p$.
2. Step $1$: cut $h$. The parent $p$ is not the root, so $1$ copy of the subtree at $p$ (now the single node $p$) grows on $r$.
3. Now $r$ has two children, $p$ and its copy, and both are heads.
4. Step $2$ cuts one of them; its parent is the root, so nothing grows.
5. Step $3$ cuts the last head. The hydra is dead after $3$ steps.

The ordinals of the [[proof|~100c3bb3]] are $\omega$, then $2$, then $1$, then $0$.
The typical use: to show that a process ends, attach an ordinal that strictly decreases.
The trap: the number of nodes can grow very fast during the battle, so the number of nodes is not a measure that decreases.

### L3 human

The hydra codes the ordinals below $\varepsilon_0$: a tree with subtrees $T_1, \dots, T_j$ at the root has the ordinal $\omega^{o(T_1)} \# \cdots \# \omega^{o(T_j)}$, a natural sum.
Every ordinal below $\varepsilon_0$ is the ordinal of a hydra, and the standard battle follows the fundamental sequences of the [[Hardy hierarchy|~ec594377]].
This is why the length of the battle grows like the function $f_{\varepsilon_0}$ of the [[fast-growing hierarchy|~24c8db71]] and outgrows the [[provably total functions|~25aedb3b]] of $\mathsf{PA}$.
The same idea with labelled trees and collapsing functions gives the [[Buchholz hydra|~bf22387c]], which measures much stronger subsystems of [[second-order arithmetic|DC60FYCSPM]].
