## Example

In [[independence-friendly logic|DPMKT2KRB8]] consider the sentence
$$\psi := \forall x\, \exists z\, (\exists y / \set{x})\, x = y$$
in a structure $\mathcal{M}$ with domain $M$ and empty vocabulary.
We show that $\psi$ is true in every structure.

1. In the game for $\psi$, Abelard chooses $a$ for $x$, then Eloise chooses $c$ for $z$, and then Eloise chooses $b$ for $y$. She wins if $a = b$.
2. At $\exists z$ nothing is hidden, so Eloise may choose $c = a$.
3. At $(\exists y / \set{x})$ the value of $x$ is hidden, but the value of $z$ is not, since $z \notin \set{x}$. So Eloise may choose $b = c$.
4. The strategy "$z := x$, then $y := z$" is uniform: the choice of $y$ depends only on the value of $z$.
5. In every play it gives $b = c = a$, so Eloise wins, and $\psi$ is true in $\mathcal{M}$.

The same proof in Skolem form: the Skolem function of $z$ takes $x$, the Skolem function of $y$ takes the variables in scope other than $x$, namely $z$.
So the [[Skolem form|GCPQGMJYEV]] is $\exists f \exists g\, \forall x\, x = g(f(x))$, and $f = g = \mathrm{id}_M$ are witnesses.

## Remarks

The example shows signalling: a player passes hidden information to her later self through an earlier move.
The slash on $x$ hides the value of $x$, but not the value of $z$, which encodes it.
Compare the [[undetermined sentence|~45b86326]] $\forall x (\exists y / \set{x})\, x = y$: it has no $\exists z$, so Eloise has nothing to read, and the sentence is not true in any structure with two elements.

The result changes with the slash set.
With $(\exists y / \set{x, z})$ the Skolem function of $y$ has no arguments, and $\psi$ becomes true exactly in the one-element structures, like the undetermined sentence.
This is why the Henkin quantifier in IF form slashes both $x_1$ and $y_1$.

Signalling depends on the convention that the slash hides only the listed variables.
Under this convention IF logic allows dependences that are not transitive: $y$ depends on $z$, $z$ depends on $x$, and $y$ does not depend on $x$ directly.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A player must guess a hidden number, and the rules say that she may not look at it.
But one step earlier she was allowed to write a note, and she copied the number into the note.
The rules do not forbid reading the note, so she reads it and wins.
In this logic, hiding one piece of information is not enough when another piece carries the same information.

### L2 human

We play $\psi$ on $M = \set{0, 1}$.

1. Abelard chooses $x = 1$.
2. Eloise sees $x = 1$ and chooses $z = 1$.
3. At $y$, Eloise sees $z = 1$ but not $x$, and she chooses $y = 1$.
4. The atomic formula $x = y$ reads $1 = 1$, so Eloise wins this play; the play with $x = 0$ is the same with $0$ in place of $1$.

The typical use of this example is to check a proposed translation of a dependence pattern into IF logic.
A trap is to slash only the universal variables: to make $y$ independent of $x$, one must also slash every existential variable that can carry $x$.
