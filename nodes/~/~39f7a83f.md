## Example

Let $U = \operatorname{diag}(i, i) = iI_2$.
We show that $U$ is in the [[unitary group|~4244c18b]] $U(2)$, but not in the [[special unitary group|~f67e0a03]] $SU(2)$.

1. $U^* = \operatorname{diag}(-i, -i)$, and $U^*U = \operatorname{diag}(-i \cdot i, -i \cdot i) = I_2$. So $U$ is unitary.
2. $\det U = i \cdot i = -1$.
3. So $\det U \neq 1$, and $U \notin SU(2)$.
4. Also $U$ is not of the form $\begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ -\overline{\beta} & \overline{\alpha} \end{pmatrix}$, because its diagonal entries $i$ and $i$ are not complex conjugates.

## Remarks

- $\abs{\det U} = 1$, as for every unitary matrix. The condition for $SU(2)$ is $\det U = 1$ exactly.
- $U$ is a scalar matrix, so it commutes with every matrix. It lies in the center of $U(2)$.
- The scalar $e^{i\pi/4}I_2$ has determinant $i$, so it is not in $SU(2)$ either. The scalars in $SU(2)$ are only $\pm I_2$.
- For $n = 3$, the scalar $iI_3$ has determinant $-i$. The scalar $\omega I_3$ with $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$ has determinant $1$ and lies in $SU(3)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix multiplies each coordinate by the complex number $i$, a quarter turn.
It keeps all lengths, so it is unitary.
But two quarter turns together make a half turn, and the determinant is $-1$.
So the matrix is not special unitary.

### L2 human

The worked check is in the Example.
The typical use: the determinant of a scalar matrix $zI_n$ is $z^n$, so it is in $SU(n)$ exactly when $z^n = 1$.
A trap: a turn by the same angle in each complex coordinate is not automatically in $SU(n)$.
