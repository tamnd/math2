## Example

On $\mathbb{C}^n$ put
$$\ip{x}{y} = x_1 \overline{y_1} + x_2 \overline{y_2} + \dots + x_n \overline{y_n},$$
where the bar is the [[complex conjugate|0BJV24C4SY]].
In matrix form, with $x$ and $y$ as columns, $\ip{x}{y} = y^* x$, where $y^*$ is the [[conjugate transpose|0DXKE10PHF]] of $y$.
We check the three axioms of an [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]], and then we compute one example in $\mathbb{C}^2$.

1. Linearity in the first argument: $\sum_k (a x_k + b z_k)\overline{y_k} = a\sum_k x_k \overline{y_k} + b\sum_k z_k \overline{y_k}$, by the distributive law.
2. Conjugate symmetry: $\overline{\ip{x}{y}} = \sum_k \overline{x_k}\, y_k = \ip{y}{x}$, because the conjugate of a sum or a product is the sum or the product of the conjugates.
3. Positive definiteness: $\ip{x}{x} = \sum_k x_k \overline{x_k} = \sum_k |x_k|^2$, where $|x_k|$ is the [[modulus|0KW2X0TFBX]]. This is a sum of nonnegative reals, and it is $0$ only when every $x_k$ is $0$.
4. A computation in $\mathbb{C}^2$. Let $x = (1 + i, 2)$ and $y = (i, 1 - i)$. Then $\ip{x}{y} = (1 + i)(-i) + 2(1 + i) = (1 - i) + (2 + 2i) = 3 + i$.
5. In the other order, $\ip{y}{x} = i(1 - i) + (1 - i) \cdot 2 = (1 + i) + (2 - 2i) = 3 - i$, the conjugate of $3 + i$.
6. The norms are $\norm{x}^2 = |1 + i|^2 + |2|^2 = 2 + 4 = 6$ and $\norm{y}^2 = |i|^2 + |1 - i|^2 = 1 + 2 = 3$.
7. The [[Cauchy-Schwarz inequality|0RFWW56Y22]] holds: $|\ip{x}{y}|^2 = 9 + 1 = 10$ and $\norm{x}^2 \norm{y}^2 = 18$.

So $\mathbb{C}^n$ with this inner product is an [[inner product space|0QVNCJXRY3]], and its [[induced norm|0MFJJV97RJ]] is $\norm{x} = \sqrt{|x_1|^2 + \dots + |x_n|^2}$.

## Remarks

The example is the model of every unitary space: each complex inner product space of dimension $n$ is isometric to this one.
On real vectors the conjugates do nothing, and the formula is the [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]] of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
Physics books, and Mathlib, use $\sum_k \overline{x_k}\, y_k = x^* y$ instead, which is our $\ip{y}{x}$.
The two formulas give conjugate values: in step 4 the physics formula gives $3 - i$.
Without the conjugates, the formula is the [[bilinear form of the non-example|~09439661]], and it is not an inner product.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For complex vectors, we multiply each entry of the first vector by the conjugate of the matching entry of the second vector, and we add.
The conjugate makes the result of a vector with itself a sum of squared sizes.
For the vector $(i, 1)$ with itself, we get $i \cdot (-i) + 1 \cdot 1 = 1 + 1 = 2$.
So the length of $(i, 1)$ is $\sqrt{2}$, as for the real vector $(1, 1)$.

### L2 human

Worked example: a unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^2$.

1. Let $v = (1, i)$. Then $\ip{v}{v} = 1 \cdot 1 + i \cdot \overline{i} = 1 + 1 = 2$.
2. So $\norm{v} = \sqrt{2}$, and $e = v / \sqrt{2} = (1, i)/\sqrt{2}$ is a unit vector.
3. The vector $w = (1, -i)$ satisfies $\ip{w}{v} = 1 \cdot 1 + (-i)\overline{i} = 1 + (-i)(-i) = 1 - 1 = 0$.
4. So $v$ and $w$ are orthogonal, and $(v, w)/\sqrt{2}$ is an [[orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^2$|~22363b43]].

The typical use is in quantum mechanics and in signal processing, where states and signals are complex vectors.
A trap: the matrix form is $y^* x$ and not $x^* y$.
The second one is the conjugate of the first.
