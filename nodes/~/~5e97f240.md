## Example

Claim: every integer $n \ge 2$ is a product of one or more [[prime numbers|01826KMG2H]].
A prime $p$ counts as a product of one prime.
We prove the claim by [[strong induction|0KMVFSHFGZ]] from the base $2$.

1. Let $P(n)$ be the statement "$n$ is a product of one or more primes".
2. Let $n \ge 2$, and assume $P(k)$ for every integer $k$ with $2 \le k < n$.
3. Case 1: $n$ is prime. Then $n$ is a product of one prime, and $P(n)$ holds.
4. Case 2: $n$ is not prime. Since $n \ge 2$, by the definition of a prime, $n = ab$ with integers $a, b$ and $1 < a < n$.
5. From $n = ab$ and $1 < a < n$ we get $b = n / a$, so $1 < b < n$.
6. So $2 \le a < n$ and $2 \le b < n$. By the hypothesis of step 2, $P(a)$ and $P(b)$ hold.
7. Write $a = p_1 \cdots p_r$ and $b = q_1 \cdots q_s$ with primes $p_i$ and $q_j$.
8. Then $n = p_1 \cdots p_r q_1 \cdots q_s$ is a product of primes, and $P(n)$ holds.
9. Cases 1 and 2 cover every $n \ge 2$. By strong induction, $P(n)$ holds for every $n \ge 2$.

For example, $360 = 18 \cdot 20$, then $18 = 2 \cdot 9 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 3$ and $20 = 4 \cdot 5 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 5$, so $360 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$.

## Remarks

1. The example shows why the strong form is needed: the factors $a$ and $b$ can be any numbers below $n$, not only $n - 1$.
2. For $n = 2$, the hypothesis of step 2 is empty. The proof still works, because $2$ is prime and case 1 applies. So no separate base case is needed.
3. Ordinary induction on $n$ does not work directly. The fact that $n - 1$ is a product of primes says nothing about $n$.
4. The proof shows existence only. Uniqueness is the second half of the [[fundamental theorem of arithmetic|084HH1NDCB]], and it needs Euclid's lemma.
5. The same proof works in any setting where a "size" decreases under factoring, for example for polynomials over a field, with the degree as the size.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take a number such as $60$.
If it is prime, we are done.
If not, it splits as a product of two smaller numbers, for example $6 \times 10$.
Each smaller number either is prime or splits again: $6 = 2 \times 3$ and $10 = 2 \times 5$.
The numbers get smaller each time, so the splitting stops, and only primes are left: $60 = 2 \times 3 \times 2 \times 5$.

### L2 human

We write the same argument as a [[minimal counterexample|~f8c099da]], to compare the two forms.

1. Suppose some integer $n \ge 2$ is not a product of primes.
2. By the [[well-ordering principle|01BX15HSRR]], there is a least such $n$.
3. This $n$ is not prime, so $n = ab$ with $1 < a, b < n$.
4. By the minimality of $n$, both $a$ and $b$ are products of primes, so $n$ is one too.
5. This contradicts step 1, so every $n \ge 2$ is a product of primes.

The typical use of strong induction is a recursive split of an object into smaller pieces of unknown size.
A trap: in step 4 of the example, $a$ must be strictly between $1$ and $n$.
The trivial factorization $n = 1 \cdot n$ gives no smaller case.
