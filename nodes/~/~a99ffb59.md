## Example

Let $h$ be "Jones goes to help the neighbours" and $t$ be "Jones tells them that he is coming".
Consider the four sentences:

1. Jones ought to go: $Oh$.
2. It ought to be that if he goes, he tells them: $O(h \to t)$.
3. If he does not go, he ought not to tell them: $\neg h \to O\neg t$.
4. He does not go: $\neg h$.

Sentence 3 is a contrary-to-duty obligation: it says what to do once the duty of sentence 1 is violated.
The four sentences seem consistent, and none seems to follow from the others.
We show that in [[standard deontic logic|~19b899d4]] no direct translation has both properties.

5. From sentence 2, the axiom $\mathrm{K}$ gives $Oh \to Ot$, and sentence 1 then gives $Ot$.
6. From sentences 3 and 4, modus ponens gives $O\neg t$.
7. $Ot \wedge O\neg t$ contradicts the theorem $\neg(Ot \wedge O\neg t)$ of [[the derived laws|~4775e384]]. So the set is inconsistent.
8. Change sentence 2 to $h \to Ot$. This is a tautological consequence of sentence 4, since $\neg h$ makes the implication true. So the set is no longer independent.
9. Change sentence 3 to $O(\neg h \to \neg t)$. The formula $h \to (\neg h \to \neg t)$ is a tautology, so necessitation and $\mathrm{K}$ give $Oh \to O(\neg h \to \neg t)$.
10. So the new sentence 3 follows from sentence 1, and again the set is not independent.
11. The fourth combination, $h \to Ot$ with $O(\neg h \to \neg t)$, is not independent, by step 8.

## Remarks

The example shows that the monadic operator $O$ cannot express conditional obligation in all four sentences at once.
The standard response is dyadic deontic logic, with a binary operator $O(q \mid p)$ for "$q$ is obligatory given $p$".
The sentences become $O(h \mid \top)$, $O(t \mid h)$, $O(\neg t \mid \neg h)$ and $\neg h$.
In Hansson's semantics, worlds are ordered by betterness, and $O(q \mid p)$ holds when every best $p$-world is a $q$-world.
Take four worlds ranked $ht > \bar h\bar t > h\bar t > \bar h t$, where a bar marks a false atom, and let the actual world be $\bar h\bar t$.
The best world overall is $ht$, so $O(h \mid \top)$ and $O(t \mid \top)$ hold; the best $h$-world is $ht$, so $O(t \mid h)$ holds.
The best $\neg h$-world is $\bar h\bar t$, so $O(\neg t \mid \neg h)$ holds, and $\neg h$ is true at the actual world.
So the dyadic version is consistent.

Chisholm published the paradox in 1963.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Jones should help his neighbours, and if he goes, he should call ahead.
If he does not go, he should not call, since a call would make them wait for nothing.
In fact he does not go.
These four rules sound fine together.
But when we write them in the simplest logic of "ought", they either clash or one of them becomes empty.
The fix is to let "ought" depend on the situation: what is best when he goes, and what is best when he does not.

### L2 human

We check step 9 in Kripke semantics.

1. Let $w \models Oh$ in a serial model.
2. Every ideal world $v$ of $w$ has $v \models h$.
3. At such $v$, $\neg h$ is false, so $\neg h \to \neg t$ is true.
4. So $w \models O(\neg h \to \neg t)$.

The typical use of the example is as a benchmark: a proposed logic of conditional obligation must make the four sentences consistent and independent.
A trap: reading $O(\neg h \to \neg t)$ as a contrary-to-duty rule.
Since $h$ holds in all ideal worlds, the formula says nothing about the non-ideal worlds where $\neg h$ is true.
