## Example

A shop records its sales of bread on four days, Monday to Thursday.
In week $1$ the sales are $x = (20, 18, 25, 22)$, and in week $2$ they are $y = (22, 16, 27, 20)$.
Both lists are [[vectors|00EBGSR5NY]] of $\mathbb{R}^4$, and we compute with them component by component.

1. The total sales of the two weeks are $x + y = (42, 34, 52, 42)$.
2. The mean of the two weeks is $\tfrac{1}{2}(x + y) = (21, 17, 26, 21)$.
3. The change from week $1$ to week $2$ is $y - x = (2, -2, 2, -2)$.
4. If each sale gives a profit of $3$ units of money, the profit vector of week $1$ is $3x = (60, 54, 75, 66)$.

## Remarks

The example shows that a vector of $\mathbb{R}^n$ need not be an arrow that we can draw.
The rules of addition and scalar multiplication are the same as in the plane, only with more components.
The order of the components matters: $(20, 18, 25, 22)$ and $(18, 20, 25, 22)$ are different vectors.
If a fifth day is added, the vectors move to $\mathbb{R}^5$, and the same rules apply.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A vector can be a list of numbers, one number for each day.
Adding two lists means adding the numbers in the same position.
For example, $20$ loaves on Monday of week $1$ and $22$ on Monday of week $2$ give $42$ loaves.

### L2 human

The vector $\tfrac{1}{2}(x + y)$ is the midpoint of $x$ and $y$ in $\mathbb{R}^4$.
The same formula gives the midpoint of a segment in the plane or in space.
A trap: we can add only vectors with the same number of components, so $(1, 2) + (1, 2, 3)$ has no meaning.
