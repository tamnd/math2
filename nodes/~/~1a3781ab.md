## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 4 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ act on $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the dot product.
The matrix is [[symmetric|0N28QN4CYA]], so $x \mapsto Ax$ is [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]].

1. Expand $\det(A - tI)$ along the first row: $(4 - t)\big[(4 - t)(5 - t) - 1\big] - (4 - t)$.
2. This equals $(4 - t)\big[(4 - t)(5 - t) - 2\big] = (4 - t)(t^2 - 9t + 18) = (4 - t)(t - 3)(t - 6)$.
3. So the eigenvalues are $3$, $4$ and $6$. Their sum $13$ equals the trace $4 + 4 + 5$.
4. Eigenvectors: $A(1, 1, 1) = (3, 3, 3)$, $A(1, -1, 0) = (4, -4, 0)$ and $A(1, 1, -2) = (6, 6, -12)$.
5. The three vectors are pairwise orthogonal: $1 - 1 + 0 = 0$, $1 + 1 - 2 = 0$ and $1 - 1 + 0 = 0$.
6. After normalization, the columns $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1, 1, 1)$, $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, -1, 0)$ and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1, 1, -2)$ form an [[orthogonal matrix|0QRBVFJ50X]] $Q$, and
$$Q^T A Q = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}.$$

## Remarks

- The eigenvalues are distinct, so each eigenspace is a line, and step 5 is an instance of [[orthogonality of eigenspaces|0JHGAV9H25]].
- The quadratic form is $x^T A x = 4x_1^2 + 4x_2^2 + 5x_3^2 - 2x_1 x_3 - 2x_2 x_3$. On the unit sphere its maximum is $6$, at $\pm \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1, 1, -2)$, and its minimum is $3$, at $\pm \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1, 1, 1)$, by [[the extreme values of the Rayleigh quotient|~5c6d5f8a]].
- All eigenvalues are positive, so $A$ is positive definite.
- The orthogonal projection onto the eigenspace for $3$ is $\tfrac{1}{3} J$, where $J$ is the $3 \times 3$ matrix of ones.

## Explanations

### L1 human

This symmetric matrix stretches space by the factors $3$, $4$ and $6$ along three perpendicular directions.
The direction $(1, 1, 1)$ gets the smallest stretch, and the direction $(1, 1, -2)$ gets the largest stretch.
In coordinates along these directions, the matrix becomes a diagonal matrix.

### L2 human

The recipe for a real symmetric matrix:

1. Find the roots of the characteristic polynomial. They are real.
2. Find an eigenvector for each root. Eigenvectors for different roots are orthogonal, so no Gram-Schmidt step is needed here.
3. Normalize, and put the vectors as the columns of $Q$. Then $Q^{-1} = Q^T$, and $Q^T A Q$ is diagonal.

A check: $\det A = 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 = 72$, and the cofactor expansion gives $4 \cdot 19 - 4 = 72$.
A trap: when an eigenvalue is repeated, the eigenvectors in its eigenspace need not be orthogonal, and Gram-Schmidt is needed inside that eigenspace.
