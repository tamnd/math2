## Statement

There are first-order theories $W$ of arithmetic, found by Dan Willard, with the following properties.

1. The language of $W$ has subtraction and division as function symbols. Addition and multiplication are not function symbols; they are predicates defined from subtraction and division.
2. $W$ is [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]].
3. $W$ proves its own consistency, where provability is expressed by an arithmetic formula that describes closed analytic tableaux (tableau proofs) from the axioms of $W$.
4. $W$ does not prove the $\Pi_2$ sentence that says that multiplication is total. So $W$ contains neither [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] $\mathsf{Q}$ nor [[Peano arithmetic|MPSCREYC3M]].
5. Any true $\Pi_1$ sentence of arithmetic can be added to $W$ while keeping it consistent.

Theories with these properties are called self-verifying.
A cited outline of the proof is in [[the proof of the existence of self-verifying theories|~2cf13cf1]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The relevant comparison is with [[Gödel's second incompleteness theorem|~c79a35b5]]: a consistent theory $T$ with decidable axioms that contains $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove $\mathrm{Con}(T)$, and Willard's self-verifying theories do not contain even $\mathsf{Q}$.
Willard's theories escape that theorem in three ways, and each one matters.

1. Weak base. $W$ does not contain $\mathsf{Q}$, because it does not prove that multiplication is total. Without total multiplication the diagonal argument of the incompleteness theorems cannot be carried out inside $W$.
2. Language. Because subtraction and division are the basic operations, the axioms of $W$ can speak about addition and multiplication as relations without asserting that every product of two numbers exists. With multiplication as a function symbol, its totality would be a law of logic.
3. Notion of proof.
   Consistency is stated for tableau proofs.
   A tableau proof is cut-free, and for weak theories the consistency statements for different proof systems need not be equivalent, because the translation between proof systems uses arithmetic that $W$ does not have.
   The name of Willard's 2002 paper reflects that versions of the second incompleteness theorem for tableaux and cut-free proofs reach theories close to $\mathsf{Q}$; we do not state its precise results here.

### Consequences

1. The second incompleteness theorem is sharp in the sense that its hypothesis of enough arithmetic cannot be simply dropped: some consistent arithmetic theories do prove their own (tableau) consistency.
2. By property 5, every true $\Pi_1$ sentence can be added to a self-verifying theory, for example $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, which is a true $\Pi_1$ sentence.

### Converse

The statement is an existence result, so it has no direct converse.
The related question "which weak theories can prove their own consistency?" has a negative side: by the second incompleteness theorem, no consistent theory with decidable axioms that contains $\mathsf{PA}$ proves its own consistency in the usual sense.

### Generalizations

Willard studied a family of such systems with different base axioms and different notions of proof, and the boundary between the systems that can prove their own consistency and those that cannot.
We do not state these boundary results here.

## History

Dan Willard introduced self-verifying theories; the main reference is his paper of 2001 in the Journal of Symbolic Logic, followed by a paper of 2002 in the same journal about extending the second incompleteness theorem for tableaux almost to $\mathsf{Q}$.
The Wikipedia article on the subject also cites Solovay's 1989 paper "Injecting inconsistencies into models of PA".

## Explanations

### L1 human

Gödel showed that a good system of axioms for arithmetic cannot prove that it is free of contradictions.
"Good" includes being able to add and multiply any two numbers.
Willard asked what happens if the system is a little weaker: it can subtract and divide, but it does not claim that every two numbers have a product.
He found such systems that can prove "I am free of contradictions".
So Gödel's theorem really needs the full power of multiplication.

### L2 human

We compare a self-verifying theory $W$ with $\mathsf{Q}$ point by point.

1. $\mathsf{Q}$ has $+$ and $\cdot$ as function symbols, so $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \forall x \, \forall y \, \exists z \, (z = x \cdot y)$ by pure logic. $W$ does not prove the corresponding sentence.
2. The diagonal lemma holds in every theory that contains $\mathsf{Q}$, and it is the first step of the proof of the second incompleteness theorem. $W$ cannot formalize the diagonal argument.
3. $W$ proves the sentence "there is no tableau proof of $0 = 1$ from the axioms of $W$", and this sentence is true.

The typical use is to locate the exact strength of arithmetic that Gödel's second theorem needs.
A trap is to read "W proves its own consistency" as "W proves $\mathrm{Con}(W)$" with the Hilbert-style consistency statement of the incompleteness theorems; Willard's statement is about tableau proofs.

### L3 human

Self-verifying theories show that the second incompleteness theorem is a statement about a threshold of arithmetic strength, not about self-reference as such.
The analysis is close to [[bounded arithmetic|RA380RWEC0]] and to the study of weak fragments, where cut elimination and the totality of fast-growing functions are tied to consistency statements.
Willard's systems prove their consistency in a cut-free sense; proof systems with cut can shorten proofs a lot, and the step from cut-free to cut uses functions that the weak theory does not prove total.
This links the topic with [[Gödel's speed-up theorem|~005bbed5]], where the price of a consistency statement is measured in the length of proofs.
