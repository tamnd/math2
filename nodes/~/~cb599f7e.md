## Summary

The language of mathematics is the mix of a natural language, most often English, with symbols, formulas and diagrams, in which mathematicians state definitions, theorems and proofs.
The words carry the logical structure of a text: "let", "suppose", "if ... then", "for every", "there is", "hence".
The symbols name the objects and carry the calculations: $x$, $\sqrt{2}$, $\int_0^1 f$, $G/H$.
Mathematical English looks like ordinary English, but it differs in three ways.
Its small words, such as "or", "if", "any" and "the", have fixed meanings.
Many of its ordinary words, such as "group", "open", "normal" and "field", are defined terms.
Its sentences are built so that each one is a [[statement|~50156c16]] with one truth value, or an instruction that fixes a name or an assumption.
The pages of this topic treat the grammar of statements: [[connectives|~ba599a3d]], [[conditionals|~38816aeb]], [[quantifiers|~aec76a22]], [[negation|~f72ba5a2]], [[existence and uniqueness|~c0a27355]] and [[ambiguity|~ea809f7c]].

## Motivation

A theorem can be used only if its readers agree on what it says.
Ordinary English is flexible, and the same sentence can mean different things to different listeners.
Pure symbolism, as in a formal logic, removes this problem but is slow to read, and a proof written only in symbols hides its ideas.
The language of mathematics is a compromise between the two.
It keeps the readability of English for the structure of an argument, and it uses symbols where precision and brevity matter most.
To read mathematics well, a student must learn both parts and the conventions that join them.

## Notation

Every symbol in a mathematical text can be read aloud as words, and a formula is a part of a sentence.
So "$x^2 - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1)$" reads "$x$ squared minus one equals $x$ minus one times $x$ plus one", and it takes a full stop at the end of a sentence.
Symbols play the roles of nouns, as $\pi$ or $f(x)$, of verbs, as $=$, $<$ or $\in$, and of whole clauses, as $x \in A$.
A few logical symbols, $\forall$, $\exists$, $\Rightarrow$, $\iff$, $\land$, $\lor$ and $\neg$, stand for words of the logical structure.
Many authors avoid them in running text and write the words, and they keep the symbols for displayed formulas.
The conventions of notation and of jargon are in the topic [[notation, conventions and jargon|Z2WNQ1DNTX]].

## Remarks

### Examples

1. "Let $G$ be a group, and let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$." The words "let" and "be" introduce two objects, and the symbols $G$ and $H$ name them for the rest of the text.
2. "For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that $|f(x) - f(a)| < \varepsilon$ whenever $|x - a| < \delta$." Words give the quantifiers and the conditional, and formulas give the inequalities.
3. "A subset $U$ of $\mathbb{R}$ is open if every point of $U$ is the centre of an interval inside $U$." The ordinary word "open" becomes a defined term, and the word "if" means "if and only if".

### Non-examples

1. The sentence "the bank is near the river" is ordinary English. The word "bank" has two meanings, and the context decides between them, as ordinary English allows.
2. A proof written for the proof assistant Lean is a text in a formal language. It has a fixed grammar and no room for interpretation, so it is not mathematical English, although it can say the same thing.

### Basic properties

1. Mathematical English is written in the present tense. A theorem is true now and always, and "we" means the author and the reader together.
2. The logical words have one meaning each. "Or" is inclusive, "if" in a theorem is one-way, and "some" means "at least one".
3. Variables are pronouns with a scope. A letter introduced by "let" keeps its meaning until the end of the proof or block.
4. A definition gives a word a meaning that overrides the everyday one. A "normal" subgroup or an "imaginary" number need not match the ordinary sense of the word.
5. Each sentence of a proof is a claim, an assumption, a choice of an object or a remark about the plan. The words "suppose", "let", "so" and "we now show" mark the kind.

### History

Early algebra was written in words, as in the works of al-Khwarizmi in the 9th century.
Robert Recorde introduced the sign $=$ in 1557, and François Viète used letters for both unknowns and given quantities near the end of the 16th century.
René Descartes, in La Géométrie of 1637, wrote $x$, $y$ and $z$ for unknowns and $a$, $b$ and $c$ for known quantities, close to the modern use.
Gottlob Frege, in his Begriffsschrift of 1879, built a formal language for logic, and Giuseppe Peano wrote much of mathematics in logical symbols near the end of the 19th century.
In The Language of Mathematics (2013), Mohan Ganesalingam studied mathematical English with the methods of formal linguistics.
The history of notation is in the topic [[history of mathematical notation and language|F1P7ZSZ6EM]].

### Common errors

1. A student writes a proof as a chain of formulas with no words. The reader cannot tell which lines are assumptions, which are claims and how they follow.
2. A student uses $\Rightarrow$ as a general "so" between calculations. The arrow means "implies" between statements, and it does not say that the first statement is true.
3. A student reads a defined word in its everyday sense, for example "a closed set is a set that is not open". In $\mathbb{R}$, the set $[0, 1)$ is neither open nor closed, and $\mathbb{R}$ is both.
4. A student copies a symbol without its words of introduction, so a letter appears with no meaning given to it.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Mathematics has its own way of speaking.
It uses ordinary words, but some words have a special job.
In a maths lesson, "or" allows both choices, and "the" means there is only one.
It also uses signs, like $+$, $=$ and $<$, which are short ways of writing words.
"$3 + 4 = 7$" is a sentence: "three plus four equals seven".
Learning mathematics is partly learning to read and write this kind of sentence.

### L2 human

We read the theorem "if $p$ is a prime and $p$ divides $ab$, then $p$ divides $a$ or $p$ divides $b$" as a sentence of mathematical English.

1. Find the hidden quantifiers. The letters $p$, $a$ and $b$ have no values, so the theorem holds for every prime $p$ and all integers $a$ and $b$.
2. Find the logical words. "If ... then" gives a conditional, "and" joins two hypotheses, and "or" joins two conclusions.
3. Fix the meaning of "or". It is inclusive, so both conclusions may hold, as for $p = 2$, $a = 2$, $b = 4$.
4. Fix the defined terms. "Prime" and "divides" have exact definitions, and the theorem uses no everyday sense of them.
5. Check one case: $p = 3$, $a = 4$, $b = 6$. Then $3 \mid 24$, and $3 \mid 6$, as the theorem says.

The typical use of this reading is at the start of a proof: once the logical words and the defined terms are clear, the plan of the proof follows from them.

A trap: in step 3, a student with the everyday "or" in mind might think the theorem excludes the case where $p$ divides both.

### L3 human

Ganesalingam argues that mathematical English, unlike natural language in general, can be given a formal analysis, because it is far more restricted in what it says and how.
In his account, the types of mathematical objects, such as numbers, sets and functions, resolve many ambiguities that grammar alone leaves open.
The same idea underlies the controlled natural languages of proof assistants, such as the language of the Naproche system, which read a restricted mathematical English and check it formally.

Other authors stress what a purely formal language loses.
William Thurston wrote that mathematical understanding passes between people through informal language, pictures and examples, not only through formal statements.
The topic [[the language and typography of mathematics|6WAPT044J8]] treats the written form, and the topic [[proof methods|G9JFJGY4TQ]] treats the structure of arguments.
