## Summary

The *full Lambek calculus* $\mathsf{FL}$ is a [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] with no structural rule; see [[the definition|~783dffb5]].
Its connectives are fusion $\cdot$, the two implications $\backslash$ and $/$, meet $\wedge$, join $\vee$ and the constants $1$ and $0$.
Its sequents $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Pi$ have a list $\Gamma$ on the left and at most one formula $\Pi$ on the right.
Its extensions $\mathsf{FL}_e$, $\mathsf{FL}_w$, $\mathsf{FL}_c$ and their combinations add exchange, weakening and contraction.
Its algebras are the [[residuated lattices|E337A6WVH3]] with an extra constant $0$, and this makes $\mathsf{FL}$ the common base of the substructural logics.

## Motivation

Linear logic, relevance logic, the Lambek calculus and many-valued logics look different, but each one is a sequent calculus with a choice of structural rules.
If we drop all structural rules and keep the logical rules, we get one calculus below all of them.
Each logic of the family is then $\mathsf{FL}$ plus some structural rules or axioms, and each one has algebras that are residuated lattices with extra equations.
This gives one proof theory and one algebra for the whole family.

## Notation

The letters after $\mathsf{FL}$ name the added rules: $e$ exchange, $w$ weakening (left and right), $c$ contraction, and $i$ and $o$ for left and right weakening alone.
So $\mathsf{FL}_{ew}$ is $\mathsf{FL}$ with exchange and weakening.
Some authors write $\rightarrow$ for $\backslash$ in $\mathsf{FL}_e$, and $\otimes$ or $*$ for fusion.
The algebras of $\mathsf{FL}$ are called FL-algebras, and those of $\mathsf{FL}_e$, $\mathsf{FL}_w$ and $\mathsf{FL}_{ew}$ are called $\mathsf{FL}_e$-algebras, and so on.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. Type raising $A \Rightarrow (B / A) \backslash B$ is derivable in $\mathsf{FL}$; see [[type raising|~099aed51]].
2. $A \Rightarrow B \backslash A$ is derivable in $\mathsf{FL}_{ew}$ but not in $\mathsf{FL}$; see [[the example|~82bbe429]].
3. $p \cdot q \Rightarrow q \cdot p$ is derivable in $\mathsf{FL}_e$; see [[fusion is commutative in FLe|~115e334f]].
4. The [[Lambek calculus|5FAC9KX4H0]] has the rules of $\mathsf{FL}$ for $\cdot$, $\backslash$ and $/$ only, restricted to nonempty antecedents.
5. Intuitionistic propositional logic is $\mathsf{FL}_{ecw}$, in which fusion and meet prove each other.

### Non-examples

1. $p \Rightarrow p \cdot p$ is not derivable in $\mathsf{FL}_{ew}$, since this needs contraction; see [[the non-example|~e66d9384]].
2. $(p \backslash q) \cdot p \Rightarrow q$ is not derivable in $\mathsf{FL}$.
   In a cut-free derivation the left rule for $\backslash$ needs $p$ to the left of $p \backslash q$, and it is on the right.

### Basic properties

1. $\mathsf{FL}$, $\mathsf{FL}_e$, $\mathsf{FL}_w$ and $\mathsf{FL}_{ew}$ have [[cut elimination|~1050a703]], and so the subformula property and decidability.
2. A sequent is derivable in $\mathsf{FL}$ if and only if it is valid in every FL-algebra; see [[the definition|~783dffb5]].
3. $\mathsf{FL}_e$, $\mathsf{FL}_w$ and $\mathsf{FL}_{ew}$ are complete for the FL-algebras that are commutative, integral with $0$ as least element, or both.

### History

Lambek introduced the calculus with $\cdot$, $\backslash$ and $/$ in 1958, for the syntax of sentences.
The full Lambek calculus adds the lattice connectives and the constants to this calculus.
The book of Galatos, Jipsen, Kowalski and Ono develops substructural logics as extensions of $\mathsf{FL}$, with residuated lattices as their algebras.

### Common errors

1. Confusing the [[Lambek calculus|5FAC9KX4H0]], which has only $\cdot$, $\backslash$ and $/$ and nonempty antecedents, with the full Lambek calculus.
2. Reading $A \backslash B$ as "$A$ divided by $B$".
   It is the implication with hypothesis $A$ on the left: $A, A \backslash B \Rightarrow B$.
3. Thinking that $\wedge$ and $\cdot$ are the same conjunction.
   They coincide only when both weakening and contraction are present.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The full Lambek calculus is a logic with no rules about copying, discarding or reordering hypotheses.
Every other substructural logic is this logic with some of these rules added.
For example, adding the rule "order does not matter" gives a logic for resources, like coins in a purse.
Adding all three rules gives ordinary intuitionistic logic.

### L2 human

We show that $\mathsf{FL}$ derives the residuation law $A \cdot (A \backslash B) \Rightarrow B$.

1. $A \Rightarrow A$ and $B \Rightarrow B$ are axioms.
2. The left rule for $\backslash$ gives $A, A \backslash B \Rightarrow B$.
3. The left rule for fusion gives $A \cdot (A \backslash B) \Rightarrow B$.
4. In a residuated lattice this says $a \cdot (a \backslash b) \leq b$, which holds since $a \backslash b \leq a \backslash b$ and residuation.

The typical use of $\mathsf{FL}$ is as a base: to study a property, such as decidability or interpolation, for all substructural logics at once.
A trap: in step 2, the formula $A$ must stand to the left of $A \backslash B$, and the other order gives a sequent that is not derivable.

### L3 human

The extensions of $\mathsf{FL}$ by axioms form a lattice, and it is dually isomorphic to the lattice of [[varieties|AJNZJD8442]] of FL-algebras.
This makes the study of substructural logics a part of universal algebra.
The Wikipedia article on residuated lattices states that Boolean algebras, Heyting algebras and the integers with $\min$, $\max$, $+$ and $-$ are commutative residuated lattices.
So classical logic, intuitionistic logic and [[abelian logic|W3CPQAFQJ3]] all fit into this algebraic frame.
Proof-theoretic methods such as cut elimination and algebraic methods such as completions then give the same results by two routes.
