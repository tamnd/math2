## Example

Two [[mathematical fallacies|~734fe138]] of geometry take a fact from a figure that is drawn wrongly.
For each one we give the argument, the step that fails, and a proof that it fails.

### Every triangle is isosceles

Let $ABC$ be any triangle.

The argument:

1. Let $\ell$ be the bisector of the angle at $A$, and let $m$ be the [[perpendicular bisector|055VNYT0PB]] of $BC$, with midpoint $D$. Let $O$ be the point where $\ell$ and $m$ meet. The figure shows $O$ inside the triangle.
2. Let $R$ and $Q$ be the feet of the perpendiculars from $O$ to the lines $AB$ and $AC$.
3. The triangles $ARO$ and $AQO$ have equal angles at $A$, right angles at $R$ and $Q$, and the common side $AO$. So they are [[congruent|093EDENVA1]], and $AR = AQ$ and $OR = OQ$.
4. The point $O$ lies on $m$, so $OB = OC$.
5. The right triangles $ORB$ and $OQC$ have $OR = OQ$ and $OB = OC$. So they are congruent, and $RB = QC$.
6. Since $R$ lies between $A$ and $B$, $AB = AR + RB$. Since $Q$ lies between $A$ and $C$, $AC = AQ + QC$.
7. By steps 3 and 5, $AB = AC$. So every triangle is isosceles.

The check:

1. Steps 3, 4 and 5 are valid congruence arguments. They hold wherever $O$, $R$ and $Q$ lie.
2. Step 6 is invalid. The figure puts $O$ inside the triangle and $R$, $Q$ on the sides, but these positions are not proved, and they are false when $AB \neq AC$.
3. We show where $O$ lies when $AB \neq AC$. The line $\ell$ meets the [[circumcircle|03VZJE6C8C]] of $ABC$ again at a point $M$. The [[inscribed angles|0GA1V8NN8C]] $BAM$ and $MAC$ are equal, so the chords $MB$ and $MC$ are equal. So $M$ lies on $m$.
4. The lines $\ell$ and $m$ are different: if they were equal, then $A$ would lie on $m$, and $AB = AC$. Two different lines meet in at most one point, so $O = M$.
5. The point $M$ lies on the arc $BC$ that does not contain $A$. So $O$ lies on the other side of $BC$ from $A$, outside the triangle.
6. The points $A$, $B$, $O$, $C$ lie on one circle in this order, so the opposite angles of the quadrilateral $ABOC$ add to $180^\circ$: $\angle ABO + \angle ACO = 180^\circ$.
7. The two angles are not both right angles. If they were, the right triangles $ABO$ and $ACO$ with the common hypotenuse $AO$ and $OB = OC$ would be congruent, and $AB = AC$.
8. So one of the angles $\angle ABO$, $\angle ACO$ is obtuse. Say $\angle ABO > 90^\circ$. Then the foot $R$ of the perpendicular from $O$ to the line $AB$ lies on the extension of $AB$ beyond $B$.
9. The other angle $\angle ACO$ is acute, and the angle $\angle OAC$ is half of the angle at $A$, so it is acute too. So $Q$ lies between $A$ and $C$.
10. The correct form of step 6 is $AB = AR - RB$ and $AC = AQ + QC$. With steps 3 and 5 this gives $AC - AB = 2RB$, which is not $0$.

A numerical check: for $A = (0.3, 2)$, $B = (0, 0)$, $C = (4, 0)$, the point $O$ is $(2, -1.404)$ to three decimals, below the side $BC$.
The foot $R$ lies beyond $B$ on the line $AB$, and $Q$ lies on the segment $AC$, as steps 8 and 9 say.

### The missing square puzzle

The puzzle uses four pieces on a square grid:

1. a right triangle with legs $8$ and $3$, of area $12$;
2. a right triangle with legs $5$ and $2$, of area $5$;
3. two L-shaped pieces made of $7$ and $8$ unit squares.

The pieces have the total area $12 + 5 + 7 + 8 = 32$.
In the first arrangement they fill a figure that looks like a right triangle with legs $13$ and $5$.
In the second arrangement the same pieces fill the same outline, except for one empty unit square.

The check:

1. A true right triangle with legs $13$ and $5$ has area $\frac{13 \cdot 5}{2} = 32.5$. This is neither $32$ nor $33$, so neither figure is a triangle.
2. The two triangular pieces have slopes $\frac{3}{8} = 0.375$ and $\frac{2}{5} = 0.4$. These are different, so the long "side" of each figure is a bent line, not a straight one.
3. Put the corner at $(0, 0)$ and the far end at $(13, 5)$. In the first figure the bend is at $(8, 3)$. The straight line through $(0, 0)$ and $(13, 5)$ has height $\frac{40}{13} \approx 3.08$ at $x = 8$, so the bend lies below it.
4. The thin triangle with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(8, 3)$ and $(13, 5)$ has area $\frac{1}{2}\left|8 \cdot 5 - 3 \cdot 13\right| = \frac{1}{2}$. So the first figure has area $32.5 - 0.5 = 32$.
5. In the second figure the bend is at $(5, 2)$, and the straight line has height $\frac{25}{13} \approx 1.92$ there, so the bend lies above it. The thin triangle has area $\frac{1}{2}|5 \cdot 5 - 2 \cdot 13| = \frac{1}{2}$, so the second figure has area $32.5 + 0.5 = 33$.
6. So the second figure is larger by $1$, and the extra unit is the "missing" square. No area is lost; the outlines are different.

## Remarks

### Lessons on betweenness and diagrams

In both fallacies, the figure supplies a fact that the argument never proves: that a point lies between two others, or that three points lie on one line.
Euclid's *Elements* used such facts from figures without proof; a [[gap in Euclid's first construction|BRAE42513N]] is of the same kind.
The axioms of [[betweenness|CE8GX6ADZS]] and [[Pasch's axiom|KJT98G16Y4]] in [[Hilbert's axioms|KM1FB8XZ2R]] make these facts provable, and a correct proof must use them.
The safe rule is: a figure may suggest a proof, but each claim of the form "this point is between those points" or "this point is inside the triangle" needs a reason.

### How the examples change

If $AB = AC$, the lines $\ell$ and $m$ are the same line, the point $O$ is not determined, and the conclusion of the false proof is true.
If the puzzle used pieces with equal slopes, such as legs $8, 3$ and $16, 6$, the long side would be straight, and both arrangements would have the same area.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A picture can lie.
In a famous trick, two arrangements of the same four pieces seem to make the same triangle, but one has a hole.
The reason is that the long side is not straight: it bends very slightly, inward in one picture and outward in the other.
The difference between the two pictures is exactly the area of the hole.
In geometry, we prove each fact that we read from a picture, because a small error in the picture can change the answer.

### L2 human

We find the position of $O$ in the isosceles fallacy for a concrete triangle, with coordinates.

1. Take $B = (0, 0)$, $C = (4, 0)$ and $A = (0, 3)$. Then $AB = 3$ and $AC = 5$, so the triangle is not isosceles.
2. The perpendicular bisector of $BC$ is the line $x = 2$.
3. The unit vectors from $A$ toward $B$ and toward $C$ are $(0, -1)$ and $\left(\frac{4}{5}, -\frac{3}{5}\right)$. Their sum $\left(\frac{4}{5}, -\frac{8}{5}\right)$ points along the angle bisector at $A$.
4. The bisector is the set of points $(0, 3) + t\left(\frac{4}{5}, -\frac{8}{5}\right)$. It meets $x = 2$ at $t = \frac{5}{2}$, which gives $O = (2, -1)$.
5. The point $O$ has a negative second coordinate, so it lies below $BC$, outside the triangle.
6. The line $AB$ is the line $x = 0$, so the foot of the perpendicular from $O$ is $R = (0, -1)$. It lies beyond $B$, outside the segment $AB$.

The typical use of such a check is to test a figure on one concrete case before we trust it.
A trap is to draw the figure with $AB$ almost equal to $AC$, where $O$ lies just outside the triangle and looks as if it lies inside.
