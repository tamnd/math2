## Proof

For a real $p \times q$ matrix $M$ with $p \le q$, let $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_p$ be the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix $M M^{\mathsf{T}}$, and put $\sigma_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}$.
They are real by the [[spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]], and $\lambda_k \ge 0$ because $x^{\mathsf{T}} M M^{\mathsf{T}} x = \norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} x}^2 \ge 0$.

Reduction to a matrix problem.

1. The map $a \mapsto Q_1 a$ is a bijection from $\R^p$ onto $U$, and $(Q_1 a) \cdot (Q_1 a') = a^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1 a' = a \cdot a'$. So it keeps norms and orthogonality. The same holds for $c \mapsto Q_2 c$ from $\R^q$ onto $W$.
2. For $u = Q_1 a$ and $w = Q_2 c$, $u \cdot w = a^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2 c = a^{\mathsf{T}} M c$.
3. By steps 1 and 2, the definition of the [[principal angles|~179768d8]] becomes: $\cos \theta_k$ is the maximum of $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c$ over unit vectors $a \in \R^p$ and $c \in \R^q$ with $a \perp a_1, \dots, a_{k-1}$ and $c \perp c_1, \dots, c_{k-1}$, where $(a_i, c_i)$ are the earlier maximizing pairs.

A lemma on the first maximum.

4. For unit $a$ and $c$, the [[Cauchy-Schwarz inequality|0RFWW56Y22]] gives $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c = (M^{\mathsf{T}} a) \cdot c \le \norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a}$.
5. $\norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a}^2 = a^{\mathsf{T}} (M M^{\mathsf{T}}) a \le \lambda_1$, because the [[Rayleigh quotient|06WHP8SE98]] of a symmetric matrix is at most its largest eigenvalue. So $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c \le \sigma_1$.
6. If $\sigma_1 = 0$, then $M^{\mathsf{T}} x = 0$ for all $x$ by step 5, so $M = 0$, and every pair gives the value $0 = \sigma_1$.
7. If $\sigma_1 > 0$, let $a$ be a unit eigenvector of $M M^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $\lambda_1$, and $c = M^{\mathsf{T}} a / \sigma_1$. Then $\norm{c}^2 = \lambda_1 / \sigma_1^2 = 1$ and $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c = \norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a}^2 / \sigma_1 = \sigma_1$. So the maximum over unit vectors is $\sigma_1$.
8. Let $\sigma_1 > 0$ and let $(a, c)$ be unit vectors with $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c = \sigma_1$. Then steps 4 and 5 are equalities. Equality in Cauchy-Schwarz with $\norm{c} = 1$ gives $c = M^{\mathsf{T}} a / \norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a}$, and $\norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a} = \sigma_1$; so $M^{\mathsf{T}} a = \sigma_1 c$.
9. Write $a = \sum_i \alpha_i e_i$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis of $M M^{\mathsf{T}}$. Then $\sum_i \lambda_i \alpha_i^2 = \lambda_1$ and $\sum_i \alpha_i^2 = 1$ force $\alpha_i = 0$ whenever $\lambda_i < \lambda_1$. So $M M^{\mathsf{T}} a = \lambda_1 a$, and $M c = M M^{\mathsf{T}} a / \sigma_1 = \sigma_1 a$.

Part 1, by induction on $p$.

10. Claim: in the problem of step 3, the $k$-th maximum is $\sigma_k$, for any choice of the maximizing pairs. For $k = 1$ this is steps 4 to 7. If $\sigma_1 = 0$, then $M = 0$ by step 6, all maxima are $0$, and all $\sigma_k$ are $0$; the constraint sets are not empty because $k - 1 < p \le q$.
11. Let $\sigma_1 > 0$, and let $(a_1, c_1)$ be the first maximizing pair. By steps 8 and 9, $M c_1 = \sigma_1 a_1$ and $M^{\mathsf{T}} a_1 = \sigma_1 c_1$.
12. Complete $a_1$ to an orthonormal basis $a_1, Y'$ of $\R^p$, and $c_1$ to an orthonormal basis $c_1, Z'$ of $\R^q$, where $Y'$ and $Z'$ are matrices of the other basis vectors. Put $M_1 = Y'^{\mathsf{T}} M Z'$, of size $(p - 1) \times (q - 1)$.
13. In these bases, $a_1^{\mathsf{T}} M c_1 = \sigma_1$, $a_1^{\mathsf{T}} M Z' = \sigma_1 c_1^{\mathsf{T}} Z' = 0$ and $Y'^{\mathsf{T}} M c_1 = \sigma_1 Y'^{\mathsf{T}} a_1 = 0$, by step 11. So $G^{\mathsf{T}} M H = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 \\ 0 & M_1 \end{pmatrix}$ for the orthogonal matrices $G = (a_1 \ Y')$ and $H = (c_1 \ Z')$.
14. Hence $G^{\mathsf{T}} (M M^{\mathsf{T}}) G = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & 0 \\ 0 & M_1 M_1^{\mathsf{T}} \end{pmatrix}$. The eigenvalues of $M M^{\mathsf{T}}$ are $\sigma_1^2$ and the eigenvalues of $M_1 M_1^{\mathsf{T}}$. Since $\sigma_1^2$ is the largest, the $k$-th singular value of $M$ is the $(k - 1)$-th singular value of $M_1$, for $k \ge 2$.
15. The vectors $a \perp a_1$ are $a = Y' \alpha$, and the vectors $c \perp c_1$ are $c = Z' \gamma$; norms and orthogonality are kept, as in step 1. Then $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c = \alpha^{\mathsf{T}} M_1 \gamma$.
16. So maxima $2, \dots, p$ for $M$ are maxima $1, \dots, p - 1$ for $M_1$. By the induction hypothesis for $M_1$, with $p - 1 \le q - 1$, they are the singular values of $M_1$ in order. By step 14 these are $\sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_p$. This proves the claim, and the case $p = 1$ is step 10 with $k = 1$.
17. $\sigma_1 \le 1$: by the [[projection formulas|~680b213e]], $Q_2 Q_2^{\mathsf{T}} = P_W$. For $u = Q_1 a$, step 1 gives $\norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a} = \norm{Q_2^{\mathsf{T}} u} = \norm{Q_2 Q_2^{\mathsf{T}} u} = \norm{P_W u}$, and the [[Pythagorean theorem|0BVTDM872V]] gives $\norm{P_W u} \le \norm{u} = \norm{a}$. With step 5, $\lambda_1 \le 1$. With step 10 and the definition of $\theta_k$, $\cos \theta_k = \sigma_k$. This proves part 1.

Parts 2 to 5.

18. Part 2: let $M = Y \Sigma Z^{\mathsf{T}}$ be a [[singular value decomposition|000NPTMX6J]]. Then $y_k^{\mathsf{T}} M z_k = \sigma_k$, and the $y_k$ and the $z_k$ are orthonormal. By step 3 and part 1, the pairs $(y_k, z_k)$ attain the $k$-th maximum, so $u_k = Q_1 y_k$ and $w_k = Q_2 z_k$ are principal vectors.
19. Part 3: $\theta_k = 0$ if and only if $\sigma_k = 1$, so the number of zero angles is the multiplicity of the eigenvalue $1$ of $M M^{\mathsf{T}} = Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} P_W Q_1$. By the spectral theorem, this is the dimension of $E = \set{a : Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} P_W Q_1 a = a}$.
20. If $a \in E$ and $u = Q_1 a$, then $\norm{P_W u}^2 = a^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} P_W Q_1 a = \norm{a}^2 = \norm{u}^2$, using $P_W^{\mathsf{T}} P_W = P_W$. By Pythagoras, $\norm{u - P_W u}^2 = \norm{u}^2 - \norm{P_W u}^2 = 0$, so $u \in U \cap W$.
21. Conversely, if $u = Q_1 a \in U \cap W$, then $P_W u = u$ and $Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} P_W Q_1 a = Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1 a = a$, so $a \in E$.
22. So $a \mapsto Q_1 a$ maps $E$ onto $U \cap W$, and it is injective by step 1. Hence $\dim E = \dim (U \cap W)$. This proves part 3.
23. Part 4: for $p = 1$, $Q_1 = u$ and $M M^{\mathsf{T}} = \norm{Q_2^{\mathsf{T}} u}^2 = \norm{P_W u}^2$ by step 17. So $\cos \theta_1 = \sigma_1 = \norm{P_W u}$.
24. Part 5: $X = P_U - P_W$ is symmetric, so $\norm{X}_F^2 = \tr(X^{\mathsf{T}} X) = \tr(P_U^2 - P_U P_W - P_W P_U + P_W^2)$ by the definition of the [[Frobenius norm|JT8Q204E4R]].
25. $P_U^2 = P_U = Q_1 Q_1^{\mathsf{T}}$, and the cyclic property of the [[trace|0XC1RDBY7A]] gives $\tr(Q_1 Q_1^{\mathsf{T}}) = \tr(Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1) = \tr(I_p) = p$. In the same way $\tr(P_W^2) = q$.
26. $\tr(P_U P_W) = \tr(Q_1 Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2 Q_2^{\mathsf{T}}) = \tr(Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2 Q_2^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1) = \tr(M M^{\mathsf{T}})$, and $\tr(P_W P_U) = \tr(P_U P_W)$.
27. $\tr(M M^{\mathsf{T}})$ is the sum of its eigenvalues, by the spectral theorem, so it is $\sum_k \sigma_k^2 = \sum_k \cos^2 \theta_k$. Steps 24 to 27 give $\norm{P_U - P_W}_F^2 = p + q - 2 \sum_k \cos^2 \theta_k$.
28. For $p = q$, $2p - 2 \sum_k \cos^2 \theta_k = 2 \sum_k (1 - \cos^2 \theta_k) = 2 \sum_k \sin^2 \theta_k$. This proves part 5.

## Idea

Orthonormal bases turn the geometric problem into the maximization of $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c$ over unit vectors.
The largest value is the largest singular value, and every maximizing pair is a pair of singular vectors.
Removing that pair leaves a smaller matrix whose singular values are the remaining ones, so induction gives all the angles.

## Variants

With the singular value decomposition known in advance, one can write $a = Y \alpha$ and $c = Z \gamma$, so that $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c = \sum_k \sigma_k \alpha_k \gamma_k$.
The bound $\sum_k \sigma_k \alpha_k \gamma_k \le \sigma_1 \sum_k \abs{\alpha_k \gamma_k} \le \sigma_1$ then replaces steps 4 and 5.
