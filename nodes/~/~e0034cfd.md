## Summary

The *ω-rule* is the inference with infinitely many premises $A(\bar 0), A(\bar 1), A(\bar 2), \ldots$ and the conclusion $\forall x \, A(x)$, where $\bar n$ is the numeral for $n$.
The infinitary system $\mathrm{PA}_\infty$ derives finite sets of closed arithmetic formulas, called sequents.
Its axioms are the true literals, and its rules are the rules for $\wedge$, $\vee$ and $\exists$, the ω-rule for $\forall$, and cut.
A derivation is a [[well-founded|0DQPNJNPYK]] tree, and its height is an [[ordinal|0YSCSX5YZC]].
Because the ω-rule makes induction derivable, [[cut elimination|AYRZ98Y0NW]] works for arithmetic, and the heights it produces give the upper bound $\abs{\mathrm{PA}} \le \varepsilon_0$.

## Motivation

In a finite [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] for [[Peano arithmetic|0QFT7Z76N5]], the [[induction axioms|1QD0KDDGYJ]] block cut elimination: a cut against an induction axiom cannot be pushed up.
Gentzen handled this with a delicate reduction procedure on finite proofs.
Replacing the universal quantifier by its infinitely many instances removes the induction axioms altogether, since each instance of induction becomes derivable.
The price is that derivations become infinite trees, and the ordinal heights of these trees take the place of Gentzen's ordinal assignment.

## Notation

We use the one-sided calculus of Tait.
Formulas are built from literals ($s = t$ and $s \ne t$ for closed terms) by $\wedge$, $\vee$, $\forall$ and $\exists$, and $\neg A$ is defined by De Morgan's laws.
The rank $\operatorname{rk}(A)$ counts nested connectives and quantifiers: literals have rank $0$, and each $\wedge$, $\vee$, $\forall$ or $\exists$ adds $1$.

The rules, for a finite set of closed formulas $\Gamma$:

- (Ax) $\Gamma, L$ for a true literal $L$.
- ($\wedge$) From $\Gamma, A$ and $\Gamma, B$ infer $\Gamma, A \wedge B$.
- ($\vee$) From $\Gamma, A$ or from $\Gamma, B$ infer $\Gamma, A \vee B$.
- ($\exists$) From $\Gamma, A(\bar n)$ for one $n$ infer $\Gamma, \exists x \, A(x)$.
- ($\omega$) From $\Gamma, A(\bar n)$ for every $n$ infer $\Gamma, \forall x \, A(x)$.
- (Cut) From $\Gamma, C$ and $\Gamma, \neg C$ infer $\Gamma$.

We write $\vdash^\alpha_\rho \Gamma$ if $\Gamma$ has a derivation of height at most $\alpha$ whose cut formulas all have rank below $\rho$.
So $\vdash^\alpha_0 \Gamma$ means a cut-free derivation.
Some books call the system $Z_\infty$ or $\mathrm{EA}_\infty$, and some use two-sided sequents.

## Remarks

### Examples

- $\forall x \, (x + 0 = x)$ has a cut-free derivation of height $1$: [[one ω-rule over axioms|~06c0db9a]].
- Each closed instance of the induction schema, written as a sequent, has a cut-free derivation of height $\omega + 1$: [[embedding an induction axiom|~fc6c0c7b]].
- Every true closed formula $A$ has a cut-free derivation of finite height at most $\operatorname{rk}(A)$. Choose a true disjunct for $\vee$, a true witness for $\exists$, and all instances for $\forall$.

### Non-examples

- [[Peano arithmetic is not closed under the ω-rule|~35929aa5]]: PA proves each instance of $\operatorname{Con}(\mathrm{PA})$ but not the sentence itself.
- The infinitary logics $L_{\omega_1 \omega}$ of [[infinitary logic|PQG7VAZ1R2]] allow infinitely long formulas; the ω-rule keeps formulas finite and makes only derivations infinite.

### Basic properties

These results are due to Schütte and Tait; we cite them.

1. Soundness and completeness for the standard model: $\vdash^\alpha_\rho A$ for some $\alpha, \rho$ if and only if $A$ is [[true in the standard model|K7XDVK5854]]. Soundness is induction on the derivation, and completeness is the third example above.
2. Embedding: if PA proves $A(x_1, \ldots, x_k)$, then there are finite $m$ and $r$ with $\vdash^{\omega + m}_r A(\bar n_1, \ldots, \bar n_k)$ for all numerals.
3. Cut elimination: if $\vdash^\alpha_{\rho + 1} \Gamma$, then $\vdash^{\beta}_\rho \Gamma$ with $\beta = 2^\alpha$ or $\omega^\alpha$, depending on the book.
4. Bound: applying item 3 $r$ times to a height $\omega + m$ gives a cut-free height below [[epsilon nought|KT50SMH3EC]].
5. Boundedness: a cut-free derivation of induction along a primitive recursive order $\prec$ of height $\alpha$ bounds the order type of $\prec$ by an ordinal computed from $\alpha$. With item 4, $\abs{\mathrm{PA}} \le \varepsilon_0$.

### History

Hilbert considered a rule of this kind in 1931.
Kurt Schütte used the ω-rule in 1951 to give a new proof of Gentzen's results on Peano arithmetic, with ordinal heights in place of Gentzen's reduction procedure.
The one-sided calculus is due to William Tait.

### Common errors

- Treating the ω-rule as a rule of a formal theory in the usual sense. It has infinitely many premises, so a derivation cannot be checked in finitely many steps.
- Confusing the height of a derivation with its size. A derivation of height $1$ can have infinitely many leaves.
- Forgetting that cut elimination raises the height. The bound $\varepsilon_0$ comes from iterating this growth, not from the embedding.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To prove that a statement holds for every natural number, an ordinary proof must give one finite argument that covers all of them.
The ω-rule instead allows infinitely many separate arguments, one for $0$, one for $1$, one for $2$, and so on.
With this rule, every true statement about numbers can be proved, so the rule is too strong to use in practice.
Logicians use it as a tool: they translate ordinary proofs into these infinite proofs and then measure the infinite proofs with ordinals.

### L2 human

We embed and measure a small PA-proof.

1. Take the induction axiom for $A(x) := (x + 0 = x)$. Its closed instances $A(\bar n)$ are true literals.
2. The ω-rule gives $\forall x \, A(x)$ with height $1$, without cuts and without the induction axiom.
3. In general, embedding an induction axiom as a sequent costs height $\omega + 1$, by the [[second example|~fc6c0c7b]].
4. A PA-proof that uses such an axiom once and then one cut of rank $r$ gives $\vdash^{\omega + 2}_{r+1}$.
5. Eliminating cuts of ranks $r, r-1, \ldots, 0$ raises the height $r + 1$ times, for example to a tower of $r + 1$ powers of $2$ over $\omega + 2$, which is still below $\varepsilon_0$.

The typical use is the upper bound in an ordinal analysis: a finite proof becomes an infinite cut-free derivation of controlled height.
The trap: cut-free derivations need not be small; the height grows by one exponential for each level of cut rank removed.

### L3 human

The ω-rule is the first case of a pattern.
Replace a quantifier by its instances, so that the infinitary system is complete for the intended model, and then control derivations by ordinal heights.
For impredicative theories this does not suffice, because cut elimination then needs to collapse derivations of uncountable height; Buchholz's [[Ω-rule|~af6dfa84]] and the [[collapsing functions|~766739ea]] of later analyses play this role.
The ω-rule also connects to ω-consistency and ω-logic, where a theory is closed under the rule, and to reflection principles, which approximate the rule by finite means.
