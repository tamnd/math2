## Example

Let $p$, $q$ and $r$ be propositional variables.
We show that
$$(p \lor q) \to r \;\equiv\; (p \to r) \land (q \to r),$$
that is, the two formulas are [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]].

1. An [[implication|0NMG7W7Q11]] $a \to b$ is false only when $a$ is true and $b$ is false.
2. So $(p \lor q) \to r$ is false exactly when $r$ is false and $p \lor q$ is true. By the definition of [[disjunction|0F72FK8EKB]], $p \lor q$ is true when at least one of $p$, $q$ is true.
3. So the left side is false exactly in the rows TTF, TFF and FTF, where the three letters give the values of $p$, $q$ and $r$.
4. By the definition of [[conjunction|025YZN3VJW]], $(p \to r) \land (q \to r)$ is false exactly when $p \to r$ is false or $q \to r$ is false.
5. The formula $p \to r$ is false exactly when $p$ is true and $r$ is false: rows TTF and TFF. The formula $q \to r$ is false exactly when $q$ is true and $r$ is false: rows TTF and FTF.
6. So the right side is false exactly in the rows TTF, TFF and FTF, the same rows as in step 3.
7. The full [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]] confirms steps 3 and 6.

| $p$ | $q$ | $r$ | $p \lor q$ | $(p \lor q) \to r$ | $p \to r$ | $q \to r$ | $(p \to r) \land (q \to r)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | T | T | T | T | T |
| T | T | F | T | F | F | F | F |
| T | F | T | T | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | F | F | T | F |
| F | T | T | T | T | T | T | T |
| F | T | F | T | F | T | F | F |
| F | F | T | F | T | T | T | T |
| F | F | F | F | T | T | T | T |

8. The fifth and the eighth columns agree in all eight rows, so the two formulas are equivalent.

## Remarks

The example is the logical form of [[proof by cases|0WCP0PVET6]].
To prove that $r$ follows from "$p$ or $q$", we prove that $r$ follows from $p$ and that $r$ follows from $q$.
The same equivalence with $n$ cases reads $(p_1 \lor \cdots \lor p_n) \to r \equiv (p_1 \to r) \land \cdots \land (p_n \to r)$, by induction on $n$.

If the disjunction in the hypothesis becomes a conjunction, the equivalence fails.
The page [[the formulas $(p \land q) \to r$ and $(p \to r) \land (q \to r)$ are not equivalent|~b3065530]] gives the row where they differ.
The correct form for a conjunctive hypothesis is $(p \land q) \to r \equiv (p \to r) \lor (q \to r)$, and a truth table confirms it.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"If it rains or it snows, the match is cancelled" makes two promises.
It says "if it rains, the match is cancelled", and it says "if it snows, the match is cancelled".
The first sentence is broken exactly when it rains or snows and the match goes on.
That is exactly when one of the two smaller promises is broken.
So the one sentence and the pair of sentences say the same thing.

### L2 human

We prove that $n^2 + n$ is even for every integer $n$, with the equivalence as a plan.

1. Let $p$ be "$n$ is even", $q$ be "$n$ is odd", and $r$ be "$n^2 + n$ is even". Each integer is even or odd, so $p \lor q$ is true.
2. By the equivalence, to prove $(p \lor q) \to r$ it is enough to prove $p \to r$ and $q \to r$.
3. Proof of $p \to r$: if $n = 2k$, then $n^2 + n = 2k(2k + 1)$, which is even.
4. Proof of $q \to r$: if $n = 2k + 1$, then $n^2 + n = (2k + 1)(2k + 2) = 2(2k + 1)(k + 1)$, which is even.
5. By steps 2 to 4, $(p \lor q) \to r$ holds, and with step 1, $r$ holds.

The typical use is a proof that splits into cases that cover all possibilities.
A trap is to forget a case: a proof of $p \to r$ alone does not prove $(p \lor q) \to r$.
