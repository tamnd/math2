## Example

Work in a Hilbert system with [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] and the contraction axiom
$$(A \to (A \to B)) \to (A \to B).$$
Add the [[naive comprehension schema|DMB4229W04]] in the form of the two axioms $t \in \set{x \mid \varphi(x)} \to \varphi(t)$ and $\varphi(t) \to t \in \set{x \mid \varphi(x)}$.
Let $p$ be any formula, let $c = \set{x \mid x \in x \to p}$, and write $C$ for $c \in c$.
Then $p$ is derivable.

1. Comprehension with $\varphi(x) = (x \in x \to p)$ and $t = c$ gives $C \to (C \to p)$.
2. The contraction axiom with $A = C$ and $B = p$ gives $(C \to (C \to p)) \to (C \to p)$.
3. Modus ponens on steps 1 and 2 gives $C \to p$.
4. Comprehension in the other direction gives $(C \to p) \to C$.
5. Modus ponens on steps 3 and 4 gives $C$.
6. Modus ponens on steps 5 and 3 gives $p$.

## Remarks

The derivation uses no negation, so it applies to logics in which explosion fails.
The only axiom of the derivation that is not comprehension is the contraction axiom in step 2.
In a logic without contraction, such as [[affine logic|K67C2N2QAH]], this derivation is blocked.
In sequent form, step 2 is the rule CL: from $C, C \Rightarrow p$ infer $C \Rightarrow p$.
The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on relevance logic gives this argument and lists Grišin, Brady, Restall and Priest among those who drop contraction.
The relevance logic $\mathbf{R}$ has the contraction axiom, so naive comprehension trivializes it as well.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Consider the sentence "if this sentence is true, then the moon is made of cheese".
If the sentence is true, then the moon is made of cheese, by what it says.
So the sentence is true, since this is exactly what it says.
So the moon is made of cheese.
The second line uses the first assumption twice, and this double use is contraction.

### L2 human

We check where step 2 uses a hypothesis twice, in natural deduction form.

1. Assume $C$.
2. From $C$ and $C \to (C \to p)$ we get $C \to p$.
3. From $C$ again and $C \to p$ we get $p$.
4. We discharge the assumption $C$ and get $C \to p$.
   The assumption was used twice, in lines 2 and 3.

The typical use of this example is to motivate set theories and truth theories over logics without contraction.
A trap: dropping only the law of excluded middle does not block the paradox, since the derivation does not use it.
