## Statement

Let $L = \set{P_1, \ldots, P_k}$ be a signature of $k$ unary relation symbols, and let $\sigma$ be an $L$-sentence of the [[monadic predicate calculus|SERN6CRVWW]] with quantifier rank $q$.

1. If $\sigma$ has no equality symbol and $\sigma$ has a model, then $\sigma$ has a model with at most $2^k$ elements.
2. If $\sigma$ may contain equality and $\sigma$ has a model, then $\sigma$ has a model with at most $\max(q, 1) \cdot 2^k$ elements.
3. Consequently, there is an algorithm that decides whether a monadic sentence is [[satisfiable|QN7M1JCGT9]], and an algorithm that decides whether it is [[valid|5SDS9T8E7J]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The relation symbols must be unary. With a binary symbol $<$, the sentence "$<$ is a strict linear order without a greatest element" has a model but no finite model; see [[a sentence with only infinite models|~27146afc]].
- There must be no function symbols. With one unary function symbol $f$, the sentence $\forall x \, \forall y \, (f(x) = f(y) \to x = y) \wedge \exists y \, \forall x \, (f(x) \neq y)$ says that $f$ is injective and not surjective, which is impossible on a finite set.
- The bound $2^k$ needs the absence of equality. The sentence $\exists x \, \exists y \, (x \neq y)$ has $k = 0$, so $2^k = 1$, but each model has at least $2$ elements.

### Consequences

1. The validity problem of the monadic predicate calculus is decidable. This is a positive solution of the [[Entscheidungsproblem|~58802204]] for this fragment.
2. For monadic sentences, satisfiability and satisfiability in a finite structure are the same, so the monadic case of [[Trakhtenbrot's theorem|~3e65890a]] fails.
3. Each syllogism of traditional logic can be checked by a finite computation.

### Converse

The converse is false: the finite model property does not imply that the signature is monadic.
The [[decidable prefix classes|~48b4c091]], such as the Bernays-Schönfinkel-Ramsey class, and the [[two-variable fragment|~d6f83b8b]] allow binary relations and still have the finite model property.

### Generalizations

The bounds are exact.
For $q \ge 1$, the sentence $\bigwedge_{\tau} \exists x_1 \cdots \exists x_q \, (\bigwedge_{i < j} x_i \neq x_j \wedge \bigwedge_{j} \tau(x_j))$, over all $2^k$ types $\tau$, has rank $q$ and needs $q$ elements of each type, so $q \cdot 2^k$ elements in all.
Without equality, the sentence $\bigwedge_\tau \exists x \, \tau(x)$ needs one element of each of the $2^k$ types.
Monadic second-order logic over a signature of unary predicates alone also has a decision procedure.

## History

Löwenheim proved the decidability of the monadic fragment in 1915.
Behmann gave another proof in 1922, by elimination of quantifiers.
The argument by types and counting, given here, is the standard proof in modern textbooks.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose a statement talks about two properties, such as "red" and "round".
Every object belongs to one of four kinds: red and round, red only, round only, or neither.
If the statement has a world that makes it true, then we keep one object of each kind and throw the rest away.
The statement cannot tell the difference, so the small world with at most four objects also makes it true.
If the statement can say "different", we keep a few objects of each kind, as many as the statement can count.

### L2 human

We decide the validity of $\sigma = \exists x \, (P(x) \to \forall y \, P(y))$, with $k = 1$ and no equality.

1. $\sigma$ is valid if and only if $\neg \sigma$ has no model.
2. $\neg \sigma$ has no equality, so by part 1 it suffices to check models with at most $2^1 = 2$ elements.
3. On one element $a$: if $a \in P$, then $P(a) \to \forall y \, P(y)$ is true; if $a \notin P$, then $P(a)$ is false and the implication is true. So $\sigma$ is true.
4. On two elements $a, b$ with $P = \set{a, b}$: $\forall y \, P(y)$ is true, so $\sigma$ is true.
5. On two elements with $P \neq \set{a, b}$: some element, say $b$, is not in $P$, and the implication at $x = b$ is true. So $\sigma$ is true.
6. So $\neg \sigma$ has no model of size at most $2$, hence no model at all, and $\sigma$ is valid.

The typical use of the theorem is to turn a question about all structures into a finite search.
A trap is to apply the bound $2^k$ to a sentence with equality: the bound is then $\max(q, 1) \cdot 2^k$.

### L3 human

The proof is an instance of a general principle: a sentence of quantifier rank $q$ cannot distinguish structures that agree up to $q$ in every counting question, which is the content of the [[Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game|2RE7YAZK8R]].
The same principle gives the finite model property of the [[two-variable fragment|~d6f83b8b]], where the role of the types is taken by the 1-types and 2-types.
The decision procedure here searches through structures whose size is exponential in $k$, so it is a brute-force method, useful for proofs and small cases.
