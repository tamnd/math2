## Example

Let $\mathcal{N} = (\N, <)$ with the usual order, and let $\varphi$ be the formula $x < 1$, written with a constant symbol $1$ interpreted as the number $1$.
We show that neither $\varphi$ nor $\neg \varphi$ is [[true|~58a589d1]] in $\mathcal{N}$.

1. A formula is true in $\mathcal{N}$ when every assignment satisfies it.
2. Let $s_0$ be an assignment with $s_0(x) = 0$. Then $0 < 1$, so $\mathcal{N} \models \varphi[s_0]$, by the clause for atomic formulas of [[satisfaction|~cf18e0d7]].
3. Let $s_5$ be an assignment with $s_5(x) = 5$. Then $5 < 1$ is false, so $\mathcal{N} \not\models \varphi[s_5]$.
4. By step 3, not every assignment satisfies $\varphi$, so $\varphi$ is not true in $\mathcal{N}$.
5. By step 2 and the clause for $\neg$, we get $\mathcal{N} \not\models \neg \varphi[s_0]$. So $\neg \varphi$ is not true in $\mathcal{N}$ either.

The condition that fails is that $\varphi$ is not a [[sentence|DKFGQTRYZE]], so its satisfaction depends on the assignment.

## Remarks

The example shows that bivalence, "exactly one of $\psi$ and $\neg \psi$ is true", holds for sentences but not for formulas.
It shows also why "$\mathcal{M} \not\models \varphi$" and "$\mathcal{M} \models \neg \varphi$" differ for formulas.
The two sentences that come from $\varphi$ behave as expected.

- The universal closure $\forall x \, (x < 1)$ is false in $\mathcal{N}$, by step 3.
- The existential closure $\exists x \, (x < 1)$ is true in $\mathcal{N}$, by step 2.

In the structure $(\set{0}, <)$, the formula $x < 1$ becomes true, because the only assignment value is $0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"$x$ is less than $1$" is not a full statement, because we do not know $x$.
If $x$ is $0$ it is true, and if $x$ is $5$ it is false.
So we cannot call it true, and we cannot call it false.
Statements such as "every $x$ is less than $1$" or "some $x$ is less than $1$" have no unknown left, and they are false and true.

### L2 human

We compare three readings of $x < 1$ in $\mathcal{N}$.

1. Under one assignment: it holds when $s(x) = 0$ and fails otherwise.
2. As a formula that is true in $\mathcal{N}$: it would need all assignments, and $s(x) = 5$ fails, so it is not true.
3. As the set that it defines: it defines the set $\set{0}$.

The typical use of the third reading is to define sets and relations by formulas.
A trap is to say "$x < 1$ is false in $\N$" and then conclude that $\neg (x < 1)$ is true; the assignment $x \mapsto 0$ refutes that conclusion.
