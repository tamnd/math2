## Proof

Write $R = F[x]$ and $M_A = F^n_A$ for the [[module of $A$|~772e5994]].

1. (a) implies (b). If $B = P^{-1} A P$ with $P \in GL_n(F)$, then $xI - B = P^{-1} (xI - A) P$. The matrices $P$ and $P^{-1}$ are invertible over $R$, so $xI - A$ and $xI - B$ are equivalent.
2. (b) is equivalent to (c). By the [[Smith normal form theorem|MV28Z5N9XX]], each matrix over the principal ideal domain $R$ is equivalent to exactly one Smith normal form with monic entries. Equivalence is an equivalence relation, so two matrices are equivalent if and only if they have the same Smith normal form.
3. Presentation lemma. Define $\pi \colon R^n \to M_A$ by $\pi(\sum_j f_j e_j) = \sum_j f_j(A) e_j$. It is $R$-linear, by the properties of the [[evaluation map|0FCCDXH97W]], and it is surjective, since it maps the constant vectors onto $F^n$.
4. Let $K = (xI - A) R^n$, the submodule spanned by the columns $x e_j - \sum_i a_{ij} e_i$ of $xI - A$. Then $\pi(x e_j - \sum_i a_{ij} e_i) = A e_j - \sum_i a_{ij} e_i = 0$. So $K \subseteq \ker \pi$.
5. Modulo $K$, $x e_j \equiv \sum_i a_{ij} e_i$, a constant vector. By induction on $k$, $x^k e_j$ is congruent to a constant vector modulo $K$. So $R^n / K$ is spanned over $F$ by the classes of $e_1, \dots, e_n$, and $\dim_F R^n / K \le n$.
6. The map $\pi$ induces a surjective $F$-linear map $R^n / K \to F^n$. Since $\dim_F F^n = n \ge \dim_F R^n/K$, this map is a bijection by [[dimension|03GSZ8RTZJ]]. So $\ker \pi = K$, and $M_A \cong R^n / (xI - A) R^n$ as $R$-modules.
7. (b) implies (a). Let $U (xI - A) V = xI - B$ with $U, V$ invertible over $R$. Since $V$ is invertible, $(xI - A) V R^n = (xI - A) R^n$. So multiplication by $U$ maps $(xI - A) R^n$ onto $U (xI - A) V R^n = (xI - B) R^n$.
8. Multiplication by $U$ is an $R$-module automorphism of $R^n$. By step 7 it induces an isomorphism $R^n / (xI - A) R^n \cong R^n / (xI - B) R^n$.
9. By step 6, $M_A \cong M_B$. By part 4 of the [[dictionary theorem|~3b7dc48a]], $A$ and $B$ are similar. This proves part 1.
10. Part 2. Let $S = U (xI - A) V = \operatorname{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_n)$ be the Smith normal form, with $s_i$ monic or zero and $s_i \mid s_{i+1}$.
11. Up to a nonzero constant, $\det S = \det U \det(xI - A) \det V$, and $\det(xI - A) = \chi_A \ne 0$ is monic. So all $s_i$ are nonzero, and $s_1 \cdots s_n = \chi_A$.
12. By steps 7 and 8 with $S$ in place of $xI - B$, $M_A \cong R^n / S R^n = R/(s_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(s_n)$. A summand with $s_i = 1$ is $0$.
13. So $M_A \cong \bigoplus_{s_i \ne 1} R/(s_i)$, with nonconstant monic $s_i$ that divide each other. By the uniqueness in the [[structure theorem|TXMV0YDACG]] and the [[cyclic decomposition theorem|~168ae65b]], these $s_i$ are the invariant factors $d_1, \dots, d_r$, and the other $n - r$ entries are $1$. This proves part 2.
14. Part 3. Let $D_k(M)$ be the monic gcd of the $k \times k$ minors of $M$. By the [[Cauchy-Binet formula|08WE2W7DYS]], each $k \times k$ minor of $UM$ or of $MV$ is an $R$-linear combination of $k \times k$ minors of $M$. So $D_k(M)$ divides $D_k(UMV)$.
15. Applying step 14 to $U^{-1}$ and $V^{-1}$ gives the converse divisibility, so $D_k(xI - A) = D_k(S)$, both monic.
16. For the diagonal matrix $S$ with $s_1 \mid \cdots \mid s_n$, a nonzero $k \times k$ minor is a product $s_{i_1} \cdots s_{i_k}$ with distinct indices, and each such product is divisible by $s_1 \cdots s_k$, which is itself a minor. So $D_k(S) = s_1 \cdots s_k$.
17. Hence $s_k = D_k / D_{k-1}$ for $1 \le k \le n$, with $D_0 = 1$. And $D_n = \det(xI - A) = \chi_A$, the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]] being the only $n \times n$ minor. This proves part 3.

## Idea

The characteristic matrix $xI - A$ presents the module $F^n_A$: its columns are the relations $x e_j = A e_j$.
Equivalent presentation matrices present isomorphic modules, and isomorphic modules mean similar matrices.
The Smith normal form then reads off the invariant factors, and gcds of minors compute it.

## Variants

Hoffman and Kunze prove part 1 by elementary operations on $xI - A$ in their section on the computation of invariant factors, without the module language.
Another route to (b) implies (a): write $U = U_0 + U_1 x + \cdots$, divide $U$ by $xI - B$ on the right and $V$ by $xI - A$ on the left, and compare constant terms.
This is the classical argument with matrix polynomials.
