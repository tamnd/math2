## Example

We test the axiom $\mathrm{T}$, the formula $\square p \to p$, under several readings of the box of [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]].
A reading accepts $\mathrm{T}$ when every instance of $\square p \to p$ is a law under that reading.
For the readings that reject $\mathrm{T}$, we use the [[Kripke model|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]] $\mathfrak{M}$ with
$$W = \set{w, v}, \quad R = \set{(w, v)}, \quad V(p) = \set{v}.$$

1. Alethic reading, "it is necessary that $p$". What is necessary is true, so the reading accepts $\mathrm{T}$.
2. Epistemic reading, "the agent knows that $p$". Knowledge is factive: a known proposition is true. So the reading accepts $\mathrm{T}$.
3. Doxastic reading, "the agent believes that $p$". An agent can believe a false proposition, so the reading rejects $\mathrm{T}$.
4. Deontic reading, "it is obligatory that $p$". Obligations can be violated, so the reading rejects $\mathrm{T}$. Deontic logic uses the weaker axiom $\mathrm{D}$, $\square p \to \lozenge p$: what is obligatory is permitted.
5. Provability reading, "$\mathsf{PA}$ proves $p$". For the sound theory $\mathsf{PA}$, each instance is true in $\N$. But $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove all instances, and the [[provability logic|WJK0YA4GP2]] $\mathsf{GL}$ does not contain $\square p \to p$.
6. Temporal reading, "$p$ holds at all later times". With strictly later times, $p$ can hold in the whole future and fail now. So this reading rejects $\mathrm{T}$.
7. Temporal reading with "now or later". Then the present time is included, and the reading accepts $\mathrm{T}$.
8. The countermodel. The only successor of $w$ is $v$, and $v \in V(p)$, so $\mathfrak{M}, w \models \square p$. Since $w \notin V(p)$, $\mathfrak{M}, w \not\models p$.
9. So $\square p \to p$ fails at $w$, as the rejecting readings predict.

## Remarks

The example shows that one formula can be a law under one reading of the box and fail under another.
The frame condition explains the split: by [[common modal axiom schemata|FDFQFWPM4X]], $\mathrm{T}$ is valid on a frame exactly when the relation is [[reflexive|0X8NMST0J2]].
In the countermodel, $w$ does not see itself.
If we add the pair $(w, w)$ to $R$, then $\square p$ becomes false at $w$, and $\mathrm{T}$ holds at both worlds.
In the strict temporal reading, the relation is "earlier than", which is irreflexive, so $\mathrm{T}$ fails.
In the reading "now or later", the relation is "earlier than or equal", which is reflexive.
For the provability reading, see [[S4 is not the provability logic of PA|AXB7DEGR2D]]: the logic of provability in $\mathsf{PA}$ is $\mathsf{GL}$, and it refutes $\mathrm{T}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The box can mean "necessarily", "known", "believed", "obligatory", "provable" or "always in the future".
The rule "if box $p$, then $p$" says that a boxed statement is true.
This is right for knowledge: if Ann knows it is raining, then it is raining.
It is wrong for belief: Ann can believe it is raining on a dry day.
It is also wrong for duty: a rule can say "pay the bill", and the bill can stay unpaid.

### L2 human

1. Read $\square p$ as "Bob believes $p$" and $p$ as "the train is late".
2. Bob believes that the train is late, so $\square p$ is true.
3. The train is on time, so $p$ is false.
4. Then $\square p \to p$ is false, so a logic of belief must not contain $\mathrm{T}$.

The typical use is to choose the axioms of a logic from the intended reading, and then the class of frames from the axioms.
A trap: under the provability reading, each instance of $\square p \to p$ is true in $\N$, but truth in $\N$ is not provability in $\mathsf{PA}$.
