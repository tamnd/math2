## Example

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$ over $\R$.

1. For $A$: $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$ gives $(0, 0, 1)$; then $R_1 \leftarrow R_1 - 3R_2$ gives $(1, 2, 0)$.
2. So the [[RREF|0NNR68BFRY]] of $A$ is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
3. For $B$: $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1$ gives $(0, 0, 1)$, and the result $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is already in RREF.
4. The RREFs agree, so $A$ and $B$ are [[row equivalent|095DDGZF16]].
5. For $C$: $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$ gives $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, which is in RREF.
6. This RREF differs from that of $A$, so by the [[uniqueness of the RREF|BF9SMZMJZE]], $C$ is not row equivalent to $A$.
7. An explicit sequence from $A$ to $B$: the two steps of step 1, then the inverse of step 3, $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 + R_1$, which turns $(0, 0, 1)$ into $(1, 2, 1)$.

## Remarks

The test needs the uniqueness theorem: without it, two different RREFs might still be row equivalent.
The rank also separates $A$ and $C$: $\rank A = 2$ and $\rank C = 1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To check whether two tables can be turned into each other by row moves, simplify both as far as possible.
If the simplest forms agree, the answer is yes; if not, the answer is no.

### L2 human

1. Reduce each matrix to its RREF.
2. Compare the RREFs entry by entry.
3. For a sequence from the first matrix to the second, reduce the first, then undo the steps of the second in reverse order.

A trap: comparing echelon forms that are not reduced can give the wrong answer, since a matrix has many echelon forms.
