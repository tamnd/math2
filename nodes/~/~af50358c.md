## Example

Consider the sentence: "an integer $n$ is even if $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$".
It looks like a theorem "if $H$, then $C$", with $H$ the condition "$n = 2k$ for some integer $k$" and $C$ the claim "$n$ is even".
We check that it is a definition, and that it does not have a [[hypothesis and a conclusion|~e44ddea6]] in the sense of a theorem.

1. Before the sentence, the word "even" has no meaning in the text. So the sentence cannot assert a fact about even numbers: it gives the meaning.
2. Nothing in the sentence needs a proof. A reader cannot check it against anything, because it fixes the use of a new word.
3. The sentence is used in both directions. From $6 = 2 \cdot 3$ we conclude that $6$ is even, which is the direction "if".
4. In [[this proof|~20e36a6d]] we pass from "$m$ is even" to "$m = 2a$ for some integer $a$". This is the direction "only if", which a one-way conditional would not give.
5. To show that $7$ is not even, we also need the direction "only if". The equation $7 = 2k$ gives $k = 3.5$, which is not an integer, so $7$ is not even.
6. So the "if" of the definition means "if and only if". The sentence is a definition, not a conditional statement with a hypothesis and a conclusion.

## Remarks

The example shows a convention of mathematical English: in a definition, "if" stands for "if and only if".
Some authors write "iff", "exactly when" or ":$\Leftrightarrow$" in definitions, and some set the defined word in italic type, to make the convention visible.
In a theorem, "if" keeps its one-way meaning, and the converse of the theorem needs its own proof.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The sentence "a number is even if it is two times a whole number" is a definition.
It tells us what the word "even" means.
It is not a fact that needs a proof, and it works both ways: even numbers are exactly the numbers of the form two times a whole number.
This is why we can say that $7$ is not even: $7$ is not two times a whole number.

### L2 human

We sort three sentences into definitions and theorems.

1. "A prime is an integer $p > 1$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$." This is a definition, and it works in both directions.
2. "If $p$ is a prime greater than $2$, then $p$ is odd." This is a theorem with the hypothesis "$p$ is a prime greater than $2$" and the conclusion "$p$ is odd". Its converse is false, because $9$ is odd and not prime.
3. "An integer $n$ is odd if $n = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$." This is a definition, so its "if" means "if and only if".

The typical use of this reading is to unfold a definition in both directions inside a proof.
A trap is to treat the "if" in a theorem as "if and only if", as in sentence 2.
