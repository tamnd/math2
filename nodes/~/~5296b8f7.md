## Example

The formula $((p \to q) \to q) \to p$ is valid in [[abelian logic|~92c8d795]], but it is not a classical [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]].
Its [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]], with $\to$ as material implication, is:

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \to q$ | $(p \to q) \to q$ | $((p \to q) \to q) \to p$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | T |
| F | T | T | T | F |
| F | F | T | F | T |

1. In the third row, $p$ is false and $q$ is true.
2. Then $p \to q$ is true, and $(p \to q) \to q$ is true.
3. So $((p \to q) \to q) \to p$ is "true implies false", which is false.

## Remarks

So abelian logic is not contained in classical logic.
The SEP entry on many-valued logic calls such a logic contraclassical.
Classical logic is not contained in abelian logic either: $p \to (q \to p)$ is a tautology, but its abelian value is $-v(q)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In classical logic, if $q$ is true, then $(p \to q) \to q$ is true whatever $p$ is.
So it cannot force $p$ to be true.

### L2 human

We check the fourth row, where both atoms are false.

1. $p \to q$ is "false implies false", which is true.
2. $(p \to q) \to q$ is "true implies false", which is false.
3. The whole formula is "false implies false", which is true.

The typical use of this formula is to show that a logic is not a fragment of classical logic.
A trap: the formula $(p \to q) \to q$ alone is classically equivalent to $p \vee q$, not to $p$.
