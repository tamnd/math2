## Statement

Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, let $U$ be a subspace of $V$, and let $\pi \colon V \to V / U$ be the [[quotient map|0J0SK5FF4M]].
Then the maps
$$X \mapsto \pi(X) = X / U, \qquad Y \mapsto \pi^{-1}(Y)$$
are inverse bijections between the [[subspaces|0EPTJA5QV4]] $X$ of $V$ with $U \subseteq X$ and the subspaces $Y$ of the [[quotient space|0SVR29SV1V]] $V / U$.
Both maps preserve inclusion.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $X$ must contain $U$. For $X = \{0\}$ in $\R^2$ and $U$ the $x$-axis, $\pi(X) = \{0\}$, and $\pi^{-1}(\{0\}) = U \ne X$. So $X \mapsto \pi(X)$ is not injective on all subspaces.
- $\pi$ must be the quotient map, or at least surjective. For a non-surjective linear map, $\pi(\pi^{-1}(Y))$ can be smaller than $Y$.

### Consequences

- The maps also preserve sums and intersections: $(X_1 + X_2)/U = X_1/U + X_2/U$ and $(X_1 \cap X_2)/U = X_1/U \cap X_2/U$.
- In finite dimension, $\dim(X / U) = \dim X - \dim U$, by the [[dimension of a quotient|0SXDQ6TN00]].
- With the [[third isomorphism theorem|07RB3EMBWW]], $(V / U)/(X / U) \cong V / X$ for each such $X$.
- A subspace $Y$ of $V / U$ is a hyperplane exactly when $\pi^{-1}(Y)$ is a hyperplane of $V$.

### Converse

The two maps are mutually inverse, so each of them determines the other.

## History

The correspondence theorem for groups is often attributed to Noether, who stated the isomorphism theorems in general form in the 1920s.
It is also called the fourth isomorphism theorem or the lattice theorem.
The analogue for groups is the [[correspondence theorem|0S9ZVZ34MD]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The subspaces of a quotient $V / U$ are the quotients of the subspaces of $V$ that contain $U$.
Nothing new appears in the quotient, and nothing that contains $U$ is lost.

### L2 human

Let $U$ be the $z$-axis in $\R^3$, so $V / U \cong \R^2$.

1. The subspaces of $\R^2$ are $\{0\}$, the lines through $0$, and $\R^2$.
2. Their preimages are $U$ itself, the planes that contain the $z$-axis, and $\R^3$.
3. For example, the line $y = 2x$ in $\R^2$ corresponds to the plane $y = 2x$ in $\R^3$.

The trap is to look for subspaces of $V / U$ that come from subspaces not containing $U$: they give the same quotients as their sum with $U$.

### L3 human

The theorem says that the interval $[U, V]$ in the lattice of subspaces of $V$ is isomorphic to the lattice of subspaces of $V / U$.
It holds for modules over any ring, for normal subgroups of groups, and for ideals of rings.
Mathlib states it as the order isomorphism `Submodule.comapMkQRelIso`.
