## Example

We compute the [[standard translation|~ff160d1c]] $\mathrm{ST}_x(\square\lozenge p)$ and check it on a model.
The clauses are $\mathrm{ST}_x(p) = Px$, $\mathrm{ST}_x(\neg\psi) = \neg\mathrm{ST}_x(\psi)$ and $\mathrm{ST}_x(\square\psi) = \forall y\, (Rxy \to \mathrm{ST}_y(\psi))$.
For $\mathrm{ST}_y$ the roles of $x$ and $y$ are swapped.
We read $\lozenge p$ as $\neg\square\neg p$, as in [[the definition of modal formulas|G8VGTW9P4F]].

1. $\mathrm{ST}_x(\square\lozenge p) = \forall y\, (Rxy \to \mathrm{ST}_y(\lozenge p))$, by the box clause at $x$.
2. $\mathrm{ST}_y(\lozenge p) = \mathrm{ST}_y(\neg\square\neg p) = \neg\mathrm{ST}_y(\square\neg p)$.
3. $\mathrm{ST}_y(\square\neg p) = \forall x\, (Ryx \to \mathrm{ST}_x(\neg p)) = \forall x\, (Ryx \to \neg Px)$, by the box clause at $y$.
4. So $\mathrm{ST}_y(\lozenge p) = \neg\forall x\, (Ryx \to \neg Px)$, which is equivalent to $\exists x\, (Ryx \wedge Px)$.
5. Hence $\mathrm{ST}_x(\square\lozenge p)$ is equivalent to $\forall y\, (Rxy \to \exists x\, (Ryx \wedge Px))$.
6. The only free variable is the outer $x$. The inner $\exists x$ binds a new occurrence.
7. The reuse is correct: inside $\exists x$, the formula speaks about $y$ and its successors only, and the old value of $x$ is no longer needed.

Now take the [[Kripke model|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]] with $W = \set{1, 2, 3}$, $R = \set{(1, 2), (1, 3), (2, 2), (3, 1)}$ and $V(p) = \set{2}$.

8. Modal side. $\lozenge p$ is true at $1$ (successor $2$), true at $2$ (successor $2$) and false at $3$ (only successor $1$).
9. So $\square\lozenge p$ is false at $1$, since its successor $3$ fails $\lozenge p$.
10. It is true at $2$, since $2$ is its only successor, and true at $3$, since $1$ is its only successor.
11. First-order side at $x = 1$. The values $y = 2$ and $y = 3$ satisfy $R1y$.
12. For $y = 2$, the witness $x = 2$ gives $R22 \wedge P2$. For $y = 3$, the only $x$ with $R3x$ is $1$, and $P1$ is false.
13. So the translation is false at $x = 1$.
14. At $x = 2$, the only $y$ is $2$, with witness $2$. At $x = 3$, the only $y$ is $1$, with witness $2$.
15. So the translation is true at $2$ and $3$, and false at $1$, as on the modal side.

## Remarks

The agreement in step 15 is an instance of [[the correctness of the standard translation|~a9fa4297]].
Two variables suffice for every modal formula, by the same alternation of $x$ and $y$.
A translation with a fresh variable at each box, $\forall y\, (Rxy \to \exists z\, (Ryz \wedge Pz))$, is equivalent but uses three variables.
The quantifiers are guarded: each quantifier ranges over the successors of the current world only.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The modal formula says: "wherever you go in one step, you can go one more step and find $p$".
The translation says the same thing in the language of points and arrows.
The name $x$ is reused for the second step, like a reused box in a form.
We checked on three points that both say yes or no at the same places.

### L2 human

1. Translate the outer box: $\forall y\, (Rxy \to \dots)$.
2. Translate the inner diamond at $y$: $\exists x\, (Ryx \wedge Px)$.
3. Plug the second into the first.

The typical use is to read a modal formula as a first-order property of a point.
A trap: reusing $x$ is safe only because the outer $x$ does not occur in the scope of the inner $\exists x$.

### L3 human

The image of the translation lies in the two-variable fragment and in the guarded fragment of first-order logic.
Both fragments are decidable, which explains part of the good behaviour of modal logic.
