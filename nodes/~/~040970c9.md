## Example

Let $\mathcal{Q} = (\Q, <)$.
A structure on the domain $\Q$ is a *reduct of $\mathcal{Q}$ up to definability* when each of its relations is [[definable|CY1TXHF6KQ]] without parameters in $\mathcal{Q}$.
Two structures on $\Q$ are *interdefinable* when each relation of one is $\emptyset$-definable in the other.
We define three relations on $\Q$, for distinct elements only.

- Betweenness: $B(x, y, z)$ when $x < y < z$ or $z < y < x$.
- Circular order: $C(x, y, z)$ when $x < y < z$ or $y < z < x$ or $z < x < y$, that is, when the increasing order of the three elements is a cyclic shift of $(x, y, z)$.
- Separation: $S(x, y, z, w)$ when exactly one of $y$ and $w$ lies between $x$ and $z$.

We check that $(\Q, <)$, $(\Q, B)$, $(\Q, C)$, $(\Q, S)$ and $(\Q, =)$ (the empty signature) are reducts of $\mathcal{Q}$ up to definability, and that no two of them are interdefinable.
We use one fact: an [[automorphism|DSQ17TNW1C]] of a structure maps each $\emptyset$-definable relation onto itself.

1. The definitions of $B$ and $C$ are quantifier-free formulas in $<$. The relation $S$ is defined by $(B(x, y, z) \land \neg B(x, w, z)) \lor (B(x, w, z) \land \neg B(x, y, z))$ with $x, y, z, w$ distinct, so it is definable in $(\Q, B)$ and hence in $\mathcal{Q}$.
2. $S$ is also definable in $(\Q, C)$, by $(C(x, y, z) \land C(x, z, w)) \lor (C(x, w, z) \land C(x, z, y))$. On a circle this says that going around from $x$ we meet $y, z, w$ or $w, z, y$ in this order, so $x$ and $z$ separate $y$ from $w$.
3. The map $\rho(x) = -x$ preserves $B$, because $y$ lies between $x$ and $z$ exactly when $-y$ lies between $-x$ and $-z$. It does not preserve $<$, since $0 < 1$ and $0 > -1$. So $<$ is not definable in $(\Q, B)$.
4. Split $\Q$ at $\sqrt{2}$ into $P = \set{q < \sqrt{2}}$ and $P' = \set{q > \sqrt{2}}$. Each is a countable dense order without endpoints, so by [[Cantor's theorem|ABN5PTXEHY]] there are order isomorphisms $P \to P'$ and $P' \to P$. Together they give a bijection $f \colon \Q \to \Q$.
5. $f$ preserves $C$. Inside each piece $f$ keeps the order, and $f$ puts the image of $P'$ below the image of $P$. So the increasing order of $f(x), f(y), f(z)$ is a cyclic shift of the increasing order of $x, y, z$, and a cyclic shift of a cyclic shift is a cyclic shift.
6. $f$ does not preserve $B$. We have $B(0, 1, 2)$ with $0, 1 \in P$ and $2 \in P'$. Then $f(2) \in P$ and $f(0) < f(1)$ in $P'$, so $f(2) < f(0) < f(1)$, and $f(1)$ is not between $f(0)$ and $f(2)$. So $B$ is not definable in $(\Q, C)$.
7. $\rho$ does not preserve $C$: $C(0, 1, 2)$ holds, and $-2 < -1 < 0$ is the order $(z, y, x)$, which is not a cyclic shift of $(x, y, z)$. So $C$ is not definable in $(\Q, B)$ or in $(\Q, S)$, since $\rho$ preserves $B$ and hence $S$.
8. $f$ preserves $S$, since $S$ is defined from $C$; so $B$ is not definable in $(\Q, S)$, by step 6.
9. The bijection $\tau$ that swaps $1$ and $2$ and fixes all other elements does not preserve $S$: $S(0, 1, 2, 3)$ holds, while $S(0, 2, 1, 3)$ fails, because neither $2$ nor $3$ lies between $0$ and $1$. Every bijection is an automorphism of $(\Q, =)$, so $S$ is not definable in $(\Q, =)$.

So the definitions go only downward: $<$ defines $B$ and $C$, both $B$ and $C$ define $S$, and $B$ and $C$ do not define each other.
No relation higher in this pattern is definable from a lower one.

## Remarks

Peter Cameron proved in 1976 that this list is complete: every reduct of $(\Q, <)$ up to definability is interdefinable with one of the five structures.
The proof uses the fact that $(\Q, <)$ is $\aleph_0$-categorical, so a reduct up to definability is determined by its automorphism group.
The five automorphism groups are the order-preserving bijections, the bijections that preserve or reverse the order, the rotations of the circle, the rotations and reflections, and all bijections of $\Q$.
The same method gives five reducts of the countable random graph, a result of Simon Thomas.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of the rational numbers on a line, with the order "less than".
We can forget part of this information.
If we forget which way the line points, we still know which point is between two others.
If we bend the line into a circle, we still know the order around the circle, but not where it starts.
If we forget both, we know only which pairs of points separate each other on the circle.
If we forget everything, we have only a set of points.
Cameron's theorem says that there are no other ways to forget part of the order.

### L2 human

We check one instance of step 5 with numbers.

1. Take $x = 0$, $y = 1$, $z = 3$, so $C(0, 1, 3)$ holds with $0 < 1 < 3$.
2. Here $0, 1 \in P$ and $3 \in P'$.
3. Then $f(3) \in P$ lies below $f(0)$ and $f(1)$, which lie in $P'$ with $f(0) < f(1)$.
4. So $f(3) < f(0) < f(1)$, the order $(z, x, y)$, which is a cyclic shift of $(x, y, z)$. So $C(f(0), f(1), f(3))$ holds.

The typical use of automorphisms is to show that a relation is not definable.
A trap is to use the method in the other direction: an automorphism-invariant relation need not be definable in general, and it is definable here only because $(\Q, <)$ is $\aleph_0$-categorical.
