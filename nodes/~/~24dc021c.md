## Example

Let $U = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
We check that $U \in U(2)$, and we write it as a diagonal phase matrix times an element of $SU(2)$.

1. $U^* = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and $U^*U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & (-i)(i) \end{pmatrix} = I_2$. So $U$ is [[unitary|0D6RMJ10M9]].
2. $\det U = 0 \cdot 0 - i \cdot 1 = -i$. So $\abs{\det U} = 1$, but $U \notin SU(2)$.
3. Let $V = \operatorname{diag}(i, 1)\,U = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \cdot i \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. $\det V = \det\operatorname{diag}(i, 1)\det U = i \cdot (-i) = 1$, and $V$ is a product of unitary matrices. So $V \in SU(2)$.
5. $\operatorname{diag}(i, 1)^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(-i, 1)$. So $U = \operatorname{diag}(-i, 1)\,V$.
6. This is the form $U = \operatorname{diag}(\det U, 1)\,V$ with $V$ in the [[special unitary group|~f67e0a03]].

## Remarks

- The same method works for each $U \in U(n)$: multiply by $\operatorname{diag}(\overline{\det U}, 1, \dots, 1)$ to get determinant $1$.
- A second method uses a scalar: $\det(cU) = c^2\det U$. With $c = e^{i\pi/4}$, $c^2 = i$ and $\det(cU) = 1$. This gives $U = \overline{c}\,(cU)$ with $cU \in SU(2)$.
- The scalar method needs an $n$-th root of $\det U$, so it is not unique: $c$ and $-c$ both work here.
- $V = R(\pi/2)$ is also a real rotation, an element of $SO(2) \subseteq SU(2)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Each unitary matrix splits into two parts.
One part is a single turn of the complex plane in the first coordinate.
The other part is a unitary matrix with determinant $1$.
Here the second part is the quarter turn of the real plane.

### L2 human

The worked computation is in the Example.
The typical use: questions about $U(n)$ often reduce to questions about $SU(n)$ and one phase.
A trap: the phase factor does not commute with $V$ in general, so $\operatorname{diag}(-i, 1)V \neq V\operatorname{diag}(-i, 1)$ here.
