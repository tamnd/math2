## Example

1. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$. Both rows are nonzero. The second column is twice the first, so the [[rank|0EWQEYRVA8]] is $1$, not $2$.
2. Subtracting $2$ times row 1 from row 2 gives the [[echelon form|0F27PBRWD7]] with rows $(1, 2)$ and $(0, 0)$. Only now does the count of nonzero rows give the rank $1$.
3. Let $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Its diagonal entries are both $0$, but its column space is spanned by $(1, 0)$. So $\rank N = 1$, not $0$.
4. Let $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Both diagonal entries are nonzero, but the two columns are equal. So $\rank M = 1$, not $2$.

## Remarks

- The number of nonzero rows gives the rank only for a matrix in echelon form.
- The number of nonzero diagonal entries gives the rank only for a diagonal matrix.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The rank counts independent rows, not nonzero rows.
Two nonzero rows can still be multiples of each other.

### L2 human

Row reduction is needed before the nonzero rows can be counted.
For triangular matrices with zeros on the diagonal, the rank can still be positive.
