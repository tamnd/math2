## Example

The first proposition of Book I of [[Euclid's Elements|83WHDAV68J]] constructs an equilateral triangle on a given segment $AB$.
We follow the proof and find the step that the axioms do not support.

1. Euclid draws the [[circle|060W5CBZP6]] with centre $A$ through $B$, and the circle with centre $B$ through $A$. His third postulate allows a circle with any centre and any radius.
2. Euclid takes a point $C$ where the two circles meet. No postulate and no common notion of Euclid says that the two circles meet.
3. Then $AC = AB$, because $C$ and $B$ lie on the circle with centre $A$. Also $BC = BA$, because $C$ and $A$ lie on the circle with centre $B$.
4. So $AB$, $AC$ and $BC$ are equal, and the triangle $ABC$ is equilateral. This step uses the common notion that things equal to the same thing are equal to each other.

Steps 1, 3 and 4 follow from Euclid's postulates, his definition of a circle and his common notions.
Step 2 does not, and a model shows that the gap is real.

5. Take as "plane" the set $\mathbb{Q}^2$ of points with rational coordinates. Let $A = (0, 0)$ and $B = (1, 0)$, so $AB = 1$.
6. The circle with centre $A$ is $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, and the circle with centre $B$ is $(x - 1)^2 + y^2 = 1$.
7. If a point $(x, y)$ lies on both circles, we subtract the equations: $x^2 - (x - 1)^2 = 0$, so $2x - 1 = 0$ and $x = \frac{1}{2}$.
8. Then $y^2 = 1 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4}$, so $y = \pm \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.
9. The number $\sqrt{3}$ is [[irrational|080T2TXVQV]]. If $y$ were rational, then $\sqrt{3} = \pm 2y$ would be rational. So $y$ is not rational.
10. Hence the two circles have no common point in $\mathbb{Q}^2$, although each circle contains infinitely many points of $\mathbb{Q}^2$, such as $(1, 0)$ and $(\frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5})$ on the first circle.

## Remarks

The example is a non-example of the [[axiomatic method|~fde3e74a]]: a step that the figure suggests is used as if it were an axiom.
It does not show that Euclid's result is false.
In the real plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ the two circles meet at $(\frac{1}{2}, \pm\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2})$, and the triangle exists.
It shows that the existence of the meeting point needs an assumption of its own.
Modern systems state such an assumption.
In [[Hilbert's axioms|KM1FB8XZ2R]] the axioms of continuity give it, and other systems add an explicit axiom that two circles meet under suitable conditions.
The same kind of gap appears in other places of the *Elements*, for example in facts about the order of points on a line, which Hilbert covered with his axioms of betweenness.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To build a triangle with three equal sides, Euclid draws two circles around the ends of a segment and takes the point where they cross.
The picture shows the crossing point at once.
But none of Euclid's starting rules says that two circles must cross.
If we only allow points whose coordinates are fractions, the two circles of radius $1$ around $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$ do not cross.
The crossing points would need the number $\sqrt{3}$, which is not a fraction.

### L2 human

We check step 10: the point $(\frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5})$ is on the first circle, and it is not on the second.

1. First circle: $(\frac{3}{5})^2 + (\frac{4}{5})^2 = \frac{9}{25} + \frac{16}{25} = 1$.
2. Second circle: $(\frac{3}{5} - 1)^2 + (\frac{4}{5})^2 = \frac{4}{25} + \frac{16}{25} = \frac{20}{25} \neq 1$.

So the circles in $\mathbb{Q}^2$ are not empty, and the gap is only at the meeting point.
The typical use of such a model is to show that a statement does not follow from a list of assumptions.
A trap is to say that the model $\mathbb{Q}^2$ satisfies "all of Euclid's geometry".
The claim here is smaller: no postulate of Euclid says that the circles meet, and in $\mathbb{Q}^2$ they do not.
