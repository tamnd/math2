## Example

The [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$ is the group of all permutations of $\{1, 2, 3\}$ under composition.
We compose from right to left: $\sigma\tau$ means first $\tau$, then $\sigma$.
The entry in row $\sigma$ and column $\tau$ of the [[Cayley table|0YB2NRP0T5]] is $\sigma\tau$.

| $\circ$ | $e$ | $(1\,2\,3)$ | $(1\,3\,2)$ | $(1\,2)$ | $(1\,3)$ | $(2\,3)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $e$ | $e$ | $(1\,2\,3)$ | $(1\,3\,2)$ | $(1\,2)$ | $(1\,3)$ | $(2\,3)$ |
| $(1\,2\,3)$ | $(1\,2\,3)$ | $(1\,3\,2)$ | $e$ | $(1\,3)$ | $(2\,3)$ | $(1\,2)$ |
| $(1\,3\,2)$ | $(1\,3\,2)$ | $e$ | $(1\,2\,3)$ | $(2\,3)$ | $(1\,2)$ | $(1\,3)$ |
| $(1\,2)$ | $(1\,2)$ | $(2\,3)$ | $(1\,3)$ | $e$ | $(1\,3\,2)$ | $(1\,2\,3)$ |
| $(1\,3)$ | $(1\,3)$ | $(1\,2)$ | $(2\,3)$ | $(1\,2\,3)$ | $e$ | $(1\,3\,2)$ |
| $(2\,3)$ | $(2\,3)$ | $(1\,3)$ | $(1\,2)$ | $(1\,3\,2)$ | $(1\,2\,3)$ | $e$ |

We check one entry.
In row $(1\,2)$ and column $(1\,3)$, the permutation $(1\,3)$ acts first.
It sends $1$ to $3$, and then $(1\,2)$ keeps $3$.
It sends $3$ to $1$, and then $(1\,2)$ sends $1$ to $2$.
It keeps $2$, and then $(1\,2)$ sends $2$ to $1$.
So the product sends $1$ to $3$, $3$ to $2$ and $2$ to $1$, and it is $(1\,3\,2)$.

## Remarks

The table is a Latin square, as for each finite [[group|G5S1M9]].
The table is not symmetric about the main diagonal.
For example, the entry in row $(1\,2)$, column $(1\,3)$ is $(1\,3\,2)$, but the entry in row $(1\,3)$, column $(1\,2)$ is $(1\,2\,3)$.
So $S_3$ is not [[abelian|082GHNPJ53]].
It is the smallest group that is not abelian, because the groups of order at most $5$ are all abelian.
The upper left $3 \times 3$ block contains only the rotations $e$, $(1\,2\,3)$ and $(1\,3\,2)$, so these three elements form a group of order $3$ inside $S_3$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of three cups in a row, numbered $1$, $2$ and $3$.
There are six ways to rearrange them, and each rearrangement is an element of $S_3$.
The table shows what happens when we do one rearrangement and then another.
The order matters: doing the swaps in the other order can give a different result.

### L2 human

We read inverses and a solution of an equation from the table.

1. In row $(1\,2\,3)$, the identity $e$ is in column $(1\,3\,2)$. So $(1\,2\,3)^{-1} = (1\,3\,2)$.
2. In row $(1\,2)$, the identity is in column $(1\,2)$. So $(1\,2)$ is its own inverse, and the same holds for the other two transpositions.
3. To solve $(1\,2) x = (1\,2\,3)$, we look in row $(1\,2)$ for the entry $(1\,2\,3)$. It is in column $(2\,3)$, so $x = (2\,3)$.

A trap is to exchange rows and columns: since the table is not symmetric, this gives $\tau\sigma$ in place of $\sigma\tau$.
