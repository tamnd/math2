## Example

Let $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a function, and consider three statements about $f$.

- $p$: $f$ is [[differentiable|0K57YG3BB8]] at every point of $\mathbb{R}$.
- $q$: $f$ is continuous at every point of $\mathbb{R}$.
- $r$: the restriction of $f$ to $[0, 1]$ is Riemann integrable.

We check that the argument "$p \to q$, $q \to r$, therefore $p \to r$" is an instance of [[hypothetical syllogism|7MT8CPCEM4]] with true premises.

1. The premise $p \to q$ is true: a function differentiable at a point is continuous at that point, by the theorem that [[differentiable functions are continuous|07ZMX4ANJS]]. Apply it at each point of $\mathbb{R}$.
2. The premise $q \to r$ is true. If $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$, its restriction to $[0, 1]$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$, and [[continuous functions on a closed bounded interval are Riemann integrable|06SESV7GQY]].
3. The consequent $q$ of the first premise is the antecedent of the second premise, word for word. So the form is $\varphi \to \psi$, $\psi \to \chi$ with $\varphi = p$, $\psi = q$, $\chi = r$.
4. Hypothetical syllogism gives $p \to r$: every function differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ is Riemann integrable on $[0, 1]$.
5. For instance, $f(x) = x\lvert x\rvert$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, with $f'(x) = 2\lvert x\rvert$, so $\int_0^1 f$ exists; its value is $\int_0^1 x^2\,dx = 1/3$.

## Remarks

The example shows how a theorem is built from two known theorems, without any new estimate.
Neither link can be reversed: $\lvert x\rvert$ is continuous but not differentiable at $0$, and the function equal to $0$ on $[0, 1/2]$ and $1$ elsewhere is integrable on $[0, 1]$ but not continuous.
So the conclusion $p \to r$ is also not reversible, and its [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] is false for the same step function.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A smooth curve has no corners, and a curve without corners has no jumps.
A curve without jumps on $[0, 1]$ has an area under it.
Putting the two steps together: a smooth curve on $[0, 1]$ has an area under it.

### L2 human

We use the chain in a typical exercise.

1. Claim: $\int_0^1 e^{x^2}\,dx$ exists.
2. The function $e^{x^2}$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, by the chain rule. This is $p$.
3. Link 1 gives $q$: $e^{x^2}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
4. Link 2 gives $r$: $e^{x^2}$ is Riemann integrable on $[0, 1]$.

The typical use is to skip the middle statement once both links are known theorems.
A trap is to read the chain backwards: a function that is integrable on $[0, 1]$ need not be continuous, and a continuous function need not be differentiable.
