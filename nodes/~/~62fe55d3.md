## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be an [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]].

1. If $S \colon V \to V$ is linear and $S T = T S$, then $\ker S$ and $\im S$ are [[$T$-invariant|0ACX5QT8TE]].
2. For each polynomial $p \in F[x]$, the subspaces $\ker p(T)$ and $\im p(T)$ are invariant under $q(T)$ for every $q \in F[x]$, in particular under $T$.
3. If $U$ and $W$ are $T$-invariant subspaces, then $U + W$ and $U \cap W$ are $T$-invariant.

Here $p(T)$ is the [[value of $p$ at $T$|0E3MV6E6MG]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- In part 1, the maps must commute. Let $T(x, y) = (y, 0)$ and $S(x, y) = (0, x)$ on $F^2$. Then $\ker S = F e_2$, but $T(e_2) = e_1$ does not lie in $\ker S$. Here $ST \ne TS$, since $ST(e_2) = e_2$ and $TS(e_2) = 0$.
- In part 2, the polynomial must be evaluated at $T$ itself. The kernel of a map that is not a polynomial in $T$ and does not commute with $T$ need not be invariant, as in the first item.

### Consequences

- Each [[eigenspace|0G0RFPG8HR]] $E_\lambda = \ker(T - \lambda \id_V)$ is $T$-invariant.
- Each [[generalized eigenspace|0AJ035ZTZE]] $\ker(T - \lambda \id_V)^k$ is $T$-invariant.
- The kernels $\ker T^k$ and the images $\im T^k$ are $T$-invariant, which is the start of the [[Fitting decomposition|~c7b346ae]].
- If $ST = TS$, then each eigenspace of $S$ is $T$-invariant. This is the main step of [[simultaneous diagonalization|0FK17N4DKA]].

### Converse

The converse of part 2 is false: an invariant subspace need not be a kernel or an image of a polynomial in $T$.
For $T = \id_V$ with $\dim V = 2$, every line is invariant, but each $p(T) = p(1) \id_V$ has kernel $0$ or $V$.

### Generalizations

The same proof works for modules over a commutative ring and for any family of commuting maps.
For a commuting family $\mathcal{F}$, the kernel of each member of $\mathcal{F}$ is invariant under all of $\mathcal{F}$.

## History

This fact is part of the standard theory of invariant subspaces, as in Hoffman and Kunze, Section 6.4.
We do not know of a first source.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose two machines act on the same space, and the order in which they act does not matter.
Then the vectors that the first machine sends to zero form a region that the second machine never leaves.
A polynomial in a map, such as $T^2 - 3T$, always commutes with the map.
So its kernel is a region that $T$ never leaves.

### L2 human

Let $T(x, y, z) = (x + y, y, 2z)$ on $\R^3$, and let $p(x) = (x - 1)^2$.

1. $(T - \id)(x, y, z) = (y, 0, z)$.
2. $(T - \id)^2(x, y, z) = (0, 0, z)$.
3. So $\ker p(T)$ is the plane $z = 0$.
4. $T(x, y, 0) = (x + y, y, 0)$ lies in the plane, as the statement predicts.

The typical use is to find invariant subspaces without guessing: take kernels of factors of the minimal polynomial.
The trap is to apply part 1 to a map $S$ that does not commute with $T$.

### L3 human

In the language of modules, $V$ is an $F[x]$-module through $T$, and each $p(T)$ is a module endomorphism.
Kernels and images of module endomorphisms are submodules, which is the statement.
More generally, the commutant of $T$ acts on the lattice of invariant subspaces, and the kernels of elements of the commutant are submodules.
