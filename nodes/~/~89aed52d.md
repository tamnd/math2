## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $B = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ and $B' = (b'_1, \dots, b'_n)$ be two bases of $V$.
Then for all $v_1, \dots, v_n \in V$, the [[determinant in a basis|0V94H674MB]] satisfies
$$\det_B(v_1, \dots, v_n) = \det_B(B') \cdot \det_{B'}(v_1, \dots, v_n).$$
In particular, $\det_B(B') \cdot \det_{B'}(B) = 1$, and $\det_{B'} = \det_{B'}(B) \cdot \det_B$ as functions on $V^n$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $B$ and $B'$ must both be bases, because $\det_B$ and $\det_{B'}$ are defined only for bases.
- The families must have exactly $n = \dim V$ vectors, so that the coordinate matrices are square.
- A field is used for convenience; the rule holds for free modules of rank $n$ over a commutative ring, with the same proof.

### Consequences

- The forms $\det_B$ for the different bases $B$ differ only by nonzero scalar factors.
- So the condition $\det_B(v_1, \dots, v_n) \ne 0$ does not depend on $B$.
- Over $\mathbb{R}$, the sign of $\det_B(B')$ decides whether $B$ and $B'$ have the same [[orientation|0QJJAQ6ZYZ]].

### Converse

The factor is forced: if $\det_B = c \cdot \det_{B'}$ for a scalar $c$, then evaluation at $B'$ gives $c = \det_B(B')$.

### Generalizations

For a linear map $T \colon V \to V$, one has $\det_B(T v_1, \dots, T v_n) = \det T \cdot \det_B(v_1, \dots, v_n)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A determinant of $n$ vectors depends on the basis that we use as the unit.
If we change the unit, every determinant changes by the same factor.
The factor is the determinant of the new basis measured in the old one.

### L2 human

In $\mathbb{R}^2$, let $E$ be the standard basis and $B = ((2, 0), (0, 1))$.

1. $\det_E(B) = 2$.
2. For $v = ((1, 2), (3, 4))$, $\det_E(v) = 4 - 6 = -2$.
3. The rule gives $-2 = 2 \cdot \det_B(v)$, so $\det_B(v) = -1$.

The [[worked example|~c479a81c]] checks this value with coordinates.
The typical use is to move a volume computation to a convenient basis.
A trap: the factor is $\det_B(B')$, the new basis in the old coordinates, not its inverse.

### L3 human

The forms $\det_B$ are the nonzero elements of the line of alternating $n$-linear forms on $V$.
The rule says that two nonzero elements of a line differ by a nonzero scalar, and it names the scalar.
