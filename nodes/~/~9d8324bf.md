## Example

For real numbers, $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$, the case $n = 2$ of the [[binomial theorem|0EHWPWGHB2]].
We test the same formula for $2 \times 2$ matrices with [[matrix multiplication|032Q9FB8MT]].
Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. The products are $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. So $AB \neq BA$.
2. The sum is $A + B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, and its square is $(A + B)^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. The squares are $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. The right side of the formula is $A^2 + 2AB + B^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 4 + 1 & 2 + 2 + 0 \\ 0 + 2 + 2 & 1 + 2 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 4 \\ 4 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.
5. The matrices of steps 2 and 4 differ in two entries. So the formula $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + 2AB + B^2$ is false for these matrices.
6. The correct expansion uses only the distributive law: $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2$.
7. Check: $A^2 + AB + BA + B^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 & 2 + 1 + 1 + 0 \\ 0 + 1 + 1 + 2 & 1 + 1 + 2 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, which agrees with step 2.

## Remarks

The proof of the formula for numbers has one step that uses [[commutativity|099E72KTF8]]: it replaces $ab + ba$ by $2ab$.
Matrices satisfy all the other rules of that proof, but not $AB = BA$.
So the generalization from numbers to matrices drops a hypothesis that the proof needs, and the formula fails.
This is a non-example of a correct generalization.

The formula does hold for two matrices that commute.
For example, if $B = A$, then $AB = BA = A^2$, and $(2A)^2 = 4A^2 = A^2 + 2A^2 + A^2$.
In each ring the binomial theorem $(a + b)^n = \sum_{k = 0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a^k b^{n - k}$ holds for commuting elements $a$ and $b$.
So the correct generalization is "to each ring, for commuting elements", not "to each ring".

## Explanations

### L1 human

For ordinary numbers, $3 \cdot 5$ and $5 \cdot 3$ are the same.
The square formula $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ uses this fact: it counts $ab$ and $ba$ as two copies of the same thing.
For matrices, the order of a product matters, and $AB$ and $BA$ can differ.
So the square formula breaks for matrices, and we must write $AB + BA$ in place of $2AB$.

### L2 human

We find the exact condition for the formula to hold for two matrices $A$ and $B$ of the same size.

1. By the distributive law, $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2$.
2. The formula claims $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + 2AB + B^2$.
3. Subtract the two lines: the formula holds if and only if $BA = AB$.

The typical use of this check is a habit: before a familiar algebraic rule is used in a new setting, list the axioms that its proof needs.
A trap is that the formula holds for many matrices, for example for diagonal matrices, so a test with diagonal matrices does not reveal the problem.
