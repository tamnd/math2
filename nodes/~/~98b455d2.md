## Statement

A *finite matrix* is a finite set $M$ with a subset $D$ of designated values and an operation on $M$ for each connective $\bot$, $\wedge$, $\vee$, $\to$.
A formula is a *tautology* of the matrix if it takes a designated value under every map from the variables to $M$.
A matrix is *characteristic* for $\mathsf{IPC}$ if its tautologies are exactly the theorems of $\mathsf{IPC}$.

For $n \geq 1$, let $D_n$ be the disjunction of all $p_i \leftrightarrow p_j$ with $0 \leq i < j \leq n$, in $n + 1$ variables.

**Theorem.**

1. If a matrix with at most $n$ elements has every theorem of $\mathsf{IPC}$ as a tautology, then $D_n$ is a tautology of it.
2. $\mathsf{IPC} \nvdash D_n$. In fact $D_n$ fails at the root of the [[Kripke model|~11b532e9]] on a chain of $n$ worlds.
3. So no finite matrix is characteristic for [[intuitionistic propositional logic|Q11N8X99FK]].

The proof is in [[the proof by pigeonhole|~2e1bd2a2]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. Finiteness is needed: the Lindenbaum algebra of $\mathsf{IPC}$, with $1$ designated, is an infinite characteristic matrix; see [[Heyting algebra semantics|~32a20f35]].
2. Item 1 needs that the matrix validates all of $\mathsf{IPC}$. The pigeonhole step turns $D_n$ into a substitution instance of a theorem, and only validity of that theorem is used.

### Consequences

1. Let $G_m$ be the set of tautologies of the $m$-element chain, a Heyting algebra with top designated. By items 1 and 2, $D_m \in G_m \setminus G_{m+1}$.
2. So $G_2 \supsetneq G_3 \supsetneq G_4 \supsetneq \cdots$ is an infinite descending chain of [[intermediate logics|~57af80cc]]. Here $G_2$ is classical logic.
3. The intersection of all $G_m$ is [[Gödel-Dummett logic|~cfc28e48]], not $\mathsf{IPC}$, since every chain validates $(p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$.

### Converse

$\mathsf{IPC}$ is characterized by an infinite family of finite matrices.
By the finite model property in [[Kripke completeness|~ac029000]], the Heyting algebras of up-sets of finite rooted frames form such a family.
Jaśkowski gave an explicit infinite sequence of finite matrices with this property.

### Generalizations

1. The same proof shows that an intermediate logic that contains no $D_n$ has no finite characteristic matrix.
2. Examples are $\mathsf{LC}$ and its sublogic $\mathsf{KC}$: each $D_n$ fails in a finite chain, which is a frame of both.

## History

Gödel proved the theorem in 1932, in a short note that uses the finite chains as matrices.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Classical logic can be checked with a table of two truth values.
Gödel showed that no finite table of truth values does the same for intuitionistic logic.
The reason: with more statements than values, two statements must share a value, and then "they are equivalent" holds.
But intuitionistic logic does not prove that two of many statements must be equivalent.

### L2 human

1. Take $n = 2$ and $D_2 = (p_0 \leftrightarrow p_1) \vee (p_0 \leftrightarrow p_2) \vee (p_1 \leftrightarrow p_2)$.
2. In the two-element Boolean algebra, two of the three variables get the same value, so $D_2$ is a classical tautology.
3. In the chain of two worlds $u \leq v$, put $p_0$ true nowhere, $p_1$ true at $v$, and $p_2$ true everywhere.
4. The three truth sets differ, so at $u$ no equivalence $p_i \leftrightarrow p_j$ holds, and $D_2$ fails at $u$.

The typical use of the theorem is to show that a logic needs infinite semantics.
A trap: $D_n$ is a tautology of each matrix with at most $n$ elements only when the matrix validates $\mathsf{IPC}$; in an arbitrary matrix the operations can be anything.

### L3 human

The argument is a pigeonhole principle on truth values combined with substitution invariance.
It shows that the variety of Heyting algebras is not generated by one finite algebra.
In contrast, every variety generated by a finite algebra is locally finite, and its logic is tabular, that is, the logic of one finite frame.
