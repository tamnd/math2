## Example

Let $V = \R^2$ with the usual addition $(x, y) + (x', y') = (x + x', y + y')$.
Define a scalar multiplication by $a \cdot (x, y) = (ax, 0)$ for $a \in \R$.
We check each axiom of the [[definition|05EGHEQZAW]] of a [[vector space|000AYPA9AJ]].

1. Closure: $(ax, 0) \in \R^2$, so the new multiplication maps into $V$.
2. Axioms 1 to 4 concern only the addition, which is the usual one. They hold, with zero $(0, 0)$ and $-(x, y) = (-x, -y)$.
3. Axiom 5: $a \cdot (b \cdot (x, y)) = a \cdot (bx, 0) = (abx, 0)$, and $(ab) \cdot (x, y) = (abx, 0)$. It holds.
4. Axiom 6 fails: $1 \cdot (0, 1) = (0, 0)$, which is not $(0, 1)$.
5. Axiom 7: $a \cdot ((x, y) + (x', y')) = (a(x + x'), 0)$, and $a \cdot (x, y) + a \cdot (x', y') = (ax + ax', 0)$. It holds.
6. Axiom 8: $(a + b) \cdot (x, y) = ((a + b)x, 0)$, and $a \cdot (x, y) + b \cdot (x, y) = (ax + bx, 0)$. It holds.

So $V$ with these operations is not a vector space, and the only axiom that fails is axiom 6.

## Remarks

The example shows that the unit law $1v = v$ does not follow from the other seven axioms.
Without axiom 6, the multiplication could even be $a \cdot v = 0$ for all $a$ and $v$.
If we change the rule to $a \cdot (x, y) = (ax, ay)$, we get the usual vector space $\R^2$.
If we change it to $a \cdot (x, y) = (ax, y)$, then axiom 6 holds, but axiom 8 fails: $(1 + 1) \cdot (0, 1) = (0, 1)$, while $1 \cdot (0, 1) + 1 \cdot (0, 1) = (0, 2)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

This rule for scaling throws away the second entry of every vector.
Scaling by $1$ should change nothing, but here it turns $(0, 1)$ into $(0, 0)$.
All other rules still work, so this one rule must be listed as an axiom.

### L2 human

We test the failing axiom and one axiom that holds, with $v = (2, 3)$.

1. Axiom 6: $1 \cdot (2, 3) = (2, 0) \ne (2, 3)$.
2. Axiom 8 with $a = 1$, $b = 2$: $3 \cdot (2, 3) = (6, 0)$, and $(2, 0) + (4, 0) = (6, 0)$.

A trap: checking only the axioms that "look interesting" can miss a failure; every axiom must be checked.
