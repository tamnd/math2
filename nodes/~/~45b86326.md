## Example

In [[independence-friendly logic|DPMKT2KRB8]] consider the sentence
$$\varphi := \forall x\, (\exists y / \set{x})\, x = y$$
in a structure $\mathcal{M}$ with domain $M$ and empty vocabulary.
We show that $\varphi$ is neither true nor false when $M$ has at least two elements, and that $\varphi$ is true when $M$ has one element.

1. In the game for $\varphi$, Abelard first chooses $a \in M$ as the value of $x$. Then Eloise chooses $b \in M$ as the value of $y$, and she wins if and only if $a = b$.
2. The slash $/ \set{x}$ hides the value of $x$ from Eloise. Her strategy must be uniform: it gives the same $b$ in all plays that differ only in the value of $x$. So a uniform strategy of Eloise is a constant $b \in M$.
3. Let $M$ have at least two elements, and let Eloise play the constant $b$. Abelard chooses $a \neq b$ and wins. So Eloise has no winning strategy, and $\varphi$ is not true.
4. Abelard moves first and sees nothing, so a strategy of Abelard is a constant $a \in M$. Against it, the strategy $b = a$ of Eloise wins. So Abelard has no winning strategy, and $\varphi$ is not false.
5. By steps 3 and 4, $\varphi$ is undetermined in every structure with at least two elements.
6. If $M = \set{e}$, then $a = b = e$ in every play, so Eloise wins with $b = e$, and $\varphi$ is true.

## Remarks

The example shows the third truth value of IF logic.
In first-order logic the game has perfect information and finite length, so one of the two players always has a winning strategy.
Here the hidden move breaks this.

The law of excluded middle fails with it.
In $\varphi \lor \neg \varphi$, Eloise first chooses a disjunct.
In $\varphi$ she has no winning strategy by step 3.
In $\neg \varphi$ the players swap roles: Eloise chooses $x$, Abelard chooses $y$ blind to $x$, and Eloise wins if $x \neq y$.
Each strategy of Eloise is a constant $x = a$, and it loses against the constant strategy $y = a$ of Abelard.
So $\varphi \lor \neg \varphi$ is not true in a structure with two elements.

The Skolem form of $\varphi$ is $\exists c\, \forall x\, x = c$, since the Skolem function of $y$ has no arguments.
So $\varphi$ is true exactly in the one-element structures.
Without the slash, $\forall x \exists y\, x = y$ is true in every structure, since $y$ may copy $x$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

One player hides a coin in the left hand or the right hand.
The other player must name the hand, but cannot see which hand holds the coin.
The guesser has no sure way to win, so we cannot call the sentence "the guesser can find the coin" true.
The hider has no sure way to win either, because a lucky guess finds the coin.
So the sentence is neither true nor false.
With only one hand, the guesser always wins, and the sentence is true.

### L2 human

We check the claims on $M = \set{0, 1}$ by listing all strategies.

1. Eloise has two uniform strategies: always $0$, or always $1$.
2. Abelard has two strategies: $x = 0$, or $x = 1$.
3. The four pairs give the plays $(0, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, $(1, 0)$, $(1, 1)$, written as (value of $x$, value of $y$). Eloise wins exactly in $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$.
4. Each strategy of Eloise loses against one strategy of Abelard, and each strategy of Abelard loses against one strategy of Eloise.
5. So neither player has a winning strategy, and $\varphi$ is undetermined in $M$.

The typical use of this example is a test of the semantics: it shows that IF logic has three truth values.
A trap is to let Eloise use a strategy that depends on $x$, such as $y = x$: this strategy is not uniform, since the slash hides $x$.
