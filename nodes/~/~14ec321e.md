## Example

Let $T \colon \R^2 \to \R^2$ be $T(x, y) = (2x + y,\ x + 2y)$.
In the standard basis $E = (e_1, e_2)$, the [[matrix|0YYMY95FWS]] is $[T]_{E,E} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, since $T(e_1) = (2, 1)$ and $T(e_2) = (1, 2)$.
Now take the basis $B = (b_1, b_2)$ with $b_1 = (1, 1)$ and $b_2 = (1, -1)$.

1. $T(b_1) = (2 + 1,\ 1 + 2) = (3, 3) = 3 b_1 + 0 b_2$. So column 1 of $[T]_{B,B}$ is $(3, 0)$.
2. $T(b_2) = (2 - 1,\ 1 - 2) = (1, -1) = 0 b_1 + 1 b_2$. So column 2 is $(0, 1)$.
3. So $[T]_{B,B} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. Take $v = (5, 1)$. The [[coordinates|0BMVBHTWV2]] $[v]_B = (a, b)$ solve $a + b = 5$ and $a - b = 1$. So $a = 3$, $b = 2$, and $v = 3b_1 + 2b_2 = (5, 1)$.
5. The product is $[T]_{B,B}(3, 2) = (9, 2)$.
6. Directly, $T(5, 1) = (11, 7)$. Its coordinates $(a, b)$ solve $a + b = 11$ and $a - b = 7$. So $a = 9$ and $b = 2$.
7. Steps 5 and 6 agree: $[T(v)]_B = [T]_{B,B}[v]_B$.

## Remarks

- One map has two different matrices in the two bases. The diagonal one shows that $T$ stretches the line through $(1, 1)$ by $3$ and fixes the line through $(1, -1)$.
- The two matrices are [[similar|0E89HQATM8]]: both have trace $4$ and determinant $3$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A good basis can make the matrix of a map much simpler.
Here the map only stretches along two diagonal lines, and the matrix in the diagonal basis shows this at once.

### L2 human

The matrix in the basis $B$ needs the $B$-coordinates of $T(b_1)$ and $T(b_2)$, not their standard coordinates.
Here these are easy because $b_1$ and $b_2$ are eigenvectors.
The check in steps 4 to 7 uses $B$-coordinates on both sides of the formula.
