## Example

Let $n \ge 1$, and let $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ be the [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] of [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] real $n \times n$ matrices under matrix multiplication.
Let $GL_n(\mathbb{R})^{\mathrm{op}}$ be its [[opposite group|D33V8HE0KY]], with the product $A \ast B = BA$.
We show that the [[transpose|09DXWYRD43]] $\tau(A) = A^T$ is an isomorphism $GL_n(\mathbb{R}) \to GL_n(\mathbb{R})^{\mathrm{op}}$.

1. The map is well defined: if $A$ is invertible, then $A^T (A^{-1})^T = (A^{-1} A)^T = I^T = I$, and in the same way $(A^{-1})^T A^T = I$. So $A^T$ is invertible.
2. For all $n \times n$ matrices, $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$, by the [[rule for the transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]].
3. So $\tau(AB) = B^T A^T = A^T \ast B^T = \tau(A) \ast \tau(B)$, and $\tau$ is a homomorphism to $GL_n(\mathbb{R})^{\mathrm{op}}$.
4. Since $(A^T)^T = A$, the map $\tau$ is its own inverse, so it is a bijection.

So $\tau$ is an isomorphism from $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ to $GL_n(\mathbb{R})^{\mathrm{op}}$.

## Remarks

Step 1 also uses the rule of step 2, for the pair $A^{-1}$ and $A$.
For $n \ge 2$ the transpose is not a homomorphism from $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ to itself.
Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Then $A^T = B$ and $B^T = A$, so $A^T B^T = BA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
But $(AB)^T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}^T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which is different.
When we compose $\tau$ with the inversion isomorphism $GL_n(\mathbb{R})^{\mathrm{op}} \to GL_n(\mathbb{R})$, $X \mapsto X^{-1}$, we get the map $A \mapsto (A^T)^{-1}$.
This map is an isomorphism from $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ to itself, since $((AB)^T)^{-1} = (B^T A^T)^{-1} = (A^T)^{-1} (B^T)^{-1}$.
For $n = 1$, the group $GL_1(\mathbb{R})$ is abelian, the opposite group is the group itself, and $\tau$ is the identity.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The transpose of a matrix flips it about its main diagonal, so rows become columns.
For two matrices $A$ and $B$, the transpose of the product $AB$ is the product of the transposes in the other order, $B^T A^T$.
So the transpose turns products around.
The opposite group is exactly the group where products are turned around.
That is why the transpose matches the general linear group with its opposite group.

### L2 human

We check step 3 for the matrices $A$ and $B$ of the Remarks.

1. $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\tau(AB) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. $\tau(A) = B$ and $\tau(B) = A$.
3. In the opposite group, $\tau(A) \ast \tau(B) = \tau(B)\tau(A) = AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. Steps 1 and 3 agree, as step 3 of the Example says.

The typical use is to pass between row vectors and column vectors: $x \mapsto xA$ on row vectors is a right action, and the transpose turns it into the left action $x^T \mapsto A^T x^T$ on column vectors.
A trap is to think that the transpose is a homomorphism from $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ to itself; the Remarks show that it is not.
