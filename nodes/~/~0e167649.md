## Example

Let the subject term $S$ be the [[prime numbers|01826KMG2H]] and the predicate term $P$ be the odd positive integers.
The four corners of the [[square of opposition|~4442a383]] are these sentences.

- A: all primes are odd.
- E: no prime is odd.
- I: some prime is odd.
- O: some prime is not odd.

We find the truth value of each corner and check each relation of the square.

1. A is false: $2$ is a prime and $2$ is even.
2. E is false: $3$ is a prime and $3$ is odd.
3. I is true: $3$ is a prime and odd.
4. O is true: $2$ is a prime and not odd.
5. Contradictories A and O: A is false and O is true, so exactly one is true.
6. Contradictories E and I: E is false and I is true, so exactly one is true.
7. Contraries A and E: both are false. Contraries only exclude that both are true, so this is allowed.
8. Subcontraries I and O: both are true. Subcontraries only exclude that both are false, so this is allowed.
9. Subalternation from A to I and from E to O: the universal sentences A and E are false, so subalternation makes no claim here, and I and O can be true.
10. The subject term is not empty, because $2$ is a prime. So the traditional and the modern square agree for this choice of terms.

## Remarks

The example shows the case in which both contraries are false and both subcontraries are true.
This happens exactly when the subject class meets the predicate class and also meets its complement; here the witnesses are $3$ and $2$.
If $P$ is changed to "integers greater than $1$", then every prime is in $P$: A becomes true, O false, E false and I true.
If $P$ is changed to "even primes greater than $2$", then no prime is in $P$: E becomes true, I false, A false and O true.
So the three possible patterns of a non-empty subject term appear with the same subject: $S \subseteq P$, $S \cap P = \varnothing$, and the mixed case of this example.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take the four sentences "all primes are odd", "no prime is odd", "some prime is odd" and "some prime is not odd".
The number $2$ is a prime that is even, and the number $3$ is a prime that is odd.
So the two "all" and "no" sentences are both false, and the two "some" sentences are both true.
Each "all" or "no" sentence is false exactly when the "some" sentence across the diagonal is true.

### L2 human

We use the square to negate a claim correctly.

1. Claim: all primes are odd. This is the A corner.
2. Its negation is the corner across the diagonal, O: some prime is not odd.
3. To disprove the claim we need one prime that is not odd. The number $2$ is such a prime.
4. The E corner "no prime is odd" is the contrary of the claim, not its negation. It is also false, as $3$ shows.

The typical use of the square is step 2: the negation of a universal claim is a particular claim with the opposite quality.
The trap is step 4: to disprove "all primes are odd" it is not necessary to show that no prime is odd.
