## Statement

Let $P$ be a nonempty set of propositional variables, of any cardinality.
A set $\Sigma$ of [[formulas|~7139c138]] is [[satisfiable|QN7M1JCGT9]] when some [[truth assignment|~41f42e3d]] $v : P \to \set{0, 1}$ makes every member of $\Sigma$ true.
The set $\Sigma$ is finitely satisfiable when every finite subset of $\Sigma$ is satisfiable.

1. If $\Sigma$ is finitely satisfiable, then $\Sigma$ is satisfiable.
2. Equivalently, for every set $\Gamma$ and every formula $\varphi$: if $\Gamma \models \varphi$, then there is a finite $\Gamma_0 \subseteq \Gamma$ with $\Gamma_0 \models \varphi$.

Here $\Gamma \models \varphi$ is [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]], a semantic relation; it is different from derivability $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, and the theorem does not mention any proof system.
Two proofs are given: a [[direct proof for countable P|~fb8216fd]] and a [[proof by Tychonoff's theorem|~6fad9a75]] for any $P$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Parts 1 and 2 are equivalent. If no finite $\Gamma_0$ has $\Gamma_0 \models \varphi$, then each set $\Gamma_0 \cup \set{\neg \varphi}$ with $\Gamma_0$ finite is satisfiable. So $\Gamma \cup \set{\neg \varphi}$ is finitely satisfiable, and part 1 gives a model of it, so $\Gamma \not\models \varphi$.
- For the other direction, the formula $\neg (p_0 \to p_0)$ is false under every assignment. If $\Sigma$ has no model, then $\Sigma \models \neg (p_0 \to p_0)$, and part 2 gives a finite $\Sigma_0$ with the same property, so $\Sigma_0$ has no model.
- The word "finite" cannot be replaced by "of size at most $k$" for a fixed $k$. The set $\set{p_1, \dots, p_k, \neg (p_1 \land \cdots \land p_k)}$ has $k + 1$ members, every $k$ of them are satisfiable, but the whole set is not.
- Formulas must be finite. With an infinite disjunction $\bigvee_n p_n$ the set $\set{\bigvee_n p_n, \neg p_0, \neg p_1, \dots}$ is finitely satisfiable but not satisfiable. Each formula of propositional logic has finitely many variables, and the proofs use this fact.

### Consequences

- The strong form of the [[completeness theorem|~e697b103]] follows from its finite form and compactness: if $\Gamma \models \varphi$, a finite $\Gamma_0$ has $\Gamma_0 \models \varphi$, and then $\Gamma_0 \vdash \varphi$.
- Infinite combinatorial problems reduce to finite ones: the de Bruijn-Erdős theorem on graph colourings, the extension of partial orders to linear orders, and Hall's theorem for infinite families of finite sets. See [[applications of propositional compactness|~ec805984]].
- König's lemma for finitely branching trees follows: use a variable for each vertex and say "the branch has exactly one vertex at each level, and it is closed under parents".

### Converse

The converse of part 1 holds and is immediate: a model of $\Sigma$ is a model of each subset of $\Sigma$.
So $\Sigma$ is satisfiable if and only if it is finitely satisfiable.

### Choice principles

For a countable $P$, the [[direct proof|~fb8216fd]] uses no form of the axiom of choice.
For an arbitrary $P$, the theorem is equivalent over ZF to the [[ultrafilter lemma|3MKZST79MK]], and so to the [[Boolean prime ideal theorem|STPBXBKSZP]].
This principle cannot be proved in ZF, and it is strictly weaker than the [[axiom of choice|0HTGB13FE2]].

### Generalizations

- The [[compactness theorem for first-order logic|1NR3HAWG9D]] is the same statement for sets of first-order sentences and their models.
- Topological form: for a countably infinite $P$, the space $\set{0, 1}^P$ is the [[Cantor space|W9VYJRBF9N]], and the sets of models of formulas are its clopen sets. Compactness of the logic is compactness of this space.
- Compactness fails for infinitary logics with countable disjunctions, as the example above shows, and for second-order logic.

## History

Propositional compactness is a special case of the compactness theorem of first-order logic, with each variable a predicate symbol of arity zero.
Kurt Gödel proved the first-order theorem for countable languages in 1930, and Anatoly Maltsev proved the uncountable case in 1936.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a list of rules, perhaps an infinite list, about which switches are on and which are off.
Suppose that any finite part of the list can be obeyed by some setting of the switches.
The compactness theorem says that one setting of the switches obeys the whole list at once.
For example, take the rules "switch 1 is on", "if switch 1 is on then switch 2 is on", "if switch 2 is on then switch 3 is on", and so on.
Each finite part can be obeyed, and the setting "all switches on" obeys all the rules.
The theorem says that such a setting exists even when nobody can guess it.

### L2 human

We use compactness to show that a set has a model, without building the model.
Let $\Sigma = \set{p_0 \lor p_1} \cup \set{p_n \to \neg p_{n+1} : n \ge 0} \cup \set{\neg p_n \to p_{n+1} : n \ge 0}$.

1. Let $\Sigma_0 \subseteq \Sigma$ be finite. Its formulas mention only $p_0, \dots, p_N$ for some $N$.
2. Put $v(p_n) = 1$ for even $n$ and $v(p_n) = 0$ for odd $n$.
3. Then $v(p_0 \lor p_1) = 1$, since $v(p_0) = 1$.
4. For each $n$, exactly one of $p_n$, $p_{n+1}$ is true under $v$, so $p_n \to \neg p_{n+1}$ and $\neg p_n \to p_{n+1}$ are true.
5. So $\Sigma_0$ is satisfiable, and $\Sigma$ is finitely satisfiable.
6. By compactness, $\Sigma$ is satisfiable.

Here the model of step 2 already works for all of $\Sigma$, so compactness was not needed.
The typical use is different: each finite part has its own model, built by a finite theorem, and the models do not fit together.
Then compactness gives a single model, as in the [[applications|~ec805984]].
The trap is to forget that each formula must be finite: an infinite disjunction is not a formula, and the theorem says nothing about it.

### L3 human

The set $\set{0, 1}^P$ of truth assignments, with the product topology, is a compact Hausdorff space by Tychonoff's theorem.
The set of models of a formula is clopen, and compactness of the logic is compactness of this space.
This is the Stone space of the free Boolean algebra on $P$.
More generally, the Stone space of the [[Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra|QC9M3Q8B8Q]] of a theory is the space of its complete extensions.

In first-order logic the same theorem gives nonstandard models of arithmetic and the upward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem.
Lindström's theorem characterizes first-order logic as the strongest logic that has compactness and the downward Löwenheim-Skolem property.
