## Example

Fix a set $W$ of worlds with a total [[preorder|0VABYWJWH9]] and the all-all lifting of [[axiological logic|4BRXSBXXWG]]: $p \succ q$ iff $|p \wedge \neg q|$ and $|\neg p \wedge q|$ are nonempty and each world of the first set is strictly better than each world of the second.
We check three principles of von Wright.

1. Conjunctive expansion: $p \succ q$ iff $(p \wedge \neg q) \succ (\neg p \wedge q)$. Put $p' = p \wedge \neg q$ and $q' = \neg p \wedge q$. These are incompatible, so $p' \wedge \neg q' \equiv p'$ and $\neg p' \wedge q' \equiv q'$, and both sides compare $|p'|$ with $|q'|$.
2. Contraposition: $p \succ q$ iff $\neg q \succ \neg p$. Here $\neg q \wedge \neg\neg p \equiv p \wedge \neg q$ and $\neg\neg q \wedge \neg p \equiv \neg p \wedge q$, so both sides make the same comparison.
3. Asymmetry: if $p \succ q$ and $q \succ p$, then a world $w$ in the nonempty set $|p \wedge \neg q|$ and a world $v$ in $|\neg p \wedge q|$ satisfy $w > v$ and $v > w$, which is impossible.

A concrete check: on worlds $11 > 10 > 01 > 00$ over atoms $p, q$, the formula $p \succ q$ holds by $10 > 01$.
Contraposition gives $\neg q \succ \neg p$, which compares the same two worlds.

## Remarks

Contraposition yields the link between good and bad: $p \succ \neg p$ iff $\neg\neg p \succ \neg p$, so $\mathrm{G}p$ iff $\mathrm{B}\neg p$.
The principle that $p \succ q$ implies $(p \wedge r) \succ (q \wedge r)$ is not valid.
On the ranking above it fails with $r = \neg p \wedge q$: here $p \wedge r$ is contradictory, so $(p \wedge r) \succ (q \wedge r)$ has an empty comparison set and does not hold.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To say that tea is better than coffee is to say that tea without coffee is better than coffee without tea.
It is also to say that having no coffee is better than having no tea.
Von Wright found these rules for the word "better".

### L2 human

1. Rewrite each comparison as a comparison of the two sets where exactly one proposition holds.
2. Check that both sides of a principle produce the same two sets.
3. A principle that changes the sets, such as adding a conjunct $r$, can fail.

The typical use is to derive value laws without fixing a particular ranking.
A trap: the principles hold for the all-all lifting; other liftings validate different principles.
