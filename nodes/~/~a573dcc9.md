## Example

Let $P(n)$ be the statement "$n$ is a [[prime number|01826KMG2H]]".
We show that $P(n)$ holds frequently, but that it does not hold [[for all sufficiently large|FEQ7WATMHF]] $n$.

1. By [[Euclid's theorem|0292EPPPT0]], there are infinitely many primes.
2. Let $N$ be given. Only finitely many natural numbers are less than $N$, so some prime $p$ satisfies $p \geq N$. So $P(n)$ holds frequently.
3. Suppose that $P(n)$ holds eventually, with some threshold $N$.
4. Put $n = 2N + 4$. Then $n \geq N$, and $n$ is even with $n \geq 4$. So $2$ divides $n$ and $2 < n$, and $n$ is not prime.
5. This contradicts step 3. So $P(n)$ does not hold eventually.
6. In the language of negation: "$n$ is not prime" holds frequently, by step 4. This is exactly the statement that $P(n)$ does not hold eventually.

## Remarks

The non-primes leave longer and longer gaps between the primes.
For $m \geq 2$, the $m - 1$ numbers $m! + 2, m! + 3, \ldots, m! + m$ are all composite, because $k$ divides $m! + k$ for $2 \leq k \leq m$; here $m!$ is the [[factorial|0XWG0JGDFW]] of $m$.
For $m = 4$ this gives $26$, $27$ and $28$, which are divisible by $2$, $3$ and $4$.
So both "prime" and "not prime" hold frequently, and neither holds eventually.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Primes never stop: there is always a prime further along the number line.
So "being prime" happens again and again, which is what "frequently" means.
But the primes do not take over from some point on: after any point there are still even numbers like $100$, $1000$ and $10000$, and they are not prime.
So "being prime" does not happen "eventually".

### L2 human

We find primes and non-primes beyond the threshold $N = 100$.

1. The number $101$ is prime: it is not divisible by $2$, $3$, $5$ or $7$, and $11^2 = 121 > 101$.
2. The number $2 \cdot 100 + 4 = 204$ is even, so it is not prime.
3. Both kinds of numbers appear after $100$, and the same holds after each threshold.

A trap: "infinitely many $n$ have property $P$" does not give "all large $n$ have property $P$".
