## Summary

A *concrete incompleteness result* is a theorem of the following form.
A specific sentence $\varphi$ is given, with three properties: it is arithmetic, usually $\Pi_1$ or $\Pi_2$; it speaks about finite objects such as numbers, finite sets, trees or graphs; and it uses no coding of syntax, proofs or truth.
The theorem then says that $\varphi$ is not provable in a stated theory $T$, and that it is provable in a stated stronger theory $T'$.
Concrete incompleteness shows that [[Gödel's incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]] reach ordinary combinatorics, and it measures how much strength ordinary statements need.

## Motivation

Gödel's unprovable sentences speak about their own provability, so they look far from ordinary mathematics.
A mathematician can ask whether any statement of the usual kind, about numbers or finite structures, needs more than the usual axioms.
Concrete incompleteness answers this with explicit statements and explicit theories, and it locates each statement on a scale of theories.

## Notation

- $T \nvdash \varphi$ means that $T$ does not prove $\varphi$, and $T' \vdash \varphi$ means that $T'$ proves it.
- $\Pi_n$ and $\Sigma_n$ are the levels of the [[arithmetical hierarchy|CV2E6JZHVP]].
- $\mathrm{Con}(T)$ is the [[consistency|VM8TQ70CVJ]] of $T$, and $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(T)$ says that every $\Sigma_1$ sentence provable in $T$ is true.
- The stronger theory $T'$ is often $T + 1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(T)$, or $T$ plus transfinite induction up to its proof-theoretic ordinal, or $\mathsf{ZFC}$ plus a [[large cardinal|X1FG7PSCTY]] axiom.

## Remarks

### Examples

Each example names the theory $T$ and a stronger theory $T'$.

- The [[Paris-Harrington theorem|509ZWA0R26]]: the strengthened finite Ramsey theorem, with $T = $ [[Peano arithmetic|MPSCREYC3M]] $\mathsf{PA}$ and $T' = \mathsf{PA} + 1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$.
- [[Goodstein's theorem|XQMNJF241J]]: termination of Goodstein sequences, with $T = \mathsf{PA}$ and $T'$ either $\mathsf{PA}$ plus transfinite induction up to $\varepsilon_0$, or $\mathsf{ZFC}$.
- [[Beklemishev's worm principle|~9689a72a]]: termination of worm sequences, with $T = \mathsf{PA}$ and $T' = \mathsf{PA} + 1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$.
- [[Friedman's finite form of Kruskal's theorem|~0c69c0b2]], with $T = \mathsf{ATR}_0$ and $T' = \Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}_0$.
- The [[Buchholz hydra theorem|~bf22387c]], with $T = (\Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}) + \mathsf{BI}$ and $T' = \mathsf{ZFC}$.
- The finite form of the [[graph minor theorem|WXNTPQGJ38]], with $T = \Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}_0$ and $T' = \mathsf{ZFC}$.

### Non-examples

- A Gödel sentence of $\mathsf{PA}$ is unprovable in $\mathsf{PA}$, but it codes provability. It fails the condition "no coding of syntax".
- The [[continuum hypothesis|0MN2MER9WN]] is independent of $\mathsf{ZFC}$, but it is about sets of reals. It fails the condition "arithmetic".
- The [[finite Ramsey theorem|YSZ82MRXT8]] is a natural statement about finite sets, but $\mathsf{PA}$ proves it. It fails the condition "unprovable in $T$" for $T = \mathsf{PA}$.
- [[Fermat's Last Theorem|0Y5X3SDBCZ]] is a $\Pi_1$ sentence whose known proof uses heavy tools. No unprovability result is known for it in $\mathsf{PA}$, so it is not an incompleteness result.

### Basic properties

- A true $\Sigma_1$ sentence is provable in $\mathsf{PA}$: a witness exists, and the check of a witness is a finite computation that $\mathsf{PA}$ proves. So concrete incompleteness for $\mathsf{PA}$ starts at the level $\Pi_1$.
- For a $\Pi_2$ sentence $\forall x\, \exists y\, \theta(x, y)$, unprovability is often shown by growth: the least $y$ grows faster than every [[provably total function|~25aedb3b]] of $T$.
- If $\varphi$ is true and $T$ proves only true sentences, then $T$ does not prove $\neg \varphi$ either. So a true unprovable sentence is independent of $T$.
- The truth value of an arithmetic sentence does not change under [[forcing|F2DTWW9HP7]]. So the independence of an arithmetic sentence from $\mathsf{ZFC}$ cannot come from forcing alone.
- Unprovability is relative to the theory. Every example becomes provable in a suitable stronger theory.

### History

Kurt Gödel gave the first unprovable arithmetic sentences in 1931, by coding provability.
In 1977 Paris and Harrington published a combinatorial statement unprovable in $\mathsf{PA}$, often described as the first natural example.
In the early 1980s Harvey Friedman showed that finite forms of Kruskal's theorem are unprovable in $\mathsf{ATR}_0$, an early success of [[reverse mathematics|TQTDKMBJ0F]].
Friedman's later program, Boolean relation theory, aims to justify large cardinal axioms by showing that they are needed to prove certain statements considered concrete.

### Common errors

- "Independent of $\mathsf{PA}$" does not mean "neither true nor false". The Paris-Harrington sentence is true, and $\mathsf{ZFC}$ proves it.
- Independence from $\mathsf{PA}$ does not give independence from $\mathsf{ZFC}$. All the examples above for $\mathsf{PA}$ are theorems of $\mathsf{ZFC}$.
- "Natural" is not a formal notion. Results state the exact sentence and the exact theories, and naturalness is a judgment about the sentence.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Mathematics starts from a list of basic rules, called axioms.
Gödel showed that every reasonable list leaves some true statements unproved, but his statements talk about proofs themselves.
Concrete incompleteness looks for ordinary statements, for example about colouring numbers or about growing trees, that a given list of axioms cannot prove.
Several such statements are known, and each of them can be proved with a stronger list of axioms.
So the answer to "is this provable?" always depends on which axioms are allowed.

### L2 human

How to read a concrete incompleteness result, with Goodstein's theorem as the worked case.

1. The sentence: every Goodstein sequence reaches $0$. It is $\Pi_2$: for every start $m$ there is a step $k$ with value $0$.
2. The theory $T$: $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove the sentence.
3. The stronger theory $T'$: $\mathsf{ZFC}$, or $\mathsf{PA}$ with transfinite induction up to $\varepsilon_0$, proves it by ordinals below $\varepsilon_0$.
4. The method: the length of the sequence of $m$ grows faster than every function that $\mathsf{PA}$ proves total.
5. The conclusion: the sentence is true and independent of $\mathsf{PA}$.

The typical use of this pattern is to compare the strength of theorems on a common scale of theories.
The trap: a result that omits the theory $T$ or the stronger theory $T'$ is not a precise statement.

### L3 human

Concrete incompleteness has two sides.
The proof-theoretic side measures each statement by the theories that prove it, through [[ordinal analysis|C5E5TG8PGP]] and growth rates of provably total functions.
In this sub-area the scale goes from $\mathsf{PA}$ with ordinal $\varepsilon_0$, through $\mathsf{ATR}_0$ with ordinal $\Gamma_0$, to $(\Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}) + \mathsf{BI}$ and beyond.

The foundational side asks how far ordinary mathematics depends on strong axioms.
Friedman's grand conjecture says that every arithmetic theorem in the Annals of Mathematics is provable in [[elementary function arithmetic|2JD80TE553]], a theory much weaker than $\mathsf{PA}$; it is open.
The Wikipedia article lists consistency statements, statements from Ramsey theory and the graph minor theorem as natural examples beyond this theory.
At the other end, Friedman's Boolean relation theory proposes concrete statements whose proofs need large cardinal axioms.
