## Proof

Let $F$ be a field with $2 \ne 0$ in $F$.
Fix a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ of $V$, and write $v = \sum_i x_i e_i$.

1. By [[the correspondence theorem|~00a7a300]], part 4, $Q(v) = \sum_{i \le j} c_{ij} x_i x_j$ for some $c_{ij} \in F$. So $Q$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $2$ in $x_1, \dots, x_n$.
2. An invertible linear change of variables $x = P y$ corresponds to the new basis $e'_j = \sum_i P_{ij} e_i$. So it suffices to find an invertible change of variables after which the polynomial has no cross terms.
3. A composite of invertible changes of variables is invertible. So we may apply several changes one after the other.
4. We prove by induction on $n$: each polynomial $q = \sum_{i \le j} c_{ij} x_i x_j$ becomes $\sum_i a_i y_i^2$ after an invertible change of variables.
5. For $n = 0$ there is nothing to prove. For $n = 1$, $q = c_{11} x_1^2$ is already diagonal.
6. Let $n \ge 2$ and assume the claim for $n - 1$ variables. We consider three cases.
7. Case A: all $c_{ij} = 0$. Then $q = 0 = \sum_i 0 \cdot x_i^2$, and the identity change of variables works.
8. Case B: some diagonal coefficient is nonzero. After renumbering the variables, $a = c_{11} \ne 0$.
9. Collect the terms with $x_1$: $q = a x_1^2 + x_1 L + q'$, where $L$ is linear and $q'$ is quadratic in $x_2, \dots, x_n$.
10. [[Complete the square|XNCV97HTZ8]]: $a x_1^2 + x_1 L = a \bigl( x_1 + \frac{L}{2a} \bigr)^2 - \frac{L^2}{4a}$. This uses $2a \ne 0$.
11. Put $y_1 = x_1 + \frac{L}{2a}$ and $y_i = x_i$ for $i \ge 2$. This change is invertible, since $x_1 = y_1 - \frac{L}{2a}$ and $L$ does not contain $x_1$.
12. Then $q = a y_1^2 + q''$ with $q'' = q' - \frac{L^2}{4a}$, a quadratic polynomial in $y_2, \dots, y_n$.
13. By the induction hypothesis, an invertible change of $y_2, \dots, y_n$ makes $q''$ diagonal. It does not change $y_1$, so $q$ becomes diagonal.
14. Case C: all $c_{ii} = 0$, but some $c_{ij} \ne 0$ with $i < j$. After renumbering, $c_{12} \ne 0$.
15. Put $x_1 = s + t$, $x_2 = s - t$, and keep $x_3, \dots, x_n$. The inverse is $s = \frac12 (x_1 + x_2)$, $t = \frac12 (x_1 - x_2)$, so the change is invertible.
16. Then $x_1 x_2 = s^2 - t^2$. The other terms of $q$ are $c_{1k} x_1 x_k$, $c_{2k} x_2 x_k$ and $c_{kl} x_k x_l$ with $k, l \ge 3$.
17. None of the terms in step 16 contains $s^2$. So the new coefficient of $s^2$ is $c_{12} \ne 0$.
18. The new polynomial is in Case B. Steps 8 to 13 make it diagonal.
19. Cases A, B and C cover all polynomials, which completes the induction.
20. By step 2, the final change of variables gives a basis in which $Q = \sum_i a_i y_i^2$.

## Idea

Remove one variable at a time by completing the square.
If no square term is left, the substitution $x_1 = s + t$, $x_2 = s - t$ makes one, since $x_1 x_2 = s^2 - t^2$.
Both steps divide by $2$, which is why the characteristic must not be $2$.

## Variants

- [[The proof by an orthogonal basis|PP93XQZ6WR]] is the same argument without coordinates: Case B is the choice of $e_1$ with $Q(e_1) \ne 0$.
- In matrix form, Case B is a symmetric row and column reduction. Subtract multiples of row $1$ from the other rows, then do the same with columns. The result is $E^T A E$, with $E$ an elementary matrix.
- Example for Case C: $Q = xy + yz$ has no square terms. With $x = s + t$, $y = s - t$, it becomes $s^2 - t^2 + sz - tz$. Then $s^2 + sz = (s + \frac z2)^2 - \frac{z^2}4$ and $-t^2 - tz = -(t + \frac z2)^2 + \frac{z^2}4$. So $Q = (s + \frac z2)^2 - (t + \frac z2)^2$.
