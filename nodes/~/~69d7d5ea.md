## Example

A course in real analysis has three lectures a week.
In each lecture the teacher states and proves the theorems of the course, for example the theorem that a convergent sequence is bounded.
Each week the students hand in proofs of exercises from the textbook, and a tutor marks them.
We compare the course with the steps of the [[Moore method|~492bc465]].

1. List of theorems for students to prove: no. The teacher proves the theorems.
2. Proofs by students: only of exercises, which lie outside the main line of the theory.
3. Presentation at the board by students: no.
4. Checking by the class: no. A tutor marks the work.
5. The class builds the theory: no. The theory comes from the teacher.

Steps 1, 3, 4 and 5 fail, so the course is not an instance of the method.

## Remarks

The students of this course do write proofs, so it trains the writing of proofs.
What it lacks is the main idea of the method: that the students produce the theory of the course.
A teacher can move the course toward the method by small changes, for example by giving one theorem a week for students to prove and present.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In this course, the teacher proves the main theorems, and the students prove some exercises at home.
In the Moore method, the students prove the main theorems themselves and show them to the class.
So homework with proofs is not enough to make a course a Moore course.

### L2 human

1. List the steps of the method.
2. For each step, ask whether the course has it.
3. Find that the course lacks the student-made theory, the presentation and the checking by the class.
4. Conclude that the course is not an instance.

A trap is to count proof homework as "inquiry".
The test is who proves the theorems on which the course is built.
