## Summary

A definite description in a definition introduces an object as "the $x$ such that $C(x)$".
Such a [[definition|~d1d36166]] is legal only after a proof that exactly one $x$ satisfies $C(x)$.
The proof has two parts: existence (at least one $x$) and uniqueness (at most one $x$).
In logic, the phrase is a [[definite description|PD2R4XT79Q]], and the claim is $\exists! x\, C(x)$ with the [[uniqueness quantifier|0QAJHFGZZJ]].
This matters because a name like "the limit" or "the identity" is used in later proofs as one fixed object.

## Motivation

The word "the" appears in many definitions: the limit of a sequence, the identity element of a group, the greatest common divisor of two integers.
Each such phrase makes a promise.
If no object satisfies the condition, the name refers to nothing, and statements about it are empty.
If two objects satisfy the condition, the name does not say which one is meant, and two readers may calculate with different objects.
For example, "the square root of $4$" is ambiguous until a convention picks $2$ and not $-2$.
So each "the" in a definition is backed by a theorem, or by a convention that removes the choice.

## Notation

1. $\exists! x\, C(x)$ means "there is exactly one $x$ with $C(x)$". It abbreviates $\exists x\,(C(x) \land \forall y\,(C(y) \Rightarrow y = x))$.
2. In logic, $\iota x\, C(x)$ denotes "the $x$ such that $C(x)$". Mathematics books rarely write the symbol $\iota$.
3. Names such as $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n$, $e_G$ and $\gcd(a, b)$ are notation for definite descriptions.
4. Books often place the uniqueness proof just before the definition, as a lemma, or just after it, as a remark that the definition is "justified".

## Remarks

### The two obligations

To define "the $x$ such that $C(x)$", prove both of these.

1. Existence: there is an $x$ with $C(x)$. Often this is a construction, or a theorem such as the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$.
2. Uniqueness: if $C(x)$ and $C(y)$, then $x = y$. The standard proof assumes two such objects and shows that they are equal.

Uniqueness is often the easier proof, and it is often proved first.
Some definitions have a uniqueness proof but no general existence proof.
Then the definition says "the limit, if it exists", and each use of the name needs its own existence proof.

### Uniqueness proofs in practice

1. The [[identity element of a group|~e02ef25c]] is unique, because two identities $e$ and $e'$ satisfy $e = ee' = e'$.
2. The [[greatest common divisor|~86714beb]] is unique among non-negative common divisors that each common divisor divides.
3. The limit of a sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ is unique, see [[uniqueness of limits|0ZXJ2455NJ]]. In a general topological space this can fail, and the [[Hausdorff condition|0D8T984XEE]] restores it.
4. The supremum of a bounded set is unique, see [[uniqueness of the supremum|04ETX30HT2]].

### When uniqueness fails

When several objects satisfy the condition, the author has three options.

1. Use "a" instead of "the": "an antiderivative of $f$", "a basis of $V$".
2. Add a condition that picks one object: "the non-negative square root", "the gcd that is non-negative".
3. Pass to classes: speak of "the" object [[up to|~2311cd14]] an equivalence, as in "the cyclic group of order $n$, up to isomorphism".

The third option is common, but it conflicts with the strict reading of "the".
Many authors write "the" for objects that are unique only up to isomorphism, such as "the product of two groups" or "the algebraic closure of $\mathbb{Q}$".
A careful reader checks which reading is meant.

### Examples

1. [[The identity element of a group|~e02ef25c]]: the axioms give existence, and a two-line proof gives uniqueness.
2. [[The greatest common divisor of two integers|~86714beb]]: existence comes from Bézout's identity, and uniqueness comes from mutual divisibility.
3. The limit of a convergent sequence of real numbers: uniqueness is a theorem, and existence is part of the hypothesis "convergent".
4. The inverse of an element $g$ of a group: the axioms give existence, and $h = h(gh') = (hg)h' = h'$ gives uniqueness.
5. The empty set: existence follows from the axioms of set theory, and uniqueness follows from the axiom of extensionality.

### Non-examples

1. [["The" square root of $-1$|~43216ca5]] is not a legal definite description in $\mathbb{C}$: both $i$ and $-i$ satisfy $z^2 = -1$, and no algebraic property tells them apart.
2. "The basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$" is not legal: there are infinitely many bases. "The standard basis" is legal, because it names one basis explicitly.
3. "The largest prime number" is not legal: no such number exists, by Euclid's theorem on the infinitude of primes.
4. "The limit of $(-1)^n$" is not legal: the sequence does not converge.

### Basic properties

1. If $\exists! x\, C(x)$ holds and $c$ denotes the unique object, then $C(c)$ holds, and each $y$ with $C(y)$ equals $c$.
2. The second fact gives a standard method of proof: to show $y = c$, show $C(y)$. For example, to show $(gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1}$, check that $h^{-1}g^{-1}$ is an inverse of $gh$.
3. Uniqueness alone, without existence, still allows the conditional name "the $x$ with $C(x)$, if one exists".

### History

In 1905 Russell analyzed sentences of the form "the $F$ is $G$" in his paper "On Denoting".
He read them as "exactly one thing is $F$, and it is $G$".
Hilbert and Bernays later used a description operator in their formal systems, with the uniqueness proof as a condition for its use.

### Common errors

1. A student writes "the solution" of an equation before proving that the solution is unique.
2. A student proves existence and forgets uniqueness, or proves uniqueness and forgets existence.
3. A student uses "the" for a choice that depends on the student, such as "the $\delta$" in an epsilon-delta proof. Many values of $\delta$ work, so "a $\delta$" is correct.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When we say "the" in mathematics, we promise that there is exactly one such thing.
"The tallest student in the class" makes sense when exactly one student is tallest.
If two students have the same height, the phrase points at nobody in particular.
In mathematics, each "the" in a definition comes with a proof that exactly one object fits.
For example, "the even prime number" means $2$, because $2$ is even and prime, and every other even number has the divisor $2$.

### L2 human

We prove that the inverse of an element $g$ of a group $G$ is unique, so that "the inverse $g^{-1}$" is a legal name.

1. Existence is an axiom of a group: there is an $h$ with $gh = hg = e$.
2. For uniqueness, let $h$ and $h'$ both satisfy $gh = hg = e$ and $gh' = h'g = e$.
3. Then $h = he$, because $e$ is the identity.
4. Next $he = h(gh')$, because $gh' = e$.
5. By associativity, $h(gh') = (hg)h'$.
6. Then $(hg)h' = eh' = h'$, because $hg = e$.
7. So $h = h'$, and the inverse is unique.

The typical use: once the inverse is unique, any element $k$ with $gk = kg = e$ is the inverse, so it equals $g^{-1}$.
A trap is step 2: the proof must take two arbitrary inverses, not one inverse and a specific element.

### L3 human

In first-order logic, a description term $\iota x\, \varphi(x)$ can be eliminated: "$\psi(\iota x\, \varphi)$" becomes $\exists x\,(\varphi(x) \land \forall y\,(\varphi(y) \to y = x) \land \psi(x))$.
So a definition by a description is a conservative extension once $\exists! x\, \varphi(x)$ is a theorem.
In Lean, a definition by description needs a proof term of existence, and Mathlib often uses classical choice with a separate uniqueness lemma.
For objects defined by a [[universal property|EN4DD2FAJH]], uniqueness holds only up to a unique isomorphism.
Category theory therefore uses "the" for such objects, and it relies on the fact that each construction is invariant under isomorphism.
