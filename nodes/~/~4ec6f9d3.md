## Example

We check three theorems of [[minimal logic|~2f9e5781]] with the Hilbert axioms and the deduction theorem.
Recall that $\neg\varphi$ is $\varphi \to \bot$.

1. $\bot \to \neg p$. This formula is $\bot \to (p \to \bot)$, an instance of axiom 1, $\varphi \to (\psi \to \varphi)$.
2. $\neg(p \wedge \neg p)$. Assume $p \wedge \neg p$. Axiom 3 gives $p$ and $p \to \bot$. Modus ponens gives $\bot$. The deduction theorem gives $(p \wedge \neg p) \to \bot$.
3. $(p \to q) \to (\neg q \to \neg p)$. Assume $p \to q$, $q \to \bot$ and $p$. Two steps of modus ponens give $q$ and then $\bot$. Three uses of the deduction theorem give the formula.
4. None of the steps uses an axiom with $\bot$ in a special role, so all three are theorems of $\mathsf{ML}$.

## Remarks

The example shows that the usual laws of negation, except ex falso, come from the rules for implication.
Item 1 is the one trace of ex falso in $\mathsf{ML}$: from $\bot$ every negation follows, but not every formula.
Replacing $\bot$ by any formula $\chi$ gives intuitionistic and classical theorems, such as $(p \to q) \to ((q \to \chi) \to (p \to \chi))$.
So each theorem of $\mathsf{ML}$ is a scheme that holds for every choice of "absurdity".

## Explanations

### L1 human

"Not $A$" in minimal logic means "$A$ leads to absurdity".
Then "$A$ and not $A$" leads to absurdity at once, so it is refuted.
And "if $A$ gives $B$, and $B$ leads to absurdity, then $A$ leads to absurdity" is just a chain of two steps.

### L2 human

1. Read $\bot$ as the statement "$1 = 0$" in arithmetic, and $\neg\varphi$ as "$\varphi$ implies $1 = 0$".
2. Item 3 then says: if $p$ implies $q$, and $q$ implies $1 = 0$, then $p$ implies $1 = 0$.
3. This holds for every reading of $\bot$, so it does not use any property of $1 = 0$.

The typical use of these laws is in double negation translations, where they hold for any choice of $\bot$, as in the [[Friedman translation|6CCK0TT69P]].
A trap: $\neg p \to (p \to q)$ looks like item 3, but it needs ex falso and fails in $\mathsf{ML}$.
