## Summary

Approximate counting in [[bounded arithmetic|~55a002f3]] is Jeřábek's method to compare the sizes of sets that are defined by [[Boolean circuits|SQXNFGVADX]], without exact counting.
For $X, Y \subseteq [0, 2^n)$ and $0 \le \varepsilon \le 1$, write $X \preceq_\varepsilon Y$ if there are a circuit $G$ and a number $v \ne 0$ such that $G$ maps $v \times (Y \sqcup [0, \varepsilon 2^n))$ onto $v \times X$.
Write $X \approx_\varepsilon Y$ if $X \preceq_\varepsilon Y$ and $Y \preceq_\varepsilon X$, and identify a number $s$ with the interval $[0, s)$.
The basic theory is $\mathsf{APC}_1 = \mathsf{PV}_1 + \mathrm{dWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$, the theory [[PV|~98af8889]] with the dual weak [[pigeonhole principle|0HGVCWQGY3]] for polynomial-time functions.
In $\mathsf{APC}_1$, for every set $X$ given by a circuit and every $\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon^{-1}$ of polynomial size in $n$, there is an $s$ with $X \approx_\varepsilon s$.
The method lets weak theories reason about probabilities, and so about [[randomized algorithms|2Y50S7GQTD]] and counting arguments in combinatorics.

## Motivation

The exact number of elements of a set given by a polynomial-size circuit is hard to compute: it counts satisfying assignments.
So a theory whose functions are polynomial-time cannot define this number.
On the other hand, the analysis of a randomized algorithm needs only the probability of an event up to a small error.
The idea is to measure size by maps: a map of $X$ onto $Y$ shows that $Y$ has at most as many elements as $X$.
The dual weak pigeonhole principle says that no polynomial-time map sends a set onto a much larger set, so the comparisons by maps are consistent.

## Notation

- $\mathrm{dWPHP}(f)$: for all $x > 0$ and $y$, some $v < x(\abs y + 1)$ is not of the form $f(u)$ with $u < x \abs y$. It says that $f$ does not map $x \abs y$ onto $x (\abs y + 1)$.
- $\mathrm{dWPHP}$ is also called the surjective weak pigeonhole principle, $\mathrm{sWPHP}$. The injective version is $\mathrm{iWPHP}$, and the retraction version is $\mathrm{rWPHP}$.
- $\mathsf{APC}_1 = \mathsf{PV}_1 + \mathrm{sWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$ and $\mathsf{APC}_2 = T^1_2 + \mathrm{sWPHP}(\mathsf{PV}_2)$, where $\mathsf{PV}_2$ means polynomial time with an $\mathrm{NP}$ oracle. The names are due to Buss, Kołodziejczyk and Thapen.
- $X \preceq_\varepsilon Y$ and $X \approx_\varepsilon Y$ as in the Summary. The error is measured relative to the ambient interval $2^n$, not relative to $X$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The axiom $\mathrm{dWPHP}(f)$ for a concrete polynomial-time $f$; [[this example|~22e906d3]] checks it for a map of $2^{n-1}$ points.
- Over $S^1_2$, $\mathrm{dWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$ is equivalent to the existence of Boolean functions with exponential circuit complexity; [[this example|~b1951c20]] gives the easy direction.
- An interval $[0, s)$ with $s \le 2^n$ satisfies $[0, s) \approx_0 s$: the identity map with $v = 1$ works in both directions.
- The even numbers below $2^n$ satisfy $X \approx_0 2^{n-1}$, by the maps $u \mapsto 2u$ and $x \mapsto \floor{x/2}$. The L2 explanation gives the details.
- In $\mathsf{PV}_1 + \mathrm{dWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$ the acceptance probability of a randomized algorithm can be estimated. Jeřábek used this to formalize the classes [[BPP|PTD7E1PKV4]], $\mathrm{APP}$, $\mathrm{MA}$ and $\mathrm{AM}$.
- $\mathsf{APC}_2$ can count $\mathrm{NP}$ sets with an error that is a polynomial fraction of the size of the set. It carries out the usual proofs of the finite Ramsey theorem and the tournament principle.

### Non-examples

- Emptiness. A set $\set{x_0}$ satisfies $\set{x_0} \approx_\varepsilon 0$ as soon as $\varepsilon 2^n \ge 1$, so approximate counting does not separate empty from non-empty sets; [[this non-example|~f80d78bf]] computes the maps.
- Exact counting. The statement "$X$ has exactly $s$ elements" for a circuit-defined $X$ is not an approximate comparison, and $\mathsf{PV}_1$ cannot define the exact number.
- A map given only by the full table of its values is not a witness in general. The table has about $2^n$ entries, and the theory need not contain a number that codes it.

### Basic properties

- The relation $\preceq_\varepsilon$ is monotone in $\varepsilon$, contains inclusion with error $0$, and is transitive with the errors added. These facts hold already in $\mathsf{PV}_1$.
- In $\mathsf{PV}_1 + \mathrm{dWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$: every circuit-defined set has an approximate size, any two such sets are comparable, and the sizes satisfy inclusion-exclusion up to the errors.
- If $s \preceq_0 t$ for numbers $s, t \le 2^n$, then $s \le t + \xi 2^n$ for each $\xi$ with $\xi^{-1}$ of polynomial size. This is where $\mathrm{dWPHP}$ enters.
- Wilkie's witnessing theorem: the $\forall \Sigma^b_1$ consequences of $S^1_2 + \mathrm{dWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$ are witnessed by randomized polynomial-time functions, and its $\Delta^b_1$ predicates are in [[ZPP|QJZ5ZY0HJ8]].
- $\mathrm{dWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$ is provable in $T^2_2$, while $\mathrm{dWPHP}(\alpha)$ for a new symbol $\alpha$ is not provable in $S^2_2(\alpha)$ (Riis).
- The schema $\mathrm{dWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$ is equivalent over $\mathsf{PV}_1$ to its one instance for the circuit evaluation function, so it is finitely axiomatizable.

### History

Wilkie first connected $\mathrm{dWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$ with randomized computation, and his witnessing theorem was published in Krajíček's book of 1995.
Krajíček studied $S^1_2 + \mathrm{dWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$ under the name $\mathsf{BT}$ in 2001.
Jeřábek related $\mathrm{dWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$ to hard Boolean functions in 2004, developed approximate counting in $\mathsf{PV}_1 + \mathrm{dWPHP}(\mathsf{PV})$ in 2007, and counting by hashing in $T^1_2 + \mathrm{sWPHP}(\mathsf{PV}_2)$ in 2009.
Buss, Kołodziejczyk and Thapen named the two theories $\mathsf{APC}_1$ and $\mathsf{APC}_2$ and studied their relativized consequences.

### Common errors

- To read $X \approx_\varepsilon s$ as "$X$ has $s \pm \varepsilon \abs X$ elements". In $\mathsf{APC}_1$ the error is $\varepsilon 2^n$, a fraction of the ambient interval.
- To confuse the dual (surjective) principle with the injective one. They are different schemas, and each of them implies the retraction version $\mathrm{rWPHP}$.
- To expect error $0$ to mean exact counting. Even with $\varepsilon = 0$, the weak pigeonhole principle only gives the size up to a small fraction of $2^n$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Counting the elements of a large set one by one takes too long.
Often it is enough to know the size roughly, for example "about one half of all numbers below $1000$".
One way to compare sizes without counting is to pair elements: if each element of $Y$ is hit by some element of $X$, then $Y$ is not larger than $X$.
Approximate counting builds a theory of sizes from such pairings.
The only extra axiom says that a small set cannot be mapped onto a set that is much larger.

### L2 human

We compute the approximate size of the even numbers below $2^n$.

1. Let $X = \set{x < 2^n : x \text{ even}}$. For $n = 3$, $X = \set{0, 2, 4, 6}$.
2. $X \preceq_0 2^{n-1}$: take $v = 1$ and $G(u) = 2u$ on $[0, 2^{n-1})$. Every even $x < 2^n$ is $G(x/2)$, so $G$ maps $[0, 2^{n-1})$ onto $X$.
3. $2^{n-1} \preceq_0 X$: take $v = 1$ and $G(x) = \floor{x/2}$ on $X$. Every $u < 2^{n-1}$ is $G(2u)$, so $G$ maps $X$ onto $[0, 2^{n-1})$.
4. Both maps are given by circuits of size polynomial in $n$. So $X \approx_0 2^{n-1}$.
5. For $n = 3$: $G$ sends $0, 1, 2, 3$ to $0, 2, 4, 6$ in step 2, and back in step 3.
6. The probability that a random $x < 2^n$ is even is therefore $1/2$, up to the error of the method.

The typical use is to estimate the acceptance probability of a randomized algorithm inside a weak theory.
A trap is to forget the copies $v \times X$: for complicated sets a single map can be too weak, and Jeřábek's definition needs the extra parameter $v$.

### L3 human

Approximate counting is the bounded-arithmetic version of the counting methods of complexity theory, such as hashing in Sipser's proof that $\mathrm{BPP} \subseteq \Sigma^p_2$.
The link between $\mathrm{dWPHP}$ and hard Boolean functions is the formal version of hardness versus randomness, and Jeřábek formalized the Nisan-Wigderson [[pseudorandom generator|MD9THSA21A]] in this setting.
So $\mathsf{APC}_1$ is a candidate theory for reasoning about $\mathrm{BPP}$ and related classes, and $\mathsf{APC}_2$ for approximate counting of $\mathrm{NP}$ sets.

The theories also matter for the [[separation problem|~4ddef1ef]].
Many combinatorial principles that separate relativized theories, such as weak [[pigeonhole principles|~55b2a273]] and the finite Ramsey theorem, are provable in $\mathsf{APC}_2$.
So unprovability results above $\mathsf{APC}_2$ need methods that go past approximate counting.
