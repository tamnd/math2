## Example

Consider the argument with two premises and a conclusion:

- Premise 1: if an integer $n$ is divisible by $4$, then $n$ is even.
- Premise 2: the integer $7$ is not even.
- Conclusion: so $7$ is not divisible by $4$.

We check that the argument is valid, and that it is not an instance of [[affirming the consequent or denying the antecedent|~b2d5374e]].

1. The form is "if $P$ then $Q$; not $Q$; so not $P$". This form is called modus tollens.
2. The form is valid. Suppose that the premises are true and that $P$ holds. Then $Q$ holds by premise 1, which contradicts premise 2. So $P$ fails.
3. Another reason: $P \Rightarrow Q$ is equivalent to its [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$, and the argument applies [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] to the contrapositive.
4. The second premise denies the consequent $Q$, not the antecedent $P$. So the form differs from denying the antecedent, which has the second premise "not $P$".
5. A direct check of the conclusion: $7 = 4 \cdot 1 + 3$, so $7$ is not divisible by $4$.

## Remarks

The example shows that the position of "not" decides whether the inference is valid.
Denying the consequent gives a valid argument, and denying the antecedent gives an invalid one.
Modus tollens is the logical form of proof by contrapositive: to prove $P \Rightarrow Q$, we prove $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"If it is a dog, then it has four legs" is true.
A bird has two legs, so the bird cannot be a dog.
This argument is correct: if the bird were a dog, it would have four legs.
Here we deny the second part of the "if" sentence, and that is allowed.

### L2 human

We use modus tollens to prove that $2$ is not the cube of a positive integer.

1. Let $P(x)$ be "$x$ is the cube of a positive integer", and let $Q(x)$ be "$x = 1$ or $x \ge 8$".
2. Premise 1: for every $x$, $P(x)$ implies $Q(x)$. If $x = m^3$ with $m = 1$, then $x = 1$, and if $m \ge 2$, then $x \ge 2^3 = 8$.
3. Premise 2: $Q(2)$ is false, because $2 \neq 1$ and $2 < 8$.
4. By modus tollens, $P(2)$ is false: $2$ is not the cube of a positive integer.

The typical use of modus tollens is to rule out a case: a property that every candidate has, and that the target lacks.
A trap is to apply it with the premises in the wrong order, which gives denying the antecedent.
