## Summary

*Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic* $\mathrm{Ł}_3$ is the [[many-valued logic|DWYCKAKXZD]] of the matrix with values $\set{0, \tfrac12, 1}$, designated set $\set{1}$, negation $\neg x = 1 - x$ and implication $x \to y = \min(1, 1 - x + y)$; see [[the definition|~09f1e8d9]].
Disjunction and conjunction are the derived operations $\max$ and $\min$.
It differs from [[Strong Kleene logic|SZHA10A9X7]] only in the value $\tfrac12 \to \tfrac12 = 1$, and this one entry makes $p \to p$ a tautology.
It is the first many-valued logic, and it starts the family of Łukasiewicz logics that leads to [[infinite-valued Łukasiewicz logic|~ca73eab3]] and to [[MV-algebras|8T4M7RN9AC]].

## Motivation

Łukasiewicz wanted a logic for indeterminism.
A sentence about a future contingent event, such as "there will be a sea battle tomorrow", is in his view neither true nor false today.
He gave such sentences the third value $\tfrac12$, read as "possible".
The tables then extend the classical tables so that the classical values $0$ and $1$ behave as before.

## Notation

$\mathrm{Ł}_3$ is also written $\mathrm{L}_3$ or $\text{Ł}3$; Łukasiewicz wrote formulas in Polish notation, with $C$ for implication and $N$ for negation.
The middle value is written $\tfrac12$, $I$ or $U$.
The strong connectives are $x \oplus y = \neg x \to y = \min(1, x + y)$ and $x \odot y = \neg(x \to \neg y) = \max(0, x + y - 1)$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[Excluded middle gets the value $\tfrac12$|6S872VHF67]], so $p \lor \neg p$ is not a tautology.
2. $p \to p$ is a tautology, since $\min(1, 1 - x + x) = 1$; it is not a tautology of $\mathsf{K}_3$.
3. [[One formula in Ł3, K3 and weak Kleene logic|~71d1e02f]] compares the three tables.
4. Wajsberg's axiom $((p \to \neg p) \to p) \to p$ is a tautology; see the check in [[the definition|~09f1e8d9]].
5. [[Łukasiewicz three-valued logic is not strong Kleene logic|~404c4155]], although the two share all tables except one entry.

### Non-examples

1. [[The four-valued Łukasiewicz logic refutes Wajsberg's axiom|~c33691b0]], so $\mathrm{Ł}_4$ is not a model of $\mathrm{Ł}_3$.
2. Classical logic is not $\mathrm{Ł}_3$: $p \lor \neg p$ is classical and fails in $\mathrm{Ł}_3$.

### Basic properties

1. Every tautology of $\mathrm{Ł}_3$ is a classical tautology, because the tables restrict to the classical tables on $\set{0, 1}$.
2. [[Modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] is valid: if $x = 1$ and $x \to y = 1$, then $\min(1, y) = 1$, so $y = 1$.
3. [[The implication is not definable from negation, conjunction and disjunction|~61164531]], while $x \lor y = (x \to y) \to y$ and $x \land y = \neg(\neg x \lor \neg y)$.
4. The deduction theorem fails in its classical form: $p \land \neg p \models q$, but $(p \land \neg p) \to q$ has value $\tfrac12$ at $p = \tfrac12$, $q = 0$.
5. Wajsberg's four axioms with modus ponens axiomatize the tautologies; see [[the definition|~09f1e8d9]].

### History

SEP reports that Łukasiewicz found the three-valued system in 1917 and published it in 1920, in order to defend indeterminism.
In 1922 he gave tables with finitely or infinitely many values in $[0, 1]$.
In 1931 Wajsberg axiomatized $\mathrm{Ł}_3$ with four axioms in implication and negation.

### Common errors

1. Defining disjunction by $\neg x \to y$. This gives the strong disjunction $\min(1, x + y)$, not $\max(x, y)$; they differ at $x = y = \tfrac12$.
2. Reading $\tfrac12$ as "both true and false". In $\mathrm{Ł}_3$ it is not designated, so it behaves as "not yet true".
3. Assuming that $\Gamma, \varphi \models \psi$ gives $\Gamma \models \varphi \to \psi$. Property 4 refutes this.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Łukasiewicz added a third truth value, "possible", written $\tfrac12$.
"Not" turns $\tfrac12$ into $\tfrac12$, and "if then" gives full truth when the condition is no truer than the conclusion.
So "if $p$ then $p$" is always fully true.
But "$p$ or not $p$" is only $\tfrac12$ when $p$ is $\tfrac12$.

### L2 human

We check that $(p \to q) \to (\neg q \to \neg p)$ is a tautology.

1. $\neg q \to \neg p = \min(1, 1 - (1 - y) + (1 - x)) = \min(1, 1 - x + y)$.
2. So $\neg q \to \neg p$ has the same value as $p \to q$.
3. Hence the formula has the form $a \to a$, with value $1$.

The typical use is to test classical laws one by one with the tables.
A trap: the contraction law $(p \to (p \to q)) \to (p \to q)$ fails, at $p = \tfrac12$, $q = 0$, where it has value $\tfrac12$.

### L3 human

The algebra of $\mathrm{Ł}_3$ is the three-element [[MV-algebra|8T4M7RN9AC]], and it satisfies the identity $x \oplus x \oplus x = x \oplus x$, which fails at $x = \tfrac13$ in $[0, 1]$.
The finite-valued logics $\mathrm{Ł}_n$ are the logics of the subalgebras $\set{0, \tfrac{1}{n-1}, \dots, 1}$ of $[0, 1]$, and their intersection is [[Łukasiewicz logic|~ca73eab3]].
Through [[Mundici's model of the Rényi-Ulam game|~924851f1]], $\mathrm{Ł}_3$ is also the logic of searching with one lie.
