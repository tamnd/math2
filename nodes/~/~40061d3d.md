## Statement

Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, of any dimension, and let $U$, $W$ be [[subspaces|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$.
Write $U^0$ for the [[annihilator|0R6M158SQ9]] of $U$.
For a subspace $X$ of $V^*$, write $X_0 = \set{v \in V : f(v) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in X}$.

1. $U^0$ is a subspace of $V^*$, with $\set{0}^0 = V^*$ and $V^0 = \set{0}$.
2. $U \subseteq W$ if and only if $W^0 \subseteq U^0$.
3. $(U^0)_0 = U$. So $U \mapsto U^0$ is injective.
4. $(U + W)^0 = U^0 \cap W^0$, for the [[sum|0JMTY8MF54]] and the [[intersection|0V33SPH7RR]] of subspaces.
5. $(U \cap W)^0 = U^0 + W^0$.
6. If $V$ is [[finite-dimensional|018W3V3TCT]], then $U \mapsto U^0$ is a bijection from the subspaces of $V$ onto the subspaces of $V^*$, with inverse $X \mapsto X_0$. It reverses inclusions.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Part 6 needs finite dimension. Let $V = F^{(\N)}$ and let $X$ be the span of the coordinate forms $e_k^*$. Then $X_0 = \set{0}$, because $e_k^*(v) = 0$ for all $k$ forces $v = 0$. So $(X_0)^0 = V^* \ne X$, since the sum of coordinates is [[not in X|~d17873c6]]. No $U$ has $U^0 = X$.
- In part 5, both sets must be subspaces. For subsets $S, T$ in place of $U, W$, the formula can fail: in $\R^2$, take $S = \set{(1, 0)}$ and $T = \set{(2, 0)}$. Then $S \cap T = \varnothing$ and $(S \cap T)^0 = (\R^2)^*$, but $S^0 + T^0 = \R \cdot y$.

### Consequences

- In finite dimension, the lattice of subspaces of $V$ and the lattice of subspaces of $V^*$ are anti-isomorphic: sums correspond to intersections, and intersections to sums.
- With the [[dimension of the annihilator|06Q1EFVH0Q]], part 5 gives $\dim(U^0 + W^0) = \dim V - \dim(U \cap W)$ in finite dimension. For two planes $U \ne W$ in $\R^3$, this is $3 - 1 = 2$.
- Part 3 says that every subspace is the common kernel of the forms that vanish on it, so every subspace is the solution set of a system of linear equations.

### Converse

The converse of part 6 holds: if $U \mapsto U^0$ is onto the subspaces of $V^*$, then $V$ is finite-dimensional, by the counterexample above, which works for every infinite basis.

### Generalizations

For a finite-dimensional space with a nondegenerate bilinear form, the same statements hold for orthogonal complements, which are the annihilators carried back to $V$ by the form.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The annihilator turns a subspace into the set of equations that it satisfies.
More vectors means fewer equations, so inclusions reverse.
Two subspaces together satisfy exactly the equations that each one satisfies, which is part 4.
In finite dimension, the equations determine the subspace, and each set of equations closed under linear combination comes from exactly one subspace.

### L2 human

We check parts 4 and 5 for the coordinate axes in $\R^2$.

1. Let $U = \R \cdot (1, 0)$ and $W = \R \cdot (0, 1)$. Then $U^0 = \R \cdot y$ and $W^0 = \R \cdot x$.
2. $U + W = \R^2$, so $(U + W)^0 = \set{0}$. Also $U^0 \cap W^0 = \R y \cap \R x = \set{0}$.
3. $U \cap W = \set{0}$, so $(U \cap W)^0 = (\R^2)^*$. Also $U^0 + W^0 = \R y + \R x = (\R^2)^*$.

The typical use is to compute the annihilator of a sum or intersection from known annihilators.
A trap: part 5 is harder than part 4, because it needs the extension of forms from a subspace to $V$.
