## Proof

This is a complete proof.
Write $\tau(t) = \max(0, \min(1, t))$ for $t \in \mathbb{R}$, and $g^\# = \tau \circ g$.
In $[0, 1]$, $x \oplus y = \min(1, x + y)$, $\neg x = 1 - x$ and $x \odot y = \max(0, x + y - 1)$.
"Definable" means "equal to $f_\varphi$ for some formula $\varphi$".

**Part 1: every definable function is a McNaughton function.**

1. Use induction on $\varphi$: a variable $p_i$ gives $x_i$, and $0$ gives the constant $0$.
2. If $f$ is continuous with integer pieces $g_j$, then $1 - f$ is continuous with pieces $1 - g_j$.
3. If $f, g$ are as in step 2, then $\min(1, f + g)$ is continuous.
4. Its pieces are $1$ and the sums $g_j + h_k$ of pieces, which have integer coefficients.
5. The connectives $\to$, $\land$, $\lor$, $\odot$ are defined from $\oplus$ and $\neg$, so the induction is complete.

**Part 2: truncations are definable.**
For an affine $g$ with integer coefficients, let its size be $|m_1| + \dots + |m_n|$, without the constant $m_0$.
Claim: $g^\#$ is definable, by induction on the size.

6. Size $0$: $g = m_0$ is constant, and $g^\#$ is $0$ or $1$, defined by $0$ or $\neg 0$.
7. For real $a$ and $x \in [0, 1]$: $\tau(a + x) = \min(1, \tau(a) + x) \odot \tau(a + 1)$.
8. Check of step 7 for $a \ge 1$: both sides are $1$.
9. For $a \le -1$: the left side is $0$, and the right side is $\max(0, \min(1, x) - 1) = 0$.
10. For $0 \le a \le 1$: $\tau(a + 1) = 1$, and both sides are $\min(1, a + x)$.
11. For $-1 \le a \le 0$: both sides are $\max(0, a + x)$, since $a + x \le 1$.
12. For real $t$: $1 - \tau(t) = \tau(1 - t)$, by the same three cases $t \le 0$, $0 \le t \le 1$, $t \ge 1$.
13. Size $s > 0$: pick $i$ with $m_i \neq 0$, and let $q = g - x_i$ if $m_i > 0$.
14. Then $q$ and $q + 1$ have size $s - 1$, so $q^\#$ and $(q + 1)^\#$ are definable.
15. By step 7, $g^\# = (q^\# \oplus x_i) \odot (q + 1)^\#$ is definable.
16. If $m_i < 0$, then $1 - g = (1 - g - x_i) + x_i$, and $1 - g - x_i$ has size $s - 1$.
17. So $(1 - g)^\#$ is definable by step 15, and $g^\# = \neg (1 - g)^\#$ by step 12.

**Part 3: max-min representation.**
Let $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be convex.
Let $f \colon D \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous, with affine $g_1, \dots, g_k$ such that $f(x) \in \set{g_1(x), \dots, g_k(x)}$ for all $x$.
Claim: for all $x, y \in D$ there is $j$ with $g_j(y) \ge f(y)$ and $g_j(x) \le f(x)$.

18. Put $\varphi(s) = f(y + s(x - y))$ and $h_j(s) = g_j(y + s(x - y))$ for $s \in [0, 1]$.
19. Each $h_j$ is affine in $s$, and $\varphi(s) \in \set{h_j(s)}$ for each $s$.
20. Let $J = \set{j : h_j(0) \ge \varphi(0)}$ and $\psi = \min_{j \in J} h_j$, where $J$ is not empty.
21. $\psi$ is concave, and $\psi(0) = \varphi(0)$, since some $h_j$ equals $\varphi$ at $0$.
22. Assume, for contradiction, that $\psi(1) > \varphi(1)$.
23. Let $s_0 = \sup \set{s \in [0, 1] : \varphi(s) \ge \psi(s)}$; then $s_0 < 1$ and $\varphi(s_0) = \psi(s_0)$, by continuity.
24. Two distinct affine $h_i \neq h_j$ agree at most at one point.
25. So there is $\delta > 0$ such that no such crossing lies in $(s_0, s_0 + \delta)$.
26. On that interval, the closed sets $\set{s : \varphi(s) = h_i(s)}$ for distinct $h_i$ are disjoint and cover it.
27. The interval is connected, so $\varphi = h_i$ on all of it, for one $i$.
28. By continuity $h_i(s_0) = \psi(s_0)$, and $h_i(s) < \psi(s)$ for $s$ just above $s_0$, by step 23.
29. $h_i - \psi$ is convex, is $0$ at $s_0$, and is negative just above $s_0$.
30. So $h_i - \psi > 0$ on $[0, s_0)$, and in particular $h_i(0) \ge \psi(0) = \varphi(0)$ in all cases.
31. Hence $i \in J$, so $\psi \le h_i$ everywhere, which contradicts step 28.
32. So $\psi(1) \le \varphi(1)$, and the index $j \in J$ with $h_j(1) = \psi(1)$ proves the claim.

**Part 4: the representation.**

33. For $y \in D$ let $S_y = \set{j : g_j(y) \ge f(y)}$ and $F_y = \min_{j \in S_y} g_j$.
34. $F_y(y) = f(y)$, since some $g_j$ equals $f$ at $y$.
35. $F_y(x) \le f(x)$ for all $x$, by Part 3.
36. So $f = \max_{y \in D} F_y$, and this is a max over the finitely many distinct sets $S_y$.

**Part 5: every McNaughton function is definable.**

37. Let $f$ be a McNaughton function with pieces $g_j$, and apply Part 4 with $D = [0, 1]^n$.
38. $\tau$ is non-decreasing, so $\tau(\max(a, b)) = \max(\tau a, \tau b)$ and $\tau(\min(a, b)) = \min(\tau a, \tau b)$.
39. $f$ has values in $[0, 1]$, so $f = \tau \circ f = \max_y \min_{j \in S_y} g_j^\#$.
40. Each $g_j^\#$ is definable by Part 2.
41. $\max(x, y)$ is defined by $(p \to q) \to q$, by [[the max example|~fc72b1ed]], and $\min(x, y) = 1 - \max(1 - x, 1 - y)$.
42. So $f$ is definable. $\blacksquare$

## Idea

Cut the function into its affine pieces.
Each piece, clipped to $[0, 1]$, is a formula, built one variable at a time.
A continuous function is a max of mins of its pieces, and Łukasiewicz logic has max and min.

## Variants

1. The proof above is constructive: from the list of pieces it computes a formula.
2. Another route triangulates the cube into unimodular simplices and writes $f$ through the hat functions of the triangulation.
