## Example

[[Fermat's little theorem|0M25PRN3WQ]] says: if $p$ is a prime and $p$ does not divide the integer $a$, then $a^{p - 1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$.
We derive it from the general theorem that [[$g^{|G|} = e$ in each finite group $G$|0ZEPNS9BXK]], which is a consequence of [[Lagrange's theorem|3C53TD]].

1. Let $G = (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times = \{\overline{1}, \overline{2}, \ldots, \overline{p - 1}\}$, the set of nonzero residues modulo $p$ with multiplication.
2. The set $G$ is a group, the [[group of units|0F8X8ZRQ53]] of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. Each nonzero residue has an inverse, because [[$\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field|0R8YXRC9AJ]].
3. The group $G$ has $p - 1$ elements, so $|G| = p - 1$.
4. Since $p$ does not divide $a$, the residue $\overline{a}$ is not $\overline{0}$, so $\overline{a} \in G$.
5. The general theorem gives $\overline{a}^{\,|G|} = \overline{1}$, that is, $\overline{a}^{\,p - 1} = \overline{1}$.
6. In terms of integers, this says $a^{p - 1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$.

We check the case $p = 7$ and $a = 3$.

7. The powers of $3$ modulo $7$ are $3^1 \equiv 3$, $3^2 = 9 \equiv 2$, $3^3 \equiv 2 \cdot 3 = 6$, $3^4 \equiv 6 \cdot 3 = 18 \equiv 4$, $3^5 \equiv 4 \cdot 3 = 12 \equiv 5$ and $3^6 \equiv 5 \cdot 3 = 15 \equiv 1$.
8. So $3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, as the theorem says. The [[order|0WE2NHZJ7B]] of $\overline{3}$ is $6$, and $6$ divides $|G| = 6$.

## Remarks

The example shows how a general theorem makes a special proof shorter.
A direct proof of Fermat's little theorem needs an argument special to residues, for example a count of necklaces or the binomial expansion of $(a + 1)^p$.
The group proof uses only two facts: the nonzero residues form a group, and the order of a finite group kills each element.
The arithmetic of residues enters only in step 2.

If we replace the prime $p$ by any integer $n \geq 2$ and $G$ by the group of residues coprime to $n$, the same steps give Euler's theorem $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$.
So the general setting also points to the next generalization.
For $n = 8$ and $a = 3$ we get $\varphi(8) = 4$ and $3^4 = 81 = 10 \cdot 8 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take the numbers $1$ to $6$ and multiply them as on a clock with $7$ hours.
Start with $3$ and keep multiplying by $3$: you get $3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1$.
After six steps you are back at $1$.
Fermat noticed that this happens for each prime clock: $p - 1$ steps always bring you back to $1$.
Group theory explains it with one general fact about all finite groups, so we do not have to check each prime.

### L2 human

We check the theorem for $p = 5$ and each $a$ from $1$ to $4$.

1. $1^4 = 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$.
2. $2^4 = 16 = 3 \cdot 5 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$.
3. $3^4 = 81 = 16 \cdot 5 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$.
4. $4^4 = 256 = 51 \cdot 5 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$.

The typical use of the general form is to reduce large powers: $2^{100} = (2^4)^{25} \equiv 1 \pmod 5$.
A trap is the hypothesis $p \nmid a$.
For $a = 5$ and $p = 5$ we get $5^4 \equiv 0 \pmod 5$, because $\overline{5} = \overline{0}$ is not in the group $G$.
