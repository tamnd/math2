## Example

Take the four points $(0, 1)$, $(1, 2)$, $(2, 2)$, $(3, 4)$ and the matrix $A$ with rows $(1, t_i)$, as in [[the least-squares line|~942c5c13]].
Consider the line $y = 1 + t$, that is, $x = (1, 1)$.

1. The line passes through $(0, 1)$, $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 4)$, since $1 + 0 = 1$, $1 + 1 = 2$ and $1 + 3 = 4$.
2. At $t = 2$ it gives $3$, so it misses $(2, 2)$ by $1$.
3. The residual is $r = (0, 0, -1, 0)$, and $\norm{r}^2 = 1$.
4. The least-squares line $y = 0.9 + 0.9 t$ has residual sum of squares $0.7 < 1$. So $(1, 1)$ is not a least-squares solution.
5. Test by orthogonality: $A^{\mathsf{T}} r = (0 + 0 - 1 + 0, 0 + 0 - 2 + 0) = (-1, -2) \ne 0$. So $r$ is not orthogonal to the column space, which confirms step 4 by [[existence and uniqueness|V9AH43ZY91]].

## Remarks

A line that fits most points exactly can still be a poor fit in the least-squares sense, because one large miss costs a lot when squared.
The least-squares line spreads the error: it misses each point by at most $0.7$, and it hits none of them.
If the point $(2, 2)$ is replaced by $(2, 3)$, all four points lie on $y = 1 + t$, and then this line is the least-squares line with residual $0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Passing exactly through three points out of four looks good, but the fourth miss is large.
Least squares prefers many small misses to one large miss.

### L2 human

To test whether a candidate $x$ is a least-squares solution, compute $A^{\mathsf{T}} (y - Ax)$.
It must be the zero vector; here it is $(-1, -2)$.
The trap is to judge a fit by how many points it hits.
