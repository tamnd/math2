## Summary

The *product* of two [[Kripke frames|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]] $\mathfrak{F}_1 = (W_1, R_1)$ and $\mathfrak{F}_2 = (W_2, R_2)$ is the bimodal frame $\mathfrak{F}_1 \times \mathfrak{F}_2 = (W_1 \times W_2, R_h, R_v)$.
Here $(u, v) R_h (u', v')$ iff $u R_1 u'$ and $v = v'$, and $(u, v) R_v (u', v')$ iff $u = u'$ and $v R_2 v'$.
A Kripke frame is a set with a binary relation, and a bimodal frame has one relation for each box.
The first box $\square_1$ moves along $R_h$, horizontally, and the second box $\square_2$ moves along $R_v$, vertically.
For Kripke complete unimodal logics $L_1$ and $L_2$, the *product logic* $L_1 \times L_2$ is the set of bimodal formulas valid in every frame $\mathfrak{F}_1 \times \mathfrak{F}_2$ with $\mathfrak{F}_1 \models L_1$ and $\mathfrak{F}_2 \models L_2$.
Products are the basic two-dimensional modal logics.

## Motivation

Many applications combine two modal dimensions, such as time and space, or time and the knowledge of an agent.
A point of a product is a pair, for example a moment and a place, and each box moves in one coordinate only.
Products also connect [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]] with first-order logic: in $\mathsf{S5} \times \mathsf{S5}$, the boxes behave as the quantifiers $\forall x$ and $\forall y$ of a language with two variables.
This makes products the modal counterpart of [[cylindric algebras|1MDT9BF81H]] of finite dimension.

## Notation

$\lozenge_1, \lozenge_2$ are the diamonds of $\square_1, \square_2$, and $\mathfrak{F}_1 \times \mathfrak{F}_2$ is the product frame.
$L_1 \otimes L_2$ is the *fusion*, the least bimodal logic that contains $L_1$ for $\square_1$ and $L_2$ for $\square_2$.
The *commutator* is $[L_1, L_2] = L_1 \otimes L_2 \oplus \mathrm{com} \oplus \mathrm{chr}$, with the axioms
$$\mathrm{com}: \ \square_1 \square_2 p \leftrightarrow \square_2 \square_1 p, \qquad \mathrm{chr}: \ \lozenge_1 \square_2 p \to \square_2 \lozenge_1 p.$$
$L^n$ is the $n$-fold product $L \times \dots \times L$, for example $\mathsf{S5}^2 = \mathsf{S5} \times \mathsf{S5}$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. In every product frame, the axioms $\mathrm{com}$ and $\mathrm{chr}$ are valid, so they are theorems of every product logic; see [[commutativity and the Church-Rosser property in product frames|~05448894]].
2. In a product of two universal relations, $\square_1$ and $\square_2$ read as $\forall x$ and $\forall y$, and the formula $\square_2 \lozenge_1 p \to \lozenge_1 \square_2 p$ fails as $\forall y \exists x$ fails to imply $\exists x \forall y$; see [[S5 times S5 and quantifier exchange|~33e1347c]].

### Non-examples

1. A bimodal frame in which two arrows from one point cannot be completed to a square refutes $\mathrm{chr}$, so it is not a product and does not validate $\mathsf{K} \times \mathsf{K}$; see [[a bimodal frame that is not a product|~7f551a07]].
2. The fusion $\mathsf{K} \otimes \mathsf{K}$ is not a product logic: its frames are all bimodal frames, and it does not prove $\mathrm{com}$.

### Basic properties

1. In a product frame, $R_h \circ R_v = R_v \circ R_h$, and whenever $x R_h y$ and $x R_v z$, there is $t$ with $y R_v t$ and $z R_h t$. These two facts give $\mathrm{com}$ and $\mathrm{chr}$.
2. A formula with $\square_1$ only is true at $(u, v)$ iff it is true at $u$ in $\mathfrak{F}_1$, under the valuation of the row $W_1 \times \set{v}$. So for consistent Kripke complete $L_1, L_2$, the $\square_1$-part of $L_1 \times L_2$ is $L_1$, and the same holds for $\square_2$.
3. Hence $L_1 \otimes L_2 \subseteq [L_1, L_2] \subseteq L_1 \times L_2$.
4. Gabbay and Shehtman proved $L_1 \times L_2 = [L_1, L_2]$ for Horn axiomatizable $L_1$ and $L_2$. Roughly, such logics have axioms whose frame conditions are [[universal Horn sentences|9A4CYWXPEA]]. This covers $\mathsf{K}$, $\mathsf{T}$, $\mathsf{K4}$, $\mathsf{S4}$ and $\mathsf{S5}$, so for example $\mathsf{K} \times \mathsf{K} = [\mathsf{K}, \mathsf{K}]$.
5. The logics $\mathsf{K} \times \mathsf{K}$ and $\mathsf{S5} \times \mathsf{S5}$ are decidable.
6. Hirsch, Hodkinson and Kurucz proved that every trimodal logic $L$ with $\mathsf{K}^3 \subseteq L \subseteq \mathsf{S5}^3$ is undecidable. So products of three decidable logics can be undecidable.

### History

Krister Segerberg and Valentin Shehtman studied two-dimensional modal logics such as $\mathsf{S5} \times \mathsf{S5}$ in the 1970s.
Dov Gabbay and Valentin Shehtman introduced products of arbitrary modal logics in 1998 and proved the product matching theorem for Horn axiomatized logics.
Robin Hirsch, Ian Hodkinson and Agi Kurucz proved the undecidability of the logics between $\mathsf{K}^3$ and $\mathsf{S5}^3$.
The monograph "Many-dimensional modal logics: theory and applications" by Gabbay, Kurucz, Wolter and Zakharyaschev appeared in 2003.

### Common errors

1. Confusing the product with the fusion. The fusion has no interaction between the boxes; the product forces $\mathrm{com}$ and $\mathrm{chr}$.
2. Assuming that every frame of $L_1 \times L_2$ is a product. The frame $W = \set{x, y}$ with $R_1 = R_2 = \set{(x, y), (y, y)}$ validates $\mathrm{com}$ and $\mathrm{chr}$, hence $\mathsf{K} \times \mathsf{K}$, but in a product no pair of distinct points is related by both relations.
3. Assuming that products of decidable logics are decidable. This holds for some products of two logics, and it fails for all logics between $\mathsf{K}^3$ and $\mathsf{S5}^3$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a chessboard.
One kind of step moves a piece along its row, and the other kind moves it along its column.
A product logic describes what is true about such boards: for example, a move right and then up reaches the same squares as a move up and then right.

### L2 human

1. Let $\mathfrak{F}_1 = \mathfrak{F}_2 = (\set{0, 1}, <)$.
2. The product has four points $(0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1)$, with $(0, v) R_h (1, v)$ and $(u, 0) R_v (u, 1)$.
3. From $(0, 0)$, the path $R_h$ then $R_v$ reaches $(1, 1)$, and the path $R_v$ then $R_h$ also reaches $(1, 1)$.
4. So $\lozenge_1 \lozenge_2 p \leftrightarrow \lozenge_2 \lozenge_1 p$ holds at $(0, 0)$ for every valuation.

The typical use of products is to model two independent dimensions, such as time and space.
A trap: the logic of all product frames can be much more complex than either component, as the three-dimensional case shows.

### L3 human

The product $\mathsf{S5}^n$ is the modal form of a fragment of first-order logic: $n$ variables, no equality, and atoms only of the form $P(x_1, \dots, x_n)$.
Its algebras are the representable diagonal-free cylindric algebras of dimension $n$.
In two dimensions, decidability parallels that of [[two-variable logic|S7C3C7DJRF]].
In three dimensions, undecidability parallels that of first-order logic with three variables.
Products are one way to combine logics, with strong interaction; the fusion is the combination without interaction.
