## Example

Let $\sigma = \forall x \, \exists y \, (x < y)$.
We play the [[evaluation game|D45Z7HETDA]] for $\sigma$ in $(\N, <)$ and in $(\set{0, 1, 2}, <)$; compare the [[Tarskian evaluation|~3f437639]] of the same sentence.

In $(\N, <)$:

1. The first symbol is $\forall x$, so Abelard, the falsifier, picks some $a \in \N$.
2. The next symbol is $\exists y$, so Eloise, the verifier, picks some $b \in \N$. Her strategy is $b = a + 1$.
3. The play reaches the atomic formula $x < y$ with $x \mapsto a$ and $y \mapsto a + 1$. Since $a < a + 1$, Eloise wins.
4. The strategy wins against every choice of $a$, so Eloise has a winning strategy, and $\sigma$ is true.

In $(\set{0, 1, 2}, <)$:

1. Abelard picks $a = 2$.
2. Eloise picks $b \in \set{0, 1, 2}$. In each case $2 < b$ is false.
3. So Abelard wins every play in which he starts with $2$. This is a winning strategy for him, and $\sigma$ is false.

## Remarks

The example shows the game reading of $\forall \exists$: the challenge comes first, and the answer may depend on it.
Eloise's strategy in $(\N, <)$ is the function $a \mapsto a + 1$, a Skolem function for $\sigma$.
Other winning strategies exist, such as $a \mapsto a + 7$ or $a \mapsto 2a + 1$; the strategy $a \mapsto 2a$ loses when Abelard picks $0$.
For $\exists y \, \forall x \, (x < y)$, Eloise must pick $b$ first; then Abelard answers $a = b$, and $b < b$ fails, so Abelard wins in both structures.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Abelard names a number, and Eloise must name a bigger one.
With all the natural numbers, Eloise always wins: she says "your number plus one".
With only $0$, $1$ and $2$, Abelard says $2$, and Eloise has nothing bigger.

### L2 human

We test two of Eloise's strategies in $(\N, <)$.

1. Strategy $b = a + 1$: wins for every $a$, as shown above.
2. Strategy $b = 5$: wins when $a \leq 4$, and loses when Abelard picks $a = 5$, because $5 < 5$ is false.

So the constant strategy is not winning, although it wins some plays.
The typical use is to see that truth needs a strategy that wins every play.
A trap is to call the sentence false because one strategy of Eloise loses.
