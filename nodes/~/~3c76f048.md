## Example

Three cards $1$, $2$ and $3$ are dealt to two agents $a$ and $b$, one card each, and the third card stays face down.
A deal is a pair $xy$: agent $a$ holds $x$ and agent $b$ holds $y$, with $x \neq y$.
So there are $3 \cdot 2 = 6$ deals: $12$, $13$, $21$, $23$, $31$ and $32$.
The variable $a_i$ says "$a$ holds $i$", and $b_i$ says "$b$ holds $i$".
This is a [[multimodal|~d172c20d]] model with two boxes $K_a$ and $K_b$.

1. $xy \mathrel{R_a} x'y'$ iff $x = x'$: agent $a$ sees only its own card.
2. $xy \mathrel{R_b} x'y'$ iff $y = y'$: agent $b$ sees only its own card.
3. Both relations are [[equivalence relations|0G8VEZ3WTK]], since equality of one coordinate is reflexive, symmetric and transitive.
4. The [[partition|B5ZMBQ3HK7]] of $R_a$ is $\set{12, 13}$, $\set{21, 23}$, $\set{31, 32}$.
5. The partition of $R_b$ is $\set{21, 31}$, $\set{12, 32}$, $\set{13, 23}$.

We evaluate at the actual deal $12$.

6. $K_a \neg b_1$ is true: the $R_a$-class of $12$ is $\set{12, 13}$, and $b$ holds $2$ or $3$ there, never $1$.
7. $K_a b_2$ is false: the deal $13$ is in the class, and there $b$ holds $3$.
8. $K_b a_1$ is false: the $R_b$-class of $12$ is $\set{12, 32}$, and $a$ holds $3$ at $32$.
9. $K_b \neg a_2$ is true: in $\set{12, 32}$, agent $a$ holds $1$ or $3$.
10. $K_a \neg K_b a_1$: we need $\neg K_b a_1$ at $12$ and at $13$.
11. At $12$ this is step 8. At $13$, the $R_b$-class is $\set{13, 23}$, and $a$ holds $2$ at $23$, so $K_b a_1$ is false.
12. So $K_a \neg K_b a_1$ is true: $a$ knows that $b$ does not know $a$'s card.
13. $K_a (b_2 \vee b_3)$ is true: $b_2$ holds at $12$ and $b_3$ holds at $13$.
14. $\neg K_a \neg b_3$ is true at $12$, since $13$ is in the class: $a$ considers it possible that $b$ holds $3$.

## Remarks

Each box alone satisfies the laws of [[S5|VC3A2VWXND]], since its relation is an equivalence relation.
The logic of all frames with two equivalence relations is the fusion $\mathsf{S5} \otimes \mathsf{S5}$, often written $\mathsf{S5}_2$.
There is no interaction axiom between $K_a$ and $K_b$: steps 6 and 8 show that one agent can know what the other does not.
Nested formulas like step 12 are the reason for several boxes: they describe knowledge about knowledge of others.
[[Common knowledge|7BCMKF2P7X]] adds a box for the transitive closure of $R_a \cup R_b$; here that closure joins all six deals.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Each player sees only their own card.
So a player cannot tell apart two deals that give them the same card.
Holding card $1$, player $a$ knows that $b$ does not hold $1$.
But $a$ cannot know whether $b$ holds $2$ or $3$.
And $a$ knows that $b$ cannot know $a$'s card, since $b$ always has two cards left to guess from.

### L2 human

1. List the six deals.
2. Group them by $a$'s card for $K_a$, and by $b$'s card for $K_b$.
3. A formula $K_i \varphi$ is true at a deal iff $\varphi$ is true at every deal of its group for agent $i$.

The typical use is to compute nested knowledge by working from the inner box outward, as in steps 10 to 12.
A trap: $K_a \neg K_b a_1$ is not the same as $\neg K_a K_b a_1$; the first is stronger and needs $\neg K_b a_1$ at every deal of $a$'s group.

### L3 human

The model is a finite structure for dynamic epistemic logic.
A public announcement deletes the deals where it is false, and the knowledge of each agent changes with the remaining classes.
For the fusion $\mathsf{S5}_n$, satisfiability is PSPACE-complete for $n \ge 2$, in contrast with the NP-complete single-agent case.
