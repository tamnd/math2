## Example

We derive the dual form of axiom $\mathrm{B}$ of the [[Brouwerian modal logic|2QPT4KCM0Q]] in the logic $\mathsf{KB} = \mathsf{K} + \mathrm{B}$, without axiom $\mathrm{T}$:
$$\mathsf{KB} \vdash \lozenge\square p \to p.$$
Then we show that $\mathsf{T}$ does not prove it.

1. The instance of $\mathrm{B}$ for $\neg p$ is $\neg p \to \square\lozenge\neg p$.
2. The tautology $\neg\neg p \leftrightarrow p$ and RE of [[normal modal logic K|~b1c67ad7]] give $\lozenge\neg p \leftrightarrow \neg\square p$.
3. RE again gives $\square\lozenge\neg p \leftrightarrow \square\neg\square p$. So step 1 becomes $\neg p \to \square\neg\square p$.
4. Contraposition gives $\neg\square\neg\square p \to \neg\neg p$, and $\neg\square\neg\square p$ is $\lozenge\square p$.
5. With $\neg\neg p \to p$, we get $\lozenge\square p \to p$. No step used $\mathrm{T}$.
6. $\mathsf{T} \nvdash \lozenge\square p \to p$. Take $W = \set{1, 2}$, $R = \set{(1, 1), (2, 2), (1, 2)}$ and $V(p) = \set{2}$.
7. The frame is reflexive, so it validates $\mathsf{T}$, by [[common modal axiom schemata|FDFQFWPM4X]] and [[soundness|~d189ee4b]].
8. $\square p$ is true at $2$, since $2$ sees only $2$, so $\lozenge\square p$ is true at $1$.
9. $p$ is false at $1$, so $\lozenge\square p \to p$ fails at $1$.

## Remarks

The example shows that $\mathrm{B}$ and $\lozenge\square p \to p$ are interderivable in $\mathsf{K}$: the same steps, read backwards with $\neg p$ again, give $\mathrm{B}$ back.
On a [[symmetric|061J5VGSPT]] frame the dual form reads as follows.
If $w$ has a successor $v$ at which $\square p$ holds, then $p$ holds at every successor of $v$.
By symmetry, $w$ is a successor of $v$, so $p$ holds at $w$.
The countermodel of step 6 fails exactly here: $1$ sees $2$, but $2$ does not see $1$.
If we add the pair $(2, 1)$, the relation becomes $W \times W$, and $\square p$ becomes false at $2$, so step 8 breaks.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Brouwerian rule says: if $p$ is true at a world, then from every world it sees, one can look back to a $p$-world.
The dual form says: if a world sees some world from which only $p$-worlds are visible, then $p$ is true at the first world.
The reason is the same in both forms: in a symmetric picture, each world sees back every world that sees it.
For example, if Ann's town sees Bob's town, and from Bob's town only sunny towns are visible, then Ann's town is sunny.

### L2 human

1. Take $W = \set{1, 2}$ with $R = W \times W$, and $V(p) = \set{2}$.
2. $\square p$ is false at both worlds, since $1$ lacks $p$.
3. So $\lozenge\square p$ is false at both worlds, and $\lozenge\square p \to p$ holds everywhere.
4. This agrees with the derivation, since this frame validates $\mathsf{KB}$.

The typical use of the dual form is in proofs about symmetric frames, where it is often shorter than $\mathrm{B}$.
A trap: the dual of $\mathrm{B}$ is $\lozenge\square p \to p$, not $\square\lozenge p \to p$; the second formula is not valid on symmetric frames.
