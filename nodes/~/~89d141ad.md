## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A$ be an alternating $2n \times 2n$ matrix over $K$: $a_{ji} = -a_{ij}$ and $a_{ii} = 0$ for all $i, j$.
Then
$$\operatorname{pf}(A)^2 = \det A,$$
where $\operatorname{pf}$ is the [[Pfaffian|C9JJD2AVJR]] and $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The matrix must be alternating, since the Pfaffian is defined only for such matrices.
- The symmetric matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{R}$ has determinant $-1$, which is not a square in $\mathbb{R}$: see the [[non-example|~f0f0643f]].
- The size must be even for the identity to have content; for odd size both sides are $0$, by the convention $\operatorname{pf} = 0$ and the [[odd size statement|~0e7877a3]].

### Consequences

- The determinant of a real skew-symmetric matrix is $\ge 0$, since it is a square.
- The determinant of an alternating integer matrix is a perfect square in $\mathbb{Z}$.
- An alternating matrix over a field is invertible if and only if its Pfaffian is nonzero.

### Converse

A matrix whose determinant is a square need not be alternating: $I_2$ has $\det I_2 = 1 = 1^2$.

### Generalizations

For every $2n \times 2n$ matrix $B$, $\operatorname{pf}(B A B^T) = \det(B) \operatorname{pf}(A)$.
For $A = J$, the standard matrix with blocks $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ on the diagonal, this gives $\operatorname{pf}(B J B^T) = \det B$.

### History

Cayley first proved the identity in 1849.
He introduced the term Pfaffian in 1852 and named it indirectly after Johann Friedrich Pfaff.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The determinant of an alternating matrix of even size is always a perfect square.
It is the square of a smaller polynomial, the Pfaffian.
For a $2 \times 2$ matrix with entries $0, a, -a, 0$, the Pfaffian is $a$ and the determinant is $a^2$.

### L2 human

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \\ -1 & 0 & 4 & 5 \\ -2 & -4 & 0 & 6 \\ -3 & -5 & -6 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. The formula for $4 \times 4$ gives $\operatorname{pf}(A) = a_{12} a_{34} - a_{13} a_{24} + a_{14} a_{23} = 6 - 10 + 12 = 8$.
2. So the theorem gives $\det A = 64$.
3. The [[worked example|~6febb6e4]] checks $\det A = 64$ by a Laplace expansion.

The typical use is to replace a determinant of size $2n$ by a Pfaffian, which has fewer terms and a definite sign.
A trap: $\operatorname{pf}(A)$ is not $\sqrt{\det A}$ with a chosen sign; its sign is fixed by the formula and can be negative.

### L3 human

The general proof uses the generic alternating matrix over $\mathbb{Z}[x_{ij}]$.
Over a field, a nonsingular alternating form has a symplectic basis, so $A = B J B^T$ for some invertible $B$.
Then $\det A = (\det B)^2$ and $\operatorname{pf}(A) = \det B$.
The identity of polynomials then holds over every commutative ring.
