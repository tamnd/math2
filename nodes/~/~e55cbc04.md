## Statement

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional inner product space over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.
Let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear, and assume that $V$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $T$.
This holds for every [[normal|0ENZN5G4CV]] $T$ if $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$, and for every [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]] $T$ if $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$.
Let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$ be the distinct eigenvalues of $T$, with [[eigenspaces|0AYEPBQMAW]] $E_1, \dots, E_m$.
Let $P_i$ be the [[orthogonal projection|0Q8HZTJC7G]] onto $E_i$.

1. Resolution of the identity. Each $P_i$ is self-adjoint with $P_i^2 = P_i$. Also $P_i P_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$, and $P_1 + \dots + P_m = I$.
2. Spectral decomposition. $T = \lambda_1 P_1 + \dots + \lambda_m P_m$.
3. Polynomials. $p(T) = p(\lambda_1) P_1 + \dots + p(\lambda_m) P_m$ for every polynomial $p$ with coefficients in $\mathbb{K}$.
4. Each $P_i$ is a polynomial in $T$:
$$P_i = \prod_{j \neq i} \frac{T - \lambda_j I}{\lambda_i - \lambda_j}.$$
5. $T^* = \overline{\lambda_1} P_1 + \dots + \overline{\lambda_m} P_m$, and $T^*$ is a polynomial in $T$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The orthonormal eigenbasis is needed for self-adjoint $P_i$. [[The trap matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$|~3859cd16]] is diagonalizable, and the formula of part 4 gives projections onto its eigenlines. But these projections are not orthogonal, because the eigenlines are not perpendicular.
- Some basis of eigenvectors is needed for part 2 at all. [[The shear|~1dc00298]] has the single eigenvalue $1$ with the eigenspace spanned by $e_1$, and $1 \cdot P_1 \neq A$.

### Consequences

- Functional calculus. For any function $f$ defined on $\set{\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m}$, define $f(T) = \sum_i f(\lambda_i) P_i$. By part 3, this agrees with $p(T)$ for every polynomial $p$ with $p(\lambda_i) = f(\lambda_i)$ for all $i$.
- A self-adjoint $T$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_i \geq 0$ has the square root $\sqrt{T} = \sum_i \sqrt{\lambda_i} P_i$. It is self-adjoint with nonnegative eigenvalues, and its square is $T$.
- If no $\lambda_i$ is $0$, then $T^{-1} = \sum_i \lambda_i^{-1} P_i$.
- Part 5 shows that $T$ and $T^*$ commute with every operator that commutes with $T$. This is used in [[simultaneous unitary diagonalization|~60326533]].

### Converse

Over $\mathbb{C}$, every operator of the form $T = \sum_i \lambda_i P_i$ is normal, when the $P_i$ are self-adjoint with $P_i P_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$.
Indeed $T^* = \sum_i \overline{\lambda_i} P_i$, and both $T T^*$ and $T^* T$ equal $\sum_i \abs{\lambda_i}^2 P_i^2$.
If, in addition, all $\lambda_i$ are real, then $T^* = T$.

### Generalizations

For bounded normal operators on a Hilbert space, the sum is replaced by an integral over a [[projection-valued measure|X71XRPJR7D]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A normal operator splits the space into perpendicular eigenspaces.
On each eigenspace it multiplies by one number.
So the operator is a sum: project onto each eigenspace, multiply by its eigenvalue, and add the results.
To apply a function to the operator, apply it to each eigenvalue.

### L2 human

Worked example: [[the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$|~a0fff7f9]].

1. The eigenvalues are $6$ and $1$, with the eigenvectors $(2, 1)$ and $(1, -2)$.
2. The projections are $P_1 = \tfrac{1}{5} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $P_2 = \tfrac{1}{5} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. Part 4 gives $P_1 = \tfrac{1}{5}(A - I)$ and $P_2 = -\tfrac{1}{5}(A - 6I)$, which agree with step 2.
4. Then $A = 6 P_1 + P_2$ and $A^2 = 36 P_1 + P_2$.

[[The matrix $I + J$|~f04f6f45]] gives an example with a repeated eigenvalue, where $P_2$ has rank $2$.
A trap: the sum runs over the distinct eigenvalues, and each $P_i$ projects onto the whole eigenspace, not onto one eigenvector.

### L3 human

Part 1 says that $\set{P_1, \dots, P_m}$ is a finite projection-valued measure on the spectrum.
The map $f \mapsto f(T)$ is an algebra homomorphism from the functions on the spectrum to the operators, and it maps $\overline{f}$ to $f(T)^*$.
In a $C^*$-algebra, the continuous functional calculus of a normal element generalizes this map.
