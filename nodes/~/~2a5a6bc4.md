## Example

Let $W = \set{w, u}$ with the [[partial order|0QV2JS9RJ3]] $w \le u$ (and $w \le w$, $u \le u$).
Let $R = \set{(u, u)}$ and $V(p) = \set{u}$.
We check that this is a model of [[intuitionistic modal logic|~e9be76d0]] in which $w \Vdash \neg \square \neg p$ but $w \not\Vdash \lozenge p$.

1. The relation $\le$ is a partial order: its pairs are $(w, w)$, $(u, u)$ and $(w, u)$.
2. $V(p) = \set{u}$ is an up-set, because the only world above $u$ is $u$.
3. F1 holds. The only pair in $R$ is $u R u$, and the only world above $u$ is $u$; take $v' = u$.
4. F2 holds. The only instance is $u R u$ with $u \le u$; take $w' = u$.
5. $w \not\Vdash \lozenge p$, because $w$ has no $R$-successor.
6. $u \not\Vdash \neg p$, because $u \le u$ and $u \Vdash p$.
7. $u \not\Vdash \square \neg p$, because $u \le u$, $u R u$ and $u \not\Vdash \neg p$.
8. $w \not\Vdash \square \neg p$, because $w \le u$, $u R u$ and $u \not\Vdash \neg p$.
9. The worlds above $w$ are $w$ and $u$, and neither forces $\square \neg p$. So $w \Vdash \neg \square \neg p$.
10. By steps 5 and 9, $w \not\Vdash \neg \square \neg p \to \lozenge p$.

## Remarks

The example shows that $\neg \square \neg p \to \lozenge p$ is not a theorem of $\mathsf{IK}$, by soundness for birelational frames.
So $\lozenge$ cannot be defined as $\neg \square \neg$ in $\mathsf{IK}$, and both modalities must be primitive.
The converse $\lozenge p \to \neg \square \neg p$ is a theorem, and it holds at both worlds here.
The failure comes from the order: $\neg$ looks at the later world $u$, while $\lozenge$ looks only at the $R$-successors of $w$.
If we add the pair $(w, u)$ to $R$, then $w \Vdash \lozenge p$ and the formula holds at $w$.
If we make $\le$ the identity, then $w \Vdash \square \neg p$, and the model becomes a classical Kripke model where the duality holds.

## Explanations

### L1 human

There are two moments, now and later.
Later there is an alternative where $p$ holds, but now there is no alternative at all.
Now we can already be sure that "$p$ is impossible" will never be established, because later it fails.
But now we cannot point to an alternative where $p$ holds, so "$p$ is possible" is not yet true.

### L2 human

1. List the pairs $(w', v)$ with $w \le w'$ and $w' R v$: there is only $(u, u)$.
2. So $w \Vdash \square \neg p$ iff $u \Vdash \neg p$, and this fails because $u \Vdash p$.
3. The same list for $u$ is $(u, u)$, so $u \not\Vdash \square \neg p$.
4. Hence no world above $w$ forces $\square \neg p$, and $w \Vdash \neg \square \neg p$.
5. The $R$-successors of $w$ form the empty set, so $w \not\Vdash \lozenge p$.

The typical use of such a model is to show that an axiom which holds classically is independent over the intuitionistic base.
A trap: in the clause for $\square$, we must look at the $R$-successors of every later world, not only of $w$.
