## Summary

Groups appear in science wherever an object has symmetries, and in technology wherever a calculation needs a reversible rule.
The [[symmetry group|0ZG3Z5JZ85]] of an object is the set of all transformations that leave the object unchanged, with composition as the operation.
In chemistry, the symmetry group of a molecule controls its spectra and its chirality.
In crystallography, the symmetry groups of crystals are the $230$ space groups.
In physics, symmetries of the laws of nature give conservation laws and organize the elementary particles.
In cryptography, the difficulty of a calculation in a large [[cyclic group|0KVGH0J5B3]] protects keys on the internet.
In puzzles and in check digits, the [[order of an element|0WE2NHZJ7B]] and the sign of a permutation answer practical questions.
These applications show that the abstract axioms of a [[group|G5S1M9]] describe structures that occur outside of pure mathematics.

## Motivation

The axioms of a group look abstract, and a student can ask what they are for.
The answer is that symmetry is everywhere, and the symmetries of any object form a group.
The composition of two symmetries is a symmetry, the identity map is a symmetry, and the inverse of a symmetry is a symmetry.
So each theorem about groups gives information about the symmetries of molecules, crystals, physical laws and puzzles.
Other applications use groups for a different reason: the group operation is easy to compute, but some questions about it are hard.

## Notation

Chemists name the symmetry group of a molecule with the Schoenflies symbols, such as $C_{2v}$, $C_{3v}$, $T_d$ and $D_{6h}$.
Crystallographers use the Hermann-Mauguin symbols, such as $mm2$ for $C_{2v}$ and $\bar{4}3m$ for $T_d$.
Physicists write $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ for the group of rotations of space and $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ for the special unitary groups.
In cryptography, the group of nonzero residues modulo a prime $p$ is written $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ or $\mathbb{Z}_p^*$.

## Remarks

### Point groups in chemistry

The point group of a molecule is the group of the rotations, reflections and other isometries of space that fix one point and map the molecule onto itself.

1. The water molecule $\mathrm{H_2O}$ has the point group $C_{2v}$ with $4$ elements: the identity, a rotation by $180^\circ$ and two reflections in planes. Each element other than the identity has order $2$, so $C_{2v}$ is a copy of the [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]].
2. The ammonia molecule $\mathrm{NH_3}$ has the point group $C_{3v}$ with $6$ elements. This group acts on the three hydrogen atoms as all $6$ permutations, so it is a copy of the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$.
3. The methane molecule $\mathrm{CH_4}$ has the point group $T_d$ with $24$ elements. It acts on the four hydrogen atoms as all $24$ permutations, so it is a copy of $S_4$.

A molecule is chiral, that is, different from its mirror image, exactly when its point group contains only rotations.
Chemists also use the [[representations|WYGRXCG2RN]] of the point group to predict which vibrations of the molecule show up in infrared and Raman spectra.

### Space groups in crystals

A crystal is a pattern of atoms that repeats in three independent directions.
The translations that map the crystal onto itself form a [[lattice|WN9V7WSJKF]], a group [[isomorphic|08MG7H8JW5]] to $\mathbb{Z}^3$.
The full symmetry group of an ideal crystal is a space group, also called a three-dimensional [[crystallographic group|7KTJKFQT2N]].
There are $230$ space groups and $32$ crystallographic point groups in three dimensions, and $17$ [[wallpaper groups|MY3CBHARYQ]] for patterns in the plane.
The crystallographic restriction theorem says that a rotation symmetry of a lattice in the plane or in space has order $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$ or $6$.
So no periodic crystal has a symmetry of order $5$.
In 1982 Dan Shechtman observed a material with a symmetry of order $5$ in its diffraction pattern.
Such a material is a [[quasicrystal|RQABRHJYWV]]: its atoms are ordered, but the pattern does not repeat.

### Symmetry in physics

[[Noether's theorem|XHPDM5T2JG]] of 1918 connects each continuous symmetry of a physical system with a [[conservation law|8RA7AMWSAF]].

1. Symmetry under translation in time gives the conservation of energy.
2. Symmetry under translation in space gives the conservation of momentum.
3. Symmetry under the [[rotation group SO(3)|263GGJFA21]] gives the conservation of angular momentum.

In special relativity, the laws of physics have the same form for all observers related by the [[Lorentz group|QWK3BYVJ9W]].
In quantum mechanics, the states of a system with a symmetry group $G$ form a representation of $G$.
For example, the irreducible representations of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ have dimensions $1$, $3$, $5$, $7, \ldots$, and these numbers are the numbers of atomic orbitals of the types s, p, d, f.
In 1961 Murray Gell-Mann and Yuval Ne'eman sorted the known hadrons with the group [[SU(3)|CZCWFPNS4N]].
Their scheme predicted a new particle, the Omega minus, and experiments found it in 1964.
The Standard Model of particle physics is a [[gauge theory|2GKJQNQMVN]] with the group $\mathrm{SU}(3) \times \mathrm{SU}(2) \times \mathrm{U}(1)$.

### Cryptography

Let $G$ be a cyclic group with a generator $g$.
The [[discrete logarithm|0HJHYMGN3C]] problem is: given $g$ and $h = g^a$, find the exponent $a$.
The power $g^a$ is fast to compute by repeated squaring, but for good choices of $G$ no fast method is known to find $a$ from $g^a$.
The [[Diffie-Hellman key exchange|01TKKESHSB]] of 1976 uses this gap.
Here is the method with small numbers, in the group $(\mathbb{Z}/23\mathbb{Z})^\times$ and the generator $g = 5$.

1. Alice and Bob agree in public on $p = 23$ and $g = 5$. The element $5$ has order $22$ modulo $23$, because $5^2 \equiv 2$ and $5^{11} \equiv -1$.
2. Alice chooses the secret $a = 6$ and sends $A = 5^6 \bmod 23 = 8$.
3. Bob chooses the secret $b = 15$ and sends $B = 5^{15} \bmod 23 = 19$.
4. Alice computes $B^a = 19^6 \bmod 23 = 2$.
5. Bob computes $A^b = 8^{15} \bmod 23 = 2$.
6. Both numbers equal $5^{ab} = 5^{90}$, by the [[laws of exponents|00DRK90Z6K]]. So Alice and Bob share the key $2$.

A listener sees $23$, $5$, $8$ and $19$, and must solve a discrete logarithm to find the key.
In practice the prime $p$ has $2048$ bits or more.
[[Elliptic-curve cryptography|X1W8HKH73X]], proposed by Neal Koblitz and Victor Miller in 1985, uses the group of points of an [[elliptic curve|427N6S6QQW]] in place of $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$.
The [[RSA cryptosystem|0HM584C465]] of 1977 uses [[Euler's theorem|0VC4DVE7DQ]] in the [[group of units of Z/nZ|0F8X8ZRQ53]].
A large quantum computer could break these systems with the algorithm that Peter Shor published in 1994.

### Puzzles and card shuffles

1. The [[Rubik's Cube group|R2TA1PAPJ7]] is the group generated by the six quarter turns of the faces. Its order is $\dfrac{8! \cdot 3^7 \cdot 12! \cdot 2^{11}}{2} = 43\,252\,003\,274\,489\,856\,000$.
2. The formula counts the positions of the $8$ corner pieces and the $12$ edge pieces, with their orientations. The factors $3^7$, $2^{11}$ and the division by $2$ come from three conditions that every turn keeps.
3. A cube taken apart and put together at random can be in $12$ times as many states, so only $1$ in $12$ of them can be solved.
4. In 2010 a computer search showed that each position of the cube can be solved in at most $20$ face turns.
5. In the fifteen puzzle, a position with the empty square in its home corner can be reached exactly when the permutation of the $15$ tiles is even. So the position with only the tiles $14$ and $15$ exchanged cannot be reached, by the [[sign of a permutation|02DKV69ZH0]].
6. A perfect out-shuffle of $52$ cards cuts the deck into two halves of $26$ and interleaves them, with the top card staying on top. The card in position $i$, counted from $0$, moves to position $2i \bmod 51$ for $i \le 50$, and position $51$ stays fixed.
7. So $k$ out-shuffles restore the deck exactly when $2^k \equiv 1 \pmod{51}$. The smallest such $k$ is $8$, because $2^8 = 256 = 5 \cdot 51 + 1$ and no smaller power works. Thus the out-shuffle has order $8$.

### Check digits

A check digit is an extra digit that lets a computer detect errors in a code number.
The ISBN-10 code of a book has digits $d_1, \ldots, d_{10}$ with
$$10 d_1 + 9 d_2 + 8 d_3 + \cdots + 2 d_9 + 1 d_{10} \equiv 0 \pmod{11}.$$
The symbol X stands for the value $10$ in the last position.
This rule detects each error in a single digit and each exchange of two adjacent different digits, because $\mathbb{Z}/11\mathbb{Z}$ has no zero divisors.
The EAN-13 code on products uses the weights $1$ and $3$ modulo $10$, and it misses the exchange of two adjacent digits that differ by $5$.
In 1969 Jacobus Verhoeff used the [[dihedral group|0JZ27DMJ5D]] of order $10$ for a check digit that detects all single errors and all adjacent exchanges.
The method combines the product of this noncommutative group with a fixed permutation of the digits.

### Other applications

1. In music, the $12$ pitch classes are often identified with $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$. The transpositions and the inversions of a melody form a dihedral group of order $24$.
2. Error-correcting codes use groups and vector spaces over finite fields. A linear code is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^n$.
3. Robotics and computer graphics describe the positions of a rigid body by the [[Euclidean group|0H3YY8CPKH]] of rotations and translations.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A snowflake looks the same after a turn by one sixth of a full circle.
A turn that leaves an object looking the same is called a symmetry.
Two symmetries done one after the other give another symmetry, and each symmetry can be undone.
So the symmetries of an object form a group.
Chemists use the group of a molecule to predict its light spectrum.
Crystals, the laws of physics and the Rubik's Cube also have symmetry groups.
Groups also protect the internet: some calculations with groups are easy to do but very hard to undo.

### L2 human

We check the ISBN-10 rule on the number $0\text{-}387\text{-}94285\text{-}8$.

1. The digits are $0, 3, 8, 7, 9, 4, 2, 8, 5, 8$, and the weights are $10, 9, \ldots, 1$.
2. The weighted sum is $0 + 27 + 64 + 49 + 54 + 20 + 8 + 24 + 10 + 8 = 264$.
3. We have $264 = 11 \cdot 24$, so the sum is $\equiv 0 \pmod{11}$ and the number is valid.
4. Suppose that one digit $d_i$ is replaced by a different digit $d_i'$. The sum changes by $w_i (d_i' - d_i)$, where $w_i = 11 - i$.
5. Both $w_i$ and $d_i' - d_i$ are nonzero and less than $11$ in absolute value, so $11$ divides neither of them.
6. Since $11$ is prime, $11$ does not divide the product $w_i (d_i' - d_i)$. So the new sum is not $\equiv 0$, and the error is detected.
7. Suppose that two adjacent digits $d_i \ne d_{i+1}$ are exchanged. The weights differ by $1$, so the sum changes by $\pm (d_{i+1} - d_i)$.
8. Since $0 < |d_{i+1} - d_i| < 11$, this change is not $\equiv 0 \pmod{11}$, and the error is detected.

The typical use is in a program that reads a code number: it computes the weighted sum and rejects the number when the sum is not $0$ modulo $11$.
A trap is to use a modulus that is not prime, such as $10$: then a weight such as $2$ times a difference such as $5$ gives $10 \equiv 0$, and the error is missed.

### L3 human

Most applications use groups through their actions and their representations.
A symmetry group acts on the object, and the orbits and stabilizers of this [[group action|0WCY42EV9V]] carry the information that chemists and crystallographers need.
Representation theory, the study of homomorphisms from a group to groups of matrices, turns symmetry into linear algebra, and this is the form in which physics uses groups.
The continuous symmetries of physics are Lie groups, and Noether's theorem links their Lie algebras with conserved quantities.
Cryptography uses a different side of groups: the complexity of computations, such as the discrete logarithm in a cyclic group of large prime order.
The later sub-areas of this area give the tools: group actions, the orbit-stabilizer theorem, the classification of finite abelian groups and the theory of the symmetric group.
