## Summary

A proof of impossibility shows that no object of a given kind has a given property, or that no method of a given kind solves a given problem.
Its logical form is $\neg \exists x \, P(x)$, which is equivalent to $\forall x \, \neg P(x)$; see [[negation of an existential statement|05SZQKH75K]].
So it is a statement about all candidates, and checking some of them is never enough.
The usual tools are a [[proof by contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]] from an assumed object, and an obstruction.
An obstruction is a property, such as a parity, a remainder, a count or the degree of a field extension, that every candidate has and that the required object would not have.
Impossibility theorems settled several problems that were open for centuries, and they mark the limits of methods in algebra, geometry, logic and computation.

## Motivation

A problem that resists every attempt may be hard, or it may have no solution.
Failed attempts cannot tell the two apart, since one more attempt might succeed.
Only a proof can show that every attempt must fail, and for that the class of allowed attempts must be described exactly: ruler and compass steps, formulas built with radicals, or programs of a fixed kind.
Once the class is exact, a property shared by everything in the class can rule out the target.
This change of view, from searching for a solution to describing all possible solutions, is what made the classical construction problems solvable.

## Notation

Impossibility results are stated as "there is no $x$ such that $P(x)$", "$P(x)$ has no solution", or "it is impossible to ... by ...".
The last form must name the allowed methods; "impossible" without them has no precise meaning.
An invariant is a quantity that does not change under the allowed moves; a monovariant is one that changes in one direction only.
In Lean, the goal `¬ ∃ x, P x` is usually attacked with `rintro ⟨x, hx⟩`, which assumes a candidate `x` with `hx : P x` and leaves the goal `False`.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The equation x^2 - 3y^2 = 2 has no integer solution|~2729afd6]]: a remainder obstruction, since $x^2$ would leave remainder $2$ on division by $3$.
2. [[The last number on the board cannot be 0|~84813e1d]]: an invariant, the parity of the sum of the numbers, rules out the final state.
3. No rational number has square $2$; see [[irrationality of the square root of 2|0DZGSDHD92]]. The obstruction is the parity of the exponent of $2$ in $a^2$ and in $2b^2$.
4. [[Impossibility of trisecting the angle|0EWXPZCSZ0]] with ruler and compass: the angle of $60°$ cannot be trisected, because $\cos 20°$ is a root of an irreducible cubic, and ruler and compass only reach numbers whose degree over $\mathbb{Q}$ is a power of $2$.
5. The [[Abel-Ruffini theorem|163A2WQQJX]]: there is no formula in radicals for the roots of the general polynomial equation of degree $5$.
6. [[Cantor's theorem|0WTEMGYV2C]]: no function from a set $A$ onto its power set exists; the diagonal set is an obstruction that no such function can reach.

### Non-examples

1. [[A failed search for a solution of x^2 - 61y^2 = 1|~35a383f5]]: no solution with $y \leq 10^6$ exists, but solutions exist; a failed search proves nothing.
2. "Nobody has trisected a general angle with ruler and compass in two thousand years, so it is impossible." The conclusion is true, but the reason is not a proof.
3. Trisection with a marked ruler. With a straightedge that carries two marks, a construction known since Archimedes trisects every angle. The impossibility theorem concerns ruler and compass only, and it does not apply to other tools.

### Basic properties

1. A proof of impossibility is a proof of a universal statement: every candidate fails. All methods for universal statements apply, including [[proving universal statements|~ad3ff00f]] with an arbitrary candidate.
2. Invariant method: if every allowed move keeps a quantity $I$ unchanged, and the start and the target have different values of $I$, the target cannot be reached.
3. Counting method: if there are more objects than places, or a set is larger than another, a map with the required property cannot exist; see the [[pigeonhole principle|0HGVCWQGY3]].
4. Algebraic method: the solutions of the problem would lie in a structure, such as a field of degree a power of $2$ or a solvable extension, that the target is not in.
5. Every impossibility theorem is relative to a precise class of methods or objects. Changing the class, for example from integers to real numbers or from ruler and compass to a marked ruler, can make the impossible possible.

### History

Paolo Ruffini published an incomplete proof of the unsolvability of the quintic in 1799, and Niels Henrik Abel gave a complete proof in 1824.
Pierre Wantzel proved in 1837 that the general angle cannot be trisected and the cube cannot be doubled with ruler and compass.
Ferdinand von Lindemann proved in 1882 that $\pi$ is transcendental, and this showed that the circle cannot be squared; see [[impossibility of the classical construction problems|W8J42M1G28]].
In 1936 Alan Turing proved the [[undecidability of the halting problem|Z7TN2B35B0]], and in 1951 Kenneth Arrow published his [[impossibility theorem|VD8RCR44GQ]] on voting rules.

### Common errors

1. Concluding impossibility from a failed search, as in non-example 1.
2. Stating the result without its class of methods, or too strongly. The theorem on trisection says that some angles, such as $60°$, cannot be trisected with ruler and compass; the angle $90°$ can be, since an angle of $30°$ is constructible.
3. Using a quantity that is not invariant under all allowed moves. Each move must be checked.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Remove two opposite corners from a chessboard, leaving $62$ squares.
Can $31$ dominoes, each covering two neighbouring squares, cover the rest?
Each domino covers one black and one white square.
The two removed corners have the same colour, so the board now has $32$ squares of one colour and $30$ of the other.
$31$ dominoes would cover $31$ squares of each colour, so the covering is impossible, without trying a single arrangement.

### L2 human

We prove: there are no integers $x$ and $y$ with $6x + 9y = 10$.

1. Suppose, for a contradiction, that $x$ and $y$ are integers with $6x + 9y = 10$.
2. The left side is $3(2x + 3y)$, and $2x + 3y$ is an integer. So $3$ divides the left side.
3. The right side is $10 = 3 \cdot 3 + 1$, so $3$ does not divide it.
4. This is a contradiction, so no such integers exist.

The typical use: find a property, here divisibility by $3$, that holds for every value of the left side and fails for the right side.

A trap: the obstruction gives no information when it does not fail.
For $6x + 9y = 12$ the test with $3$ says nothing, and indeed $x = 2$, $y = 0$ is a solution; for $6x + 9y = 3$ the test says nothing either, and $x = -1$, $y = 1$ is a solution.

### L3 human

Galois theory turns the questions of trisection and of the quintic into questions about field extensions and groups, and impossibility becomes a statement that some group is not solvable or some degree is not a power of $2$.
Computability theory gives impossibility results for algorithms, starting with the halting problem, and Gödel's incompleteness theorems are impossibility results for complete and consistent axiom systems of arithmetic.
In computer science, lower bounds play the same role: every comparison sort of $n$ items needs at least $\log_2 n!$ comparisons in the worst case, since a decision tree with fewer leaves than $n!$ cannot separate all orders.
Invariants, counting and extremal choices as general tools are treated in the topic [[counting, invariant and extremal arguments|GB14W4SYPK]], and the [[Cantor diagonal argument|0XCH8QVDCQ]] is the model of many impossibility proofs in logic.
