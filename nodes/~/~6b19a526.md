## Example

Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Its [[reduced row echelon form|0NNR68BFRY]] has the nonzero rows $(1, 2, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 0, 1, 2)$, by the [[column space example|~e2144491]].
The [[pivot columns|0BCMGDHZGR]] are 1 and 3, so $r = 2$.

1. Take $C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, columns 1 and 3 of $A$, and $R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Row 1 of $CR$ is $1 \cdot (1, 2, 0, 1) + 0 \cdot (0, 0, 1, 2) = (1, 2, 0, 1)$, by the [[matrix product|076JGP6R3T]].
3. Row 2 of $CR$ is $2 \cdot (1, 2, 0, 1) + 1 \cdot (0, 0, 1, 2) = (2, 4, 1, 4)$.
4. Row 3 of $CR$ is $3 \cdot (1, 2, 0, 1) + 1 \cdot (0, 0, 1, 2) = (3, 6, 1, 5)$. So $CR = A$.
5. $C$ has rank $2$, since its columns $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$ are not proportional. $R$ has rank $2$, since it has pivots in columns 1 and 3.
6. Rank-one terms: $(1, 2, 3)^\top (1, 2, 0, 1)$ has rows $(1, 2, 0, 1)$, $(2, 4, 0, 2)$, $(3, 6, 0, 3)$.
7. And $(0, 1, 1)^\top (0, 0, 1, 2)$ has rows $(0, 0, 0, 0)$, $(0, 0, 1, 2)$, $(0, 0, 1, 2)$.
8. Their sum has rows $(1, 2, 0, 1)$, $(2, 4, 1, 4)$, $(3, 6, 1, 5)$, which is $A$.
9. A rank-one matrix: $u = (1, 2)$ and $v = (3, -1, 0)$ give $uv^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 & 0 \\ 6 & -2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Row 2 is twice row 1, so the rank is $1$.

## Remarks

- Another basis of the column space gives another factorization. For $G = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $CG$ has columns $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(1, 3, 4)$, and $A = (CG)(G^{-1} R)$.
- The columns of $R$ give the coordinates of the columns of $A$ in the basis of $C$. Column 4 of $R$ is $(1, 2)$, so $a_4 = a_1 + 2a_3$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix $A$ has $12$ entries, but it is fixed by two columns and two rows.
The two columns are the pivot columns, and the two rows come from the reduced form.

### L2 human

Each column of $A$ is a combination of the two pivot columns, with coefficients in the matching column of $R$.
Expanding the product writes $A$ as the sum of two rank-one matrices.
