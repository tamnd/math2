## Statement

Let $L$ be a finite relational [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]], and let $n, k \ge 0$.
There is a finite set $\Phi_{n,k}$ of $L$-[[formulas|HYJ10AF6RT]] of [[quantifier rank|~e5a7f04b]] at most $n$, with free variables among $x_1, \dots, x_k$, such that every such formula is logically equivalent to a member of $\Phi_{n,k}$.

In particular, for each $n$ the relation $\equiv_n$ has finitely many classes, and each class is the set of models of one [[sentence|DKFGQTRYZE]] of rank at most $n$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Finiteness of $L$ is needed. If $L = \set{P_0, P_1, \dots}$ with unary $P_i$, the rank $1$ sentences $\exists x \, P_i(x)$ are pairwise inequivalent: the structure with one element in $P_i$ only satisfies the $i$-th and no other.
- The absence of function symbols is needed. If $L = \set{f}$ with $f$ unary, the [[atomic formulas|MCZRXR3SVB]] $f^m(x_1) = x_1$, for $m \ge 1$, have rank $0$ and are pairwise inequivalent: in a cycle of length $m$ under $f$, the $m$-th holds and the $j$-th fails for $0 < j < m$.

### Consequences

- For each $n$, the rank $n$ type of a tuple, the set of formulas of rank at most $n$ that it satisfies, is fixed by one formula: the conjunction of the members of $\Phi_{n,k}$ it satisfies and the negations of the rest.
- This is the finiteness fact used in the proof of the [[Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé theorem|~9f527b73]].
- If $\mathcal{A} \equiv_n \mathcal{B}$ fails, a single sentence of rank at most $n$ witnesses it.

### Converse

The finiteness holds for each $n$ separately, and no finite set of formulas works for all $n$ at once.
The [[counting sentences|~b3bcbe39]] $\sigma_{\ge n}$, for $n \ge 1$, are pairwise inequivalent, in every signature.
The bound on $\abs{\Phi_{n,k}}$ given by the proof is a tower of exponentials of height about $n$.

### Generalizations

- Constant symbols may be added: they behave like extra free variables.
- With function symbols, the statement holds for *unnested* formulas, in which each atomic formula contains at most one function symbol. Games for signatures with functions are often stated with this measure, as in the [[Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game|2RE7YAZK8R]].

## History

The lemma is part of the standard proof of the Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé theorem.

## Explanations

### L1 human

With finitely many symbols, finitely many variables and a bounded depth of quantifiers, there is only a finite amount that can be said.
Many formulas look different but say the same thing, and the lemma counts the meanings, not the strings.

### L2 human

We find all meanings of quantifier-free formulas in one variable $x_1$ for $L = \set{P}$ with $P$ unary.

1. The atomic formulas in $x_1$ are $P(x_1)$ and $x_1 = x_1$.
2. $x_1 = x_1$ is always true, so a quantifier-free formula is equivalent to a Boolean combination of $P(x_1)$.
3. Up to equivalence these are $\top$, $\bot$, $P(x_1)$ and $\neg P(x_1)$, so $\Phi_{0,1}$ can have $4$ members.
4. Every rank $1$ sentence is equivalent to a Boolean combination of $\exists x_1 \, P(x_1)$ and $\exists x_1 \, \neg P(x_1)$, which gives at most $16$ classes.
5. There are fewer, because structures are nonempty. The conjunction $\neg\exists x_1 \, P(x_1) \wedge \neg\exists x_1 \, \neg P(x_1)$ has no model, so it is equivalent to $\bot$.

### L3 human

The lemma says that $\equiv_n$ is a finite-index equivalence relation on $L$-structures.
In finite model theory this gives the finite-index argument behind the composition method for sums of structures.
