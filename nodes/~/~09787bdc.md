## Example

Let
$$J = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. Each row of $J$ is $(1, 1)$, so each row of $JA$ is the sum of the rows of $A$: $(1 + 3, 2 + 4) = (4, 6)$.
2. So $JA = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 4 & 6 \end{pmatrix} \neq A$.
3. Each column of $J$ is $(1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$, so each column of $AJ$ is the sum of the columns of $A$: $(3, 7)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
4. So $AJ = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 7 & 7 \end{pmatrix} \neq A$.
5. Thus $J$ is not the [[identity matrix|~495c2486]] $I_2$, which satisfies $I_2 A = A I_2 = A$.

## Remarks

The all-ones matrix is the identity for the entrywise product, the [[Hadamard product|SQT6QCEXZ8]], and not for the matrix product.
It satisfies $J^2 = 2J$, so $\tfrac{1}{2}J$ is an [[idempotent matrix|0KCE8CG86P]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The number $1$ is the identity for multiplication of numbers.
A table full of ones is not the identity for multiplication of matrices.
The identity matrix has ones only on the diagonal.

### L2 human

Multiplying by $J$ on the left adds the rows, and on the right adds the columns.
The only matrix that leaves every $A$ unchanged is $I_2$, because $I_2$ is the unique identity of the ring.
