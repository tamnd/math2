## Example

Let $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3} = -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i$, so that $\omega^3 = 1$, $\overline{\omega} = \omega^2$ and $1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0$.
Let
$$F = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & \omega & \omega^2 \\ 1 & \omega^2 & \omega \end{pmatrix},$$
with entries $F_{jk} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\omega^{jk}$ for $j, k = 0, 1, 2$.

1. Column $k$ is $c_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1, \omega^k, \omega^{2k})$. Each entry has absolute value $\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$, so $\norm{c_k}^2 = 3 \cdot \frac{1}{3} = 1$.
2. For $j \neq k$, $\ip{c_k}{c_j} = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{m=0}^{2}\omega^{mk}\overline{\omega^{mj}} = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{m=0}^{2}\omega^{m(k - j)}$.
3. Here $\omega^{k - j}$ is $\omega$ or $\omega^2$, and in both cases the sum is $1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0$.
4. So the columns are orthonormal, and $F$ is [[unitary|0D6RMJ10M9]].
5. The $(j, k)$ entry of $F^2$ is $\frac{1}{3}\sum_m\omega^{m(j + k)}$. This is $1$ when $j + k$ is a multiple of $3$, and $0$ otherwise.
6. So $F^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the swap of the coordinates $2$ and $3$, and $F^4 = I_3$.
7. The eigenvalues of $F^2$ are $1$, $1$ and $-1$. So each eigenvalue of $F$ is $\pm 1$ or $\pm i$, two of them square to $1$, and one squares to $-1$.
8. $\tr F = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1 + 2\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(i\sqrt{3}) = i$. The only choice with sum $i$ is $1$, $-1$ and $i$.
9. So the eigenvalues of $F$ are $1$, $-1$ and $i$, and $\det F = 1 \cdot (-1) \cdot i = -i$.

## Remarks

- The example illustrates [[the properties of unitary matrices|~2220a569]]: the eigenvalues and $\det F = -i$ lie on the unit circle.
- The map $x \mapsto Fx$ is the discrete Fourier transform of length $3$, with the unitary normalization $\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$.
- $F$ is symmetric, $F^T = F$, but $F^* = \overline{F} \neq F$. So $F$ is not Hermitian.
- For each $n$, the Fourier matrix $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\omega_n^{jk})$ with $\omega_n = e^{2\pi i/n}$ is unitary, and its fourth power is $I_n$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Fourier matrix splits a list of three numbers into three waves.
Each column is one wave, made from the cube roots of $1$.
The three waves are perpendicular in complex space, and each has length $1$.
So the matrix keeps the length of each list.

### L2 human

The worked computation is in the Example.
The typical use: a unitary transform keeps the energy $\sum_k\abs{x_k}^2$ of a signal.
A trap: without the factor $\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$, the columns have length $\sqrt{3}$, and the matrix is not unitary.
