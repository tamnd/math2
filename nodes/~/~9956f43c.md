## Example

Let $n$ and $k$ be integers with $1 \le k \le n$.
We prove $k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ by a [[combinatorial proof|~a16dfc7e]]: both sides count the same set.

1. Let $S$ be the set of pairs $(A, c)$, where $A$ is a subset of $[n]$ with $k$ elements and $c$ is an element of $A$. Think of $A$ as a committee and of $c$ as its chair.
2. First count: choose $A$, then $c$. There are $\binom{n}{k}$ choices of $A$, and for each of them $k$ choices of $c$ in $A$.
3. Each pair in $S$ arises from exactly one such pair of choices. By the [[rule of product|0VPK4X73Q0]], $|S| = k\binom{n}{k}$.
4. Second count: choose $c$, then the other members. There are $n$ choices of $c$ in $[n]$.
5. For each $c$, the set $A \setminus \set{c}$ is a subset of $[n] \setminus \set{c}$ with $k - 1$ elements, and each such subset gives one $A$. So there are $\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ choices.
6. Each pair in $S$ arises from exactly one such pair of choices. So $|S| = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$.
7. Steps 3 and 6 count the same set $S$. So $k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$.
8. Check for $n = 4$ and $k = 2$: the committees are the $6$ subsets of size $2$ of $[4]$, each with $2$ choices of chair, so $|S| = 12$; and $4\binom{3}{1} = 12$.

## Remarks

The example shows double counting in its plainest form: one set, two orders of the same two choices.
The proof uses only the meaning of $\binom{n}{k}$ as a number of subsets, and never the factorial formula.
For $n = 5$ and $k = 2$, both sides equal $20$.

The same set with committees of every size proves $\sum_{k} k\binom{n}{k} = n 2^{n-1}$.
The identity also gives $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n}{k}\binom{n-1}{k-1}$, and repeated use of it gives the factorial formula for $\binom{n}{k}$.
An [[algebraic proof of the same identity|~3f33c823]] goes the other way, from the factorial formula.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A club has $n$ members and wants a committee of $k$ members, one of whom is the chair.
One way to choose: pick the committee, then pick the chair from it.
Another way: pick the chair first, then the $k - 1$ other members from the rest of the club.
Both ways produce the same list of possible committees with chairs.
So the two counts are equal, and this is the identity.

### L2 human

We list the set $S$ for $n = 4$ and $k = 2$, and we see both counts.

1. The committees are $\set{1,2}$, $\set{1,3}$, $\set{1,4}$, $\set{2,3}$, $\set{2,4}$ and $\set{3,4}$, so there are $\binom{4}{2} = 6$.
2. Each has $2$ choices of chair, so $S$ has $6 \cdot 2 = 12$ elements.
3. Group the $12$ pairs by the chair instead. With chair $1$, the other member is $2$, $3$ or $4$, so $3 = \binom{3}{1}$ pairs.
4. The same holds for each of the $4$ chairs, so $S$ has $4 \cdot 3 = 12$ elements.

The typical use is a product of a binomial coefficient and a factor: the factor is often a choice of a special element.
A trap is to choose the chair from all $n$ people in step 2 of the example; then the chair need not be on the committee, and the count is $n\binom{n}{k}$.
