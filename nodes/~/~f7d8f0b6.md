## Summary

Let $A$ and $B$ be statements.
The statement $A$ is stronger than $B$, and $B$ is weaker than $A$, when $A$ [[implies|0NMG7W7Q11]] $B$.
The statement $A$ is strictly stronger than $B$ when $A$ implies $B$ but $B$ does not imply $A$.
When each statement implies the other, they are [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]], and each one is stronger than the other.
Two statements can also be incomparable, when neither implies the other.
For a theorem "if $H$, then $C$", a weaker hypothesis $H$ or a stronger conclusion $C$ gives a stronger theorem.
For properties of objects, a stronger property defines a smaller class: each object with the stronger property also has the weaker one.

## Motivation

A theorem with a stronger conclusion tells us more, and a theorem with a weaker hypothesis applies to more objects.
So mathematicians compare results by strength, and they try to strengthen known results.
For example, Fermat's little theorem is about a prime modulus, and Euler's theorem gives the same kind of statement for every modulus.
Euler's theorem is stronger, because Fermat's theorem is its [[special case|FWG3D8760C]] for a prime modulus.
Strength is also a guide in proofs.
To prove $B$, it is enough to prove a stronger statement $A$, and sometimes the stronger statement is easier to prove.
To disprove $A$, it is enough to disprove a weaker consequence $B$.

## Notation

There is no special symbol: "$A$ is stronger than $B$" is written $A \Rightarrow B$.
The words "$A$ strengthens $B$", "$A$ is a strengthening of $B$", "$A$ refines $B$" and "$A$ improves $B$" have the same meaning.
The words "$B$ weakens $A$", "$B$ is a weakening of $A$" and "$B$ is a consequence of $A$" describe the other direction.
In the phrase "$A$ is a [[necessary condition|09BJQQB3VS]] for $B$", the condition $A$ is the weaker statement.
In the phrase "$A$ is a sufficient condition for $B$", the condition $A$ is the stronger statement.
Many technical terms contain the words "strong" and "weak", such as strong induction, weak convergence and strongly regular graph.
In these terms the word is part of a fixed name, and the page of each term gives its exact meaning.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The strong Goldbach conjecture implies the weak Goldbach conjecture|~50cd8f12]]. If each even number greater than $2$ is a sum of two primes, then each odd number greater than $5$ is a sum of three primes.
2. [[Euler's theorem is stronger than Fermat's little theorem|~1f0e7f59]]. Fermat's theorem is the case of a prime modulus $p$, where $\varphi(p) = p - 1$.
3. "$f$ is [[differentiable|0K57YG3BB8]] at $a$" is strictly stronger than "$f$ is [[continuous|0X3WGX95FE]] at $a$". Each differentiable function is continuous at the point, and the [[absolute value|0FFXKTKKQC]] $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous but not differentiable at $0$.
4. For a real number $x$, the statement "$x > 2$" is strictly stronger than "$x^2 > 4$". If $x > 2$, then $x^2 > 2x > 4$. The number $x = -3$ satisfies $x^2 = 9 > 4$ but not $x > 2$.
5. The statement "$\sum_{k=1}^{n} 1/k^2 \leq 2 - 1/n$ for all $n \geq 1$" is stronger than "$\sum_{k=1}^{n} 1/k^2 < 2$ for all $n \geq 1$". A direct induction on the weaker statement fails, while the stronger statement has a short proof by induction; the details are in the L2 explanation.
6. A [[sharp|~07c20fd3]] bound is stronger than a bound that is not sharp. For real $x$, "$x^2 \geq 2x - 1$" implies "$x^2 \geq 2x - 5$", because $2x - 1 > 2x - 5$.

### Non-examples

1. [[Divisibility by 4 and divisibility by 6 are incomparable|~fa47a2ca]]. The number $4$ is divisible by $4$ but not by $6$, and $6$ is divisible by $6$ but not by $4$.
2. For an integer $n$, "$n$ is prime" and "$n$ is odd" are incomparable. The number $2$ is prime and even, and the number $9$ is odd and not prime.
3. "$f$ is continuous at $a$" is not stronger than "$f$ is differentiable at $a$". The function $|x|$ at $a = 0$ is a counterexample, so the [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] of example 3 is false.

### Basic properties

1. Transitivity. If $A$ is stronger than $B$ and $B$ is stronger than $C$, then $A$ is stronger than $C$, because implications chain.
2. Conjunction and disjunction. For all statements $A$ and $B$, "$A$ and $B$" is stronger than $A$, and $A$ is stronger than "$A$ or $B$".
3. Hypotheses and conclusions. Let $T$ be "$H \Rightarrow C$" and $T'$ be "$H' \Rightarrow C'$", with $H \Rightarrow H'$ and $C' \Rightarrow C$. Then $T'$ implies $T$: from $H$ we get $H'$, then $C'$ by $T'$, then $C$.
4. Domains of quantifiers. Let $S \subseteq S'$. Then "$P(x)$ for all $x \in S'$" is stronger than "$P(x)$ for all $x \in S$", and "$P(x)$ for some $x \in S$" is stronger than "$P(x)$ for some $x \in S'$".
5. Order of quantifiers. "There is a $y$ with $P(x, y)$ for all $x$" is stronger than "for each $x$ there is a $y$ with $P(x, y)$". The converse fails in general: over $\mathbb{N}$, each $x$ has a $y$ with $x < y$, but no $y$ satisfies $x < y$ for all $x$. The page on the [[order of quantifiers|0FYQMKFE77]] has more cases.
6. Negation reverses strength. If $A$ is stronger than $B$, then "not $B$" is stronger than "not $A$". This is the rule of the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]].
7. Classes. A property $A$ is stronger than a property $B$ exactly when the class of objects with $A$ is a [[subset|0BJR9QR08E]] of the class of objects with $B$. For example, the class of differentiable functions is a subset of the class of continuous functions.

### History

The glossary of mathematical jargon on Wikipedia gives the chain Fermat's little theorem, Euler's theorem, [[Lagrange's theorem|3C53TD]], in which each result is stronger than the one before.
Christian Goldbach stated his conjecture in a letter to Leonhard Euler in 1742, and the weak form is named "weak" because the strong form implies it.
Harald Helfgott announced a proof of the weak form in 2013.
George Pólya, in his book "How to Solve It", described the inventor's paradox: a more ambitious plan can have more chance of success.

### Common errors

1. A student reverses the direction. "$A$ is stronger than $B$" means $A \Rightarrow B$, and a theorem with a stronger hypothesis is a weaker theorem.
2. A student treats "stronger" between proved theorems as an exact notion. A true statement is implied by each statement, so two proved theorems formally imply each other. Between theorems, "stronger" means that the second follows from the first by a short argument, and not conversely.
3. A student thinks that a stronger statement is always harder to prove. In a proof by induction, a stronger statement gives a stronger induction hypothesis, and this can make the step easier, as example 5 shows.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A stronger statement says more.
"I live in Paris" is stronger than "I live in France": if the first is true, then the second is true, but not the other way round.
In mathematics, "$x > 2$" is stronger than "$x^2 > 4$", because $x = -3$ makes the second true and the first false.
A stronger theorem is better: it either needs less to start with or tells us more at the end.

### L2 human

We prove $\sum_{k=1}^{n} 1/k^2 < 2$ for all $n \geq 1$ by first proving a stronger statement.
Write $S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} 1/k^2$.

1. A direct [[induction|0ASJVEFA9J]] fails. From $S_n < 2$ we get $S_{n+1} = S_n + 1/(n+1)^2 < 2 + 1/(n+1)^2$, and this bound is larger than $2$.
2. We strengthen the statement to $Q(n)$: "$S_n \leq 2 - 1/n$". For $n = 1$, $S_1 = 1 = 2 - 1/1$, so $Q(1)$ holds.
3. Induction step. Suppose $Q(n)$. Since $(n+1)^2 > n(n+1)$, we have $1/(n+1)^2 < 1/(n(n+1)) = 1/n - 1/(n+1)$.
4. Then $S_{n+1} = S_n + 1/(n+1)^2 < (2 - 1/n) + (1/n - 1/(n+1)) = 2 - 1/(n+1)$, so $Q(n+1)$ holds.
5. By induction, $Q(n)$ holds for all $n \geq 1$, and $Q(n)$ is stronger than the original statement: $S_n \leq 2 - 1/n < 2$.

The typical use of strength is this one: we replace a goal by a stronger goal that carries more information through the induction step.
Pólya calls this effect the inventor's paradox.
A trap: the strengthened statement must still be true, so a check of small cases comes first; here $S_2 = 1.25 \leq 1.5$ and $S_3 = 1.36\ldots \leq 1.66\ldots$.

### L3 human

Implication is a preorder on statements: it is reflexive and transitive.
If we identify logically equivalent statements, the preorder becomes a partial order, and for propositional logic the result is the [[Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra|QC9M3Q8B8Q]], a Boolean algebra.
In this algebra, conjunction is the greatest lower bound and disjunction is the least upper bound, which is basic property 2.
For properties of the elements of a set $X$, the order "stronger than" corresponds to the inclusion of subsets of $X$, so a stronger property has a smaller extension.
For proved theorems, the order is trivial, as common error 2 says.
[[Reverse mathematics|7SQS69T17R]] gives an exact substitute: over a weak base theory, it compares theorems by the axioms that are needed to prove them.
The related jargon words are on the pages [[sharp and best possible results|~07c20fd3]] and [[mathematical jargon|~390406b4]].
