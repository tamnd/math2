## Example

Let $L = \set{\cdot, {}^{-1}, e}$, with a binary function symbol, a unary function symbol and a constant symbol.
A [[group|G5S1M9]] $G$ is an $L$-structure in which $\cdot$ is the product, ${}^{-1}$ is the inverse and $e$ is the identity.
We show that a subset $H \subseteq G$ is the domain of a [[substructure|676H7EGTKJ]] of $G$ exactly when $H$ is a [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] of $G$.

1. Suppose $H$ is the domain of a substructure. Then $H$ contains the constant $e$, so $H$ is nonempty.
2. The substructure is closed under the function $\cdot$, so $g, h \in H$ gives $gh \in H$.
3. The substructure is closed under the function ${}^{-1}$, so $h \in H$ gives $h^{-1} \in H$. By steps 1 to 3, $H$ is a subgroup.
4. Conversely, let $H$ be a subgroup. It contains $e$ and is closed under products and inverses, so it contains the constant and is closed under both functions.
5. By the closure criterion for substructures, $H$ is the domain of exactly one substructure, and the restricted operations make it a group.

Now take the smaller signature $L' = \set{\cdot}$ and the group $(\Z, +)$, with $+$ as the interpretation of $\cdot$.

6. The set $\N = \set{0, 1, 2, \dots}$ is closed under $+$, so it is the domain of a substructure of $(\Z, +)$ in $L'$.
7. The structure $(\N, +)$ is not a group, because $1$ has no inverse in $\N$: $1 + n = 0$ has no solution $n \in \N$.

So in $L'$ a substructure of a group need not be a subgroup.

## Remarks

The example shows that the substructures of an object depend on the signature, not only on the object.
In $L$ all group axioms are universal sentences: $\forall x \, \forall y \, \forall z \, ((x y) z = x (y z))$, $\forall x \, (x e = x \land e x = x)$ and $\forall x \, (x x^{-1} = e \land x^{-1} x = e)$.
Universal sentences pass to substructures, so every substructure of a group in $L$ is a group.
In $L'$ the axioms of a group need the quantifier pattern "there is an $e$ such that for all $x$ there is a $y$", and such sentences need not pass to substructures.
The same pattern holds for rings: in $\set{+, -, \cdot, 0, 1}$ the substructures of a ring are its subrings, while in $\set{+, \cdot}$ the set $\N$ is a substructure of $\Z$ that is not a ring.
The two signatures for groups are compared in [[groups in two signatures|CBKC2DMXEQ]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A group is a set with a way to combine two elements, a way to undo an element, and a special neutral element.
If we keep only some elements and the three operations never lead out of the kept part, the kept part is a smaller group, a subgroup.
For example, in the integers with addition, the multiples of $5$ form a subgroup: $5 + 10 = 15$, the opposite of $5$ is $-5$, and $0$ is a multiple of $5$.
If we forget the "undo" operation, the numbers $0, 1, 2, \dots$ are also closed under addition, but they do not form a group.

### L2 human

We check that $H = \set{0, 3}$ is the domain of a substructure of $\Z/6\Z$ in the signature $\set{+, -, 0}$.

1. The constant $0$ lies in $H$.
2. Sums: $0 + 0 = 0$, $0 + 3 = 3$, $3 + 3 = 6 = 0$ in $\Z/6\Z$, so $H$ is closed under $+$.
3. Negatives: $-0 = 0$ and $-3 = 3$ in $\Z/6\Z$, so $H$ is closed under $-$.
4. So $H$ is a substructure, and it is the subgroup of order $2$.

In a finite group a nonempty subset closed under the product is already closed under inverses, because $h^{-1} = h^{k - 1}$ when $h^k = e$.
A trap is to use this fact in an infinite group, where it fails for $\N \subseteq \Z$.
