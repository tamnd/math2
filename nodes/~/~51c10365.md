## Example

We compare the [[modal logics T, D and K4|~5580ab65]] $\mathsf{K}$, $\mathsf{D} = \mathsf{KD}$ and $\mathsf{T} = \mathsf{KT}$, and show $\mathsf{K} \subsetneq \mathsf{D} \subsetneq \mathsf{T}$.

1. $\mathsf{T} \vdash \mathrm{D}$. Axiom $\mathrm{T}$ gives $\square p \to p$, the dual of $\mathrm{T}$ gives $p \to \lozenge p$, and the chain rule gives $\square p \to \lozenge p$.
2. So $\mathsf{D} \subseteq \mathsf{T}$, since $\mathsf{T}$ is a normal logic that contains the axiom of $\mathsf{D}$.
3. $\mathsf{K} \nvdash \mathrm{D}$. On one world without successors, $\square p$ is true and $\lozenge p$ is false. By [[soundness|~d189ee4b]], $\mathrm{D}$ is not in $\mathsf{K}$.
4. $\mathsf{D} \nvdash \mathrm{T}$. Take $W = \set{1, 2}$, $R = \set{(1, 2), (2, 2)}$ and $V(p) = \set{2}$.
5. The frame of step 4 is serial, so it validates $\mathsf{D}$, by [[common modal axiom schemata|FDFQFWPM4X]] and soundness.
6. At $1$, the only successor $2$ has $p$, so $\square p$ is true, and $p$ is false. So $\mathrm{T}$ fails at $1$.
7. So $\mathsf{K} \subsetneq \mathsf{D} \subsetneq \mathsf{T}$.
8. $\mathsf{D} \vdash \lozenge\top$. [[Necessitation|G989VP2NGP]] gives $\square\top$, and the instance $\square\top \to \lozenge\top$ of $\mathrm{D}$ gives $\lozenge\top$.
9. Conversely, $\mathsf{K} + \lozenge\top \vdash \mathrm{D}$. The tautology $p \to (\neg p \to \bot)$, necessitation and axiom $\mathrm{K}$ twice give $\square p \to (\square\neg p \to \square\bot)$.
10. The tautology $\neg\top \leftrightarrow \bot$ and RE of [[normal modal logic K|~b1c67ad7]] give $\square\neg\top \leftrightarrow \square\bot$. So $\lozenge\top$, which is $\neg\square\neg\top$, is equivalent to $\neg\square\bot$.
11. From $\neg\square\bot$ and step 9, propositional logic gives $\square p \to \neg\square\neg p$, which is $\square p \to \lozenge p$.
12. So $\mathsf{D} = \mathsf{K} + \lozenge\top$.

## Remarks

The example shows that seriality, reflexivity and the empty condition give three different logics.
The formula $\lozenge\top$ says "the world has a successor", so it expresses seriality directly at each world.
The same countermodels show more: the frame of step 4 is also transitive, so $\mathsf{KD4} \nvdash \mathrm{T}$.
If we remove the pair $(2, 2)$ from the frame of step 4, then $2$ has no successor and $\mathrm{D}$ fails at $2$.
The logic $\mathsf{D}$ is the basic deontic logic: what is obligatory is permitted.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Three rules for "box" are compared here.
Rule T says "box $p$ implies $p$", and rule D says "box $p$ implies diamond $p$".
Rule T is stronger: from "box $p$" it gives $p$, and from $p$ it gives "diamond $p$".
Rule D does not give T: an obligation can be unmet, but an obligation still has to be possible.
The basic logic K proves neither rule, since a world with no successors makes every "box" true and every "diamond" false.

### L2 human

1. Read $\square p$ as "$p$ is obligatory".
2. $\mathrm{D}$ says that obligations are consistent: $\square p$ and $\square\neg p$ cannot both hold.
3. In step 9, this is the formula $\square p \to (\square\neg p \to \square\bot)$ with $\neg\square\bot$.
4. In the model of step 4, the world $1$ is a world where the obligation $p$ is not met.

The typical use is to choose between $\mathsf{D}$ and $\mathsf{T}$ in an application: $\mathsf{T}$ for knowledge, $\mathsf{D}$ for obligation or belief.
A trap: $\mathsf{K} \vdash \square\top$ does not give $\lozenge\top$, since a world without successors makes $\square\top$ true and $\lozenge\top$ false.
