## Example

In [[incidence geometry|CSXT0BG2CM]], "point", "line" and "lies on" are [[primitive notions|~d06afda5]].
Consider three axioms about them.

- (I1) For two distinct points, exactly one line contains both.
- (I2) Each line contains at least two points.
- (I3) There are three points that do not all lie on one line.

We give a model, that is, an interpretation of the primitive notions in which I1, I2 and I3 are true.

1. Let the points be the letters $A$, $B$ and $C$.
2. Let the lines be the three [[sets|0SJ3RX9TXA]] $\{A, B\}$, $\{A, C\}$ and $\{B, C\}$.
3. Let "the point $P$ lies on the line $\ell$" mean $P \in \ell$.
4. Check I1: the two points $X$ and $Y$ lie together on the line $\{X, Y\}$. Any other line misses $X$ or $Y$, because each line has exactly two elements.
5. Check I2: each line has exactly two elements.
6. Check I3: no line contains all three points $A$, $B$, $C$.

So this is a model, and each theorem proved from I1, I2 and I3 holds in it.

## Remarks

1. The model shows that I1, I2 and I3 do not imply "each line contains at least three points". The model satisfies the axioms, and its lines have two points.
2. In this model, any two distinct lines meet in exactly one point. So the axioms do not prove the existence of parallel lines.
3. The Euclidean plane is another model of I1, I2 and I3. A theorem from these axioms holds in both models, because its proof uses only the axioms.
4. The words "point" and "line" have no meaning in this model beyond the interpretation. The letters could be any three objects.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Imagine a game with three tokens, called $A$, $B$ and $C$.
We call each token a "point", and each pair of tokens a "line".
The rules say that two points always share exactly one line, that each line has at least two points, and that not all points share one line.
All three rules hold in this tiny game.
So the rules of geometry can describe things that look nothing like drawings.

### L2 human

We use the model to show that I1, I2 and I3 do not prove the statement "each line has at least three points".

1. Suppose that a proof of the statement from I1, I2 and I3 exists.
2. Each step of the proof uses only the axioms, so the statement would hold in each model of the axioms.
3. In the three-point model, the line $\{A, B\}$ has only two points.
4. So the statement fails in a model, and no such proof exists.

The typical use: a small model shows that an axiom system does not prove a statement.
A trap is to conclude that the negation is provable.
The Euclidean plane is a model in which each line has infinitely many points.
