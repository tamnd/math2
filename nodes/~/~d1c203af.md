## Counterexample

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ over $F = \mathbb{R}$, the rotation by a quarter turn.
Claim: $A$ is not [[similar|027KDW2K72]] over $\mathbb{R}$ to any matrix of Jordan blocks with real entries.

1. The [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]] is $\det(xI - A) = x \cdot x - (1)(-1) = x^2 + 1$.
2. $x^2 + 1 > 0$ for all real $x$, so $A$ has no real [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]], and $\chi_A$ does not split over $\mathbb{R}$.
3. A real Jordan matrix $J$ of size $2$ is $J_2(\lambda)$ or $J_1(\lambda) \oplus J_1(\mu)$ with $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{R}$. It is upper triangular, so its characteristic polynomial is $(x - \lambda)^2$ or $(x - \lambda)(x - \mu)$.
4. Similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial. A product of real linear factors is never $x^2 + 1$, since it has a real root. So $A$ is similar to no real Jordan matrix.
5. Over $\mathbb{C}$, $\chi_A = (x - i)(x + i)$ splits. The vectors $(1, -i)$ and $(1, i)$ satisfy $A(1, -i) = (i, 1) = i (1, -i)$ and $A(1, i) = (-i, 1) = -i (1, i)$.
6. With $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -i & i \end{pmatrix}$, $AP = \begin{pmatrix} i & -i \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = P \operatorname{diag}(i, -i)$. So the complex Jordan form is $J_1(i) \oplus J_1(-i)$.

## Remarks

This shows that the hypothesis "the characteristic polynomial splits" in the Jordan normal form theorem cannot be dropped.
Over $\mathbb{R}$, $A$ is already in [[real Jordan form|~fd1ae118]], as a single rotation-scaling block with $a = 0$ and $b = 1$.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ it is also its own rational canonical form $C(x^2 + 1)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A quarter turn of the plane moves every line through the origin to another line.
So it has no real eigenvector, and no real basis can make its matrix triangular.
With complex numbers, it becomes diagonal.

### L2 human

The test is the characteristic polynomial $x^2 + 1$, which has no real root.
Every real Jordan matrix has a characteristic polynomial with real roots on its diagonal.
So the two can never be similar over $\mathbb{R}$, but over $\mathbb{C}$ the eigenvalues $i$ and $-i$ give a diagonal form.
