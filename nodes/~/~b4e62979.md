## Example

Consider the argument with two premises and a conclusion.

- Premise 1: for every integer $n$, if $n$ is a [[perfect square|HCGCPW8ZFE]], then $n \equiv 0$ or $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.
- Premise 2: $2023 \not\equiv 0$ and $2023 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 4$.
- Conclusion: $2023$ is not a perfect square.

We check that the premises are true and that the argument is an instance of [[modus tollens|~c229ad75]].

1. Premise 1 is true. Let $n = m^2$ for an integer $m$. If $m = 2k$, then $n = 4k^2 \equiv 0 \pmod 4$.
2. If $m = 2k + 1$, then $n = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 4(k^2 + k) + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Every integer $m$ is even or odd, so steps 1 and 2 cover every square.
3. Premise 2 is true, because $2023 = 4 \cdot 505 + 3$, so $2023 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.
4. Premise 1 with $n = 2023$ gives $\varphi \to \psi$, where $\varphi$ is "$2023$ is a perfect square" and $\psi$ is "$2023 \equiv 0$ or $1 \pmod 4$".
5. Premise 2 is $\neg \psi$. Modus tollens on $\varphi \to \psi$ and $\neg \psi$ gives $\neg \varphi$: $2023$ is not a perfect square.
6. A direct check: $44^2 = 1936 < 2023 < 2025 = 45^2$, so $2023$ lies strictly between two consecutive squares.

## Remarks

The example shows the typical use of modus tollens: a property that every square has, and that the number lacks.
The same argument shows that no integer of the form $4k + 2$ or $4k + 3$ is a square, for example $10$, $11$, $2022$.
The argument gives nothing for numbers $\equiv 0$ or $1 \pmod 4$: the number $5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is not a square, and $9 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is a square.
Concluding "$5$ is a square" from "$5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$" would be [[affirming the consequent|J7KFCFQRBT]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

When a square number is divided by $4$, the remainder is always $0$ or $1$.
For example, $36 = 4 \cdot 9$ leaves $0$, and $49 = 4 \cdot 12 + 1$ leaves $1$.
The number $2023$ leaves remainder $3$.
So $2023$ cannot be a square, and we know this without trying any square roots.

### L2 human

We use the same method with the modulus $3$ to show that $2^{100} + 2$ is not a perfect square.

1. Every square is $\equiv 0$ or $1 \pmod 3$, because $(3k)^2 \equiv 0$, $(3k \pm 1)^2 = 9k^2 \pm 6k + 1 \equiv 1$.
2. We have $2^2 = 4 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, so $2^{100} = (2^2)^{50} \equiv 1 \pmod 3$.
3. So $2^{100} + 2 \equiv 1 + 2 = 3 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$, and the test of step 1 is passed.
4. The test with the modulus $3$ gives no conclusion, so we try the modulus $4$: $2^{100} + 2 = 4 \cdot 2^{98} + 2 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$.
5. By modus tollens with premise 1 of the example, $2^{100} + 2$ is not a perfect square.

The typical use is to choose a modulus in which the number lacks the property of squares.
A trap is step 3: passing the test modulo $3$ proves nothing, because it only affirms the consequent.
