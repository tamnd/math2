## Proof

Let $n = \dim V$.
Since $V \ne 0$, $n \ge 1$.

Step A: a nonzero polynomial that kills one vector.

1. Choose a vector $v \in V$ with $v \ne 0$. This is possible because $V \ne 0$.
2. The list $v, Tv, T^2 v, \dots, T^n v$ has $n + 1$ vectors in a space of dimension $n$. By the [[Steinitz exchange lemma|08ZDRN6H41]], an independent list in $V$ has at most $n$ vectors. So this list is linearly dependent.
3. So there are scalars $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n \in F$, not all zero, with $a_0 v + a_1 Tv + \cdots + a_n T^n v = 0$.
4. Let $p(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_n x^n$. Then $p \ne 0$, and $p(T)v = 0$ by the definition of the [[value of a polynomial at an endomorphism|0BNEXSKNRV]].
5. The polynomial $p$ is not constant. If $a_1 = \cdots = a_n = 0$, then $a_0 \ne 0$ by step 3, and $a_0 v = 0$ gives $v = 0$. This contradicts step 1.
6. Let $m = \deg p$. By step 5, $m \ge 1$.

Step B: factor $p$ into linear factors.

7. We claim that $p = c(x - r_1)(x - r_2) \cdots (x - r_m)$ for some $c \in F$ with $c \ne 0$ and some $r_1, \dots, r_m \in F$. We prove this by induction on $m$.
8. If $m = 0$, then $p = c$ is a nonzero constant, and the empty product gives the claim.
9. If $m \ge 1$, then $p$ has a root $r_m \in F$, because $F$ is [[algebraically closed|02SJG1HC32]].
10. By the [[factor theorem|0K3MWA7DMC]], $p = (x - r_m) q$ for a polynomial $q$ of degree $m - 1$. The induction hypothesis applied to $q$ gives the claim for $p$.

Step C: one factor is not injective.

11. The map $f \mapsto f(T)$ from $F[x]$ to $\End_F(V)$ preserves sums and products, by the remarks on the [[value of a polynomial at an endomorphism|0BNEXSKNRV]]. So step 7 gives $p(T) = c\,(T - r_1 I)(T - r_2 I) \cdots (T - r_m I)$.
12. Suppose, for a contradiction, that every map $T - r_j I$ is injective.
13. A composite of injective maps is injective, and multiplication by $c \ne 0$ is injective. So $p(T)$ is injective, by step 11.
14. But $p(T)v = 0 = p(T)0$ with $v \ne 0$, by steps 1 and 4. So $p(T)$ is not injective. This contradicts step 13.
15. So some $T - r_j I$ is not injective. There is $w \ne 0$ with $(T - r_j I) w = 0$, that is, $Tw = r_j w$.
16. By the definition of an [[eigenvalue|0ZND7NQ970]], $r_j$ is an eigenvalue of $T$, with eigenvector $w$.

## Idea

The powers of $T$ applied to one vector cannot stay independent for long, because $V$ is finite-dimensional.
A dependence gives a nonzero polynomial $p$ with $p(T)v = 0$.
Over an algebraically closed field, $p$ splits into linear factors, and $p(T)$ is the product of the maps $T - r_j I$.
Since $p(T)$ kills a nonzero vector, one of these factors also kills a nonzero vector.
The proof uses no determinant and no characteristic polynomial.

## Variants

- The [[proof by the characteristic polynomial|JZQEV913ZV]] finds a root of $\det(tI - A)$. It is shorter once determinants are known.
- The same argument with the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] in place of $p$ shows that every root of the minimal polynomial is an eigenvalue. This is the theorem that the [[eigenvalues are the roots of the minimal polynomial|0QF4ZEFG52]].
- Over $\R$, the polynomial $p$ splits into linear and quadratic factors. The same argument then gives an [[invariant subspace of dimension 1 or 2|~9af39e83]].
