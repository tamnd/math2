## Summary

The first-order language of groups is the language of [[predicate logic|086QKPF0A6]] with the [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]] $\{\cdot, {}^{-1}, e\}$: a binary function symbol, a unary function symbol and a constant symbol.
In this language a [[group|G5S1M9]] is a [[structure|983GZBQYT4]] that satisfies the three [[sentences|DKFGQTRYZE]] $\forall x \forall y \forall z \, ((x \cdot y) \cdot z = x \cdot (y \cdot z))$, $\forall x \, (x \cdot e = x \wedge e \cdot x = x)$ and $\forall x \, (x \cdot x^{-1} = e \wedge x^{-1} \cdot x = e)$.
The elementary theory $\operatorname{Th}(G)$ of a group $G$ is the set of all sentences of this language that are true in $G$.
For a class $\mathcal{K}$ of groups, $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{K})$ is the set of sentences that are true in every group of $\mathcal{K}$.
The elementary theory of groups is $\operatorname{Th}$ of the class of all groups.
By [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]], it is also the set of [[logical consequences|1HK0VYWTV7]] of the three group axioms.
The elementary theory measures what the group axioms can say about elements, and it connects group theory with [[model theory|HFTBG5PMXC]] and with questions of decidability.

## Motivation

Many properties of groups are statements about elements, built from equations with "and", "or", "not", "for all elements" and "there is an element".
Commutativity, exponent $n$, and a nontrivial centre are properties of this form.
Other properties need quantifiers over subsets or over integers: "the group is finite", "the group is finitely generated", "every element has finite order".
Three questions then arise.
The first question is which properties of groups a first-order sentence can express.
The second question is when two groups satisfy the same sentences.
The third question is whether an algorithm decides if a sentence is true in all groups, or in one given group.
The answers are different for the class of all groups, for abelian groups and for free groups.

## Notation

We write $G \models \varphi$ when the sentence $\varphi$ is true in $G$.
Two groups $G$ and $H$ are [[elementarily equivalent|P3B1V182N4]], written $G \equiv H$, when $\operatorname{Th}(G) = \operatorname{Th}(H)$.
For a term $t$ and an integer $n \ge 1$, the term $t^n$ is the product $t \cdot t \cdots t$ with $n$ factors; this is an abbreviation, not a new symbol.
Some books use the smaller signature $\{\cdot\}$ or $\{\cdot, e\}$.
The inverse and the identity are definable from the product, so the same properties are expressible.
But the substructures change: in the signature $\{\cdot\}$, with $\cdot$ read as $+$, the [[natural numbers|0MWKJ44V5A]] form a substructure of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ that is not a group.
For [[abelian groups|082GHNPJ53]] the additive signature $\{+, -, 0\}$ is common.
The words "elementary theory" and "first-order theory" have the same meaning.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The class of abelian groups is axiomatized by the group axioms and one more sentence, $\forall x \forall y \, (x \cdot y = y \cdot x)$: see [[the theory of abelian groups|~5fc21e61]].
2. For a fixed $n \ge 1$, the groups of [[exponent|0ZDW0H0VX4]] dividing $n$ are the models of the group axioms and the sentence $\forall x \, (x^n = e)$.
3. The [[torsion-free groups|PEMNN32A43]] are axiomatized by the infinite set of sentences $\forall x \, (x^n = e \to x = e)$ for $n \ge 2$, and [[no finite set of sentences is enough|~a1d976d4]].
4. The sentence $\exists x \, (x \ne e \wedge x \cdot x = e)$ is true in $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and false in $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, because no nonzero integer $x$ satisfies $2x = 0$. So these two groups are not elementarily equivalent.
5. The sentence $\exists x \, (x \ne e \wedge \forall y \, (x \cdot y = y \cdot x))$ says that the centre is nontrivial. It is false in the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$ and true in every nontrivial abelian group.
6. Let $G$ be a finite group with elements $g_1 = e, g_2, \ldots, g_n$. One sentence says that there are $n$ distinct elements $x_1 = e, \ldots, x_n$, that every element is one of them, and that $x_i \cdot x_j$ and $x_i^{-1}$ are given by the table of $G$. A group satisfies this sentence if and only if it is isomorphic to $G$.

### Non-examples

1. [[Finiteness is not a first-order property of groups|~7ff4f6a9]]: no set of sentences has exactly the finite groups as its models.
2. "$G$ is finitely generated" is not expressed by any set of sentences. The [[Löwenheim-Skolem theorem|484M0TZ1S7]] gives an uncountable group $H \equiv \mathbb{Z}$, and an uncountable group is not [[finitely generated|082DWWXMRJ]], because a finitely generated group is countable.
3. "$G$ is a [[torsion group|JZNQTTMG58]]" is not expressed by any set of sentences $\Sigma$. Add a constant $c$ and the sentences $c^n \ne e$ for $n \ge 1$; each finite part has the model $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$, a torsion group, with $c = 1$ and $m$ large, so the [[compactness theorem|1NR3HAWG9D]] gives a model of $\Sigma$ with an element of infinite order.

### Basic properties

1. The three group axioms are universal sentences, so every substructure of a group in the signature $\{\cdot, {}^{-1}, e\}$ is a [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]]. A universal sentence that is true in $G$ is true in each subgroup of $G$.
2. For each group $G$, the theory $\operatorname{Th}(G)$ is a [[complete theory|655K8Y7VS8]]: for each sentence $\varphi$, either $\varphi$ or $\neg \varphi$ is in $\operatorname{Th}(G)$.
3. Isomorphic groups are elementarily equivalent. The converse is false for infinite groups: $(\mathbb{Q}, +) \equiv (\mathbb{R}, +)$ by [[Szmielew's theorem|~efe20356]], but the two groups have different [[cardinalities|0G0ASKNA6Z]].
4. The elementary theory of groups is not complete, because $\forall x \forall y \, (x \cdot y = y \cdot x)$ is true in some groups and false in others.
5. The elementary theory of groups is [[computably enumerable|TN9G24VABN]], because it is the set of consequences of three axioms. It is not [[decidable|HWC687ES0Q]]: Tarski proved this, and the proof was published in 1953.
6. Let $G = \langle x_1, \ldots, x_n \mid r_1, \ldots, r_k \rangle$ be a [[finitely presented group|TJKANTKASZ]] and let $w$ be a word in the $x_i$. We read $r_1, \ldots, r_k$ and $w$ as terms in the variables $\bar{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_n)$. Then $w = e$ in $G$ if and only if the [[quasi-identity|94GVY3APPQ]] $\forall \bar{x} \, ((r_1 = e \wedge \cdots \wedge r_k = e) \to w = e)$ is true in all groups.
7. If $w = e$ in $G$, then $w$ is a product of conjugates of the $r_i^{\pm 1}$ in the free group, so $w(\bar{h}) = e$ for each tuple $\bar{h}$ in a group with all $r_i(\bar{h}) = e$. Conversely, the quasi-identity applied in $G$ to the generators gives $w = e$ in $G$. This proves property 6.
8. Some finitely presented groups have an unsolvable [[word problem|PBGTTAVMMT]], so by property 6 the set of universal sentences true in all groups is undecidable. This short proof is not Tarski's proof, since it uses the later theorem of Novikov and Boone.
9. For one finitely presented group $G$ as in property 6, $w \ne e$ in $G$ if and only if $G \models \exists \bar{x} \, (r_1 = e \wedge \cdots \wedge r_k = e \wedge w \ne e)$. So if the existential theory of $G$ (the set of existential sentences true in $G$) is decidable, then $G$ has a solvable word problem. The reason: a tuple $\bar{g}$ with all $r_i(\bar{g}) = e$ defines an endomorphism of $G$ that maps $w$ to $w(\bar{g})$, so $w = e$ in $G$ forces $w(\bar{g}) = e$.
10. The elementary theory of abelian groups is decidable, by [[Szmielew's theorem|~efe20356]]. All non-abelian free groups of finite rank are elementarily equivalent, and their common theory is decidable: this is the [[solution of Tarski's problems for free groups|~c44acebf]].

### History

Around 1945 Alfred Tarski asked whether all non-abelian free groups have the same elementary theory.
The undecidability of the elementary theory of groups is due to Tarski, and the proof appeared in 1953 in the book Undecidable Theories by Tarski, Andrzej Mostowski and Raphael Robinson.
Wanda Szmielew, a student of Tarski, proved in 1955 that the elementary theory of abelian groups is decidable.
The questions on free groups were answered in papers by Olga Kharlampovich and Alexei Myasnikov, and by Zlil Sela, published in 2006.

### Common errors

1. A reader confuses the theory of groups with the theory of one group. The theory of groups contains only the sentences true in all groups, so it is not complete, while $\operatorname{Th}(G)$ is complete.
2. A reader treats "there is an integer $n$" as a quantifier of the language. The quantifiers range over the elements of the group only, so "every element has finite order" is not a first-order sentence, as non-example 3 shows.
3. A reader thinks that $G \equiv H$ implies $G \cong H$. This holds for finite groups, by example 6, but it fails for $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A sentence about a group may use letters for elements, the product, the inverse and the identity.
It may also use "and", "or", "not", "for every element" and "there is an element".
For example, "for every $x$ and $y$, $xy = yx$" says that the group is commutative.
The sentence "there is an element $x$, not the identity, with $x \cdot x$ equal to the identity" is true for the numbers $0$ and $1$ with addition modulo $2$, since $1 + 1 = 0$ there.
The same sentence is false for the integers with addition, because no integer other than $0$ gives $0$ when we add it to itself.
The elementary theory of a group is the list of all such sentences that are true in it.
Some properties, such as "the group has only finitely many elements", cannot be written as sentences of this kind at all.

### L2 human

We show that $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}^2 = \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$, both with addition, are not elementarily equivalent.
We use the additive signature $\{+, -, 0\}$ and write $2y$ for the term $y + y$.

1. Let $\varphi$ be the sentence $\exists a \, \forall x \, \exists y \, (x = 2y \vee x = 2y + a)$. It says that the [[quotient group|0KYX2N69J0]] $G/2G$ has at most two elements.
2. In $\mathbb{Z}$, take $a = 1$. Each integer $x$ is even or odd, so $x = 2y$ or $x = 2y + 1$ for some integer $y$. Thus $\mathbb{Z} \models \varphi$.
3. In $\mathbb{Z}^2$, let $a = (a_1, a_2)$. The elements $2y$ have two even coordinates. The elements $2y + a$ have coordinates with the same parities as $a_1$ and $a_2$.
4. The four elements $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$ have four different patterns of parities. Step 3 gives only two patterns, so one of these four elements is not of the form $2y$ or $2y + a$.
5. Since $a$ was arbitrary, $\mathbb{Z}^2 \models \neg \varphi$. Thus $\varphi \in \operatorname{Th}(\mathbb{Z})$ and $\varphi \notin \operatorname{Th}(\mathbb{Z}^2)$, so $\mathbb{Z} \not\equiv \mathbb{Z}^2$.

The typical use is the one above: to show that two groups are not elementarily equivalent, we find one sentence that separates them.
The trap is the other direction.
No finite list of checked sentences shows that $G \equiv H$, because there are infinitely many sentences.
For that direction we need a theorem, such as [[Szmielew's theorem|~efe20356]] for abelian groups or the [[Łoś-Vaught test|ZR7Y5C235P]].

### L3 human

The class of groups is a [[variety of algebras|AJNZJD8442]], and its [[equational theory|F1M51B4MDG]] is the part of the elementary theory that consists of universally quantified equations.
Property 6 shows that already the universal Horn part, the quasi-identities, encodes the word problems of all finitely presented groups.
So the undecidability of the theory of groups is a reflection of the undecidable word problem, and it is similar to [[Church's theorem|VA6BNNY2YC]] on the undecidability of first-order logic.
In contrast, special classes of groups can have tame theories.
The theory of abelian groups is decidable, its complete extensions are classified by invariants, and every complete theory of abelian groups is [[stable|T7JW5QY6KR]].
The common theory of the non-abelian free groups is decidable by the work of Kharlampovich-Myasnikov, and Sela proved that it is stable.
Model theory studies groups that are definable in a structure, for example the groups of finite Morley rank, and here group theory and geometric model theory meet.
The later sub-areas on [[presentations|0JGEQXQ4AC]], [[free groups|0KTW26C80J]] and [[hyperbolic groups|JWJ8YPR7EB]] give the group-theoretic side of these results.
