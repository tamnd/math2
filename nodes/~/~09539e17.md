## Example

Let $U = \{(x, y, z) \in \R^3 : x + y + z = 0\}$.
We construct a [[complement|04C3BAHV03]] of $U$ by extending a basis, as in the [[existence theorem|0P9T8WHNMH]].

1. The vectors $u_1 = (1, -1, 0)$ and $u_2 = (0, 1, -1)$ lie in $U$ and are independent. Since $\dim U = 2$, they form a basis of $U$.
2. Add $e_1 = (1, 0, 0)$. The matrix with columns $u_1, u_2, e_1$ has determinant $1$, so $u_1, u_2, e_1$ is a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] of $\R^3$.
3. Let $W = \R e_1$. By the basis extension proof, $\R^3 = U \oplus W$.
4. Check of the [[intersection|0V33SPH7RR]]: $t e_1 = (t, 0, 0)$ lies in $U$ only when $t = 0$.
5. Check of the sum: $(x, y, z) = (x + y + z) e_1 + (-y - z, y, z)$, and $(-y - z) + y + z = 0$, so the second vector lies in $U$.

A second complement is $W' = \R(1, 1, 1)$.

6. A vector $t(1, 1, 1)$ lies in $U$ only when $3t = 0$, so $U \cap W' = \{0\}$.
7. $(x, y, z) = m(1, 1, 1) + (x - m, y - m, z - m)$ with $m = (x + y + z)/3$, and the second vector lies in $U$.

## Remarks

The two complements give different decompositions of the same vector.
For $v = (3, 0, 0)$, the complement $W$ gives $v = 3 e_1 + 0$, and $W'$ gives $v = (1, 1, 1) + (2, -1, -1)$.
Both complements are lines, as the dimension formula $\dim W = 3 - 2 = 1$ requires.
The complement $W'$ is the [[orthogonal complement|0XJXTBCB2T]] of $U$ for the standard inner product, and $W$ is not.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Any line through the origin that does not lie in the plane is a complement of the plane.
The $x$-axis and the line through $(1, 1, 1)$ are two such lines.

### L2 human

Step 2 is the heart of the recipe.
Any vector outside $U$ would do in place of $e_1$, since a vector outside a plane always completes a basis of the plane to a basis of $\R^3$.
The choice of the added vector is exactly the choice of a complement.
