## Statement

Let $T$ be a [[theory|92SWXST1ZM]] in a signature $L$, and let $\kappa = \abs{L} + \aleph_0$.
Assume that $T$ is [[inductive|~ae02f1e0]]: the union of every chain of [[models|MVCH6N9SNW]] of $T$ is a model of $T$.
Then for every model $M$ of $T$ there is an [[existentially closed model|~fc6b5431]] $N$ of $T$ with $M \subseteq N$ and $\abs{N} \le \abs{M} + \kappa$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Inductivity. Let $T$ be the theory of dense linear orders with a least and a greatest element. A model $M$ with greatest element $m$ lies in the model $N$ obtained by adding a copy of $(0, 1] \cap \Q$ above $M$. Then $\exists y \, (m < y)$ holds in $N$ and fails in $M$, so no model of $T$ is e.c.
- The bound on $\abs{N}$ needs the term $\kappa$: if $M$ is finite, an e.c. model may have to be infinite, as for linear orders.

### Consequences

- Every field embeds into an algebraically closed field, and every linear order into a dense linear order without endpoints, by the [[examples of e.c. models|~524465b6]].
- Every group embeds into an [[algebraically closed group|4SPKGZBTTT]], and every countable group into a countable one.
- If $T$ is inductive and has a model, then it has an e.c. model. This is the starting point of the theory of [[model companions|~7269c0e3]].

### Converse

The converse is false: a theory can have e.c. models above every model without being inductive.
Let $T$ say that $<$ is a linear order that is either dense without endpoints or has a greatest element.
The chain of models $[0, n] \cap \Q$ has the union $[0, \infty) \cap \Q$, which is not a model, so $T$ is not inductive.
Every linear order embeds into a dense linear order without endpoints, and such an order is e.c. for $T$ by the argument of [[this example|~4d9cadf3]].

### Generalizations

- The same proof works for any class of structures that is closed under unions of chains and under elementary substructures.
- In Robinson forcing, the same chain construction, with a stronger condition at each step, gives generic models.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Start with a model, for example the field of fractions.
List all the systems of equations with coefficients in it.
Whenever a system can be solved in a bigger model, move to such a bigger model.
Do this again and again, also for the new numbers that appear.
The union of all these models is a model where every solvable system already has a solution, here an algebraically closed field.

### L2 human

We follow the construction for the linear order $M = \set{0}$.

1. The formula $\exists y \, (0 < y)$ holds in the extension $\set{0, 1}$, so we add a point above $0$.
2. In the same way we add a point below $0$ and a point between $0$ and $1$.
3. Each new point creates new formulas, for example "there is a point between $0$ and the new point", and later stages solve them.
4. The union of all stages is a countable dense linear order without endpoints, so an e.c. linear order.

The typical use of the lemma is to show that a class of e.c. models is nonempty, before one asks whether it is elementary.
A trap is to stop after one round: new elements give new formulas, so one needs a chain of rounds of length $\omega$.

### L3 human

The lemma is a transfinite version of the construction of the algebraic closure of a field.
It works for any inductive class, and the e.c. models that it produces depend on the choices made.
For groups, different choices give non-isomorphic countable e.c. groups, and the class of e.c. groups is not elementary.
When the class of e.c. models is elementary, its theory is the model companion; when it is not, one studies the finitely and infinitely generic models of [[Robinson forcing|~357e6442]].
