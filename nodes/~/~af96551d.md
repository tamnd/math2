## Example

Let $D$ be a set with at least two elements.
A binary relation $R \subseteq D \times D$ is permutation invariant when $\pi[R] = R$ for every [[permutation|05RWFKVTJ7]] $\pi$ of $D$, where $\pi[R] = \{(\pi(a), \pi(b)) : (a, b) \in R\}$.
Let $I = \{(a, a) : a \in D\}$ be the identity relation and $J = \{(a, b) \in D \times D : a \neq b\}$ the diversity relation.
We show that exactly four binary relations on $D$ are permutation invariant: $\emptyset$, $I$, $J$ and $D \times D$.

1. Each permutation $\pi$ maps $I$ into $I$, because $(a, a)$ goes to $(\pi(a), \pi(a))$.
2. Each permutation $\pi$ maps $J$ into $J$, because $a \neq b$ gives $\pi(a) \neq \pi(b)$, since $\pi$ is [[injective|0SNQEF7Z53]].
3. Step 1 applied to $\pi^{-1}$ gives $I \subseteq \pi[I]$, so $\pi[I] = I$. The same argument gives $\pi[J] = J$.
4. The image of a union is the union of the images, so $\emptyset$, $I$, $J$ and $D \times D = I \cup J$ are invariant.
5. Now let $R$ be invariant, and suppose that $R$ contains one pair $(a, a)$. For each $c \in D$, a permutation that swaps $a$ and $c$ maps $(a, a)$ to $(c, c)$, so $(c, c) \in R$. So $I \subseteq R$.
6. Suppose that $R$ contains one pair $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$, and let $(c, d)$ be any pair with $c \neq d$.
7. There is a permutation $\pi$ with $\pi(a) = c$ and $\pi(b) = d$: send $a$ to $c$ and $b$ to $d$, and complete the map by a bijection between $D \setminus \{a, b\}$ and $D \setminus \{c, d\}$, which exists because both sets are $D$ minus two points.
8. So $(c, d) = (\pi(a), \pi(b)) \in R$, and $J \subseteq R$.
9. By steps 5 and 8, $R$ contains all of $I$ or none of it, and all of $J$ or none of it. So $R$ is one of the four unions $\emptyset$, $I$, $J$ and $I \cup J$.

## Remarks

The example shows that, by Tarski's criterion, the only logical binary relations are the ones that first-order logic with identity builds from $=$: never true, $x = y$, $x \neq y$ and always true.
The four relations are different from one another when $D$ has at least two elements.
When $D$ has exactly one element, $J$ is empty and $I = D \times D$, so only two relations exist and both are invariant.
For $n$-ary relations the same argument works with the "equality patterns" of an $n$-tuple: an invariant relation is a union of the sets of tuples with a given pattern of equal and different entries.
Step 7 uses a bijection between $D \setminus \{a, b\}$ and $D \setminus \{c, d\}$; for an infinite $D$ such a bijection exists because removing two points does not change an infinite cardinality.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take the set of three people Ann, Bob and Cleo.
The relation "is the same person as" does not care about names: if we rename everybody, it still holds between each person and that person.
The relation "is different from" also survives every renaming.
A relation such as "is older than" does not survive: if we swap the names of Ann and Bob, the relation changes.
So only very few relations survive every renaming, and these are the relations that logic can see.

### L2 human

We check the claim on $D = \{1, 2\}$, which has $2^4 = 16$ binary relations.

1. The two permutations of $D$ are the identity map and the swap $\sigma$ with $\sigma(1) = 2$ and $\sigma(2) = 1$.
2. The swap maps $(1, 1) \mapsto (2, 2)$, $(2, 2) \mapsto (1, 1)$, $(1, 2) \mapsto (2, 1)$ and $(2, 1) \mapsto (1, 2)$.
3. So $\sigma$ has two orbits on pairs: $\{(1, 1), (2, 2)\} = I$ and $\{(1, 2), (2, 1)\} = J$.
4. A relation $R$ is invariant exactly when $\sigma[R] = R$, that is, when $R$ is a union of orbits.
5. The unions of the two orbits are $\emptyset$, $I$, $J$ and $I \cup J$, so exactly $4$ of the $16$ relations are invariant.
6. For example $R = \{(1, 2)\}$ is not invariant, because $\sigma[R] = \{(2, 1)\}$.

The typical use is to decide quickly whether a relation is a candidate for a logical constant: compute its orbits under the permutations.
A trap is to think that each symmetric relation is invariant.
On this $D$ the relation $\{(1, 2), (2, 1)\}$ equals $J$ and is invariant.
On $\{1, 2, 3\}$ it is not invariant, because the swap of $2$ and $3$ maps $(1, 2)$ to $(1, 3)$.
