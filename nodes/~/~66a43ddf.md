## Summary

The word "complete" has two meanings in logic.
A proof system for a logic is complete when it derives every logical consequence: $\Gamma \models \varphi$ implies $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$.
A theory $T$ is complete, or [[negation complete|319P85G2C1]], when $T \vdash \sigma$ or $T \vdash \neg \sigma$ for each sentence $\sigma$ of its language.
[[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]] of 1929 gives the first property for first-order logic.
Gödel's [[incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]] of 1931 deny the second property for every consistent, recursively axiomatizable theory that contains enough arithmetic.
The two results do not conflict, and together they explain the nonstandard models of arithmetic.

## Motivation

Students meet both theorems under Gödel's name and often read them as opposites.
The completeness theorem is about the logic: the rules of proof capture every consequence.
The incompleteness theorem is about a theory: one list of axioms cannot decide every arithmetical sentence.
Put together, they say that an undecided sentence is true in some models of the theory and false in others.
This essay separates the two notions and shows how each theorem uses the other.

## Notation

$\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ is derivability in a fixed proof system, for example the Hilbert system $H$ of the [[soundness theorem|~331f6430]].
$\Gamma \models \varphi$ is logical consequence: every model of $\Gamma$ satisfies $\varphi$.
$\mathrm{PA}$ is first-order [[Peano arithmetic|0QFT7Z76N5]], and $\mathcal{N} = (\N, 0, S, +, \cdot)$ is its standard model.
Some books say "semantically complete" for the first notion and "syntactically complete" or "negation complete" for the second.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The theory of dense linear orders without endpoints is complete. All its countable models are isomorphic to $(\Q, <)$, and the Łoś-Vaught test gives completeness.
- Presburger arithmetic, the theory of $(\N, 0, S, +)$ with induction for its language, is complete and decidable.
- $\mathrm{PA}$ is incomplete, if it is consistent. The Gödel sentence $G$ of $\mathrm{PA}$ is neither provable nor refutable in $\mathrm{PA}$.
- $\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{N})$, the set of all sentences true in $\mathcal{N}$, is complete. By the incompleteness theorem, it is [[not recursively axiomatizable|~10f45dc4]].

### Non-examples

- The theory of groups is incomplete, but not for Gödel's reason: $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x y = y x)$ holds in some groups and fails in others. This needs no arithmetic.
- Second-order Peano arithmetic has one model up to isomorphism, so it decides every sentence semantically. But second-order logic has no complete and effective proof system, so this does not give a complete recursively axiomatized first-order theory.

### Basic properties

1. By the completeness theorem, $T \nvdash \sigma$ if and only if some model of $T$ satisfies $\neg \sigma$.
2. So a consistent theory $T$ is complete if and only if all its models satisfy the same sentences, that is, they are elementarily equivalent.
3. A complete, [[recursively axiomatizable|3ANXZC3BEG]] theory is decidable. List all derivations until $\sigma$ or $\neg \sigma$ appears; one of them does.
4. By property 1 and the incompleteness theorem, $\mathrm{PA}$ has models in which $G$ is false. Such a model is not isomorphic to $\mathcal{N}$, so it is a [[nonstandard model of arithmetic|1QWN5CV4CG]].
5. The proof of the incompleteness theorem uses only derivability, not models. The completeness theorem turns its conclusion into a statement about models.

### History

Kurt Gödel proved the completeness theorem in his thesis of 1929, and published it in 1930.
He published the incompleteness theorems in 1931.
Mojżesz Presburger proved in 1929 that his arithmetic of addition is complete and decidable.

### Common errors

- Reading the completeness theorem as "every theory is complete". It says that every consequence is provable, not that every sentence or its negation is a consequence.
- Saying that $G$ is "true but unprovable" with no model named. $G$ is true in $\mathcal{N}$, and false in some nonstandard models of $\mathrm{PA}$.
- Thinking that incompleteness needs a weak proof system. A stronger sound proof system for the same logic proves nothing new, by the completeness theorem; the gap lies in the axioms.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"Complete" can describe the rules of reasoning or a list of axioms.
The rules of first-order logic are complete: whatever follows from the axioms can be proved.
A list of axioms is complete when it answers every yes-or-no question in its language.
Gödel showed that the rules are complete, and that no reasonable list of axioms for arithmetic is complete.
Both are true because some questions have different answers in different worlds that obey the same axioms.

### L2 human

We show that $\mathrm{PA}$ has a nonstandard model, if $\mathrm{PA}$ is consistent.

1. Let $G$ be the Gödel sentence of $\mathrm{PA}$. By the first incompleteness theorem, $\mathrm{PA} \nvdash G$.
2. By the completeness theorem, $\mathrm{PA} \not\models G$. So some model $\mathcal{M}$ of $\mathrm{PA}$ satisfies $\neg G$.
3. The sentence $\neg G$ says that some number codes a proof of $G$ in $\mathrm{PA}$.
4. No standard number codes such a proof, since $\mathrm{PA} \nvdash G$. So the witness in $\mathcal{M}$ is a nonstandard number.
5. Hence $\mathcal{M}$ is a nonstandard model of $\mathrm{PA}$.

The typical use of the two theorems together is this passage from "not provable" to "false in some model".
The trap is in step 4: the witness exists in $\mathcal{M}$, but it does not code a real finite proof.

### L3 human

The completeness theorem makes provability a $\Sigma_1$ property of the code of a sentence.
The incompleteness theorem shows that no consistent $\Sigma_1$ theory that contains Robinson arithmetic is complete.
Together they say that the first-order theories of arithmetic cannot pin down $\mathcal{N}$; second-order logic can, but then the logic loses its complete proof system.
[[Lindström's theorem|FYZ3CJGZYV]] makes this trade-off precise: a regular logic stronger than first-order logic loses compactness or the Löwenheim-Skolem property.
See also the [[Henkin construction|~95e9f5bb]] and the [[model existence theorem|G0DTR159RN]].
