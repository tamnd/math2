## Example

[[Euclid's theorem|0292EPPPT0]] says that there are infinitely many [[prime numbers|0SN0MAEKYX]].
We give Euclid's proof, and then we check it against each criterion of a [[proof from the Book|~3e9515d6]].

1. Let $p_1, \ldots, p_k$ be any finite list of primes, with $k \ge 1$.
2. Put $N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1$. Each $p_i$ is at least $2$, so $N \ge 3$.
3. Since $N \ge 2$, $N$ has a [[prime divisor|0SPBEDVNW1]] $q$.
4. Suppose $q = p_i$ for some $i$. Then $q$ [[divides|096PW7Q014]] the product $p_1 \cdots p_k$ and also $N$.
5. Then $q$ divides the difference $N - p_1 \cdots p_k = 1$. But $q \ge 2$, so this is impossible.
6. So $q$ is a prime that is not in the list.
7. Step 6 holds for every finite list. So no finite list holds all the primes, and there are infinitely many.
8. Check with the list $2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13$: $N = 30031 = 59 \cdot 509$. The new primes $59$ and $509$ are not in the list.

Now we check the criteria of a Book proof.

- Correctness: each step follows from the previous steps and from the existence of a prime divisor.
- Brevity: the argument has seven short steps.
- One idea: the number $N$ of step 2 is the whole idea. The rest checks that $N$ works.
- Few prerequisites: the proof needs only divisibility and the existence of a prime divisor. A school student can follow it.
- Explanation: the proof shows why the primes never run out. A product of primes plus one shares no prime factor with them.

So the proof meets each criterion, and it is a standard example of a Book proof.

## Remarks

Step 8 shows a common error.
The number $N$ need not be prime: $30031$ is not prime.
The proof only claims that the prime factors of $N$ are new.

The proof is often written as a [[proof by contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]], starting from a supposed list of all primes.
The version above is direct: it builds a new prime from each finite list.
Both versions are correct, and the direct one avoids an extra layer of supposition.

The existence of a prime divisor in step 3 is the one fact that the proof takes from outside.
Its proof is also short: the least divisor of $N$ that is greater than $1$ is prime.
The variant [[with n! + 1|TMXSM5BNVK]] uses the same idea with a different number.

The book of Aigner and Ziegler gives six proofs of the infinitude of primes, including Euclid's and Furstenberg's.
That several proofs appear shows that the Book can hold more than one proof of a theorem.
The theorem is Proposition 20 of Book IX of Euclid's *Elements*.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose someone hands us a list of primes and says that it has all of them.
Multiply all the primes of the list together, and add $1$.
Each prime of the list leaves remainder $1$ when it divides this new number.
So no prime of the list divides the new number.
But every whole number larger than $1$ has some prime factor, so the new number has a prime factor that is missing from the list.
The list was not complete, and no list can be.

### L2 human

We run the proof for the list $2, 3, 5$.

1. The product is $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 = 30$, and $N = 31$.
2. Division of $31$ by $2$, $3$ and $5$ leaves the remainders $1$, $1$ and $1$.
3. So no prime of the list divides $31$.
4. The prime divisor of $31$ is $31$ itself, and it is a new prime.
5. With the list $2, 3, 5, 31$ the number is $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 31 + 1 = 931 = 7^2 \cdot 19$. The new primes are $7$ and $19$.

The typical use of the example is to show the shape of a Book proof: one construction, then a short check.
A trap is to say that $N$ is a new prime; step 5 shows that $N$ can be composite.
