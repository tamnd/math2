## Proof

1. The [[quotient map|0J0SK5FF4M]] $\pi$ is linear, surjective, and has kernel $U$.
2. Let $X$ be a [[subspace|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$ with $U \subseteq X$. Its [[image|0JB228DCWB]] $\pi(X)$ is a subspace of $V / U$, since the image of a subspace under a linear map is a subspace.
3. Let $Y$ be a subspace of $V / U$. Its preimage $\pi^{-1}(Y)$ is a subspace of $V$, since the preimage of a subspace under a linear map is a subspace. It contains $U = \pi^{-1}(\{0\})$, since $0 \in Y$.
4. So both maps are well defined between the two sets.
5. $\pi(\pi^{-1}(Y)) = Y$. The inclusion $\subseteq$ holds for every map. For $\supseteq$, let $y \in Y$. Since $\pi$ is surjective, $y = \pi(v)$ for some $v$, and then $v \in \pi^{-1}(Y)$.
6. $\pi^{-1}(\pi(X)) = X$. The inclusion $X \subseteq \pi^{-1}(\pi(X))$ holds for every map.
7. For the other inclusion, let $v \in \pi^{-1}(\pi(X))$. Then $\pi(v) = \pi(x)$ for some $x \in X$, so $v - x \in \ker \pi = U \subseteq X$. Hence $v = x + (v - x) \in X$.
8. By steps 5 and 7, the two maps are inverse to each other.
9. If $X_1 \subseteq X_2$, then $\pi(X_1) \subseteq \pi(X_2)$. If $Y_1 \subseteq Y_2$, then $\pi^{-1}(Y_1) \subseteq \pi^{-1}(Y_2)$. So both maps preserve inclusion.

## Idea

The quotient map is surjective, so images of preimages give back the original subspace.
A subspace that contains the kernel is a union of cosets, so preimages of images give it back as well.

## Variants

- The same proof works for submodules, for normal subgroups containing a fixed normal subgroup, and for ideals.
- One can also prove step 7 by noting that $X$ is a union of cosets of $U$ because $U \subseteq X$.
