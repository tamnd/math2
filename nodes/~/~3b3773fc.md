## Example

The SEP entry gives an example of this kind, with two speakers called Jc and Michael.
We make it concrete.
Jc says two things: "$2 + 2 = 4$" and (J) "everything Michael says is true".
Michael says two things: "$2 + 2 = 5$" and (M) "everything Jc says is true".
We show that the four claims have definite truth values, but that no [[Tarskian hierarchy|Z6MCGB0ZFX]] can express both J and M.

1. *Truth values.* Michael says "$2 + 2 = 5$", which is false. So not everything Michael says is true, and J is false.
2. Jc says J, and J is false by step 1. So not everything Jc says is true, and M is false.
3. So J and M are both false, and the values follow from facts about numbers in two steps.
4. *Levels.* Suppose J uses the predicate $T_j$, so J is a sentence of level $j + 1$. Suppose M uses $T_m$, so M has level $m + 1$.
5. J speaks of the truth of M, so M must be a sentence of $\mathcal{L}_j$. This needs $m + 1 \le j$.
6. M speaks of the truth of J, so J must be a sentence of $\mathcal{L}_m$. This needs $j + 1 \le m$.
7. Steps 5 and 6 give $m + 2 \le j + 1 \le m$, which is impossible. So no choice of levels works.

## Remarks

The example shows a criticism that the SEP entry attributes to Kripke (1975).
The level of an ordinary claim cannot be fixed by its syntax alone.
Here the claims are not paradoxical at all, yet the hierarchy cannot express them.
If Michael says only "$2 + 2 = 5$" and nothing about Jc, then levels exist: Michael's claim has level 0 and J can use $T_0$.
In [[Kripke's theory of truth|~1dd87a57]], with one type-free predicate, J and M are both grounded and false, since their values come from facts about numbers.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Jc says "everything Michael says is true", and Michael says "everything Jc says is true".
Michael also says "$2 + 2 = 5$", so Jc's claim is false.
Then Jc said something false, so Michael's claim is false too.
These answers are clear.
But in a system with floors, Jc must speak from a higher floor than Michael, and Michael from a higher floor than Jc.
That is impossible.

### L2 human

1. Assign levels: J at level $a$, M at level $b$.
2. J talks about M, so $b < a$.
3. M talks about J, so $a < b$.
4. Steps 2 and 3 give $a < b < a$, a contradiction.

The typical use of the example is to motivate type-free theories of truth with one predicate.
The trap is to think that the problem is a paradox.
The two claims have ordinary truth values, and the problem is only with the levels.
