## Proof

We use the notation of the [[statement|~fe9d17e9]].
For a word $\alpha$ and $i < j$ we write $\alpha[i, j)$ for the finite word $a_i a_{i+1} \cdots a_{j-1}$.
Parts A to C are about automata, part D connects them with logic, and parts E and F prove parts 4 and 5.

**Part A: Union and letter maps.**

1. Union. The disjoint union of two Büchi automata, with the union of the initial states, recognizes $L(A_1) \cup L(A_2)$, because each run stays inside one of the two automata.
2. Let $h \colon \Gamma \to \Delta$ be a map of alphabets, applied letter by letter to infinite words. For a Büchi automaton $A$ over $\Delta$, the automaton with the transitions $(p, b, q)$ for $(p, h(b), q)$ in $A$ recognizes $h^{-1}(L(A))$. A run on $\beta$ of the new automaton is the same sequence of states as a run of $A$ on $h(\beta)$.
3. For a Büchi automaton $A$ over $\Gamma$, the automaton with the transitions $(p, h(b), q)$ for $(p, b, q)$ in $A$ recognizes $h(L(A))$. An accepting run on $\gamma$ chooses letters $b_i$ with $h(b_i) = \gamma_i$, so it is an accepting run of $A$ on $\beta = b_0 b_1 \cdots$ with $h(\beta) = \gamma$, and conversely.

**Part B: Complementation.**

Let $A = (Q, \Sigma, \Delta, I, F)$.
For $u \in \Sigma^+$ and $p, q \in Q$, write $p \xrightarrow{u} q$ when some path of $A$ labelled $u$ leads from $p$ to $q$, and $p \overset{u}{\Longrightarrow} q$ when some such path visits a state of $F$, endpoints included.
The profile of $u$ is $\tau(u) = (R_u, R^F_u)$ with $R_u = \set{(p, q) : p \xrightarrow{u} q}$ and $R^F_u = \set{(p, q) : p \overset{u}{\Longrightarrow} q}$.
Let $T$ be the finite set of all pairs of relations on $Q$, and for $\tau \in T$ let $U_\tau = \set{u \in \Sigma^+ : \tau(u) = \tau}$.

4. Each $U_\tau$ is regular. A path labelled $va$ splits at the state $s$ reached after $v$, and it visits $F$ exactly when its part on $v$ or its part on $a$ does, since $s$ belongs to both parts. So $R_{va} = R_v \circ R_a$ and $R^F_{va} = (R^F_v \circ R_a) \cup (R_v \circ R^F_a)$, where $\circ$ is composition of relations.
5. Hence a [[deterministic automaton|W65B6BKZRY]] with the states $T$ and a start state reaches $\tau(v)$ after reading $v$. With accepting state $\tau$, it recognizes $U_\tau$.
6. Saturation. Let $\tau, \tau' \in T$, $U = U_\tau$, $V = U_{\tau'}$. We claim that $U V^\omega$ is either contained in $L(A)$ or disjoint from it.
7. Let $\alpha = u v_1 v_2 \cdots$ and $\beta = u' v'_1 v'_2 \cdots$ with $u, u' \in U$ and all $v_i, v'_i \in V$, and let $\alpha \in L(A)$. Take an accepting run on $\alpha$, and let $s_0 \in I$, $s_1, s_2, \ldots$ be its states at the borders of the blocks $u, v_1, v_2, \ldots$.
8. Then $(s_0, s_1) \in R_u$ and $(s_i, s_{i+1}) \in R_{v_i}$ for $i \ge 1$. A state of $F$ occurs infinitely often, and each block is finite, so $(s_i, s_{i+1}) \in R^F_{v_i}$ for infinitely many $i$.
9. Since $u, u'$ have the same profile and all $v_i, v'_i$ have the same profile, the same memberships hold for $u'$ and $v'_i$. Choose paths of $A$ on $u'$ and on each $v'_i$ between these border states, visiting $F$ in the blocks found in step 8. Their concatenation is an accepting run on $\beta$, so $\beta \in L(A)$.
10. Ramsey's theorem for pairs. Let each pair $i < j$ of numbers $\ge 1$ get one of finitely many colours. We show that some infinite set $H$ has all its pairs of the same colour. This is the infinite form of [[Ramsey's theorem|00TQV3F5S4]]; we prove it in steps 11 and 12.
11. Put $H_0 = \set{1, 2, 3, \ldots}$. Given an infinite set $H_t$, let $h_t = \min H_t$. The numbers $j > h_t$ in $H_t$ fall into finitely many classes by the colour of $(h_t, j)$, so one class is infinite. Call it $H_{t+1}$ and call its colour $d_t$.
12. For $t < t'$ we have $h_{t'} \in H_{t+1}$, so the pair $(h_t, h_{t'})$ has colour $d_t$. Some colour $d$ equals $d_t$ for infinitely many $t$, and $H = \set{h_t : d_t = d}$ has all its pairs of colour $d$.
13. Every $\alpha \in \Sigma^\omega$ lies in some $U_\tau U_{\tau'}^\omega$. Colour the pair $i < j$ by $\tau(\alpha[i, j))$. Steps 11 and 12 give $h_0 < h_1 < \cdots$, all at least $1$, with $\tau(\alpha[h_t, h_{t'})) = \tau'$ for all $t < t'$.
14. Let $\tau = \tau(\alpha[0, h_0))$; this prefix is nonempty since $h_0 \ge 1$. Then $\alpha = \alpha[0, h_0) \, \alpha[h_0, h_1) \, \alpha[h_1, h_2) \cdots \in U_\tau U_{\tau'}^\omega$.
15. So $\Sigma^\omega \setminus L(A)$ is the union of the sets $U_\tau U_{\tau'}^\omega$ that are disjoint from $L(A)$. These sets lie in the complement. Conversely, a word outside $L(A)$ lies in some $U_\tau U_{\tau'}^\omega$ by step 13, and this set meets the complement, so it is disjoint from $L(A)$ by step 6.
16. For regular $U, V \subseteq \Sigma^+$, the set $U V^\omega$ is Büchi-recognizable. Take a [[nondeterministic automaton|SFMYM96C1B]] for $V$ whose start state $p_0$ has no incoming transitions. Add a transition $(p, a, p_0)$ for each transition $(p, a, f)$ with $f$ accepting, and make $p_0$ the only accepting state.
17. Between two consecutive visits to $p_0$, a run reads a nonempty word of $V$: it starts at $p_0$, and its last transition is a new one, which replaces a transition into an accepting state. Conversely a factorization into words of $V$ gives a run through $p_0$ infinitely often. So this automaton recognizes $V^\omega$.
18. Put an automaton for $U$ in front, with an added transition $(p', a, p_0)$ for each transition $(p', a, f')$ into an accepting state of the $U$-automaton. A run must leave the $U$-part, once, to visit $p_0$ infinitely often, so the result recognizes $U V^\omega$.
19. Effectiveness. If $U_\tau$ or $U_{\tau'}$ is empty, so is $U_\tau U_{\tau'}^\omega$; emptiness of the automata of step 5 is decidable. Otherwise pick $u \in U_\tau$ and $v \in U_{\tau'}$. By step 6, $U_\tau U_{\tau'}^\omega$ is disjoint from $L(A)$ exactly when $u v^\omega \notin L(A)$.
20. To test $u v^\omega \in L(A)$, run $A$ in parallel with the counter of positions in the lasso $u v^\omega$. This is a Büchi automaton with states $Q \times \set{0, \ldots, |u| + |v| - 1}$, accepting when the $Q$-part is in $F$, and the emptiness test of part C decides it.
21. By steps 1, 15 to 18 and 20, a Büchi automaton for $\Sigma^\omega \setminus L(A)$ is computed from $A$. This proves part 2 of the theorem.

**Part C: Emptiness.**

22. Suppose $A$ has an accepting run. Since $F$ is finite, some $f \in F$ occurs infinitely often. Its first occurrence gives a path from an initial state to $f$, and two later occurrences give a nonempty path from $f$ to $f$.
23. Conversely, let a path labelled $u$ lead from an initial state to $f \in F$, and a nonempty path labelled $v$ from $f$ to $f$. Following the first path and then the cycle forever is an accepting run on $u v^\omega$.
24. Reachability in a finite graph is decidable, so part 3 holds.

**Part D: Logic and automata.**

25. From automata to sentences. Let $A$ be a Büchi automaton. Take set variables $X_q$, $q \in Q$, read as "the state before position $i$ is $q$". Let $\varphi_A$ say: the $X_q$ partition $\N$; the first position lies in some $X_q$ with $q \in I$; for all $x, y$ with $S(x, y)$ there is $(q, a, q') \in \Delta$ with $x \in X_q$, $P_a(x)$ and $y \in X_{q'}$; and for some $q \in F$, $\forall x \, \exists y \, (x < y \wedge y \in X_q)$.
26. The relation $x < y$ is definable, as in consequence 1 of the statement. Partitions satisfying the first three clauses are exactly the runs of $A$, via $r_i = $ the unique $q$ with $i \in X_q$. The last clause says that the run is accepting. So $\varphi_A$ defines $L(A)$.
27. From formulas to automata. Translate first-order variables into singleton set variables, exactly as in steps 14 to 17 of the [[proof of the Büchi-Elgot-Trakhtenbrot theorem|~fe20905e]], with $S(x, y)^* = \operatorname{Succ}(X_x, X_y)$. The set variables $X_1, \ldots, X_m$ become tracks: an infinite word over $\Sigma \times \set{0, 1}^m$ is the same as a word $\alpha \in \Sigma^\omega$ with sets $U_1, \ldots, U_m \subseteq \N$. This correspondence is a bijection.
28. Atomic formulas. $\operatorname{Sing}(X_j)$: states $0$ and $1$, with $1$ accepting; bit $0$ loops on both, and bit $1$ goes from $0$ to $1$. $X_i \subseteq X_j$: one accepting state, with no transition on bits $(1, 0)$; $X_i \subseteq P_a$ is similar. $\operatorname{Succ}(X_i, X_j)$: states $0, 1, 2$ with $2$ accepting; bits $(0,0)$ loop on $0$ and $2$, bits $(1,0)$ go from $0$ to $1$, bits $(0,1)$ from $1$ to $2$.
29. In each case an infinite run exists exactly when the relation holds, and every infinite run then stays in an accepting state from some point on.
30. Connectives and quantifiers. Negation is complement (part B), disjunction is union (step 1), and $\psi \wedge \chi$ is $\neg(\neg \psi \vee \neg \chi)$. For $\exists X_j \, \psi$, let $g_j$ set bit $j$ to $0$; the language is $g_j^{-1}(g_j(L_\psi))$ by steps 2 and 3. No padding problem arises, because all words are infinite.
31. By [[structural induction|12MQ2NJ29A]], every formula gets a Büchi automaton. For a sentence $\varphi$ over $\Sigma$, the language $\set{\alpha : \alpha \models \varphi}$ is $e^{-1}(L_{\varphi^*})$ with $e(a) = (a, 0, \ldots, 0)$. This proves part 1.

**Part E: Decidability.**

32. Let $\varphi$ be an S1S sentence. Use the alphabet $\set{0,1}^m$ of the tracks alone. Truth of a sentence does not depend on the sets, so $L_{\varphi^*}$ is either all infinite words or empty.
33. The automaton for $L_{\varphi^*}$ is computed by part D, and part C decides whether its language is empty. So S1S is decidable.

**Part F: Complexity, by reduction to a cited result.**

34. Relativize each set quantifier of a WS1S sentence $\psi$ to finite sets: $\exists X$ becomes $\exists X \, (\operatorname{Fin}(X) \wedge \ldots)$ and $\forall X$ becomes $\forall X \, (\operatorname{Fin}(X) \to \ldots)$, with $\operatorname{Fin}(X) = \exists y \, \forall z \, (z \in X \to z < y)$.
35. The result $\psi'$ is true in $(\N, S)$ in the sense of S1S exactly when $\psi$ is true in the sense of WS1S. The map $\psi \mapsto \psi'$ is computable in linear time.
36. Meyer proved that WS1S is not [[elementary recursive|RJ3MCAAZSV]]; we cite this result without proof. An elementary decision procedure for S1S, composed with step 35, would decide WS1S in elementary time. So S1S is not elementary recursive.

This proves parts 1 to 5 of Büchi's theorem, with part 5 resting on Meyer's cited lower bound.

## Idea

Formulas and automata translate into each other as for finite words: tracks for variables, union for disjunction, projection for set quantifiers.
The new problem is complement, since Büchi automata cannot be made deterministic.
Büchi's argument classifies finite words by a finite profile that records the paths of the automaton.
Ramsey's theorem cuts every infinite word into a prefix and blocks of one profile, and the complement is a finite union of such block languages.
Emptiness reduces to finding a reachable accepting state on a cycle.

## Variants

The complement can also be built by determinization: McNaughton's theorem gives a deterministic Muller automaton, whose complement is again a Muller automaton, and Safra's construction does this with $2^{O(n \log n)}$ states.
Another route uses ranking functions on the run graph of the automaton, due to Kupferman and Vardi, and avoids both Ramsey's theorem and determinization.
An algebraic route replaces automata by finite semigroups and $\omega$-semigroups, with the same Ramsey factorization at its core.
