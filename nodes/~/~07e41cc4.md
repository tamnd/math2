## Example

Let $I$ be a nonempty set, let $(\mathcal{M}_i)_{i \in I}$ be $L$-structures with nonempty domains, and let $F = \set{I}$.
We check that the [[reduced product|F4WRD396PB]] $\prod_{i \in I} \mathcal{M}_i / F$ is the [[direct product|WJ73XYBNE4]] $\prod_{i \in I} \mathcal{M}_i$, up to the identification $a \mapsto [a]_F$.

1. $F = \set{I}$ is a [[filter|15PF73BKY6]]: it is nonempty, it does not contain $\emptyset$ because $I \neq \emptyset$, and $I \cap I = I$; the only superset of $I$ in $\mathcal{P}(I)$ is $I$.
2. Equality: $a \sim_F b$ means $\set{i : a(i) = b(i)} = I$, that is, $a = b$. So each class $[a]_F$ is $\set{a}$, and $a \mapsto [a]_F$ is a bijection.
3. Functions and constants: by the [[definition|84XXPH6XJ8]] they act coordinatewise on representatives, as in the direct product.
4. Relations: $R([a_1]_F, \dots, [a_n]_F)$ holds when $R^{\mathcal{M}_i}(a_1(i), \dots, a_n(i))$ holds for every $i \in I$, which is the relation of the direct product.

So the bijection of step 2 is an isomorphism of $L$-structures.

## Remarks

The example shows that reduced products generalize direct products.
For two factors $\Q \times \Q$, it also shows that a reduced product of fields need not be a field: $(1, 0) \cdot (0, 1) = (0, 0)$, so $(1, 0)$ is a zero divisor.
If $F$ is enlarged to the filter $\set{X \subseteq I : i_0 \in X}$ of all sets that contain a fixed $i_0$, the reduced product becomes isomorphic to the single factor $\mathcal{M}_{i_0}$.
Between these extremes, the filter of all sets that contain a fixed nonempty $J$ gives the product $\prod_{j \in J} \mathcal{M}_j$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If only the whole index set counts as "large", two lists are equal only when they agree everywhere.
Then nothing is identified, and we get the ordinary product, where everything is done entry by entry.

### L2 human

Take $I = \set{1, 2}$, $\mathcal{M}_1 = \mathcal{M}_2 = (\Z, +, \cdot)$ and $F = \set{I}$.

1. The elements are the pairs $(m, n)$ of integers, since each class has one element.
2. $(1, 2) + (3, 4) = (4, 6)$ and $(1, 2) \cdot (3, 4) = (3, 8)$, coordinate by coordinate.
3. The sentence $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x \cdot y = 0 \to x = 0 \lor y = 0)$ holds in $\Z$ but fails here: $(1, 0) \cdot (0, 1) = (0, 0)$.

The trap is to expect that products keep "no zero divisors"; that sentence is not a Horn sentence.
