## Proof

We use the notation of the [[statement|~2cb7df7c]] and of [[tree automata|3VSFM7K70Z]].
Part A proves in full that MSO sentences about finite labelled trees are equivalent to deterministic bottom-up tree automata, the theorem of Thatcher and Wright and of Doner.
Part B encodes graphs of treewidth at most $k$ as labelled trees; it is an outline, and the linear-time computation of tree decompositions is cited.

**Part A: MSO on terms and tree automata.**

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a ranked alphabet with arities at most $r$.
A term $t \in T(\mathcal{F})$ is the structure whose domain is its set of nodes, with a predicate $P_f$ for the nodes labelled $f$ and relations $\operatorname{Ch}_i(x, y)$, "$y$ is the $i$-th child of $x$", for $1 \le i \le r$.
For set variables $X_1, \ldots, X_m$, an annotated term is a term over $\mathcal{F} \times \set{0, 1}^m$, where $(f, b)$ has the arity of $f$; track $j$ marks the nodes in $X_j$.

1. Only set variables. Replace each first-order variable $x$ by a set variable $X$ that is a singleton. The atomic formulas become $\operatorname{Sing}(X)$, $X \subseteq Y$, $X \subseteq P_f$ and $\operatorname{Ch}_i(X, Y)$, where $x = y$ is $X \subseteq Y \wedge Y \subseteq X$ and $x \in Y$ is $X \subseteq Y$.
2. The quantifier $\exists x \, \psi$ becomes $\exists X \, (\operatorname{Sing}(X) \wedge \psi^*)$. By structural induction, $\psi$ holds for nodes $u_1, \ldots$ exactly when $\psi^*$ holds for $\set{u_1}, \ldots$, as in Part B of the [[proof of the Büchi-Elgot-Trakhtenbrot theorem|~fe20905e]].
3. Claim: for every formula $\varphi(X_1, \ldots, X_m)$ of this form there is a deterministic bottom-up automaton $A_\varphi$, computable from $\varphi$, whose language is the set of annotated terms that satisfy $\varphi$. We prove it by structural induction on $\varphi$.
4. Deterministic automata. A bottom-up automaton is deterministic when for each $f$ and states $q_1, \ldots, q_n$ there is exactly one rule $f(q_1, \ldots, q_n) \to q$. Then it reaches exactly one state on each term, computed from the leaves upwards in one pass.
5. Determinization. Given a nondeterministic automaton with states $Q$, use the subsets of $Q$ as states, with the rule $f(S_1, \ldots, S_n) \to S$ where $S$ is the set of $q$ with a rule $f(q_1, \ldots, q_n) \to q$ and all $q_i \in S_i$. By induction on terms, the new automaton reaches the set of all states that the old one reaches. Take as final the sets that contain a final state.
6. Complement: in a deterministic automaton, exchange final and non-final states. Union: run two deterministic automata in parallel on pairs of states, and accept when either accepts.
7. Letter maps. Let $h$ map letters to letters of the same arity, applied node by node. If $A$ recognizes $L$, the rules $h(f)(q_1, \ldots, q_n) \to q$, for the rules $f(q_1, \ldots, q_n) \to q$ of $A$, recognize $h(L)$. A run of $A$ on $s$ is a run on $h(s)$. Conversely, a run on $t$ uses at each node a rule from some $f$ with $h(f)$ the label, and these $f$ form $s$ with $h(s) = t$.
8. The automaton with the rules $f(q_1, \ldots, q_n) \to q$ for each $f$ such that $h(f)(q_1, \ldots, q_n) \to q$ is a rule of $A$ recognizes $h^{-1}(L)$, since its runs on $s$ are the runs of $A$ on $h(s)$. This adds unused tracks to an automaton.
9. Atomic $\operatorname{Sing}(X)$. The states are $0$, $1$ and $2$, the number of marked nodes in the subtree, capped at $2$. A node with bit $b$ and children in states $c_1, \ldots, c_n$ gets $\min(2, b + c_1 + \cdots + c_n)$. The final state is $1$.
10. Atomic $X \subseteq Y$ and $X \subseteq P_f$. The states are "ok" and "bad". A node becomes bad when a child is bad, or when it is marked in $X$ but not in $Y$, or marked in $X$ with a label other than $f$. The final state is "ok".
11. Atomic $\operatorname{Ch}_i(X, Y)$, meaning $X = \set{u}$ and $Y = \set{v}$ with $v$ the $i$-th child of $u$. The states are $0$ (no marks below), $y$ (the root of the subtree is the only mark, in $Y$ only), "done", and "bad".
12. Rules for $\operatorname{Ch}_i$. An unmarked node with all children in $0$ gets $0$. A node marked in $Y$ only, with all children in $0$, gets $y$. A node marked in $X$ only, with $i$-th child in $y$ and the others in $0$, gets "done". An unmarked node with one child in "done", the others in $0$, gets "done". All other cases give "bad", and "done" is final.
13. By induction on the term, the state $y$ marks exactly the subtrees whose only mark is a $Y$-mark at the root, and "done" marks the subtrees whose marks are exactly $u \in X$ and its $i$-th child in $Y$. So the automaton is correct.
14. Inductive step. Bring the automata of the subformulas to the same tracks by step 8. Then $\neg$ is step 6, $\wedge$ is complement of the union of complements, and $\exists X_{m+1}$ is step 7 for the map that erases track $m+1$, followed by step 5. This proves the claim of step 3.
15. Conversely, every tree automaton language is MSO-definable: guess one set $X_q$ per state for the run, and say that the sets partition the nodes, that each node obeys a rule, and that the root lies in a final $X_q$. We do not need this direction below.
16. Evaluation. For a sentence $\varphi$, $A_\varphi$ is a deterministic automaton over $\mathcal{F}$. Deciding $t \models \varphi$ means running $A_\varphi$ on $t$, which takes time linear in the size of $t$ for fixed $\varphi$.
17. Emptiness. Let $R$ be the least set of states closed under the rules: if $f(q_1, \ldots, q_n) \to q$ is a rule and all $q_i \in R$, then $q \in R$. It is computed by iteration in at most $|Q|$ rounds, and by induction it is the set of states reached on some term. So $L(A) \ne \emptyset$ exactly when $R$ contains a final state.

**Part B: Graphs of bounded treewidth as terms (outline).**

18. Computing a decomposition. For fixed $k$, Bodlaender's algorithm finds a [[tree decomposition|FJ5E6FZVRJ]] of width at most $k$ in time linear in $n$, or reports that none exists. We cite this result.
19. Normal form. The decomposition is turned in linear time into a rooted one whose nodes are of five kinds: a leaf with an empty bag; "introduce vertex"; "forget vertex"; "introduce edge", which adds one edge between two vertices of the bag; and "join", with two children and the same bag. The root bag is empty, and each edge is introduced exactly once.
20. Colours. The vertices are coloured with $k + 1$ colours so that the vertices of each bag have distinct colours. Going down from the root, a vertex receives a colour when it is forgotten, and the colours of the other vertices of the bag at that node are already fixed.
21. The term. The labels are $\mathrm{leaf}$ of arity $0$, $\mathrm{intro}_j$, $\mathrm{forget}_j$ and $\mathrm{edge}_{jl}$ of arity $1$ for colours $j, l \le k$, and $\mathrm{join}$ of arity $2$. This is a finite ranked alphabet $\mathcal{F}_k$, and the term $t_G$ has size linear in $n$.
22. Interpretation. Every vertex of $G$ is forgotten exactly once, so vertices correspond to the $\mathrm{forget}$ nodes. Edges correspond to the $\mathrm{edge}$ nodes. An $\mathrm{edge}_{jl}$ node is incident to the vertex of the nearest ancestor labelled $\mathrm{forget}_j$, and to that of the nearest ancestor labelled $\mathrm{forget}_l$. The ancestor relation is the least relation that contains $\operatorname{Ch}_1, \operatorname{Ch}_2$ and is transitive, which is MSO-definable, so incidence is MSO-definable in $t_G$.
23. Translation. Replacing vertex and edge variables by node variables restricted to these labels, and $\operatorname{inc}$ by its definition, turns each MSO₂ sentence $\varphi$ into an MSO sentence $\varphi_k$ over $\mathcal{F}_k$ with $G \models \varphi$ exactly when $t_G \models \varphi_k$.
24. Algorithm. Compute $t_G$ by steps 18 to 21 and run the automaton $A_{\varphi_k}$ of Part A on it. Each stage takes time linear in $n$ for fixed $\varphi$ and $k$, which proves the theorem.
25. Decidable theory. One MSO sentence over $\mathcal{F}_k$ says that a term is well formed: colours are used consistently, the children of a join agree, and the root has no colour. Every well-formed term encodes a graph of treewidth at most $k$. So $\varphi$ holds in such a graph exactly when the automaton for "well formed and $\varphi_k$" has a nonempty language, which step 17 decides.

So Part A is proved in full, step 18 is cited, and steps 19 to 25 are an outline.

## Idea

An MSO sentence about trees becomes a finite automaton that runs from the leaves to the root, by the same closure properties that work for words.
A graph of treewidth at most $k$ is built from a tree of small bags, so it can be written as a term over finitely many operations.
The sentence about the graph becomes a sentence about the term, and the automaton evaluates it in one pass.

## Variants

Courcelle's own proof is algebraic: graphs with $k$ distinguished vertices form an algebra under gluing, and MSO-definable sets are recognizable, that is, they have finitely many classes under a congruence.
A game-based proof uses Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games for MSO: the MSO type of a glued graph of fixed quantifier depth depends only on the types of its parts.
For clique-width, the same scheme works with terms over the operations of clique-width expressions, for MSO₁ only.
