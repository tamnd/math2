## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $p(x) = x^2 - 4x$.
We compute the [[value p(A)|0BNEXSKNRV]] and its eigenvalues.

1. $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 + 1 & 2 + 2 \\ 2 + 2 & 1 + 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. $p(A) = A^2 - 4A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 - 8 & 4 - 4 \\ 4 - 4 & 5 - 8 \end{pmatrix} = -3I$.
3. The [[eigenvalues|0ZND7NQ970]] of $A$ are $1$ and $3$, with eigenvectors $(1, -1)$ and $(1, 1)$.
4. $p(1) = 1 - 4 = -3$ and $p(3) = 9 - 12 = -3$.
5. Check on eigenvectors: $p(A)(1, -1) = (-3, 3) = p(1)(1, -1)$, and $p(A)(1, 1) = (-3, -3) = p(3)(1, 1)$.
6. So the eigenvalues of $p(A)$ are $p(1)$ and $p(3)$, both equal to $-3$, as the theorem on the [[eigenvalues of a polynomial in an endomorphism|~ceb4bf0e]] predicts.

## Remarks

- Step 2 says $A^2 - 4A + 3I = 0$. This is the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]] for $A$, since $p_A(t) = t^2 - 4t + 3$.
- Two different eigenvalues of $A$ can give the same eigenvalue of $p(A)$. Here $p(A)$ has the single eigenvalue $-3$ with multiplicity $2$.
- The eigenvectors of $A$ are eigenvectors of $p(A)$, but $p(A) = -3I$ has more eigenvectors: every nonzero vector.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If $Av = \lambda v$, then $A^2 v = \lambda^2 v$.
So a polynomial in $A$ acts on $v$ as the same polynomial in $\lambda$.
Here the polynomial takes the same value $-3$ at both eigenvalues, so $p(A) = -3I$.

### L2 human

The typical use is to find the eigenvalues of $p(A)$ without computing $p(A)$.
A trap is to expect that $p(A)$ has only the eigenvectors of $A$: when $p(\lambda) = p(\mu)$, the two eigenspaces merge.
