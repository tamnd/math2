## Example

Let $V$ be the space of real polynomials of degree at most $2$, and let $T(p)(x) = p(2x)$.
The map $T$ is linear, since $(p + q)(2x) = p(2x) + q(2x)$ and $(cp)(2x) = c\, p(2x)$.

1. In the basis $1, x, x^2$: $T(1) = 1$, $T(x) = 2x$ and $T(x^2) = 4x^2$.
2. So $[T] = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2, 4)$, and the [[definition|03F5KP94K0]] gives $\det T = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = 8$.
3. Check in the basis $1, 1 + x, 1 + x + x^2$: $T(1) = 1$.
4. $T(1 + x) = 1 + 2x = -1 \cdot 1 + 2 (1 + x)$.
5. $T(1 + x + x^2) = 1 + 2x + 4x^2 = -1 \cdot 1 - 2 (1 + x) + 4 (1 + x + x^2)$.
6. So the matrix in this basis is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.
7. It is [[upper triangular|0K1KKMFHYH]], so its determinant is $1 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = 8$, as the [[invariance statement|0KMN32XGPF]] predicts.

## Remarks

The map $T$ is invertible, with inverse $p(x) \mapsto p(x/2)$, and $\det T^{-1} = 1/8$.
On polynomials of degree at most $n$, the same map has determinant $2^{0 + 1 + \dots + n} = 2^{n(n+1)/2}$.
The substitution $p(x) \mapsto p(cx)$ has determinant $c^{n(n+1)/2}$, so it is singular for $c = 0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The map doubles the variable in a polynomial.
It keeps $1$, doubles $x$, and multiplies $x^2$ by $4$.
So it stretches three independent directions by $1$, $2$ and $4$, and the determinant is $8$.

### L2 human

The basis $1, x, x^2$ gives a diagonal matrix, which is the easiest choice.
The second basis gives a different matrix with the same determinant, as it must.
A trap: $T(x^2)$ is $(2x)^2 = 4x^2$, not $2x^2$.
