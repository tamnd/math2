## Statement

Let $\mathsf{GL}$ be the modal logic of the [[definition of provability logic|61THBNS8WE]].
A frame $(W, R)$ is finite transitive irreflexive when $W$ is finite, $R$ is [[transitive|0GJZW9JFZQ]], and no $w$ has $w R w$.
For every modal formula $\varphi$, the following are equivalent.

1. $\mathsf{GL} \vdash \varphi$.
2. $\varphi$ is valid on every frame $(W, R)$ with $R$ transitive and conversely [[well-founded|0DQPNJNPYK]].
3. $\varphi$ is valid on every finite transitive irreflexive frame.
4. $\varphi$ is valid on every transitive irreflexive frame with at most $2^n$ worlds, where $n$ is the number of subformulas of $\varphi$.

Validity on a frame is defined in [[Kripke semantics|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]], and converse well-foundedness in [[Löb's axiom in provability logic|QQ6PZFGGB3]].
The proof is in [[the proof of the Kripke completeness of GL|~c3ae1e1d]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. Transitivity cannot be dropped from 3. The frame $a R b$, $b R c$ is finite and irreflexive, and it refutes Löb's axiom; see [[Löb's axiom in provability logic|QQ6PZFGGB3]].
2. Irreflexivity cannot be dropped from 3. The one-world frame with $a R a$ is finite and transitive, and it refutes Löb's axiom.
3. In item 3, finiteness cannot be replaced by irreflexivity alone. The frame $(\N, <)$ is transitive and irreflexive, and it refutes Löb's axiom, since it is not conversely well-founded.
4. The completeness is weak, not strong. The set $\Gamma = \set{\lozenge p_0} \cup \set{\square(p_i \to \lozenge p_{i+1}) : i \in \N}$ is consistent with $\mathsf{GL}$, since each finite part is true at the root of a finite chain. But no world of a transitive conversely well-founded frame makes all of $\Gamma$ true, since it would see an infinite chain of worlds. So $\mathsf{GL}$ is not compact.

### Consequences

1. Finite model property: if $\mathsf{GL} \nvdash \varphi$, then $\varphi$ fails at a world of a finite model of $\mathsf{GL}$.
2. Decidability: by item 4, to decide $\mathsf{GL} \vdash \varphi$ we check the finitely many frames with at most $2^n$ worlds and all valuations of the variables of $\varphi$.
3. Complexity: the problem "$\mathsf{GL} \vdash \varphi$" is in PSPACE. The worlds of the countermodel of the proof form chains of length at most the number of boxed subformulas of $\varphi$, so a depth-first search for a countermodel needs only polynomial space. The problem is also [[PSPACE-complete|JWC9CE07Q2]]; we cite the hardness without proof.
4. The canonical model of $\mathsf{GL}$ is not a frame of $\mathsf{GL}$. Otherwise every consistent set, such as $\Gamma$ in item 4 above, would be true at a world of a transitive conversely well-founded frame.
5. Together with [[Solovay's theorem|~5cc205a1]], it gives a decision procedure for the schemes that $\mathsf{PA}$ proves about its own provability.

### Converse

The direction from 1 to 2 is soundness, and it holds by part 3 of [[Löb's axiom in provability logic|QQ6PZFGGB3]].
The direction from 3 to 1 is the completeness.
So the theorem is an equivalence, and its converse is part of it.

### Generalizations

1. $\mathsf{GL}$ is also complete for finite transitive irreflexive trees, by unravelling a finite countermodel into a tree.
2. [[Grzegorczyk logic|~18306319]] is complete for finite partial orders, by a similar finite construction.
3. Polymodal analogues fail: [[Japaridze's polymodal logic GLP|~fc4764bd]] is not complete for any class of Kripke frames.

## History

Krister Segerberg proved the completeness of $\mathsf{GL}$ for finite transitive irreflexive frames in his 1971 work "An essay in classical modal logic".
The finite construction of the proof is the standard one in Boolos's "The Logic of Provability".

## Explanations

### L1 human

A modal formula is a theorem of $\mathsf{GL}$ exactly when it holds in every finite diagram of points and arrows where the arrows never loop and always compose.
For example, $\square p \to p$ fails on the diagram with one point and no arrow, so it is not a theorem.
Since we can list all small diagrams, a computer can decide whether a formula is a theorem.

### L2 human

We show that $\mathsf{GL} \nvdash \square(p \vee q) \to (\square p \vee \square q)$.

1. Take the frame with worlds $1$, $2$, $3$ and the relation $1 R 2$, $1 R 3$ only. It is finite, transitive and irreflexive.
2. Put $p$ true only at $2$, and $q$ true only at $3$.
3. The successors of $1$ are $2$ and $3$, and $p \vee q$ is true at both, so $1 \models \square(p \vee q)$.
4. $1 \not\models \square p$, since $p$ is false at $3$, and $1 \not\models \square q$, since $q$ is false at $2$.
5. So the formula fails at $1$, and by the theorem $\mathsf{GL}$ does not prove it.

The typical use is negative: to show $\mathsf{GL} \nvdash \varphi$, give one finite transitive irreflexive countermodel.
A trap: the canonical model method of basic modal logic does not work for $\mathsf{GL}$, by Consequence 4; the proof must use finite models.

### L3 human

The proof is a filtration-style construction: maximal consistent sets are taken only inside the finite set of subformulas of $\varphi$, and the relation is chosen so that it is transitive and irreflexive by design.
The failure of compactness separates $\mathsf{GL}$ from the canonical logics of the [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]] foundations, and it is the same phenomenon as the failure of compactness for well-foundedness in first-order logic.
Arithmetically, the finite frames of this theorem are the input of Solovay's construction, which turns each finite countermodel into sentences of arithmetic.
