## Statement

Let $n \ge 1$, and let $D = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, \dots, 1)$.

1. The map $\det \colon O(n) \to \set{1, -1}$ is a surjective [[group homomorphism|0HV9Y7WG5R]], and its [[kernel|0Z424HK8CQ]] is $SO(n)$.
2. So $SO(n)$ is a [[normal subgroup|0W77CVBCHQ]] of $O(n)$ of [[index|04QEBF8S49]] $2$. The two cosets are $SO(n)$ and $D \cdot SO(n) = \set{Q \in O(n) : \det Q = -1}$.
3. The map $\phi(a + ic) = \begin{pmatrix} a & -c \\ c & a \end{pmatrix}$ is a group isomorphism from the [[circle group|04EAFVFTF7]] $\set{z \in \C : \abs{z} = 1}$ onto $SO(2)$. So $SO(2) = \set{R(\theta) : \theta \in \R}$, where $R(\theta) = \phi(e^{i\theta})$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Part 1 needs orthogonal matrices. On all of $GL_n(\R)$, the determinant takes every non-zero value, and its kernel $SL_n(\R)$ has infinite index.
- Part 3 needs determinant $1$. The matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ is orthogonal, but it is not of the form $\phi(z)$, because its diagonal entries differ.

### Consequences

- An orthogonal matrix with determinant $-1$ is $D$ times a rotation: $Q = D(DQ)$, and $DQ \in SO(n)$.
- $SO(2)$ is commutative, because the circle group is commutative. So $R(\theta)R(\varphi) = R(\theta + \varphi) = R(\varphi)R(\theta)$.
- The product of two orthogonal matrices with determinant $-1$ is a rotation. For example, the product of two reflections is a rotation.

### Converse

For $n = 2$, a converse holds: $SO(2)$ is the only subgroup of index $2$ in $O(2)$.

1. Let $H$ be a subgroup of index $2$. It is normal, by [[this theorem|0V547YQRMV]], and the quotient $O(2)/H$ has order $2$. So the quotient is commutative, and $H$ contains every commutator $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$.
2. Let $S = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$. A direct computation gives $SR(\theta)S^{-1} = R(-\theta)$, so $SR(\theta)S^{-1}R(\theta)^{-1} = R(-2\theta)$.
3. Each rotation $R(\varphi)$ equals $R(-2\theta)$ for $\theta = -\varphi/2$. So $SO(2) \subseteq H$, and $H = SO(2)$, because both have index $2$.

### Generalizations

- Over $\C$, the determinant $\det \colon U(n) \to \set{z : \abs{z} = 1}$ is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $SU(n)$, see [[the unitary group|~4244c18b]].
- For a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form over any field of characteristic not $2$, the same proof shows that $SO(B)$ has index $2$ in $O(B)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every orthogonal matrix is either a rotation or a mirror image of a rotation.
The determinant tells which one: $1$ for rotations and $-1$ for the others.
So the orthogonal group splits into two halves of the same size.
In the plane, each rotation matches exactly one point of the unit circle.

### L2 human

Worked example with $n = 2$ and $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the reflection in the line $y = x$:

1. $\det Q = -1$, so $Q$ is in the coset of $D = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1)$.
2. $DQ = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = R(\pi/2)$, a rotation.
3. So $Q = D R(\pi/2)$: first turn by a quarter turn, then reflect in the $y$-axis.
4. Under $\phi$, the rotation $R(\pi/2)$ comes from $z = i$, because $\phi(0 + 1 \cdot i) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

The typical use: to study all of $O(n)$, study $SO(n)$ and one extra element $D$.
A trap: the set of matrices with determinant $-1$ is a coset, not a subgroup.

### L3 human

The theorem says that $O(n)$ is an extension of $\Z/2\Z$ by $SO(n)$, and the extension splits, because $D^2 = I_n$.
So $O(n)$ is a semidirect product $SO(n) \rtimes \Z/2\Z$.
For $n$ odd, $-I_n$ is central with determinant $-1$, so the product is direct: $O(n) \cong SO(n) \times \Z/2\Z$.
For $n$ even, $O(n)$ is not this direct product, because its center is $\set{I_n, -I_n} \subseteq SO(n)$.
