## Counterexample

Let $\ell^2$ be the space of real sequences $x = (x_1, x_2, \dots)$ with $\sum_n x_n^2 < \infty$, with $\ip{x}{y} = \sum_n x_n y_n$, as in [[the sequence spaces|08P6T2WH6R]].
Let $c_{00}$ be the set of sequences with only finitely many nonzero entries.
Let $e_n$ be the sequence with $1$ in place $n$ and $0$ elsewhere.
We show that $\ell^2 \ne c_{00} \oplus c_{00}^\perp$ and $(c_{00}^\perp)^\perp \ne c_{00}$.

1. $c_{00}$ is a subspace of $\ell^2$: a finite sum has finite square sum, $0 \in c_{00}$, and a linear combination of two finitely supported sequences is finitely supported.
2. Each $e_n$ lies in $c_{00}$.
3. Let $x \in c_{00}^\perp$. Then $x_n = \ip{x}{e_n} = 0$ for every $n$, by step 2. So $x = 0$, and $c_{00}^\perp = \set{0}$.
4. So $c_{00} + c_{00}^\perp = c_{00}$.
5. Let $y = (1, 1/2, 1/3, \dots)$, so $y_n = 1/n$. The series $\sum_n 1/n^2$ converges, so $y \in \ell^2$. Every entry of $y$ is nonzero, so $y \notin c_{00}$.
6. By steps 4 and 5, $c_{00} + c_{00}^\perp \ne \ell^2$. So the [[orthogonal decomposition|0SP7P0G7KQ]] fails for $c_{00}$.
7. By step 3 and part 6 of the [[basic properties|~2c397fab]], $(c_{00}^\perp)^\perp = \set{0}^\perp = \ell^2$. By step 5 this is not $c_{00}$, so the [[double orthogonal complement|0PXN9G4MX1]] formula fails too.
8. $c_{00}$ is not closed. Let $y^{(N)} = (1, 1/2, \dots, 1/N, 0, 0, \dots) \in c_{00}$. Then $\norm{y - y^{(N)}}^2 = \sum_{n > N} 1/n^2$, the tail of a convergent series, which tends to $0$. So $y$ lies in the [[closure|0QEAJB91GZ]] of $c_{00}$ but not in $c_{00}$.

## Remarks

The space $c_{00}$ is infinite-dimensional, so the hypothesis "$U$ finite-dimensional" of the decomposition theorem fails.
The same truncation as in step 8 works for every $x \in \ell^2$, so the closure of $c_{00}$ is all of $\ell^2$.
This agrees with the rule $(U^\perp)^\perp = \overline{U}$ in a Hilbert space.

Here is a sketch of why closedness is the exact condition in a Hilbert space.
Each set $S^\perp$ is closed, because $x \mapsto \ip{x}{s}$ is continuous.
If $V = U \oplus U^\perp$, take $x \in (U^\perp)^\perp$ and write $x = u + u'$ with $u \in U$ and $u' \in U^\perp$.
Then $u' = x - u$ lies in $(U^\perp)^\perp$ and in $U^\perp$, so $\ip{u'}{u'} = 0$ and $x = u$.
Hence $U = (U^\perp)^\perp$, and $U$ is closed.
Conversely, for a closed $U$ the [[Hilbert projection theorem|J5YNTHQMFX]] gives a closest point in $U$ to each $v$, and $v$ minus that point lies in $U^\perp$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In infinite dimension, a subspace can be so thin that no nonzero vector is at right angles to it, and still not fill the whole space.
The sequences that stop after finitely many steps are such a subspace inside $\ell^2$.
The sequence $1, 1/2, 1/3, \dots$ never stops, so it is not in the subspace, but it is not at right angles to it either.

### L2 human

The proof of the finite-dimensional theorem fails at one point: there is no finite orthonormal basis of $c_{00}$.
The natural guess for the part of $y$ in $c_{00}$ is $\sum_n \ip{y}{e_n} e_n = y$ itself, which is an infinite sum and lies outside $c_{00}$.
So the "projection" would leave the subspace.
The trap is to assume that every subspace of a Hilbert space is closed; in infinite dimension, many are not.
