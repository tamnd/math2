## Example

The [[triangle inequality|03HF5PMY5T]] for real numbers states:
$$|a + b| \le |a| + |b| \quad \text{for all real } a, b.$$
We show that the constant $1$ in front of $|a| + |b|$ is [[sharp|~07c20fd3]], and we find all equality cases.

1. The bound holds. For each real $t$ we have $-|t| \le t \le |t|$. Adding the cases $t = a$ and $t = b$ gives $-(|a| + |b|) \le a + b \le |a| + |b|$, which is the inequality.
2. The bound is attained. At $a = 2$, $b = 3$, the left side is $|5| = 5$ and the right side is $2 + 3 = 5$.
3. No smaller constant works. Let $c < 1$. At $a = 1$, $b = 0$, the bound $|a + b| \le c(|a| + |b|)$ would say $1 \le c$, which is false.
4. The equality cases. Squaring both sides, which are not negative, gives $a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = a^2 + 2|a||b| + b^2$, so equality holds exactly when $ab = |ab|$, that is, when $ab \ge 0$.

So the constant $1$ is best possible, and the extremal cases are the pairs $(a, b)$ in which $a$ and $b$ do not have opposite signs.

## Remarks

The bound is not close to the truth for every pair.
At $a = 1$, $b = -1$, the left side is $0$ and the right side is $2$.
Sharpness is a statement about the whole class of pairs, not about each pair.
The same proof works for vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the Euclidean norm, and there the equality cases are the pairs in which one vector is a nonnegative multiple of the other.
The reverse triangle inequality $\big||a| - |b|\big| \le |a - b|$ is also sharp, with equality at $a = 2$, $b = 1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The distance from $0$ to $a + b$ is at most the distance $|a|$ plus the distance $|b|$.
For $2$ and $3$, both sides equal $5$.
For $2$ and $-3$, the left side is $1$ and the right side is $5$.
Because the two sides are equal for $2$ and $3$, no smaller bound of this kind is true.

### L2 human

In the plane, the triangle inequality says that one side of a triangle is at most the sum of the other two sides.

1. Let $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (1, 0)$ and $C = (2, 0)$. The three points lie on one line.
2. Then $AC = 2$, $AB = 1$ and $BC = 1$, so $AC = AB + BC$.
3. So the bound is attained by a degenerate triangle, with $B$ on the segment $AC$.

The typical use is in estimates: we replace $|a + b|$ by $|a| + |b|$ and lose nothing in the worst case.
A trap: for a nondegenerate triangle the inequality is strict, so an equality case needs the degenerate triangles in the class.
