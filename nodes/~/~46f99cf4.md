## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over $F$, and let $T \colon V \to W$ be a linear map.
Then the [[kernel|02YFME3RGW]] $\ker T$ is a [[linear subspace|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$.
Also, for $u, v \in V$, $T(u) = T(v)$ if and only if $u - v \in \ker T$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Linearity is needed. For the non-linear map $f(x, y) = xy$ on $\R^2$, the zero set $\{f = 0\}$ is the union of the two axes. It contains $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ but not their sum $(1, 1)$.
- The value $0$ is needed. The set $\{v : T(v) = w\}$ with $w \ne 0$ [[is not a subspace|~feffaec0]].

### Consequences

- The solution set of a homogeneous system $Ax = 0$ is a subspace of $F^n$, the [[null space|0PHDYNR24D]].
- If $T(v_0) = w$, then the solution set of $T(v) = w$ is $v_0 + \ker T$. This is the [[structure of the solution set|PDH3HKXWAZ]] for linear systems.
- The kernel has a dimension, the [[nullity|0APCFW7639]], when $V$ is finite-dimensional.

### Converse

Every subspace $U$ of $V$ is a kernel: it is the kernel of the quotient map $V \to V/U$ onto the [[quotient space|0BR3FY5Q8M]].

### Generalizations

The kernel of a homomorphism of modules is a submodule, and the kernel of a [[group homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]] is a normal subgroup.
The proof is the same.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The vectors sent to zero by a linear map form a subspace.
The zero vector is one of them, and sums and multiples of them are again sent to zero.

### L2 human

For $T(x, y) = x - 2y$ from $\R^2$ to $\R$, the kernel is the line $x = 2y$.

1. It contains $(0, 0)$.
2. It contains $(2, 1)$ and $(4, 2)$, and their sum $(6, 3)$ satisfies $6 - 2 \cdot 3 = 0$.
3. It contains $-3\,(2, 1) = (-6, -3)$, since $-6 - 2 \cdot (-3) = 0$.

The second part of the lemma is the useful form in practice: two vectors have the same image exactly when they differ by a kernel vector.
