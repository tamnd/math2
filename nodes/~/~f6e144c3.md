## Summary

To compute the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]] of a square matrix $A$ over a field $F$, first find the eigenvalues, then count blocks from the numbers $\dim \ker (A - \lambda I)^k$.
To find a matrix $P$ with $A = P J P^{-1}$, build Jordan chains $N^{k-1} u, \dots, N u, u$ for $N = A - \lambda I$ and put them as the columns of $P$.
The result is checked by the equation $AP = PJ$ and $\det P \ne 0$, without computing $P^{-1}$.

## Motivation

The Jordan form theorem says that $J$ exists and is unique, but it does not say how to find it.
The proof by [[chains for nilpotent maps|~29b2bf0c]] gives a method: the block sizes are fixed by the ranks of the powers of $A - \lambda I$.
So the block structure needs only rank computations, which Gaussian elimination does in exact arithmetic.
The transition matrix $P$ needs a little more work, because each chain must start at a vector of the right height.
The form is used to compute powers, exponentials and other functions of $A$, and to decide if two matrices are similar.

## Notation

For an eigenvalue $\lambda$, write $N = A - \lambda I$ and $d_k = \dim \ker N^k$, with $d_0 = 0$.
A *Jordan chain* of length $k$ for $\lambda$ is a list $N^{k-1} u, \dots, N u, u$ with $N^k u = 0$ and $N^{k-1} u \ne 0$; its first vector is an eigenvector.
For the upper Jordan blocks of this page, the chain goes into $P$ in this order, from the eigenvector to $u$.
Books with lower Jordan blocks put the chain in the reverse order, $u, N u, \dots, N^{k-1} u$.
The vector $u$ is a *generalized eigenvector of height $k$*: $u \in \ker N^k$ and $u \notin \ker N^{k-1}$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The [[worked $4 \times 4$ example|~360b00b3]] has one eigenvalue $2$, kernel dimensions $2, 3, 4$ and Jordan form $J_3(2) \oplus J_1(2)$.
- The [[worked $3 \times 3$ example|~5a329e2c]] has eigenvalues $1$ and $3$ and Jordan form $J_2(1) \oplus J_1(3)$.
- The [[companion matrix of $(x - 1)^2$|~ebd6aae2]] has $d_1 = 1$, $d_2 = 2$, so its Jordan form is $J_2(1)$.

### Non-examples

- The [[rational matrix with characteristic polynomial $x^2 - 2$|~b9a2abfd]] has no Jordan form over $\mathbb{Q}$, since $\sqrt 2 \notin \mathbb{Q}$; the method stops at its first step.
- The rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{R}$ is the same kind of failure: [[it has no real Jordan form|~d1c203af]].

### Basic properties

The method has five steps.

1. Compute $\chi_A$ and its roots $\lambda$ with their multiplicities $m_\lambda$. If $\chi_A$ does not split over $F$, stop, or pass to a larger field.
2. For each $\lambda$, compute $d_k = \dim \ker (A - \lambda I)^k$ for $k = 1, 2, \dots$ until $d_k = m_\lambda$. The sequence then stays constant.
3. The number of blocks $J_j(\lambda)$ of size at least $j$ is $b_j = d_j - d_{j-1}$, and the number of size exactly $j$ is $b_j - b_{j+1}$. In particular the number of blocks is $d_1$, the geometric multiplicity.
4. Build chains from the top. Let $k$ be the largest block size. Choose $u$ in $\ker N^k$ outside $\ker N^{k-1}$, one for each block of size $k$, independent modulo $\ker N^{k-1}$. Then go down: at height $j$, choose new vectors of height $j$, independent modulo $\ker N^{j-1}$ together with the chain vectors already at height $j$.
5. Put the chains into $P$, each from the eigenvector to the top vector, in the order of the blocks of $J$. Check $AP = PJ$ and $\det P \ne 0$.

Step 3 follows from the [[normal form of nilpotent endomorphisms|~29b2bf0c]], applied on the [[generalised eigenspace|0AJ035ZTZE]] of $\lambda$.
By the [[rank-nullity theorem|0W4FSW1TVB]], $d_k = n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)^k$, so ranks can be used in place of [[kernels|02YFME3RGW]].

### Common errors

- Taking an arbitrary eigenvector as the bottom of a long chain. The bottom must be $N^{k-1} u$ for a top vector $u$; an eigenvector that is not in $\operatorname{im} N^{k-1}$ gives no chain of length $k$.
- Building chains from the bottom by solving $N w = v$ for an eigenvector $v$ of the wrong kind; the system can have no solution.
- Putting a chain into $P$ in the reverse order, which gives the lower Jordan form $J^{\mathsf T}$ instead of $J$.
- Checking the answer by computing $P^{-1} A P$ with an arithmetic error in $P^{-1}$; the check $AP = PJ$ is shorter and safer.
- Reading the block sizes from $d_k$ directly; only the jumps $d_k - d_{k-1}$ count blocks.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To find the Jordan form, you first find the eigenvalues.
Then, for each eigenvalue, you see how much the kernel grows when you apply $A - \lambda I$ again and again.
These growth numbers tell you how many blocks there are and how long they are.
Last, you look for chains of vectors that the matrix pushes down to zero, and they give the new basis.

### L2 human

Take $A$ with one eigenvalue $\lambda$ of multiplicity $5$, and suppose $d_1, d_2, d_3 = 2, 4, 5$.

1. The jumps are $b_1, b_2, b_3 = 2, 2, 1$.
2. So there are $2$ blocks, $2$ blocks of size at least $2$, and $1$ block of size at least $3$.
3. The sizes are $3$ and $2$, and $J = J_3(\lambda) \oplus J_2(\lambda)$.
4. For $P$, choose $u_1 \in \ker N^3 \setminus \ker N^2$, then $u_2 \in \ker N^2$ independent of $N u_1$ modulo $\ker N$.
5. The columns of $P$ are $N^2 u_1, N u_1, u_1, N u_2, u_2$.

The trap is step 4: $u_2$ must not be a combination of $N u_1$ and vectors of $\ker N$, or the second chain overlaps the first.

### L3 human

The numbers $d_k - d_{k-1}$ form the Weyr characteristic of $\lambda$, the conjugate of the partition of block sizes.
In floating point arithmetic, the Jordan form is not a continuous function of $A$, and the rank decisions are unstable; numerical work uses the Schur form instead.
In exact arithmetic over $\mathbb{Q}$, the block structure is computed without roots by the [[Smith normal form of $xI - A$|~57adc122]], factoring the invariant factors only at the end.
