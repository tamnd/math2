## Example

Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} = \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ be the [[integers modulo 4|03VFZ2WETT]] with addition.
Let $S = \{0, 1\}$.
We show that the translates $g + S$ do not behave like [[cosets|564VVAQTNR]].

1. The set $S$ is not a [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]]. We have $1 \in S$, but $1 + 1 = 2 \notin S$.
2. The translates are $0 + S = \{0, 1\}$, $1 + S = \{1, 2\}$, $2 + S = \{2, 3\}$ and $3 + S = \{3, 0\}$. These are $4$ different sets.
3. The translates overlap but are not equal. For example, $\{0, 1\} \cap \{1, 2\} = \{1\}$. So they do not form a [[partition|08QMKTWF9T]] of $G$.
4. None of the four sets is a coset of a subgroup. A coset with $2$ elements is a coset of a subgroup of order $2$, by the [[lemma on the size of cosets|0DKMBWTD7P]]. The only subgroup of order $2$ is $\{0, 2\}$, and its cosets are $\{0, 2\}$ and $\{1, 3\}$.
5. The count does not match the [[index|0DTXBQPA2S]] formula. There are $4$ translates, but $|G| / |S| = 4 / 2 = 2$.

## Remarks

The example shows that the subgroup condition is what makes the cosets disjoint.
Without it, the translates of a set have the same size, but they can overlap.
If we replace $S$ by the subgroup $\{0, 2\}$, then the translates are $\{0, 2\}$, $\{1, 3\}$, $\{2, 0\}$ and $\{3, 1\}$.
Only $2$ of them are different, they are disjoint, and $2 = 4 / 2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of $4$ seats around a round table, with numbers $0, 1, 2, 3$.
Take the two seats $0$ and $1$, and turn the table one step at a time.
You get the pairs $\{0, 1\}$, $\{1, 2\}$, $\{2, 3\}$ and $\{3, 0\}$.
Each seat is in two pairs, so the pairs overlap.
Cosets of a subgroup never overlap like this.

### L2 human

We compare the subsets $S = \{0, 1\}$ and $T = \{0, 2\}$ of $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.

1. For $S$, we get $1 + 1 = 2 \notin S$, so $S$ is not a subgroup.
2. For $T$, we get $0 + 0 = 0$, $0 + 2 = 2$, $2 + 2 = 0$, and $-2 = 2$. So $T$ is a subgroup.
3. The translates of $T$ are $\{0, 2\}$ and $\{1, 3\}$, a partition into $2$ blocks.
4. The translates of $S$ are $4$ overlapping sets.

The typical use of this example is to see why the proof of Lagrange's theorem needs a subgroup.
The trap is to define cosets for any subset and then to count them as if they were disjoint.
