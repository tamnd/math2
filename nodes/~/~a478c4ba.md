## Statement

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear with [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] $m_T$.
Then there is a vector $v \in V$ whose [[$T$-annihilator|~33677c91]] $m_v$ equals $m_T$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The lemma needs a field, or at least a principal ideal domain, so that annihilators are generated by one polynomial and factor into primes.
For infinite-dimensional $V$ the minimal polynomial need not exist: the map $f \mapsto xf$ on $F[x]$ satisfies no polynomial.

### Consequences

- $\deg m_T \le \dim V$, since $\deg m_v = \dim Z(v; T) \le \dim V$ by the [[lemma on cyclic subspaces|~9c6edc2a]].
- $T$ is cyclic [[if and only if $m_T = \chi_T$|~55322f5b]].
- The last invariant factor in the [[cyclic decomposition|~168ae65b]] is $m_T$.

### Converse

Every $v$ satisfies $m_v \mid m_T$.
So the lemma says that the largest annihilator is reached, and $m_T$ is the least common multiple of all $m_v$.

### Generalizations

For a finitely generated torsion module $M$ over a principal ideal domain, there is an element whose annihilator equals the annihilator of $M$.
For a finite abelian group this says that some element has order equal to the exponent of the group.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Each vector has its own polynomial: the shortest rule that sends it to zero under the map.
The minimal polynomial is the shortest rule that sends every vector to zero.
The lemma says that one well chosen vector already needs the full minimal polynomial.
For example, for the map that doubles the second coordinate of a point, the point one, one needs both factors.

### L2 human

Let $T = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 2)$ on $\mathbb{Q}^3$, so $m_T = (x - 1)(x - 2)$.

1. $m_{e_1} = x - 1$ and $m_{e_3} = x - 2$. Neither equals $m_T$.
2. These two annihilators are coprime, so the proof takes $v = e_1 + e_3$.
3. $(T - I) v = e_3$ and $(T - 2I) v = -e_1$, both nonzero, so no polynomial of degree $1$ kills $v$.
4. $(T - I)(T - 2I) v = (T - I)(-e_1) = 0$. So $m_v = (x - 1)(x - 2) = m_T$.

The typical use is in the proof that $T$ is cyclic when $m_T = \chi_T$.
The trap is to add vectors whose annihilators share a factor.
For $T = J_2(0)$, $m_{e_1} = x$ and $m_{e_2} = x^2$, but $e_1 + e_2$ has annihilator $x^2$, not $x^3$.

### L3 human

The proof splits $m_T$ into powers of distinct irreducible polynomials, finds a vector for each power and adds them.
The same argument shows that a finite abelian group has an element of order equal to its exponent.
In the [[cyclic decomposition theorem|~168ae65b]], the generator of the largest cyclic summand is such a vector.
