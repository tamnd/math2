## Statement

Let $F$ be a [[field|0QA8CHH8MG]] and let $n \ge 1$.
For $n \ge 2$, write $T_{ij}(c)$ for the [[transvection matrices|0NMN00FNW1]], and $D_n(\delta)$ for the [[elementary matrix|0M79HRJCRR]] $\operatorname{diag}(1, \dots, 1, \delta)$.

1. Each $A \in \mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is a product $A = T \, D_n(\det A)$, where $T$ is a finite product of transvection matrices. For $n = 1$, $T$ is the empty product $I_1$.
2. The [[special linear group|0V9PMN0RBH]] $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$ is the set of finite products of transvection matrices. So the transvections generate $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$.
3. The [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is generated by the transvection matrices and the matrices $D_n(\delta)$ with $\delta \ne 0$.

Here $\det$ is the [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The proof divides by a nonzero entry, so $F$ must be a field.
Over a commutative ring $R$, the transvections generate a subgroup $E_n(R)$ of $\mathrm{SL}_n(R)$, and it can be smaller.
For $R = F[x, y]$ and $n = 2$, Cohn showed that $E_2(R) \ne \mathrm{SL}_2(R)$.
For $n = 1$ there are no transvections, and the statement says only that $\mathrm{SL}_1(F) = \set{(1)}$.

### Consequences

- Each transvection has determinant $1$, so a product of transvections has determinant $1$; this gives the easy half of part 2.
- The theorem refines the result that an [[invertible matrix is a product of elementary matrices|1ZK55FS2BF]]. Row exchanges are not needed, and one scaling at the end is enough.
- A property that holds for transvections and is kept by products holds on all of $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$. For example, each $A \in \mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ can be joined to $I_n$ by a path in $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$, because $t \mapsto T_{ij}(tc)$ joins $I_n$ to $T_{ij}(c)$.
- The subgroup generated by transvections is normal, because a conjugate of a transvection is a transvection of $F^n$.

### Converse

Each product of transvections has determinant $1$, so it lies in $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$.
Each product of transvections and matrices $D_n(\delta)$ with $\delta \ne 0$ is invertible, so it lies in $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$.
So the inclusions in parts 2 and 3 are equalities.

### Generalizations

- The [[lemma on reduction to diagonal form|CFB41AZX87]] treats all square matrices, invertible or not.
- For $n \ge 3$, the transvections satisfy the Steinberg relations, which are described in the concept of a [[transvection|0DTCBCKEJP]]. Presentations of $\mathrm{SL}_n$ and the group $E_n(R)$ over rings belong to group theory and algebraic K-theory.

## History

Dickson studied the linear groups over finite fields in his book *Linear groups* of 1901.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The moves "add a multiple of one row to another" can turn each matrix of determinant $1$ into the identity matrix.
Undo the moves, and you get the matrix as a product of these simple matrices.
For a general invertible matrix, you need one more move at the end: scale the last row by the determinant.

### L2 human

Worked example: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, with $\det A = 4 - 3 = 1$.

1. The entry $a_{21} = 1$ is nonzero. Subtract row 2 from row 1, which is $T_{12}(-1)$ on the left: $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Subtract row 1 from row 2, which is $T_{21}(-1)$ on the left: $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. Subtract column 1 from column 2, which is $T_{12}(-1)$ on the right: $I_2$.
4. So $T_{21}(-1)\, T_{12}(-1)\, A\, T_{12}(-1) = I_2$.
5. Invert each factor: $A = T_{12}(1)\, T_{21}(1)\, T_{12}(1)$.
6. Check: $T_{12}(1) T_{21}(1) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = A$.

A trap: the exchange matrix $P_{12}$ has determinant $-1$, so it is not a product of transvections.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $1$, and it equals $T_{12}(1)\, T_{21}(-1)\, T_{12}(1)$.

### L3 human

When $n \ge 3$ or $|F| \ge 3$, each transvection is a commutator in $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$.
With part 2, this shows that $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$ is the commutator subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ in these cases.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the Euclidean algorithm replaces division, and $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$ is also generated by transvections.
The orders and the simplicity of these groups over finite fields belong to group theory.
