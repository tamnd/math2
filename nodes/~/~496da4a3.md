## Summary

*Kreisel-Putnam logic* $\mathsf{KP}$ is the [[intermediate logic|~57af80cc]] axiomatized by $(\neg p \to q \vee r) \to (\neg p \to q) \vee (\neg p \to r)$; see [[the definition|~676a33e5]].
It is a proper extension of $\mathsf{IPC}$ with the [[disjunction property|~59f11dc8]].
So it refuted Łukasiewicz's conjecture that $\mathsf{IPC}$ is the only intermediate logic with this property.
It is contained in [[Medvedev's logic|~6aab2c7f]] and in [[the logic KC|H9Z8BWRN69]].
Scott's logic $\mathsf{SL}$ is a second classical example of a proper intermediate logic with the disjunction property.

## Motivation

The disjunction property is the main constructive feature of $\mathsf{IPC}$.
One may ask whether it determines $\mathsf{IPC}$ among intermediate logics.
Łukasiewicz conjectured that it does.
Kreisel and Putnam found an axiom that is not intuitionistically provable but keeps the disjunction property.
Its content is that negated hypotheses are constructively empty, so a disjunction derived from them can be split in advance.
The same principle is valid for Medvedev's finite problems.

## Notation

$\mathrm{kp}$ denotes the axiom $(\neg p \to q \vee r) \to (\neg p \to q) \vee (\neg p \to r)$.
The weak Kreisel-Putnam axiom replaces $q$ and $r$ by $\neg q$ and $\neg r$.
$\mathsf{SL} = \mathsf{IPC} + ((\neg \neg p \to p) \to p \vee \neg p) \to \neg \neg p \vee \neg p$ is Scott's logic.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[Medvedev frames validate the Kreisel-Putnam axiom|~b9dcfaf6]], so $\mathsf{KP} \subseteq \mathsf{ML}$.
2. [[Frames with a greatest element validate the Kreisel-Putnam axiom|~c88d5ab0]], so $\mathsf{KP} \subseteq \mathsf{KC}$.
3. The fork with two leaves is a frame of $\mathsf{KP}$, since it is a Medvedev frame.

### Non-examples

1. [[KC is a proper extension of KP|~d7112e72]]: the fork validates $\mathsf{KP}$ and refutes weak excluded middle.
2. [[The rooted frame with three leaves|~4458905c]] refutes the Kreisel-Putnam axiom, so $\mathsf{IPC} \neq \mathsf{KP}$.

### Basic properties

1. $\mathsf{IPC} \subsetneq \mathsf{KP} \subsetneq \mathsf{KC}$, by the examples and non-examples.
2. Kreisel and Putnam proved that $\mathsf{KP}$ has the disjunction property; we cite this without proof.
3. By SEP, $\mathsf{KP}$ has the finite frame property, and it does not satisfy all the Visser rules.
4. By SEP and Wikipedia, $\mathsf{SL}$ also has the disjunction property, and $\mathsf{KP}$ and $\mathsf{SL}$ are incomparable.
5. By Zorn's lemma, every intermediate logic with the disjunction property lies in a maximal one, since the union of a chain of such logics has the property. By Wikipedia, there is no largest one.
6. By a theorem of Maksimova, a variant of a theorem of Levin, Medvedev's logic is the largest extension of the weak Kreisel-Putnam logic with the disjunction property.

### History

Łukasiewicz conjectured in 1952 that $\mathsf{IPC}$ is the only intermediate logic with the disjunction property.
Kreisel and Putnam refuted the conjecture in 1957, in "Eine Unableitbarkeitsbeweismethode für den intuitionistischen Aussagenkalkül".
Wroński later showed that there are continuum many intermediate logics with the disjunction property.
Scott's axiom is related to Rose's counterexample to the realizability completeness of $\mathsf{IPC}$.

### Common errors

1. Thinking that the disjunction property characterizes $\mathsf{IPC}$. $\mathsf{KP}$ is a counterexample.
2. Confusing the Kreisel-Putnam rule with the axiom. The rule is admissible in $\mathsf{IPC}$, while the axiom is not a theorem.
3. Dropping the negation in the premise. The resulting schema axiomatizes $\mathsf{LC}$, which lacks the disjunction property.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Intuitionistic logic has a famous feature: a proof of "$A$ or $B$" gives a proof of one of them.
For a long time one could think that only intuitionistic logic has it among the logics between it and classical logic.
Kreisel-Putnam logic adds a new principle and keeps the feature.
The new principle says that a negative assumption can not help to choose between two alternatives.

### L2 human

1. To show $\varphi \in \mathsf{KP}$, derive it from instances of $\mathrm{kp}$ in $\mathsf{IPC}$.
2. To show $\varphi \notin \mathsf{KP}$, refute it on a finite frame of $\mathsf{KP}$, such as a Medvedev frame or a frame with a top.
3. To compare with $\mathsf{KC}$, use the fork: it validates $\mathsf{KP}$ and refutes $\neg p \vee \neg \neg p$.

The typical use is as a counterexample and a test case for the disjunction property and admissible rules.
A trap: a frame of $\mathsf{KP}$ may branch, as the fork does; the axiom constrains where the worlds forcing $\neg p$ lie.

### L3 human

The disjunction property of $\mathsf{KP}$ is harder than for $\mathsf{IPC}$, because gluing a root below two frames of $\mathsf{KP}$ need not give a frame of $\mathsf{KP}$.
Below the fork and a single point it gives a frame that refutes the axiom: make $p$ true at one leaf of the fork and use the other two maximal worlds for $q$ and $r$.
Medvedev's logic is not finitely axiomatizable, so $\mathsf{KP}$ is a finitely axiomatized logic strictly below it.
