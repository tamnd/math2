## Example

Let $\bullet$ be the one-point irreflexive frame and let $\mathsf{KD} = \mathsf{K} \oplus \lozenge \top$, the logic of [[serial|NRNKV2YYWJ]] frames.
A pair $\langle L_1, L_2 \rangle$ *splits* $\mathrm{NExt}(\mathsf{K})$ if every normal modal logic $L$ satisfies exactly one of $L \subseteq L_1$ and $L_2 \subseteq L$.
We show that $\langle \mathrm{Log}(\bullet), \mathsf{KD} \rangle$ splits the [[lattice of normal modal logics|~d43c867f]].

1. Not both: $\lozenge \top$ is false in $\bullet$, so $\mathsf{KD} \not\subseteq \mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$. If $L \subseteq \mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$ and $\mathsf{KD} \subseteq L$, then $\mathsf{KD} \subseteq \mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$, which is false.
2. At least one: let $L$ be inconsistent. Then $\mathsf{KD} \subseteq L$.
3. Let $L$ be consistent, and consider its canonical model, as in [[Makinson's theorem|~244c9df6]]. If some world has no successor, then $L \subseteq \mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$ by Case 1 of that theorem.
4. Otherwise every maximal $L$-consistent set $\Gamma$ has a successor $\Delta$. Then $\lozenge \top \in \Gamma$, since otherwise $\square \bot \in \Gamma$ and $\bot \in \Delta$.
5. A formula that belongs to every maximal $L$-consistent set is a theorem of $L$, by Lindenbaum's lemma. So $\lozenge \top \in L$ and $\mathsf{KD} \subseteq L$.
6. So every normal modal logic is below $\mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$ or above $\mathsf{KD}$, and not both.

## Remarks

The example shows the simplest splitting of the lattice: it cuts $\mathrm{NExt}(\mathsf{K})$ into the logics of the irreflexive point and the logics that prove seriality.
The frame $\bullet$ has no cycles, and Blok proved that the logic of a finite rooted frame splits $\mathrm{NExt}(\mathsf{K})$ exactly when the frame has no cycles.
The reflexive point $\circ$ has a cycle, and it does not split the lattice in this way.
The second logic of a splitting is axiomatized by one formula, the splitting formula of the frame; for $\bullet$ it is $\lozenge \top$.
Splittings give a way to axiomatize logics by excluding finite frames, and in $\mathrm{NExt}(\mathsf{K4})$ every finite rooted frame gives one.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A splitting divides all logics into two parts with a single test.
Here the test is: does the logic allow a world with no next world?
If it allows such a world, it is part of the logic of one dead-end point.
If it does not, it proves that every world has a next world.

### L2 human

1. $\mathsf{S4}$ proves $\lozenge \top$, from $\square \bot \to \bot$. So $\mathsf{KD} \subseteq \mathsf{S4}$, and $\mathsf{S4}$ is on the upper side.
2. $\mathsf{GL}$ does not prove $\lozenge \top$, since a single irreflexive point is a $\mathsf{GL}$ frame. By the splitting, $\mathsf{GL} \subseteq \mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$.
3. $\mathsf{K}$ itself is on the lower side, since $\bullet$ validates $\mathsf{K}$.

The typical use of a splitting is to decide where a given logic lies with respect to a finite frame.
A trap: the lower side contains logics such as $\mathsf{K}$ with many frames that have no dead end; the test is about what the logic proves, not about each frame.
