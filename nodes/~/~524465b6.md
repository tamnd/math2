## Summary

Let $T$ be a [[theory|92SWXST1ZM]].
A [[model|MVCH6N9SNW]] $M$ of $T$ is *existentially closed* when for every model $N$ of $T$ that contains $M$ as a [[substructure|676H7EGTKJ]], every existential [[formula|HYJ10AF6RT]] with parameters in $M$ that is true in $N$ is already true in $M$.
So every finite system of equations and inequations over $M$ that has a solution in an extension has a solution in $M$; the formal page is [[this definition|~fc6b5431]].
Existentially closed models generalize algebraically closed fields to any theory, and their theory, when it is elementary, is the [[model companion|~7269c0e3]].

## Motivation

A field $K$ is algebraically closed when every polynomial over $K$ that has a root in some extension field has a root in $K$.
The same property makes sense for systems of several equations, with inequations, and for any class of structures.
Abraham Robinson used this as the general notion of "algebraically closed" in model theory.
The existentially closed models of a theory are its models with the most solutions, and they often have a geometric description.
For groups, rings and other classes with no natural closure, the notion still gives a class of rich models.

## Notation

One writes $M \preceq_1 N$ when $M$ is existentially closed in a larger structure $N$.
Common abbreviations are e.c. and $\mathcal{E}_T$ for the class of e.c. models of $T$.
In algebra one says *algebraically closed group*, *algebraically closed division ring*, and so on, for the e.c. models of the theory of groups and of division rings.
Some authors call a structure *generic* when it is e.c. in a stronger, forcing sense; see [[Robinson forcing|~357e6442]].

## Remarks

### Examples

- The [[e.c. fields are the algebraically closed fields|~5fc11853]], since adjoining a root of a polynomial gives an extension field.
- The [[e.c. linear orders are the dense linear orders without endpoints|~4d9cadf3]], since a point can be inserted between two points or at the ends.
- The e.c. ordered fields are the real closed fields, and the e.c. differential fields of characteristic $0$ are the [[differentially closed fields|KPCN3KHG50]].
- The e.c. groups are the [[algebraically closed groups|4SPKGZBTTT]]. Every group embeds into one, and every e.c. group is simple.
- Every division ring embeds into an e.c. division ring, by the same chain construction as for groups.
- Every model of a [[model complete theory|YR6HCMABTE]] is existentially closed.

### Non-examples

- The field $\Q$ is [[not existentially closed|~e419eaea]]: the equation $y^2 = 2$ has a solution in $\Q(\sqrt 2)$.
- The order $(\Z, <)$ is not e.c. among linear orders: the formula $\exists y \, (0 < y \wedge y < 1)$ holds in $\Q$.
- The field $\R$ is not e.c. among fields, although it is e.c. among ordered fields: $y^2 + 1 = 0$ has a solution in $\C$.

### Basic properties

- If the class of models of $T$ is closed under unions of chains, every model $M$ of $T$ is contained in an [[e.c. model|~71e6cae7]] of size at most $\abs{M} + \abs{L} + \aleph_0$.
- Existential formulas go up from $M$ to every extension. So for an e.c. model $M \subseteq N$, existential formulas with parameters in $M$ have the same truth value in $M$ and $N$.
- $T$ is [[model complete|YR6HCMABTE]] exactly when every model of $T$ is e.c., by [[Robinson's test|~3847820a]].
- If $T$ is [[inductive|~719ccde0]], the e.c. models of $T$ and of its universal part $T_\forall$ are the same structures.
- The class of e.c. models of an inductive theory is elementary exactly when $T$ has a model companion, and then the model companion is its theory.

### History

Abraham Robinson introduced existentially closed structures as a general form of algebraic closure.
Algebraically closed groups were studied by Scott in 1951.

### Common errors

- Forgetting the class. A structure is e.c. relative to a class, and $\R$ is e.c. among ordered fields but not among fields.
- Assuming that the e.c. models form an elementary class. For groups and for commutative rings they do not; see [[theories without a model companion|~826703ac]].
- Checking only sentences without parameters. The field $M = \overline{\Q}(t)$ satisfies every existential sentence without parameters that $\C$ satisfies, since it contains $\overline{\Q}$. But $y^2 = t$ has a solution in an extension and not in $M$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A model is existentially closed when it does not need to grow to solve its own problems.
If a system of equations with numbers from the model can be solved in some larger model, it can already be solved in the model.
The complex numbers have this property among fields: every polynomial equation already has a complex root.
The rational numbers do not: $x^2 = 2$ needs the larger field $\Q(\sqrt 2)$.

### L2 human

We show that an e.c. field $K$ is algebraically closed.

1. Let $p \in K[x]$ be a polynomial of degree at least $1$.
2. Let $q$ be an irreducible factor of $p$. The ring $F = K[x]/(q)$ is a [[field extension|0EQWVHF3W5]] of $K$, and the class of $x$ is a root of $p$ in $F$.
3. So the existential formula $\exists y \, (p(y) = 0)$, with the coefficients of $p$ as parameters, is true in $F$.
4. Since $K$ is e.c. and $F$ is a field containing $K$, the formula is true in $K$. So $p$ has a root in $K$.

The typical use of e.c. models is to replace a class without a closure operation by its richest members.
A trap is to apply the definition to extensions outside the class: the extension $F$ must be a field, not only a ring.

### L3 human

For an inductive theory $T_0$, the e.c. models form the natural "closed" part of the class of models.
When this class is elementary, its theory is the model companion of $T_0$, and it is model complete by [[Robinson's test|~3847820a]].
When it is not elementary, as for groups, one still studies it through the forcing companion and the Kaiser hull, the set of $\forall\exists$ sentences true in all e.c. models.
In algebra, e.c. groups give embedding theorems: every countable group embeds into a countable e.c. group, and every e.c. group is simple.
In geometry, the e.c. models of the theory of fields with extra structure, such as derivations or automorphisms, are the setting of differential and difference algebraic geometry.
