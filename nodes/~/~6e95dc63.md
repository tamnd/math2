## Summary

A coloring proof shows that a region cannot be tiled by given pieces, or that a path cannot exist, with the help of a coloring chosen for the purpose.
We give each cell of the region one of finitely many colors.
The coloring is chosen so that every allowed piece covers the same number of cells of each color, in every position where it fits.
Then the color counts of any tiling are sums of these fixed counts.
If the color counts of the region are not such a sum, no tiling exists.
The coloring defines an [[invariant|~6e8e1c09]] of the pieces, and the method turns a geometric question into a count.
It is the standard tool for the impossibility of [[domino tilings|83PJCPB9VA]] and of tilings by larger polyominoes.

## Motivation

A region of $62$ cells may have no tiling by $31$ dominoes, and the number $62$ does not show the reason.
A search over all placements of $31$ dominoes is far too long by hand.
A coloring can give the reason in one line.
If every domino covers one black and one white cell, then a tiling needs equal numbers of black and white cells.
The [[parity argument|~d211a525]] on the number of cells sees only the total, while the coloring sees how the total splits.
This finer count decides many problems that the total cannot decide.

## Notation

A coloring of a region $R$ is a function $c : R \to \{0, 1, \ldots, k - 1\}$.
The cell in row $i$ and column $j$ is written $(i, j)$.
The chessboard coloring is $c(i, j) = (i + j) \bmod 2$, with the value $0$ called black and $1$ called white.
The diagonal coloring with $k$ colors is $c(i, j) = (i + j) \bmod k$, which uses [[congruence modulo n|0WAY5WX8NY]].
Books also write "colouring", and "checkerboard coloring" for the chessboard coloring.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The mutilated chessboard: the $8 \times 8$ board without two opposite corners has $30$ cells of one color and $32$ of the other, so it has no domino tiling. The [[full proof|~9f833be6]] gives the details, and the [[topic on chessboard problems|WPGRNSZ1ZN]] lists related puzzles.
- Bars of length four: the $10 \times 10$ board has no tiling by $1 \times 4$ bars. In the coloring $(i + j) \bmod 4$ each bar covers one cell of each color, but the board has $26$ cells of one color and $24$ of another. The [[full proof|~b5292e24]] gives the details.
- The five tetrominoes: the straight, square, T, S and L tetrominoes, each used once, do not tile a $4 \times 5$ rectangle. In the chessboard coloring the T covers $3$ cells of one color and $1$ of the other, and each other piece covers $2$ and $2$. So the five pieces cover $11$ and $9$, while the rectangle has $10$ and $10$.
- Corner-to-corner tours: a piece that moves one cell up, down, left or right cannot visit all $64$ cells of a chessboard exactly once, from one corner to the opposite corner. Each move changes the color, and $63$ moves are needed, so the last cell has the other color. Opposite corners have the same color, so such a [[Hamiltonian path|AT8GWPWQWT]] does not exist.
- Bricks in a box: by de Bruijn's theorem, a $6 \times 6 \times 6$ box cannot be filled with $1 \times 2 \times 4$ bricks. The proof colors the unit cubes in the same spirit as the chessboard.

### Non-examples

- The chessboard coloring does not decide the $10 \times 10$ board with $1 \times 4$ bars. Each bar covers $2$ black and $2$ white cells, and the board has $50$ of each, so no contradiction appears, as [[this example|~6da884ec]] shows.
- Equal color counts do not prove that a tiling exists. The six cells $(1, 2), (2, 1), (2, 2), (2, 3), (2, 4), (3, 3)$ form a region with $3$ black and $3$ white cells. It has no domino tiling, because the cells $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$ both have $(2, 2)$ as their only neighbor in the region.
- A coloring in which a piece covers different counts in different positions gives no direct argument. Color each cell by the parity of its row. Then a vertical domino covers one cell of each color, but a horizontal domino covers two cells of the same color.

### Basic properties

- The method needs a coloring for which each piece has fixed color counts, in every position and in every orientation that the problem allows.
- The method proves only impossibility. When the counts agree, the question stays open.
- The chessboard coloring is a proper coloring of the grid graph with $2$ colors. So the grid graph is a [[bipartite graph|0ZVQKTRKVQ]], with [[chromatic number|0C5M7AM6FH]] $2$. A domino tiling is a [[perfect matching|0NHK9MFFW9]] of this graph, and a bipartite graph with a perfect matching has two classes of equal size.
- For the full $8 \times 8$ board with two cells removed, the chessboard count is the only obstruction. By Gomory's theorem, the board without two cells of different colors always has a domino tiling. A computer check of all $32 \cdot 32 = 1024$ such pairs confirms this.

### The steps

1. Fix the region and the set of pieces, with the positions and orientations that the problem allows.
2. Choose a coloring, often $(i + j) \bmod k$ or a pattern of blocks, so that each piece covers a fixed number of cells of each color.
3. Count the cells of each color in the region.
4. Show that the counts of step 3 are not a sum of the counts of the pieces. Then no tiling exists.

### History

The philosopher Max Black posed the mutilated chessboard problem in his book "Critical Thinking" (1946), with a hint at the coloring solution.
Solomon Golomb discussed it in 1954, and Martin Gardner wrote about it in his "Mathematical Games" column in 1957.
Ralph Gomory proved that the removal of any two cells of different colors from the chessboard leaves a region that dominoes can tile.

### Common errors

- To choose a coloring where some position of a piece covers other counts, often after a rotation. Each position and each orientation must be checked.
- To read equal color counts as a proof that a tiling exists. The six-cell region of the non-examples shows that this fails.
- To use the chessboard coloring for every problem. The $1 \times 4$ bars on the $10 \times 10$ board need a coloring with four colors.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Paint a chessboard in its usual black and white pattern.
A domino always covers two squares side by side, so it always covers one black square and one white square.
So any set of dominoes covers as many black squares as white squares.
Now cut off two opposite corners of the board, which have the same color, say white.
The rest has $32$ black and $30$ white squares, and no set of dominoes can cover it exactly.
The colors turn a hard puzzle into a quick count.

### L2 human

We show that the $4 \times 5$ rectangle cannot be tiled by the five tetrominoes, each used once.

1. Color the cell $(i, j)$ black when $i + j$ is even and white otherwise.
2. The rectangle has $4$ rows of $5$ cells. The rows have $3, 2, 3, 2$ black cells, so the rectangle has $10$ black and $10$ white cells.
3. Two cells side by side have different colors, so a domino covers one black and one white cell.
4. The straight, square, S and L tetrominoes each split into two dominoes, in every orientation. So each of them covers $2$ black and $2$ white cells.
5. The T tetromino has a center cell joined to the other three cells. These three cells have the color opposite to the center, so the T covers $3$ cells of one color and $1$ of the other.
6. So the five pieces cover $2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 11$ cells of one color and $9$ of the other.
7. This differs from the counts $10$ and $10$ of step 2, so no tiling exists.

The typical use is a quick impossibility proof before any search.
The trap is step 4: each piece must be checked in every orientation.
The T tetromino cannot split into two dominoes, and that is why it breaks the balance.

### L3 human

A coloring with $k$ colors sends each cell to a basis vector of $\mathbb{Z}^k$, and a region to the sum of the vectors of its cells.
A tiling is impossible when the vector of the region is not a sum of vectors of placed pieces.
More general weights give stronger invariants.
The coloring $(i + j) \bmod k$ is a shadow of the weight $x^i y^j$, with $x$ and $y$ complex roots of unity.
This weight proves that an $m \times n$ rectangle has a tiling by $1 \times k$ bars only when $k$ divides $m$ or $k$ divides $n$.
Conway and Lagarias, and later Thurston, replaced colors by elements of a group given by generators and relations, the tiling group of a set of pieces.

In graph theory, the two-color count is the simplest obstruction to a perfect matching in a bipartite graph.
[[Hall's marriage theorem|08WCB90WND]] lists all the obstructions, one for each set of vertices with too few neighbors.
The [[domino tiling|83PJCPB9VA]] page and the [[topic on polyominoes|CQJPRK86KN]] treat the counting and the structure of tilings.
In this sub-area, coloring proofs are a special case of [[invariants|~6e8e1c09]], and the two-color case is close to a [[parity argument|~d211a525]].
