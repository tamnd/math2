## Summary

The rules for equality are the axioms or rules that fix the meaning of the symbol $=$ in a proof system for [[first-order logic|086QKPF0A6]].
Two principles are enough.
Reflexivity says that $t = t$ for every term $t$.
The substitution principle says: from $s = t$ and $\varphi[s/x]$ infer $\varphi[t/x]$, when $s$ and $t$ are substitutable for $x$ in $\varphi$.
It is also called Leibniz's law or the [[indiscernibility of identicals|SCGE8NF69Q]].
Symmetry, transitivity and the compatibility of $=$ with every function and relation symbol follow from these two principles.
A [[Hilbert system|~4ea8b834]] states them as axioms, and [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]] as the rules $=$I and $=$E.
The [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] uses initial sequents or rules, and resolution provers use paramodulation or superposition.
Equality matters because almost every mathematical theory uses it.
Its rules decide what a calculus can prove and how fast a machine can search for proofs.

## Motivation

In the semantics, the symbol $=$ always means identity: $\mathcal{M} \models s = t$ exactly when $s$ and $t$ denote the same element of $M$.
A complete proof system must prove all sentences that are valid under this reading, such as $\forall x\,\forall y\,(x = y \to f(x) = f(y))$.
These sentences do not follow from the rules for connectives and quantifiers alone, since those rules treat $=$ as an ordinary binary relation.
So every calculus needs special rules for $=$.

No set of first-order rules can force $=$ to be identity in every model of the rules.
The rules can only force $=$ to be a [[congruence|DCSXXT4SER]]: an [[equivalence relation|0G8VEZ3WTK]] that is compatible with all symbols.
This is enough, because the quotient by a congruence satisfies the same formulas, and in the quotient $=$ is identity.

In automated reasoning a second problem appears.
The equality axioms, written as clauses, interact with every clause in the search space and make the search very large.
Paramodulation and superposition build the substitution principle into one rule, so that the axioms are not needed as clauses.

## Notation

We write $s \neq t$ for $\neg\, s = t$.
In the automated reasoning literature the object-language equality is often written $\approx$, to separate it from the identity of terms, written $\equiv$ or $=$.
Some books write $\doteq$ for the formal symbol.
The substitution principle has several names: Leibniz's law, the indiscernibility of identicals, substitutivity, and the replacement rule.
The names of the natural deduction rules vary too: $=$I is also called reflexivity, and $=$E is also called substitution or replacement.

## Remarks

### Equality in a Hilbert system

In Enderton's system (see [[Hilbert-style predicate calculus|~4ea8b834]]), the logical axioms include all generalizations of two equality schemas.

1. Axiom 5: $x = x$, for each variable $x$.
2. Axiom 6: $x = y \to (\alpha \to \alpha')$, where $\alpha$ is atomic and $\alpha'$ comes from $\alpha$ by replacing $x$ at zero or more places by $y$.

The variant with Mendelson's axioms has $\forall x\,(x = x)$ and the schema $x = y \to (\varphi(x,x) \to \varphi(x,y))$.
Here $\varphi(x,y)$ comes from $\varphi(x,x)$ by replacing some free occurrences of $x$ by $y$, at places where $y$ is free for $x$.
Mendelson's schema allows any formula; Enderton's schema allows only atomic formulas, and the version for all formulas is then a theorem, proved by induction on formulas.
Symmetry and transitivity are derived in a few lines: see [[symmetry, transitivity and congruence from the Hilbert equality axioms|~be1d0ab6]].

### Equality in natural deduction

Natural deduction has one introduction rule and one elimination rule.

1. $=$I: infer $t = t$, with no premises.
2. $=$E: from $s = t$ and $\varphi[s/x]$ infer $\varphi[t/x]$, when $s$ and $t$ are substitutable for $x$ in $\varphi$.

Symmetry: from $s = t$, apply $=$E with $\varphi$ the formula $x = s$; the premise $\varphi[s/x]$ is $s = s$, given by $=$I, and the conclusion is $t = s$.
Transitivity: from $s = t$ and $t = u$, apply $=$E to $t = u$ with $\varphi$ the formula $s = x$; the premise $\varphi[t/x]$ is $s = t$, and the conclusion is $s = u$.

### Equality in the sequent calculus

A simple way to add equality to Gentzen's $\mathsf{LK}$ is to add the initial sequents $\Rightarrow t = t$ and $s = t, \varphi[s/x] \Rightarrow \varphi[t/x]$, for every atomic $\varphi$.
Then full cut elimination fails.
The sequent $a = b \Rightarrow b = a$ is derivable: cut the initial sequent $a = b, a = a \Rightarrow b = a$ (with $\varphi$ the formula $x = a$) against $\Rightarrow a = a$.
It has no cut-free derivation.
By the subformula property, a cut-free derivation of a sequent of atomic formulas uses no logical rule.
So it is one initial sequent followed by weakenings, contractions and exchanges.
That initial sequent has only $a = b$ on the left and only $b = a$ on the right.
The identity sequents and $\Rightarrow t = t$ are not of this form, since $a$ and $b$ are distinct constants.
An equality sequent of this form needs $s = a$, $t = b$, $\varphi[a/x] = (a = b)$ and $\varphi[b/x] = (b = a)$.
The third condition forces $\varphi$ to be $x = b$ or $a = b$, and then $\varphi[b/x]$ is $b = b$ or $a = b$, not $b = a$.
Gentzen's method still removes every cut on a compound formula, so the remaining cuts are on atomic formulas.

A standard remedy, used by Negri and von Plato, writes the equality principles as rules that act on the antecedent.
$$\frac{t = t, \Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta}{\Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta}\ \mathrm{Ref} \qquad \frac{\varphi[t/x], s = t, \varphi[s/x], \Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta}{s = t, \varphi[s/x], \Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta}\ \mathrm{Repl}$$
Here $\varphi$ is atomic.
With these rules, $a = b \Rightarrow b = a$ is derived without cut: Repl with $\varphi$ the formula $x = a$ takes $b = a, a = b, a = a \Rightarrow b = a$ to $a = b, a = a \Rightarrow b = a$, and Ref removes $a = a$.
Negri and von Plato proved that cut can be eliminated when these rules are added to the contraction-free calculus $\mathsf{G3c}$.

### Logic without equality and congruences

First-order logic can also be set up without a logical equality symbol; then a binary relation symbol $E$ is an ordinary symbol.
For a language $L$, let $\mathrm{Eq}_L$ be the following set of sentences.

1. Reflexivity: $\forall x\, E(x,x)$.
2. For each $n$-ary function symbol $f$: $\forall \bar x\,\forall \bar y\,(E(x_1, y_1) \land \cdots \land E(x_n, y_n) \to E(f(\bar x), f(\bar y)))$.
3. For each $n$-ary relation symbol $R$, including $E$: $\forall \bar x\,\forall \bar y\,(E(x_1, y_1) \land \cdots \land E(x_n, y_n) \to (R(\bar x) \to R(\bar y)))$.

In every model of $\mathrm{Eq}_L$, the relation $E^{\mathcal{M}}$ is a congruence; symmetry and transitivity follow from item 3 for $R = E$.
The quotient structure $\mathcal{M}/E$ has the classes of $E^{\mathcal{M}}$ as elements, and $E$ becomes identity there.
For every formula $\varphi$ and every assignment, $\varphi$ holds in $\mathcal{M}$ exactly when it holds in $\mathcal{M}/E$ at the classes of the assigned elements.
So $\Gamma \models \varphi$ in logic with equality exactly when $\Gamma \cup \mathrm{Eq}_L \models \varphi$ in logic without equality, with $=$ read as $E$.
A worked case is [[the parity congruence and its quotient|~57fd7dbb]].

### Paramodulation and superposition

[[First-order resolution|~787204b5]] treats $=$ as an ordinary predicate, so a prover must either add $\mathrm{Eq}_L$ as clauses or use a rule for equality.
Paramodulation is such a rule.
Let $C \cup \{s = t\}$ and $D \cup \{L\}$ be clauses with no common variable, where the literal $L$ contains a subterm $u$ at some position.
If $s$ and $u$ have an mgu $\sigma$, the paramodulant is $(C \cup D \cup \{L'\})\sigma$, where $L'$ comes from $L$ by replacing that occurrence of $u$ by $t$.
The equation can also be used from right to left, since $s = t$ and $t = s$ are interchangeable.
Resolution, factoring and paramodulation, together with the reflexivity clause $\{x = x\}$, form a refutation complete calculus for clauses with equality.
A computed step is in [[a paramodulation refutation|~560b7bc4]].

Unrestricted paramodulation produces very many clauses, for example by rewriting in both directions and into variables.
Superposition restricts it with a reduction ordering $\succ$ on terms that is total on ground terms.
An equation $s = t$ is used only from its larger side, with $s\sigma \not\preceq t\sigma$, and only into a maximal side of a maximal literal.
No step rewrites at a variable position.
Superposition also has rules called equality resolution and equality factoring, and it keeps refutation completeness under these restrictions.
For sets of unit equations, superposition becomes a form of [[Knuth-Bendix completion|4JKD5FBJCS]].

### Leibniz's law and the identity of indiscernibles

The substitution principle is one direction of a principle attributed to Leibniz: identical things have the same properties.
The converse, the identity of indiscernibles, says that things with the same properties are identical.
In [[second-order logic|G6X7SZSNC5]] both directions are one sentence, and equality is even definable: $x = y$ holds exactly when $\forall X\,(X(x) \to X(y))$ holds.
In first-order logic only the first direction is a logical law.
The converse fails for first-order formulas.
In $(\mathbb{Z}, <)$ the map $n \mapsto n + 1$ is an [[automorphism|DSQ17TNW1C]], so $0$ and $1$ satisfy the same formulas with one free variable, but $0 \neq 1$.

### Examples

1. [[Symmetry, transitivity and congruence from the Hilbert equality axioms|~be1d0ab6]]: three short derivations in Enderton's system, each line checked.
2. [[A paramodulation refutation|~560b7bc4]]: the equation $f(x) = x$ rewrites $f(g(y))$ with the mgu $\{x \mapsto g(y)\}$, and two resolution steps finish the refutation.
3. [[The parity congruence and its quotient|~57fd7dbb]]: on $\mathbb{Z}$, congruence modulo $2$ satisfies all equality axioms of a small language, and the quotient has two elements.
4. Natural deduction: from $a = b$ and $P(a)$, the rule $=$E with $\varphi = P(x)$ gives $P(b)$ in one step.
5. Sequent calculus: the derivation of $a = b \Rightarrow b = a$ with the rules Ref and Repl above.

### Non-examples

1. [[An equivalence relation that violates the substitution schema|~d0c774c8]]: parity on $\mathbb{Z}$ with the predicate "positive" satisfies reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity, but not $x = y \to (P(x) \to P(y))$.
2. Substitution without substitutability. The "instance" $x = y \to (\exists y\, x \neq y \to \exists y\, y \neq y)$ puts $y$ into the scope of $\exists y$. It fails in a two-element structure when $x$ and $y$ denote the same element.
3. The identity of indiscernibles as a first-order principle. In $(\mathbb{Z}, <)$ the elements $0$ and $1$ agree on every formula with one free variable, but they are distinct.

### Basic properties

1. Symmetry, transitivity and $\vdash_{\mathsf{H}} x = y \to f(x) = f(y)$ are derivable from the two equality schemas; see [[example 1|~be1d0ab6]].
2. For every formula $\varphi$ and every term $u$, $\vdash s = t \to (\varphi[s/x] \to \varphi[t/x])$ and $\vdash s = t \to u[s/x] = u[t/x]$, when the substitutions are allowed. The proofs are inductions on $\varphi$ and on $u$.
3. The equality axioms are valid in every structure, because $=$ is read as identity; so they keep the calculus sound.
4. With the equality axioms, the calculus is complete for logic with equality; see [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]]. The proof builds a model in which $=$ is a congruence and passes to the quotient.
5. Every model of $\mathrm{Eq}_L$ is equivalent, sentence by sentence, to its quotient, in which $=$ is identity. So first-order sentences cannot tell identity from a congruence.

### History

The substitution principle and its converse are traditionally named after Leibniz, who wrote that no two distinct things have all properties in common.
George Robinson and Lawrence Wos introduced paramodulation in 1969, as an extension of resolution to equality.
Bachmair and Ganzinger developed superposition with its ordering restrictions in the early 1990s; their journal paper appeared in 1994.

### Common errors

1. Using $=$ in a resolution prover as an ordinary predicate, with neither equality axioms nor paramodulation. Then unsatisfiable sets can look satisfiable, as in [[example 2|~560b7bc4]].
2. Substituting a term that is not substitutable, as in non-example 2. The rule $=$E and the axioms need the substitutability condition.
3. Believing that the equality axioms force $=$ to be identity. They force only a congruence, as [[example 3|~57fd7dbb]] shows; the quotient then gives an equivalent structure in which $=$ is identity.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Equality has two basic rules.
First, every thing equals itself, for example $3 = 3$.
Second, equal things can replace each other: if $x = 3$ and $x + 1$ is even, then $3 + 1$ is even.
From these two rules we get the other familiar facts.
For example, if $a = b$ then $b = a$, and if $a = b$ and $b = c$ then $a = c$.
Computers that prove theorems use the second rule as a rewriting step: they replace one side of an equation by the other inside a formula.

### L2 human

We derive symmetry in natural deduction from the premise $s = t$.

1. Choose the formula $\varphi$ to be $x = s$, with a variable $x$ that does not occur in $s$ or $t$.
2. Then $\varphi[s/x]$ is $s = s$, and $\varphi[t/x]$ is $t = s$.
3. The rule $=$I gives $s = s$ with no premise.
4. The rule $=$E, applied to $s = t$ and $s = s$, gives $t = s$.

The typical use of $=$E is the step "replace equals by equals" in algebra, made precise by the choice of $\varphi$.
The trap is the choice of $\varphi$.
In step 1, $\varphi$ must leave the second $s$ alone, so the place of $x$ marks which occurrences are replaced.

### L3 human

The rules for equality show a general pattern.
A logical symbol whose meaning the semantics fixes is axiomatized only up to a congruence, and a quotient construction closes the gap.
The same pattern appears in the construction of the canonical model in the completeness proof, where the terms modulo provable equality form the domain.
In [[equational logic|72S3YSQFZ7]] the rules are reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, congruence and substitution of terms for variables, and [[Birkhoff's completeness theorem|WDHWCQ6Y2Y]] states that they are complete.

In proof theory, equality is a test case for adding axioms to a cut-free calculus.
Initial sequents for axioms break full cut elimination, while rules that act on the antecedent keep it, and this method extends to other universal axioms.
In automated reasoning, superposition, together with redundancy criteria and simplification by rewriting, is the basis of the strongest current provers for first-order logic with equality.
It connects resolution with [[term rewriting|A6J731Y193]], where orientations of equations by a reduction ordering decide what counts as a simplification.
