## Example

We show that [[minimal logic|~2f9e5781]] proves $(p \vee q) \wedge \neg p \to \neg\neg q$ but not $(p \vee q) \wedge \neg p \to q$.

Derivation of the weak form.

1. Assume $(p \vee q) \wedge \neg p$. By $\wedge$-elimination, we have $p \vee q$ and $\neg p$.
2. Assume $\neg q$, to derive $\bot$.
3. Case $p$: apply $\neg p$ to $p$, and get $\bot$.
4. Case $q$: apply $\neg q$ to $q$, and get $\bot$.
5. By $\vee$-elimination on $p \vee q$, we get $\bot$.
6. Discharge $\neg q$, and get $\neg\neg q$. Then discharge the first assumption.

No step uses ex falso, so this is a derivation in $\mathsf{ML}$.

Countermodel for the strong form.

7. Take a minimal Kripke model with one world $w$, with $p$ true, $q$ false and $\bot$ true at $w$.
8. $p \vee q$ is true at $w$, since $p$ is.
9. $\neg p$, that is, $p \to \bot$, is true at $w$, since $\bot$ is true at $w$.
10. So the antecedent $(p \vee q) \wedge \neg p$ is true at $w$, but $q$ is false. The strong form fails at $w$.
11. Minimal logic is sound for minimal Kripke models, so $\mathsf{ML} \nvdash (p \vee q) \wedge \neg p \to q$.

## Remarks

In intuitionistic logic, both forms are theorems: in case $p$ of step 3, ex falso turns $\bot$ into $q$.
So disjunctive syllogism is exactly as strong as ex falso, in the presence of the rules for $\vee$.
Indeed, with $q := \varphi$ and $p := \bot$, disjunctive syllogism gives $(\bot \vee \varphi) \wedge \neg\bot \to \varphi$, and $\neg\bot$ is a theorem of $\mathsf{ML}$.
From $\bot$ we get $\bot \vee \varphi$, so the strong form yields $\bot \to \varphi$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"Either it rains or it snows; it does not rain; so it snows" is the disjunctive syllogism.
Minimal logic only gets the weaker conclusion "it can not be that it does not snow".
The reason is that the first case, "it rains", contradicts the premise, and minimal logic does not turn a contradiction into any conclusion it likes.

### L2 human

1. Take a world where both "it rains" and the contradiction $\bot$ are true.
2. Then "it does not rain" is also true there, since it means "rain leads to $\bot$".
3. "It snows" is false there, so the strong syllogism fails.
4. The weak conclusion "not not snow" means "no snow leads to $\bot$", and that is true, since $\bot$ is true.

The typical use of this example is to show what ex falso adds: it turns a weak conclusion into a strong one.
A trap: the countermodel makes $\bot$ true at a world, which is allowed in minimal Kripke models but not in intuitionistic ones.
