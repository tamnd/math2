## Example

Let $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product, and let $T(x_1, x_2) = (x_2, 0)$, so $T(x) = Ax$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Let $B = (b_1, b_2)$ with $b_1 = (1, 0)$ and $b_2 = (1, 1)$.
This basis is not orthonormal, since $b_1 \cdot b_2 = 1$.
We show that the [[matrix|0YYMY95FWS]] of $T^*$ in the basis $B$ is not the transpose of the matrix of $T$ in $B$.

1. The adjoint. The standard basis is orthonormal, so $T^*(y) = A^T y$, that is, $T^*(y_1, y_2) = (0, y_1)$, by [[the matrix of the adjoint|0DBTE6HVBD]].
2. The matrix of $T$ in $B$. We have $T(b_1) = (0, 0) = 0$ and $T(b_2) = (1, 0) = b_1$. So $[T]_B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. The matrix of $T^*$ in $B$. We have $T^*(b_1) = (0, 1) = -b_1 + b_2$ and $T^*(b_2) = (0, 1) = -b_1 + b_2$. So $[T^*]_B = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. Comparison. The transpose of $[T]_B$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. It differs from $[T^*]_B$, for example in the entry $(1, 1)$.
5. The correct formula. The [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]] of $B$ is $G = \begin{pmatrix} b_1 \cdot b_1 & b_2 \cdot b_1 \\ b_1 \cdot b_2 & b_2 \cdot b_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, with $G^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
6. Then $[T]_B^T G = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
7. And $G^{-1} [T]_B^T G = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. This is $[T^*]_B$ from step 3.

## Remarks

- The example shows that the rule "adjoint equals conjugate transpose" is a statement about orthonormal bases. In a general basis the rule is $[T^*]_B = G^{-1} [T]_B^* G$, as in the [[generalization of the matrix rule|0DBTE6HVBD]].
- When $b_2$ changes to $(0, 1)$, the basis becomes orthonormal, $G = I$, and the formula reduces to the transpose.
- The trace and the rank of $[T^*]_B$ and $[T]_B$ still agree, since these are properties of the maps. Both traces are $0$, and both ranks are $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For the dot product, the adjoint of a matrix is its transpose.
This rule works only when the coordinates come from perpendicular unit vectors.
Here the two basis vectors $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$ make an angle of $45$ degrees.
In these coordinates, the adjoint has a matrix with four nonzero entries, while the transpose of the matrix of the map has only one.

### L2 human

The computation has three parts.

1. Compute the adjoint in an orthonormal basis, where the transpose rule holds.
2. Express the images of the new basis vectors in the new basis, column by column.
3. Compare with the transpose, and correct with the Gram matrix.

The typical use of this example is as a warning in numerical work, where the basis is often not orthonormal.
A trap: the matrix of a self-adjoint map in a basis that is not orthonormal need not be symmetric.
