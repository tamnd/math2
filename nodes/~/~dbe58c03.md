## Example

Consider the argument form with premises $p \to q$ and $r \to q$, and conclusion $p \to r$.
We check that this form is not an instance of [[hypothetical syllogism|7MT8CPCEM4]], and that it is invalid.

1. Hypothetical syllogism needs premises $\varphi \to \psi$ and $\psi \to \chi$, where the consequent of the first is the antecedent of the second.
2. Here the consequent of the first premise is $q$, and the antecedent of the second premise is $r$. They differ, so the form is not hypothetical syllogism.
3. The form is invalid. Take the valuation with $p$ true, $q$ true and $r$ false.
4. In this valuation, $p \to q$ is true and $r \to q$ is true.
5. The conclusion $p \to r$ is false, because $p$ is true and $r$ is false.
6. So the valuation of steps 3 to 5 is a [[countermodel|FWHBT4HABW]].

A mathematical instance: for an integer $n$, "if $4 \mid n$, then $n$ is even" and "if $6 \mid n$, then $n$ is even" are both true, but "if $4 \mid n$, then $6 \mid n$" is false for $n = 4$.

## Remarks

The example shows that two statements with a common consequence need not be related to each other.
The valid form with the same premises is different: from $p \to q$ and $r \to q$ we derive $(p \lor r) \to q$, by [[proof by cases|0WCP0PVET6]].
The argument becomes a hypothetical syllogism if the second premise is reversed to $q \to r$, but "if $n$ is even, then $6 \mid n$" is false, so the conclusion is not proved.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"Dogs have four legs" and "cats have four legs" are both true.
They do not give "dogs are cats".
Two things that share a property are not the same thing.

### L2 human

We find the error in a short wrong proof.

1. Wrong claim: every function $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ that is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$ is a polynomial.
2. Wrong proof: "every differentiable function is continuous, and every polynomial is continuous, so every differentiable function is a polynomial".
3. Both premises are true theorems, and both end in "continuous". The form is $p \to q$, $r \to q$, therefore $p \to r$, as in the example.
4. The claim is false: $\sin x$ is differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$, and it is not a polynomial, because it has infinitely many zeros and is not the zero function.

The typical use of this check is to underline the middle term in each link of a chain and to confirm that it matches.
A trap is that a broken chain may have a true conclusion by accident, so a true conclusion does not show that the chain is correct.
