## Summary

Let $\mathcal{M}$ be an $L$-[[structure|983GZBQYT4]] with domain $M$ and let $\kappa$ be an infinite [[cardinal|0EBACQFAZW]].
The structure $\mathcal{M}$ is *$\kappa$-homogeneous* when every [[partial elementary map|XTNYY0WRQ5]] $f$ from $\mathcal{M}$ to $\mathcal{M}$ with $\abs{\operatorname{dom} f} < \kappa$ extends to a partial elementary map with any given element in its domain.
It is *strongly $\kappa$-homogeneous* when every such $f$ extends to an [[automorphism|DSQ17TNW1C]] of $\mathcal{M}$.
It is *homogeneous* or *strongly homogeneous* when this holds for $\kappa = \abs{M}$.
In a strongly homogeneous structure, two tuples have the same [[type|9V71Q3SD1G]] over a small set exactly when an automorphism over that set maps one to the other.

## Motivation

A [[type|9V71Q3SD1G]] describes a tuple by formulas, and an automorphism moves a tuple without changing any formula.
So tuples in the same orbit of the automorphism group have the same type.
Homogeneity is the converse: whenever formulas cannot tell two tuples apart, the structure has a symmetry that exchanges them.
In a homogeneous structure, first-order properties of tuples and symmetries of the structure carry the same information.

## Notation

Some authors write $\kappa$-homogeneous for what is called strongly $\kappa$-homogeneous here, so the definition must be checked in each source.
For countable structures, "homogeneous" often means $\aleph_0$-homogeneous.
In combinatorics, a homogeneous structure is one in which isomorphisms between finite substructures extend to automorphisms; this is ultrahomogeneity, which is a different notion when the theory lacks quantifier elimination.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The order $(\Q, <)$ is strongly homogeneous; see [[the rational order is saturated|~c8f8cf10]].
- The order $(\Z, <)$ is strongly homogeneous, since a partial elementary map on a finite set is the restriction of a translation; see [[the integer order is homogeneous but not saturated|~1121b6eb]].
- Every [[saturated model|RMTZ5J64FX]] is strongly homogeneous, by [[uniqueness of saturated models|~a2cbf168]] with $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{N}$.
- The field $\overline{\Q}$ of algebraic numbers is homogeneous, although it is not saturated; see [[the algebraic numbers are not saturated|~26b4029f]].
- A nonzero [[vector space|000AYPA9AJ]] $V$ over $\Q$ is strongly homogeneous: a partial elementary map preserves linear independence, so it extends to a linear bijection of $V$.

### Non-examples

- The rational order with constants $c_n = 1 - 1/(n+1)$ is not $\aleph_0$-homogeneous; see [[an order whose types are not orbits|~eb3dc704]].
- The order $(\R, <)$ is $\aleph_0$-homogeneous but not homogeneous; see [[the real order is not special|~a1ead269]].

### Basic properties

- Strongly $\kappa$-homogeneous implies $\kappa$-homogeneous, by restriction of the automorphism.
- If $\abs{M} = \kappa$, then $\kappa$-homogeneous implies strongly $\kappa$-homogeneous, by the back-and-forth argument of [[uniqueness of saturated models|~a2cbf168]] with $\mathcal{N} = \mathcal{M}$.
- Two countable homogeneous models of the same complete theory that realize the same $n$-types for every $n$ are isomorphic.
- A countable structure is saturated exactly when it is homogeneous and [[universal|~40536de0]].
- If $\mathcal{M}$ is strongly $\kappa$-homogeneous and $\abs{A} < \kappa$, then the orbits of the automorphisms over $A$ are the sets of realizations of types over $A$; see [[types as orbits|~3d38bf9d]].

### History

Morley and Vaught studied homogeneous and universal structures together in 1962, in the paper in which they introduced saturated models.

### Common errors

- Reading homogeneity as a condition on substructures: the maps must preserve all formulas, not only the quantifier-free ones.
- Thinking that homogeneous models are rich: $(\Z, <)$ and $\overline{\Q}$ are homogeneous and omit many types.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A structure is homogeneous when any two lists of elements that look the same to first-order logic can be exchanged by a symmetry of the whole structure.
The rational numbers are homogeneous: any two increasing lists of the same length can be moved onto each other by an order-preserving bijection of $\Q$.
Homogeneity says nothing about which elements exist, only that the existing ones are as symmetric as their descriptions allow.

### L2 human

We check that $(\Z, <)$ is strongly $\aleph_0$-homogeneous.

1. For $a < b$, the formula "there are exactly $b - a - 1$ elements between $x$ and $y$" holds of $(a, b)$.
2. So a partial elementary map $f$ preserves all distances, and on a finite nonempty domain it equals $x \mapsto x + c$ with $c = f(a) - a$.
3. The translation $x \mapsto x + c$ is an automorphism that extends $f$.

The typical use is to turn equality of types into an automorphism.
The trap is to stop at the first step: preserving the order alone does not make a map partial elementary in $(\Z, <)$.

### L3 human

In a [[monster model|~84f3dbc1]], strong homogeneity turns the model theory of the structure into the study of its automorphism group.
Definable closure becomes the set of points fixed by all automorphisms over a set, and invariant definable sets become definable over that set.
For countable structures in combinatorics, homogeneity in the sense of finite substructures is the setting of Fraïssé limits, such as the [[Rado graph|RMXR0FEF54]].
