## Example

Let $L_m = (\set{1, \dots, m}, <)$ be the [[linear order|0FRXBVVR03]] with $m$ elements, and allow $L_0 = \emptyset$ inside the proof.
For $n \ge 1$, Duplicator wins the [[Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game|2RE7YAZK8R]] $G_n(L_m, L_k)$ if and only if $m = k$, or $m \ge 2^n - 1$ and $k \ge 2^n - 1$.
By the [[Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé theorem|~9f527b73]], this says when $L_m$ and $L_k$ agree on all sentences of [[quantifier rank|~e5a7f04b]] at most $n$.

### Duplicator's strategy

Let $m, k \ge 2^n - 1$, and add virtual points $0$ and $m + 1$ to $L_m$, paired with $0$ and $k + 1$ in $L_k$.
For points $u \le v$ let $d(u, v) = v - u$.
Duplicator keeps this invariant with $r$ rounds left: any two paired points have the same order on both sides, and their distances are equal or both at least $2^r$.

1. At the start $r = n$, and $d(0, m + 1) = m + 1$, $d(0, k + 1) = k + 1$ are both at least $2^n$.
2. With $r + 1$ rounds left, let Spoiler pick $a$ in $L_m$ between neighbors $u < a < v$ among the paired points. The case of $L_k$ is the same, and a point paired before is answered by its partner.
3. If $d(u, a) < 2^r$, Duplicator picks $b$ at the same distance above the partner of $u$. If $d(a, v) < 2^r$, Duplicator picks $b$ at the same distance below the partner of $v$.
4. Otherwise $d(u, v) \ge 2^{r+1}$, so the partners of $u$ and $v$ are at least $2^{r+1}$ apart, and Duplicator picks $b$ at distance $2^r$ above the partner of $u$.
5. In each case the new distances are equal or both at least $2^r$, so the invariant holds. At $r = 0$ the order and the equalities agree, and Duplicator wins.

### Spoiler's strategy

We show by [[induction|0ASJVEFA9J]] on $n$: if $m < k$ and $m < 2^n - 1$, Spoiler wins $G_n(L_m, L_k)$.

1. If Spoiler wins $G_{n-1}$ on the parts below, or above, the first pair, Spoiler wins $G_n$: an answer outside the part breaks the order with the first pair.
2. For $n = 1$, $m = 0$, and Spoiler picks in $L_k$, where Duplicator has no answer.
3. Let $n \ge 2$ and $h = 2^{n-1} - 1$, so $m \le 2h$. If $k \le h$, then $m < h$, and the induction hypothesis applies.
4. If $k > h$, Spoiler picks $h + 1$ in $L_k$, with $h$ points below it. Let Duplicator answer $p$ in $L_m$.
5. If $p - 1 < h$, the parts below have sizes $p - 1 < h$ and $h$, and step 1 applies. If $p - 1 \ge h$, the parts above have sizes $m - p \le h - 1$ and $k - h - 1 > m - p$, and step 1 applies.

## Remarks

### The bound is sharp

$L_{2^n - 2}$ and $L_{2^n - 1}$ differ at rank $n$.
A sentence of rank $n$ that separates them says "there are at least $2^n - 1$ elements"; see [[the counting sentences|~b3bcbe39]].

### Parity

For each $n$, $L_{2^n}$ has even size, $L_{2^n + 1}$ has odd size, and $L_{2^n} \equiv_n L_{2^n + 1}$.
So no first-order sentence in the signature $\set{<}$ holds exactly in the finite linear orders of even size.

### Connectivity

For $m \ge 3$, on $L_m$ define a graph by joining $x$ and $y$ when $y = x + 2$, and also $m - 1$ with $1$ and $m$ with $2$.
This is the graph $x \mapsto x + 2 \bmod m$, which is [[connected|0KWFFMDF8T]] exactly when $m$ is odd.
The edges are first-order definable from $<$.
If a sentence $\sigma$ expressed connectivity of finite graphs, replacing each edge atom in $\sigma$ by its definition would give a sentence about $<$ that expresses odd size.
By the parity result, no such $\sigma$ exists.

### Sums of orders

If Duplicator wins $G_n(\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{A}')$ and $G_n(\mathcal{B}, \mathcal{B}')$, Duplicator wins $G_n$ on the [[ordinal sums|NJY30DQG3H]] $\mathcal{A} + \mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{A}' + \mathcal{B}'$.
Duplicator answers a move in $\mathcal{A}$ inside $\mathcal{A}'$ by the first strategy, and a move in $\mathcal{B}$ inside $\mathcal{B}'$ by the second.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Spoiler can measure lengths in a finite order by halving.
With one round Spoiler can tell nothing about size, with two rounds Spoiler can tell $1$ and $2$ from larger sizes, and with $n$ rounds sizes below $2^n - 1$.
Above that bound, Duplicator can always fake the answer, so no sentence with $n$ nested quantifiers can count higher.
This is why even size and connectivity cannot be said in first-order logic.

### L2 human

We play $G_2(L_3, L_4)$; here $3 \ge 2^2 - 1$, so Duplicator should win.

1. With $2$ rounds left the virtual distances are $4$ and $5$, both at least $4$.
2. Round 1, $r = 1$: Spoiler picks $2 \in L_4$, at distance $2$ from $0$ and $3$ from $5$. Both are at least $2^1$, so by step 4 Duplicator picks $2 \in L_3$.
3. Now the distances are $2, 3$ in $L_4$ and $2, 2$ in $L_3$, equal or both at least $2$.
4. Round 2, $r = 0$: Spoiler picks $1 \in L_3$. Duplicator picks $1 \in L_4$, at distance $1$ above $0$. The order of the pairs agrees, and Duplicator wins.

In $G_3(L_3, L_4)$, Spoiler wins by step 4 with $h = 3$: Spoiler picks $4 \in L_4$, with $3$ points below it, and the part of $L_3$ below any answer has at most $2$ points.
A trap is to think that larger orders always need more rounds; $L_{100}$ and $L_{1000}$ agree on every sentence of rank $6$.
