## Example

Let $P(x)$ be the predicate "$x^2 = x + 2$".
We show that $\exists! x \in \mathbb{N} \; P(x)$ is true, with the definition of the [[uniqueness quantifier|0N3ADS4CHH]].
Here $\mathbb{N}$ is the set of natural numbers, with or without $0$; the result is the same.

1. Factor the equation. $x^2 - x - 2 = (x - 2)(x + 1)$, so $P(x)$ holds exactly when $x = 2$ or $x = -1$. Reason: a product of two numbers is $0$ exactly when one factor is $0$.
2. Existence. $2 \in \mathbb{N}$ and $2^2 = 4 = 2 + 2$, so $P(2)$ is true.
3. Uniqueness. Let $y \in \mathbb{N}$ with $P(y)$. By step 1, $y = 2$ or $y = -1$. Since $-1 \notin \mathbb{N}$, we get $y = 2$.
4. Steps 2 and 3 show $P(2) \land \forall y \in \mathbb{N} \, (P(y) \to y = 2)$. So $\exists! x \in \mathbb{N} \; P(x)$ is true, with the unique element $2$.

## Remarks

The example shows that the truth of $\exists! x \, P(x)$ depends on the domain of discourse.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the same predicate has the truth set $\{-1, 2\}$.
Then existence still holds, but uniqueness fails with $x = -1$ and $y = 2$, so $\exists! x \in \mathbb{Z} \; P(x)$ is false.
Over the set of even integers, the truth set is $\{2\}$, and $\exists!$ is true again.
If we change the equation to $x^2 = x + 6$, then $(x - 3)(x + 2) = 0$, and over $\mathbb{N}$ the unique solution is $3$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

We look for whole numbers $0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ whose square is $2$ more than the number.
For $0$, the square is $0$ and the number plus $2$ is $2$, so they differ.
For $1$, they are $1$ and $3$, so they differ.
For $2$, they are $4$ and $4$, so they agree.
From $3$ on, the square grows faster: $9$ against $5$, $16$ against $6$, and so on.
So $2$ is the only whole number that works.
If negative numbers are allowed, $-1$ also works, since $(-1)^2 = 1 = -1 + 2$.

### L2 human

The typical structure of such a proof has two labelled parts.

1. Existence: show one element of the domain with the property, here $2$.
2. Uniqueness: take an arbitrary element $y$ of the domain with the property, and show that $y$ equals the element of step 1.

The domain enters only in the uniqueness part, where it removes the solution $-1$.
A trap is to forget the domain and to say that the equation has "two solutions" or "one solution" without naming the set.
The statement $\exists! x \, P(x)$ has no truth value until the domain is fixed.
