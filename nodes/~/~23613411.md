## Example

Statement: among the integers $0, 1, \ldots, 6$, exactly one $x$ satisfies $3x \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, namely $x = 5$.
Here $a \equiv b \pmod 7$ means $7 \mid a - b$; see [[congruence modulo n|0WAY5WX8NY]].
We prove existence and then [[uniqueness|~0d8083c0]] in the first form.

1. Existence. $3 \cdot 5 = 15 = 2 \cdot 7 + 1$, so $3 \cdot 5 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$.
2. Uniqueness. Suppose $x$ and $y$ are in $\{0, 1, \ldots, 6\}$ with $3x \equiv 1$ and $3y \equiv 1 \pmod 7$.
3. Subtracting, $7 \mid 3x - 3y = 3(x - y)$.
4. The number $7$ is prime and $7 \nmid 3$. By [[Euclid's lemma|051VYKA1EX]], $7 \mid x - y$.
5. But $-6 \leq x - y \leq 6$, and the only multiple of $7$ in this range is $0$. So $x = y$.
6. Hence $x = 5$ is the only solution in $\{0, 1, \ldots, 6\}$.

In the language of [[modular inverses|0CEC45PRJN]], $5$ is the inverse of $3$ modulo $7$.

## Remarks

A table confirms the result.
The products $3 \cdot 0, 3 \cdot 1, \ldots, 3 \cdot 6$ are $0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18$, with remainders $0, 3, 6, 2, 5, 1, 4$ on division by $7$.
The remainder $1$ appears once, at $x = 5$.
The proof explains why each remainder appears only once, and it works for every modulus that is coprime to $3$.

Uniqueness can fail when the modulus shares a factor with the coefficient.
Modulo $6$, the congruence $2x \equiv 0$ has the two solutions $x = 0$ and $x = 3$ in $\{0, \ldots, 5\}$, and $3x \equiv 1$ has no solution at all.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Count in steps of $3$ on a clock with $7$ hours, numbered $0$ to $6$.
Starting at $0$, the hand visits $0, 3, 6, 2, 5, 1, 4$.
It lands on $1$ after exactly $5$ steps, and no other number of steps from $0$ to $6$ lands there.
The proof shows that two different step counts can never land on the same hour.

### L2 human

We use the result to solve $3x \equiv 4 \pmod 7$.

1. Multiply both sides by $5$: $15x \equiv 20 \pmod 7$.
2. Since $15 \equiv 1$ and $20 \equiv 6 \pmod 7$, this gives $x \equiv 6 \pmod 7$.
3. Check: $3 \cdot 6 = 18 = 2 \cdot 7 + 4$.
4. Uniqueness follows as in the example: two solutions differ by a multiple of $7$.

The typical use: once the inverse of $3$ is known to be unique, it can be called "the" inverse and used to divide by $3$ modulo $7$.

A trap: step 1 multiplies a congruence by $5$, which keeps all solutions only because $5$ itself has an inverse modulo $7$.
Multiplying by $7$ would turn every congruence into $0 \equiv 0$.
