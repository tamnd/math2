## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $p(x) = x^2 + 1$, so $p(A) = A^2 + I$.

1. $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. $p(A) = A^2 + I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. $A \, p(A) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 5 \\ 0 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 8 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. $p(A) \, A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 0 & 2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 2 \\ 5 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 1 + 5 \cdot 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 8 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$.
5. The two products agree in all four entries, so $A$ and $p(A)$ [[commute|MX26N71ZD3]].

## Remarks

The same holds for every polynomial $p$ and every square matrix $A$.
The reason is $A \cdot A^j = A^{j + 1} = A^j \cdot A$, and the distributive law extends this to sums of powers.
So the polynomials in $A$ form a commutative subring of the matrix ring.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A matrix and its own powers can be multiplied in either order.
So a matrix commutes with every sum of its powers.

### L2 human

Here $p(A)$ is not a multiple of $I$, so the commuting is not trivial.
The matrix $A$ has two different eigenvalues, so every matrix that commutes with $A$ is a polynomial in $A$.
