## Example

Let $S(x) = 2x$ and $T(x) = 3x$ on $\R$.
Both are [[linear maps|0RJF668P1T]].
Let $P$ be the pointwise product, $P(x) = S(x) \cdot T(x)$.

1. $P(x) = 2x \cdot 3x = 6x^2$.
2. The [[composite|05YBKMRVAR]] is $(T \circ S)(x) = T(2x) = 6x$.
3. At $x = 1$, $P(1) = 6$ and $(T \circ S)(1) = 6$. At $x = 2$, $P(2) = 24$ but $(T \circ S)(2) = 12$. So $P \ne T \circ S$.
4. $P$ is not linear: $P(1 + 1) = 24$, but $P(1) + P(1) = 12$.

So the pointwise product is not the composite, and it is not even a linear map.

## Remarks

- The pointwise product needs a multiplication in the target. A general vector space has no product of two vectors, so the pointwise product is not defined there.
- The pointwise sum $S + T$ is linear, and it gives the vector space structure on the [[space of linear maps|0X9MBPP2Z3]].
- For maps $\R \to \R$, the composite of $x \mapsto ax$ and $x \mapsto bx$ is $x \mapsto abx$, which is linear.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Multiplying the outputs of two linear maps is not the same as doing one map after the other.
Here the product gives $6x^2$, a parabola, while the composite gives $6x$, a line.

### L2 human

The product of linear maps in the algebra $\End(V)$ is composition, not the pointwise product.
For matrices this is why the matrix product is not the entrywise product.
The trap is to write $TS$ and read it as a pointwise product.
