## Summary

Hilbert's epsilon calculus extends [[first-order logic|086QKPF0A6]] by a term-forming operator $\varepsilon$.
For a formula $A(x)$, the term $\varepsilon x \, A(x)$ denotes "some $x$ with $A(x)$, if there is one".
Its one axiom is the critical formula $A(t) \to A(\varepsilon x \, A(x))$, and the quantifiers become definable: $\exists x \, A(x)$ is $A(\varepsilon x \, A(x))$.
Hilbert and his school used it for consistency proofs, and the two epsilon theorems show that $\varepsilon$-terms add nothing new to first-order logic.

## Motivation

In Hilbert's program, consistency proofs had to treat quantifiers over infinite domains by finite means.
The epsilon operator replaces each quantifier by a term, so a proof with quantifiers becomes a proof with terms and quantifier-free formulas.
A consistency proof then has to find numerical values for finitely many $\varepsilon$-terms, which is a finite search.
The operator also makes explicit the witnesses that $\exists$ only asserts, like a Skolem function that is built into the syntax.

## Notation

1. The term $\varepsilon x \, A$ binds $x$ in $A$, like a quantifier. Its free variables are those of $A$ other than $x$.
2. Bourbaki writes $\tau_x(A)$ for $\varepsilon x \, A$, and draws links from $\tau$ to the occurrences of $x$ instead of writing $x$.
3. The critical formula of $A$ and $t$ is $A(t) \to A(\varepsilon x \, A)$, with $t$ substitutable for $x$ in $A$.
4. In Isabelle/HOL the operator is written $\mathrm{SOME}\ x.\ A\, x$, and in Lean it is `Classical.epsilon`.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[Quantifiers as epsilon terms|~832d6adb]]: the sentence $\exists x \, \forall y \, R(x, y)$ becomes a quantifier-free formula with nested $\varepsilon$-terms.
2. [[An epsilon term in the natural numbers|~a6148471]]: with the choice "least element", $\varepsilon x \, (3 < x)$ denotes $4$.
3. A Skolem function is an $\varepsilon$-term: $\forall x \, \exists y \, R(x, y)$ is equivalent to $\forall x \, R(x, \varepsilon y \, R(x, y))$.

### Non-examples

1. [[An epsilon term is not a first-order term|~74071687]]: the term rules of first-order logic have no binding operator, so $\varepsilon x \, (3 < x)$ is not a first-order term.
2. The description $\iota x \, A$ of a [[definite description|PD2R4XT79Q]] is not an $\varepsilon$-term. It needs exactly one $x$ with $A$, while $\varepsilon x \, A$ denotes an object even when there are several or none.
3. A new constant $c$ with the axiom $A(c)$ is not an $\varepsilon$-term. It is tied to one formula, while $\varepsilon$ applies to all formulas uniformly.

### Basic properties

1. Quantifiers are definable: $\exists x \, A \leftrightarrow A(\varepsilon x \, A)$ and $\forall x \, A \leftrightarrow A(\varepsilon x \, \neg A)$ are provable from the critical formulas.
2. Semantics: in a structure $\mathcal{M}$ fix a [[choice function|0FWBQ53MPJ]] $\Phi$ on the nonempty subsets of $M$, with a default value for $\emptyset$. Then $\varepsilon x \, A$ denotes $\Phi$ of the set of $a \in M$ that satisfy $A$.
3. The first epsilon theorem: an $\varepsilon$-free, quantifier-free formula derivable in the $\varepsilon$-calculus from quantifier-free axioms is derivable from them without quantifiers or $\varepsilon$.
4. The second epsilon theorem: the $\varepsilon$-calculus is conservative over first-order logic. An $\varepsilon$-free formula derivable with $\varepsilon$ is derivable in first-order logic.
5. The extensional calculus adds $\forall x \, (A \leftrightarrow B) \to \varepsilon x \, A = \varepsilon x \, B$. The semantics of property 2 satisfies it.

### History

Hilbert introduced the epsilon operator in the early 1920s, as a tool of his program for the foundations of mathematics.
Ackermann's dissertation of 1924 used the $\varepsilon$-substitution method for a consistency proof of arithmetic, which turned out to cover only a fragment.
Hilbert and Bernays proved the two epsilon theorems in the second volume of their Grundlagen der Mathematik, in 1939.
Ackermann completed the substitution method for arithmetic in 1940, with induction up to $\varepsilon_0$, after Gentzen's work.

### Common errors

1. A student reads $\varepsilon x \, A$ as "the $x$ with $A$". It is "some $x$ with $A$", and it denotes an object also when no $x$ satisfies $A$.
2. A student assumes $\varepsilon x \, A = \varepsilon x \, B$ for equivalent $A$ and $B$. This needs the extensionality axiom.
3. A student substitutes into $\varepsilon x \, A$ without regard to binding. The variable $x$ is bound in it, so the rules of [[substitution|NCDZ969V7X]] for quantifiers apply.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The symbol $\varepsilon x \, A(x)$ is a name for "an object with property $A$, if there is one".
For example, $\varepsilon x \, (x \text{ is a prime number})$ names some prime number, without saying which.
With this name, "there is a prime" can be written as "$\varepsilon x \, (x \text{ is prime})$ is prime".
The words "there is" disappear, and only a name remains.

### L2 human

We derive $\exists x \, A \to A(\varepsilon x \, A)$ from the critical formula, in a calculus with quantifiers.

1. The critical formula gives $A(y) \to A(\varepsilon x \, A)$ for a variable $y$ not occurring in $A$.
2. The variable $y$ is not free in $A(\varepsilon x \, A)$. So the rule for $\exists$ on the left of $\to$ gives $\exists y \, A(y) \to A(\varepsilon x \, A)$.
3. By renaming the bound variable, $\exists y \, A(y)$ is $\exists x \, A(x)$.
4. The converse $A(\varepsilon x \, A) \to \exists x \, A$ is an instance of the axiom $A(t) \to \exists x \, A$ with $t = \varepsilon x \, A$.

The typical use is to drop $\exists$ altogether and to take $A(\varepsilon x \, A)$ as its definition.
A trap: the critical formula is not "$A(\varepsilon x \, A)$" alone.
That would claim that some $x$ satisfies $A$ for every $A$, including $x \ne x$.

### L3 human

The epsilon calculus is a first-order logic with a global choice operator built into its terms.
Its two epsilon theorems are conservation results, proved by eliminating the critical formulas one $\varepsilon$-term at a time.
The extended first epsilon theorem yields Herbrand's theorem, with $\varepsilon$-terms in place of Skolem functions.
The $\varepsilon$-substitution method assigns values to $\varepsilon$-terms by a convergent trial and error process, and its termination proof for arithmetic uses induction up to $\varepsilon_0$.
In set theory, $\varepsilon$ gives global choice: $\varepsilon x \, (x \in S)$ picks an element of each nonempty set $S$.
Bourbaki's $\tau$ and the choice operators of higher-order provers descend from it.
