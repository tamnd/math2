## Example

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product, let $\theta = \pi/6$, so $\cos\theta = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ and $\sin\theta = \frac12$.
Let $e_1 = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = (\frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \frac12)$ and $e_2 = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta) = (-\frac12, \frac{\sqrt3}{2})$.

1. $\norm{e_1}^2 = \frac34 + \frac14 = 1$ and $\norm{e_2}^2 = \frac14 + \frac34 = 1$.
2. $e_1 \cdot e_2 = -\frac{\sqrt3}{4} + \frac{\sqrt3}{4} = 0$.
3. So $(e_1, e_2)$ is an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of $\mathbb{R}^2$, since it has $2$ vectors.
4. Let $v = (2, 0)$. Its coordinates are $v \cdot e_1 = \sqrt3$ and $v \cdot e_2 = -1$.
5. Check: $\sqrt3\, e_1 - e_2 = (\frac32 + \frac12, \frac{\sqrt3}{2} - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}) = (2, 0)$.
6. Check the norm: $(\sqrt3)^2 + (-1)^2 = 4 = \norm{v}^2$.

## Remarks

The same computation works for every $\theta$, since $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$.
The matrix $Q = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ has columns $e_1$ and $e_2$, and it is an [[orthogonal matrix|0W02QESPJQ]].
The coordinate vector of $v$ is $Q^\top v$: here $Q^\top (2, 0)^\top = (\sqrt3, -1)^\top$.
So the inverse of $Q$ is its transpose, and no linear system is needed.
In the new axes, $v$ has a negative second coordinate, because $e_2$ points to the upper left and $v$ points to the right.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Turn the usual axes of the plane by $30$ degrees.
The new axes still have length $1$ and meet at a right angle.
The vector $(2, 0)$ goes $\sqrt3$ along the first new axis and $-1$ along the second one.

### L2 human

Worked example: the coordinates of $w = (0, 1)$ in the same basis.

1. $w \cdot e_1 = \frac12$ and $w \cdot e_2 = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$.
2. Check: $\frac12 e_1 + \frac{\sqrt3}{2} e_2 = (\frac{\sqrt3}{4} - \frac{\sqrt3}{4}, \frac14 + \frac34) = (0, 1)$.
3. Check the norm: $\frac14 + \frac34 = 1$.

The typical use is to change axes in mechanics or graphics.
A trap: the coordinates use $Q^\top$, not $Q$.
With $Q$ the vector turns the wrong way.
