## Example

Problem: let $T(n) = n/2$ for even $n$ and $T(n) = 3n + 1$ for odd $n$.
Prove that for every positive integer $n$, repeated application of $T$ reaches $1$.
This is the [[Collatz conjecture|6PCQEXEX4N]].
We follow [[Pólya's four steps|~e58f9baf]] and see where they stop.

1. Phase 1 works. The hypothesis is "$n$ is a positive integer", and the conclusion is "$T^k(n) = 1$ for some $k \ge 0$". The statement is precise.
2. Specialize. From $6$ we get $6, 3, 10, 5, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1$, so $8$ steps. From $7$ we need $16$ steps.
3. Specialize further. From $27$ the sequence takes $111$ steps and rises to $9232$ before it falls to $1$. The special cases show no pattern that a proof could follow.
4. Look for a related problem. For the map with $3n - 1$ in place of $3n + 1$, the start $5$ gives $5, 14, 7, 20, 10, 5$, a cycle that never reaches $1$. So the analogous statement is false, and any proof must use the exact number $+1$.
5. Generalize. John Conway showed in 1972 that a general class of maps of this kind has an undecidable behaviour, so the most general version cannot be settled by any algorithm.
6. Try an auxiliary problem. The weaker claim "almost every orbit gets small" has partial answers, but they do not give the full statement.

Each question of phase 2 was asked, and each answer was informative.
None of them gave a plan for a proof.
The four steps did their job of organizing the search, and the search still failed.

## Remarks

The non-example shows the limit of a [[heuristic|~fd7a5df5]] plan.
The four steps say what to ask, not what the answer is, and for an open problem the questions may have no useful answer.
This is the reason why heuristics must not be presented as algorithms.

The phase that still works is phase 4 on the partial results.
For example, the false variant in step 4 is a useful warning: an argument that does not use the difference between $3n + 1$ and $3n - 1$ cannot prove the conjecture.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take a number, halve it if it is even, and triple it and add one if it is odd.
Repeat, and the numbers always seem to come down to $1$.
From $27$ they climb to $9232$ first, and need $111$ steps.
Nobody has proved that every starting number comes down to $1$.
Good habits for solving problems help us explore this question, but they do not solve it.

### L2 human

We check the claim of step 3 and draw the lesson.

1. Compute by hand the start $7$: $7, 22, 11, 34, 17, 52, 26, 13, 40, 20, 10, 5, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1$.
2. Count the steps: there are $16$ steps after the start.
3. The largest value is $52$, more than seven times the start.
4. Such growth before the fall is typical, so no simple bound on the values follows from the first steps.

The typical use of the four steps here: they produce data, related problems and warnings, which are the start of research.

A trap: to conclude from many checked cases that the statement is true; checking cases is phase 4 of a solution, not a proof.
