## Example

Statement: the equation $x^5 - x - 1 = 0$ has a real solution between $1$ and $2$.
We give a [[non-constructive existence proof|~796d0c7e]].

1. Let $f(x) = x^5 - x - 1$. As a polynomial, $f$ is [[continuous|0X3WGX95FE]] on $[1, 2]$.
2. $f(1) = 1 - 1 - 1 = -1 < 0$.
3. $f(2) = 32 - 2 - 1 = 29 > 0$.
4. By the [[intermediate value theorem|00KAFBR32E]], there is a $c$ with $1 < c < 2$ and $f(c) = 0$.

The proof does not give $c$.
It shows only that $c$ exists in the interval $(1, 2)$.

## Remarks

The root cannot be found by a formula of the usual kind.
By the [[Abel-Ruffini theorem|163A2WQQJX]] and Galois theory, the roots of $x^5 - x - 1$ cannot be written with $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$ and $n$-th roots starting from integers.
So a proof of existence that does not name the root is the natural one here.

The proof can be made more informative by bisection.
$f(1.5) \approx 5.09 > 0$, so the root lies in $(1, 1.5)$.
$f(1.25) \approx 0.80 > 0$, so it lies in $(1, 1.25)$.
$f(1.125) \approx -0.32 < 0$, so it lies in $(1.125, 1.25)$.
Each step halves the interval, and the root is $c \approx 1.1673$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Draw the graph of $y = x^5 - x - 1$ without lifting the pencil.
At $x = 1$ the graph is below the $x$-axis, at height $-1$.
At $x = 2$ it is above the axis, at height $29$.
A curve drawn without lifting the pencil must cross the axis somewhere in between.
The crossing point is a solution, even though we do not know its exact value.

### L2 human

We use the same method to show that $\cos x = x$ has a solution.

1. Let $g(x) = \cos x - x$. It is continuous on $[0, 1]$.
2. $g(0) = 1 > 0$.
3. $g(1) = \cos 1 - 1 < 0$, since $\cos 1 < 1$.
4. By the intermediate value theorem, $g(c) = 0$ for some $c$ in $(0, 1)$, that is, $\cos c = c$.

The typical use: move everything to one side, and find two points where the sign is different.

A trap: the theorem needs continuity on the whole interval.
For $h(x) = \frac{1}{x}$, $h(-1) = -1 < 0$ and $h(1) = 1 > 0$, but $h$ has no zero; it is not defined at $0$.
