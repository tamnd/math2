## Example

The Kreisel-Putnam rule is $\neg p \to q \vee r \;/\; (\neg p \to q) \vee (\neg p \to r)$.
It separates admissibility from derivability in $\mathsf{IPC}$; see [[admissible rules|~41ee4c18]].

1. Admissible: let $\sigma$ be a substitution with $\vdash \neg \sigma p \to \sigma q \vee \sigma r$.
2. By [[the slash argument|~4bd1c685]], $\vdash \neg \sigma p \to \sigma q$ or $\vdash \neg \sigma p \to \sigma r$. So the conclusion under $\sigma$ is a theorem.
3. Not derivable: we refute $(\neg p \to q \vee r) \to (\neg p \to q) \vee (\neg p \to r)$ in a [[Kripke model|~11b532e9]].
4. Take a root $w$ below three maximal worlds $u_1$, $u_2$, $u_3$. Let $q$ hold only at $u_1$, $r$ only at $u_2$, and $p$ only at $u_3$.
5. The worlds that force $\neg p$ are $u_1$ and $u_2$. The root does not, since $u_3 \Vdash p$.
6. $u_1 \Vdash q$ and $u_2 \Vdash r$, so $w \Vdash \neg p \to q \vee r$.
7. $u_2 \Vdash \neg p$ and $u_2 \nVdash q$, so $w \nVdash \neg p \to q$. In the same way, $u_1$ shows $w \nVdash \neg p \to r$.
8. So $w$ forces the premise and not the conclusion. By soundness in [[Kripke completeness|~ac029000]], the implication is not a theorem, and the rule is not derivable.

## Remarks

Three maximal worlds are needed.
Without the $p$-world $u_3$, the root forces $\neg p$, and then it must force $q \vee r$, which makes one of the conclusion's disjuncts hold.
Adding the implication of step 3 as an axiom scheme gives the Kreisel-Putnam logic $\mathsf{KP}$.
So $\mathsf{IPC}$ is not structurally complete.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The rule says: if it is provable that "not $p$" gives $q$ or $r$, then it is provable which one it gives.
This is true for theorems, by a proof-theoretic argument.
It is not true as an implication inside the logic, because a state of knowledge may leave the choice open.

### L2 human

1. For admissibility, use the slash, or glue countermodels under a new root.
2. For non-derivability, build a countermodel to the implication with three maximal worlds.
3. Read off: $q$ and $r$ each fail at some world where $\neg p$ holds.

The typical use is as the standard example of an admissible, non-derivable rule.
A trap: a countermodel to the rule's implication says nothing about admissibility.
