## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Both are symmetric, and $B$ is [[positive definite|0QQCG3JWJ4]], since its leading minors are $2$ and $1$.

1. $\det(A - \lambda B) = (3 - 2\lambda)(1 - \lambda) - (1 - \lambda)^2 = (1 - \lambda)(2 - \lambda)$. So $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$.
2. For $\lambda_1 = 1$: $A - B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, with null vector $p_1 = (0, 1)$.
3. For $\lambda_2 = 2$: $A - 2B = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, with null vector $p_2 = (1, -1)$.
4. Scaling: $B p_1 = (1, 1)$ and $p_1^T B p_1 = 1$; $B p_2 = (1, 0)$ and $p_2^T B p_2 = 1$. So no scaling is needed.
5. $B$-orthogonality: $p_1^T B p_2 = (0, 1) \cdot (1, 0) = 0$.
6. Let $P = (p_1\ p_2) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$. Then $B P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $P^T B P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
7. $A P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $P^T A P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
8. Check with $B^{-1} A$: $B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B^{-1} A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. This matrix is triangular with eigenvalues $2$ and $1$.

## Remarks

- $B^{-1} A$ is not symmetric, but its eigenvalues are real, as the theorem says.
- The vectors $p_1 = (0, 1)$ and $p_2 = (1, -1)$ are not orthogonal in the standard sense, since $p_1 \cdot p_2 = -1$. They are orthogonal for the inner product given by $B$.
- Both $\lambda_i$ are positive, so $A$ is positive definite by part 4. This agrees with its leading minors $3$ and $2$.
- In the coordinates $x = P y$, the two forms are $x^T B x = y_1^2 + y_2^2$ and $x^T A x = y_1^2 + 2 y_2^2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

We have two quadratic forms, $3x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + x_2^2$ and $2x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + x_2^2$.
With the new coordinates $x = Py$, they become $y_1^2 + 2y_2^2$ and $y_1^2 + y_2^2$.
So both forms are sums of squares at the same time.

### L2 human

The recipe is: solve $\det(A - \lambda B) = 0$, find null vectors, and scale them with $B$.
Steps 6 and 7 check the two congruences by multiplication.
A trap: the ordinary eigenvalues of $A$ are $2 \pm \sqrt2$, which are not the generalized eigenvalues $1$ and $2$.
