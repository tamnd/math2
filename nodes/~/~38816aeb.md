## Summary

A conditional statement is a [[statement|~50156c16]] of the form "if $P$, then $Q$", where $P$ and $Q$ are statements.
The statement $P$ is the hypothesis, and $Q$ is the conclusion.
In mathematics, "if $P$, then $Q$" is false when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false, and it is true in the three other cases.
The same conditional has many English forms: "$P$ implies $Q$", "$Q$ if $P$", "$P$ only if $Q$", "$Q$ whenever $P$", "$P$ is sufficient for $Q$", "$Q$ is necessary for $P$" and "$Q$ provided that $P$".
The biconditional "$P$ if and only if $Q$", often written "$P$ iff $Q$", means "if $P$ then $Q$, and if $Q$ then $P$".
Most conditionals in mathematics contain a hidden quantifier: "if $n$ is even, then $n^2$ is even" means "for every integer $n$, if $n$ is even, then $n^2$ is even".
Most theorems are conditionals, so a reader must find the hypothesis and the conclusion of each one before reading its proof.

## Motivation

A theorem usually says that some conditions force a result.
"If a triangle has a right angle, then the square of the longest side is the sum of the squares of the other two sides" is an example.
In daily speech, "if" also carries ideas of cause and of time, as in "if you drop the glass, it will break".
Mathematics keeps only the part that a proof can check: whenever the hypothesis is true, the conclusion is true.

This choice explains the truth values of a conditional with a false hypothesis.
The theorem "for every integer $n$, if $4$ divides $n$, then $n$ is even" is true.
For it to be true, each of its instances must be true, including $n = 6$ (false hypothesis, true conclusion) and $n = 7$ (false hypothesis, false conclusion).
So a conditional with a false hypothesis counts as true.
The only instance that could make the theorem false is a number $n$ that is divisible by $4$ and odd, and there is none.

## Notation

The conditional "if $P$, then $Q$" is written $P \Rightarrow Q$, $P \to Q$ or, in older books, $P \supset Q$.
The biconditional is written $P \Leftrightarrow Q$, $P \leftrightarrow Q$ or $P \equiv Q$, and in words "iff".
The hypothesis is also called the antecedent or the premise, and the conclusion is also called the consequent.
The arrow $\to$ also denotes functions, as in $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, and the horseshoe $\supset$ also denotes "is a superset of".
For this reason many authors of proofs use $\Rightarrow$ and $\Leftrightarrow$ between statements.
A chain such as "$x = 2 \Rightarrow x^2 = 4$" is a conditional, not a calculation, and the arrow must not join numbers or expressions that are not statements.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The conditional "if $4$ divides $n$, then $n$ is even" in its English forms|~56745f0a]]: seven English sentences with one meaning.
2. [[A conditional with a hidden quantifier: "if $x > 2$, then $x^2 > 4$"|~bfb3d23a]]: the variable $x$ is bound by an unwritten "for every real $x$".
3. "Every square is a rectangle" has no word "if", but it is a conditional: for every quadrilateral $Q$, if $Q$ is a square, then $Q$ is a rectangle. The form "every $A$ is a $B$" always hides a conditional.
4. "An integer $n$ is even if and only if $n^2$ is even" is a true biconditional. If $n = 2k$, then $n^2 = 2(2k^2)$ is even. If $n = 2k + 1$, then $n^2 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$ is odd, so "$n^2$ even" forces "$n$ even".
5. "For a real number $x$, $x > 2$ only if $x > 1$" is true, because it means "if $x > 2$, then $x > 1$". The statement "$x > 1$ only if $x > 2$" is false: $x = 1.5$ satisfies $x > 1$ but not $x > 2$.

### Non-examples

1. [[The "if" of a definition is not a one-way conditional|~e6d228b2]]: in "an integer is even if it is divisible by $2$", the word "if" means "if and only if".
2. "Since $n$ is even, $n^2$ is even" is not a conditional. The word "since" claims that $n$ is even, and it gives a reason for the conclusion. A conditional makes no claim about its hypothesis.
3. "If a check is wanted, put $n = 3$" is not a conditional statement. Its main clause is a command, so it has no truth value.

### Basic properties

1. "If $P$, then $Q$" is false exactly when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false. In logic, this is the [[implication|0NMG7W7Q11]], also called the material conditional.
2. A conditional with a false hypothesis is true. This is [[vacuous truth|0V4QXPGJQK]].
3. "If $P$, then $Q$" has the same truth value as its [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] "if not $Q$, then not $P$". This is the basis of [[proof by contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]].
4. The [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] "if $Q$, then $P$" and the [[inverse|VB7B8766D2]] "if not $P$, then not $Q$" can have a different truth value. For example, the converse of "if $4$ divides $n$, then $n$ is even" is "if $n$ is even, then $4$ divides $n$", and it is false for $n = 2$.
5. "$P$ only if $Q$" and "$Q$ is necessary for $P$" both mean "if $P$, then $Q$". See [[necessary and sufficient conditions|09BJQQB3VS]].
6. "$P$ if and only if $Q$" is true exactly when $P$ and $Q$ have the same truth value. A proof of it proves two conditionals, as on the page [[biconditional|0S6GMNJ62Y]].
7. The negation of "if $P$, then $Q$" is "$P$ and not $Q$". See [[negating mathematical statements|~f72ba5a2]].

### History

Philo the Dialectician, a Greek logician of about 300 BC, held that a conditional is false only when its antecedent is true and its consequent is false.
This is the [[Philonian conditional|V4HMDD1R0D]], and it is the conditional of modern mathematics.
Gottlob Frege took this conditional as a basic connective of his Begriffsschrift of 1879.
Paul Halmos wrote that he introduced the abbreviation "iff" for "if and only if".

### Common errors

1. A student proves the converse instead of the statement. To prove "if $P$, then $Q$", we assume $P$ and reach $Q$, not the other way.
2. A student reads "$P$ only if $Q$" as "if $Q$, then $P$". The phrase "only if" introduces the conclusion: "$P$ only if $Q$" means "if $P$, then $Q$".
3. A student thinks that a conditional with a false hypothesis is false or meaningless. "If $1 = 2$, then $5$ is even" is true, because its hypothesis is false.
4. A student writes "$x^2 = 4 \Rightarrow x = 2$". For a real $x$ this conditional is false, because $x = -2$ satisfies the hypothesis and not the conclusion.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A conditional is a promise: "if it rains, I will take an umbrella".
The promise is broken only when it rains and I do not take an umbrella.
If it does not rain, the promise is not broken, whatever I do.
In mathematics, "if a number ends in $0$, then it is even" works the same way.
For $20$ the promise is kept: $20$ ends in $0$ and is even.
For $14$ the promise is not tested, because $14$ does not end in $0$, so it is not broken.
No number breaks the promise, so the statement is true.

### L2 human

We analyse the statement "a function $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous whenever it is differentiable".

1. Find the parts. "$Q$ whenever $P$" means "if $P$, then $Q$". So $P$ is "$f$ is differentiable", and $Q$ is "$f$ is continuous".
2. Find the hidden quantifier. The statement is about every function $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, so it reads "for every $f$, if $f$ is differentiable, then $f$ is continuous". This is the theorem that [[differentiable functions are continuous|07ZMX4ANJS]].
3. Write the other forms. "$f$ is differentiable only if $f$ is continuous." "Continuity is necessary for differentiability." "Differentiability is sufficient for continuity."
4. Write the contrapositive: "if $f$ is not continuous, then $f$ is not differentiable". It is true, because the statement is true.
5. Write the converse: "if $f$ is continuous, then $f$ is differentiable". It is false: $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous but not differentiable at $0$.

The typical use of this analysis is at the start of a proof: the hypothesis gives what we may assume, and the conclusion gives what we must reach.

A trap: in step 3, "differentiable only if continuous" sounds to many readers like "continuous implies differentiable".
It means the opposite direction.
A safe test is to rewrite each "only if" as "if ... then" before using it.

### L3 human

The conditional of mathematics is the material conditional of classical logic.
It ignores relevance, so "if $2 + 2 = 4$, then $\pi$ is irrational" is true, although the parts have nothing to do with each other.
Such cases are the [[paradoxes of material implication|3T7ZJFC9QF]], and relevance logics and strict implication try to avoid them.
In practice, a mathematical conditional almost always carries a universal quantifier, and "for all $x$, $P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x)$" says that the set $\{x : P(x)\}$ is a [[subset|0BJR9QR08E]] of the set $\{x : Q(x)\}$.

In a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]], the conditional $P \Rightarrow Q$ is $\neg P \lor Q$, and it equals the top element exactly when $P \leq Q$.
The [[deduction theorem|9X08XK5RV2]] says that $Q$ follows from the assumption $P$ exactly when $P \Rightarrow Q$ can be proved, which is why "assume $P$, then derive $Q$" is a proof of a conditional.
In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]] and in type theory, a proof of $P \Rightarrow Q$ is a method that turns each proof of $P$ into a proof of $Q$, and the conditional is a function type.
The proof methods for conditionals are in the topic [[proof methods|G9JFJGY4TQ]].
