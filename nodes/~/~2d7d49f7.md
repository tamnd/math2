## Statement

We use Buss's theories $S^i_2$ and $T^i_2$ of [[bounded arithmetic|~55a002f3]], and $\square^p_{i+1} = \mathrm{FP}^{\Sigma^p_i}$.
For $i = 0$ we read $\mathsf{PV}_1$ in place of $T^0_2$, as in [[Cook's theory PV|~98af8889]], and $\square^p_1 = \mathrm{FP}$.

**Theorem.** Let $i \ge 0$.

1. (KPT witnessing.) Let $B(x, y, z)$ be a $\Sigma^b_{i+1}$ formula with the free variables shown, and assume $T^i_2 \vdash \forall x \, \exists y \, \forall z \, B(x, y, z)$. Then there are $k \ge 1$ and functions $f_1, \dots, f_k$ with the following properties.
   - Each $f_m$ has $m$ arguments and is $\Sigma^b_{i+1}$-definable in $T^i_2$, so $f_m \in \square^p_{i+1}$.
   - $T^i_2$ proves
   $$B(a, f_1(a), b_1) \lor B(a, f_2(a, b_1), b_2) \lor \cdots \lor B(a, f_k(a, b_1, \dots, b_{k-1}), b_k).$$
2. (Collapse.) If $T^i_2 = S^{i+1}_2$, then $\Sigma^p_{i+1} \subseteq \Pi^p_{i+1}/\mathrm{poly}$, and the [[polynomial hierarchy|TDQ7AXGM6P]] collapses.

The disjunction in part 1 has a game reading.
A student proposes $y_1 = f_1(a)$; a teacher answers with a counterexample $b_1$, so that $\neg B(a, y_1, b_1)$.
The student proposes $y_2 = f_2(a, b_1)$, and so on.
Part 1 says that the student wins in at most $k$ rounds against every teacher, with a $\square^p_{i+1}$ strategy.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The theory must be $T^i_2$, not $S^{i+1}_2$. $S^{i+1}_2$ proves a length-maximization sentence of the required form. If part 1 held for $S^{i+1}_2$, the proof of part 2 would give $\Sigma^p_{i+1} \subseteq \Pi^p_{i+1}/\mathrm{poly}$ with no hypothesis.
- The formula $B$ must be $\Sigma^b_{i+1}$. The proof uses that $B$ is existential in the universal theory $T^i_2(\square^p_{i+1})$, so its negation passes to substructures. This step fails for formulas with more alternations.
- The student needs the teacher's answers. In the L2 example below, a strategy with one round would find satisfying assignments in polynomial time.

### Consequences

- Part 2 ties the [[separation problem|~4ddef1ef]] for bounded arithmetic to complexity theory. A proof that the polynomial hierarchy is infinite would separate $T^i_2$ from $S^{i+1}_2$ for every $i$.
- Buss and Zambella showed that $T^i_2$ proves the collapse itself: every $\Sigma^p_{i+3}$ predicate is a Boolean combination of $\Sigma^p_{i+2}$ predicates, and lies in $\Sigma^p_{i+1}/\mathrm{poly}$.
- Buss showed that $S_2$ is finitely axiomatizable if and only if $S_2$ proves that the polynomial hierarchy collapses.
- Krajíček, Pudlák and Takeuti proved that the relativized theory $S_2(\alpha)$ is not finitely axiomatizable.

### Converse

The conclusion of part 1 implies its hypothesis, by pure logic.
Assume $\forall y \, \exists z \, \neg B(a, y, z)$.
Then choose $b_1$ with $\neg B(a, f_1(a), b_1)$, then $b_2$ with $\neg B(a, f_2(a, b_1), b_2)$, and so on up to $b_k$.
This contradicts the disjunction, so $\exists y \, \forall z \, B(a, y, z)$.
For part 2 no converse is known: a collapse of the polynomial hierarchy is not known to collapse the hierarchy of theories.

### Generalizations

- Krajíček, Pudlák and Takeuti obtained the stronger inclusion $\Sigma^p_{i+1} \subseteq \Delta^p_{i+1}/\mathrm{poly}$. A [[Karp-Lipton|HM7DNQ2H5D]] argument then collapses the hierarchy to $\Sigma^p_{i+2}$. Jeřábek showed in 2009 that $T^i_2$ proves this stronger form.
- The proof of part 1 is an application of [[Herbrand's theorem|VZ21F10WRS]], and it works for every universal theory and every existential matrix $B$.
- Pudlák gave a generalized student-teacher game for prenex formulas of any complexity in universal theories. Buss, Kołodziejczyk and Thapen used a simplified version of it.

## History

Krajíček, Pudlák and Takeuti proved the witnessing theorem and the collapse in 1991.
Their paper introduced the student-teacher reading of the Herbrand disjunction.
Buss, in a paper of 1995, and Zambella, in 1996, independently showed that the collapse is provable in $T^i_2$, and related it to the finite axiomatizability of $S_2$.
Jeřábek formalized the stronger collapse in $T^i_2$, using approximate counting.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose a theory proves: "for every $x$ there is a $y$ that beats every $z$".
A fast program cannot always find such a $y$ directly.
The theorem says that a fast program can find it if it may make a few wrong guesses.
After each wrong guess, a teacher shows a $z$ that refutes it, and the program learns from that.
The number of guesses is a fixed number, such as $3$, for all inputs $x$.

### L2 human

We play the game for a sentence with an $\mathrm{NP}$ flavour.
Let $R(a, w)$ say: $w \le a$, and $w$ is a satisfying assignment of the propositional formula coded by $a$.

1. Let $B(a, y, z)$ be $(y \ge 1 \land R(a, y - 1)) \lor (y = 0 \land \neg R(a, z))$. It is open in $\mathsf{PV}_1$.
2. $\mathsf{PV}_1 \vdash \forall a \, \exists y \, \forall z \, B(a, y, z)$: if some $w$ satisfies $R(a, w)$, take $y = w + 1$; otherwise take $y = 0$.
3. Round 1: the student plays $f_1(a) = 0$, the claim that $a$ is unsatisfiable.
4. A counterexample $b_1$ makes $B(a, 0, b_1)$ false, so $R(a, b_1)$ holds: the teacher has shown a satisfying assignment.
5. Round 2: the student plays $f_2(a, b_1) = b_1 + 1$. Then $B(a, b_1 + 1, b_2)$ holds for every $b_2$.
6. So $k = 2$ rounds suffice, and the disjunction $\neg R(a, b_1) \lor R(a, b_1)$ is provable.
7. One round would need a polynomial-time $f_1$ with $\forall z \, B(a, f_1(a), z)$. It would find satisfying assignments, so $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$.

The typical use of the theorem is to show that a theory does not prove a $\forall \exists \forall$ sentence: one shows that no student wins in a fixed number of rounds.
A trap is to forget that the student strategy may use the teacher's earlier answers; step 7 shows how much they help.

### L3 human

The theorem is a bounded form of Kreisel's no-counterexample interpretation, for one alternation and a universal theory.
It is a standard tool for unprovability results in $\mathsf{PV}_1$ and $T^i_2$.
To show that a $\forall \exists \forall$ sentence is unprovable, one shows that no $\square^p_{i+1}$ student wins in a constant number of rounds.

Part 2 shows that the question of [[separating the theories|~4ddef1ef]] is not independent of complexity theory.
A collapse $T^i_2 = S^{i+1}_2$ would give polynomial-size advice for $\Sigma^p_{i+1}$ sets, which most researchers consider unlikely.
So the hierarchy of theories is expected to be strict, but no separation is known.
