## Example

We put $\varphi = (p \to q) \to r$ into [[conjunctive normal form|07C4A8Y18K]] with the laws of logic.
Each step replaces a part of the formula by a [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]] part.

1. Remove the outer implication with $\alpha \to \beta \equiv \neg\alpha \lor \beta$: $\varphi \equiv \neg(p \to q) \lor r$.
2. Remove the inner implication in the same way: $\varphi \equiv \neg(\neg p \lor q) \lor r$.
3. Apply [[De Morgan's law|09PKJKA6J0]] $\neg(\alpha \lor \beta) \equiv \neg\alpha \land \neg\beta$: $\varphi \equiv (\neg\neg p \land \neg q) \lor r$.
4. Apply the [[double negation law|0NFAGWH84K]] $\neg\neg p \equiv p$: $\varphi \equiv (p \land \neg q) \lor r$. This is negation normal form, and it is also a disjunctive normal form.
5. Distribute $\lor$ over $\land$ with $(\alpha \land \beta) \lor \gamma \equiv (\alpha \lor \gamma) \land (\beta \lor \gamma)$: $\varphi \equiv (p \lor r) \land (\neg q \lor r)$.
6. The result is a conjunction of two clauses, $\set{p, r}$ and $\set{\neg q, r}$, so it is in CNF.

We check the result with the truth table.

7. $\varphi$ is false exactly when $p \to q$ is true and $r$ is false. With $r = 0$, the formula $p \to q$ is true at $(p, q) = (0,0), (0,1), (1,1)$. So $\varphi$ is false at the three rows $(p,q,r) = (0,0,0), (0,1,0), (1,1,0)$.
8. At $(0,0,0)$ and $(0,1,0)$ the clause $p \lor r$ is false. At $(1,1,0)$ the clause $\neg q \lor r$ is false. So the CNF is false at these three rows.
9. At the other five rows, either $r = 1$, and both clauses are true; or $(p,q,r) = (1,0,0)$, and $p = 1$ and $\neg q = 1$ make both clauses true. So the CNF is true at these rows, as $\varphi$ is.

## Remarks

The two clauses say: "if $r$ is false, then $p$ is true" and "if $r$ is false, then $q$ is false".
The truth-table method gives the full CNF with three clauses, one for each false row: $(p \lor q \lor r) \land (p \lor \neg q \lor r) \land (\neg p \lor \neg q \lor r)$.
The CNF found with the laws is shorter, so a CNF of a formula is not unique.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The formula "if ($p$ implies $q$), then $r$" can be rewritten as two simple rules.
The first rule is "$p$ or $r$", and the second rule is "not $q$, or $r$".
The formula is true exactly when both rules hold.
We get the rules by removing the arrows and moving the negations inward.

### L2 human

The method has a fixed order: first remove $\to$, then move $\neg$ inward, then distribute.
A typical mistake is at step 1: $(p \to q) \to r$ is not the same as $p \to (q \to r)$, because $\to$ is not associative.
For example, at $p = q = r = 0$ the formula $(p \to q) \to r$ is false, but $p \to (q \to r)$ is true.
The second formula has the CNF $\neg p \lor \neg q \lor r$, with one clause only.
