## Summary

Machine-assisted mathematical discovery is the use of programs that propose [[conjectures|WMEMMHZ3BH]], formulas or constructions, rather than only computing data for a person to read.
Its main forms are conjecture generators, which search a space of candidate statements against data, and search systems, which look for objects with an extremal property.
Since about 2021, systems based on machine learning and on large language models have joined these forms.
The output of such a system is a candidate: a formula still needs a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]], and a construction still needs an independent check.
The topic extends [[experimental mathematics|~8427f205]] from the search for one identity to the automated search over large spaces of statements and objects.
It changes quickly; this page describes systems and events from 1986 to 2025, with their dates.

## Motivation

[[Forming conjectures|~ac95ca3f]] is a search through many candidate statements, and a person can examine only a few.
A program can test millions of candidates against a table of data and report the ones that survive.
Many problems also ask for an object, such as a packing, a colouring or an algorithm, that beats a known bound.
Such problems have a cheap check and a huge search space, and that combination suits a machine.

The same features create risks.
A program that tests many candidates will find some that fit the data by chance, as the [[strong law of small numbers|~c43ae046]] predicts.
A system that writes text can state results that it has not established, or rediscover results that are already in the literature.
So the method needs explicit rules for checking its claims.

## Notation

The following terms are used in the descriptions below.

1. A conjecture generator takes a database of objects and a set of invariants, and outputs inequalities or identities between invariants that hold on the whole database.
2. A search with an evaluator takes a scoring function on candidate objects, such as the size of a set with a property, and proposes candidates that improve the score.
3. A candidate is verified when an independent program checks it, for example by checking that a proposed configuration of spheres has no overlaps.
4. A claim is proved when a proof is written, read by experts, or checked by a proof assistant.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. Graffiti. Siemion Fajtlowicz wrote this program at the University of Houston in the mid-1980s. It proposed inequalities between invariants of graphs, and many were studied and proved by other mathematicians. Fan Chung proved some of its conjectures on the average distance in a graph in 1988.
2. The Ramanujan Machine. A group at the Technion described in Nature in 2021 algorithms that propose continued fractions for constants such as $\pi$, $e$ and values of the zeta function. Some of the proposed formulas were later proved.
3. Machine learning as a guide to intuition. Alex Davies and coauthors reported in Nature in 2021 that models trained on data found relations between invariants of knots, which led to a theorem, and suggested progress on a conjecture about Kazhdan-Lusztig polynomials.
4. Counterexamples by reinforcement learning. Adam Zsolt Wagner showed in 2021 that a simple learning method can construct graphs that refute several published conjectures in graph theory.
5. Search with language models and evaluators. FunSearch (Google DeepMind, Nature, December 2023) evolved programs that produce [[cap sets|B7SKJ1GP5P]], and it found larger cap sets in dimension $8$ than were known.
6. AlphaEvolve (Google DeepMind, May 2025) found an algorithm that multiplies $4 \times 4$ complex matrices with $48$ scalar multiplications, against $49$ from the recursive [[Strassen algorithm|3CVWXDS4JQ]]. It also found a configuration of $593$ spheres touching a central sphere in dimension $11$, which raised the lower bound for the [[kissing number|B408FZYXF9]] from $592$.
7. In November 2025 Bogdan Georgiev, Javier Gómez-Serrano, Terence Tao and Adam Zsolt Wagner reported tests of AlphaEvolve on $67$ problems in analysis, combinatorics and geometry.

### Non-examples

1. A program that proves a stated conjecture, such as the proof of the [[Robbins conjecture|X74S395RZD]] by the prover EQP in 1996, is [[automated theorem proving|A51M1KK9DJ]]. It does not discover the statement.
2. A computer proof that checks all cases of a finite problem settles a known question; its status is discussed in [[computer-assisted proof and verification|54HCSZ6NBT]].
3. A search in a database for a known result, such as a lookup in the OEIS, retrieves knowledge but proposes nothing new; see [[mathematical databases|956TNX0HG9]].

### Basic properties

1. A machine-proposed formula has the status of a conjecture, with the same tests as any experimental result: higher precision, new data and a proof.
2. A machine-proposed construction can often be verified completely by a short independent program. Such a verification turns the construction into a theorem about one object, such as a lower bound.
3. The evaluator decides what the search can find. A search improves the score that it is given, and a badly chosen score gives useless or misleading results.
4. A claim that a problem is "solved" requires a literature search. A system may reproduce a known result, and a list of open problems may be out of date.
5. Systems that write proofs in natural language can make errors that look like valid steps. A proof checked in a proof assistant such as Lean removes this risk for the formal statement, but the statement itself must still be checked.

### History

Graffiti began in the mid-1980s, and its first conjectures were circulated in 1986.
Douglas Lenat's program AM had explored concepts of elementary number theory in the 1970s.
Simon Plouffe found the BBP formula with an integer relation algorithm in 1995, an early discovery of a formula by a program.
In 2021 the Ramanujan Machine, the work of Davies and coauthors, and the work of Wagner appeared.
FunSearch appeared in December 2023, and AlphaEvolve in May 2025.
In October 2025 some OpenAI staff announced that GPT-5 had solved ten open problems from the website erdosproblems.com.
Thomas Bloom, who maintains the site, replied that the problems were listed as open only because he did not know of existing solutions.
GPT-5 had found papers that already solved them, and the posts were deleted.

### Common errors

1. Accepting a formula because a program reported it. The program reports agreement on finite data, which is evidence and not proof.
2. Announcing a solution before a literature search. The October 2025 episode is an example: the "new" solutions were old papers.
3. Treating an improved bound as a proof of optimality. A new construction gives one bound; the matching bound in the other direction is a separate problem.
4. Confusing the verification of a construction with the verification of a proof. The first is a finite computation, and the second needs a checked argument.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Computers can now suggest new mathematics, not just do arithmetic.
Some programs look at many examples and guess rules that connect them.
Other programs search for clever objects, like a better way to multiply small tables of numbers.
In 2025 one such program found a way to multiply two $4 \times 4$ tables of complex numbers with $48$ multiplications instead of $49$.
Every suggestion still has to be checked: a guessed rule needs a proof, and a found object needs an independent test.

### L2 human

We follow the checks that a claimed improvement of a bound should pass.
The example is the claim of a configuration of $593$ unit spheres touching a central unit sphere in dimension $11$.

1. The claim is a list of $593$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{11}$, the centres of the touching spheres.
2. Check 1: each vector has length $2$, so each sphere touches the central sphere.
3. Check 2: any two vectors are at distance at least $2$, so no two spheres overlap.
4. Both checks are finite, and with exact or interval arithmetic they are rigorous.
5. Check 3: the previous best configuration had $592$ spheres, which is confirmed by the literature.
6. Conclusion: the kissing number in dimension $11$ is at least $593$. Nothing is claimed about the upper bound.

The typical use: a construction comes with data that anyone can check, independently of how it was found.
A trap: floating point coordinates can hide a tiny overlap, so the check of step 3 needs exact arithmetic or a safe margin.

### L3 human

The systems differ in what they search.
Conjecture generators search a space of statements and test them on data, so their risk is overfitting, the formal side of the strong law of small numbers.
Search systems with evaluators search a space of objects or programs, and their output is certified by the evaluator, so their risk lies in the choice of the problem and the score.
Language models can propose both statements and proofs, and their output needs the strongest checks.

The checks fall into three kinds.
Constructions are verified by independent computation.
Proofs are verified by experts or by a proof assistant; see [[mathematical software and formalization|F3F5GGYHMM]].
Novelty is verified by a literature search.
The software itself belongs to [[tools of experimental mathematics|KY55MDRBEY]], and the acceptance of computer proofs to [[rigour and the acceptance of proofs|1BTHSHST4M]].
