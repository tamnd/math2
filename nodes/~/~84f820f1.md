## Example

We use [[Barbara|AC6XQ4KRNT]] twice in a row to prove a fact about multiples of $12$.
Barbara says: from "all $M$ are $P$" and "all $S$ are $M$", infer "all $S$ are $P$".

1. All multiples of $6$ are multiples of $3$, because $6k = 3 \cdot 2k$.
2. All multiples of $12$ are multiples of $6$, because $12k = 6 \cdot 2k$.
3. Barbara with $S$ the multiples of $12$, $M$ the multiples of $6$ and $P$ the multiples of $3$ gives: all multiples of $12$ are multiples of $3$.
4. All multiples of $3$ are integers whose digit sum is a multiple of $3$, by the divisibility test for $3$.
5. Barbara with $S$ the multiples of $12$, $M$ the multiples of $3$ and $P$ the integers with digit sum a multiple of $3$ gives: every multiple of $12$ has a digit sum that is a multiple of $3$.
6. A check: $12 \cdot 17 = 204$, and its digit sum $2 + 0 + 4 = 6$ is a multiple of $3$.

In step 5 the conclusion of step 3 is the minor premise, and step 4 is the major premise.
Each step is a syllogism of mood AAA in the first figure, so each is an instance of Barbara.

## Remarks

The example shows the typical use of Barbara in mathematics, a chain of inclusions $A \subseteq B \subseteq C \subseteq D$.
In set notation the argument is: the multiples of $12$ form a [[subset|0BJR9QR08E]] of the multiples of $6$, which form a subset of the multiples of $3$.
A chain of $n$ inclusions needs $n - 1$ uses of Barbara.
Such a chain of syllogisms, where each conclusion is a premise of the next, is called a polysyllogism.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every multiple of $12$ is a multiple of $6$, and every multiple of $6$ is a multiple of $3$.
So every multiple of $12$ is a multiple of $3$.
That is one use of Barbara.
Then every multiple of $3$ has a digit sum divisible by $3$, so every multiple of $12$ has one too.
That is a second use of Barbara, with the result of the first as a premise.

### L2 human

We show what goes wrong if one link of the chain is reversed.

1. "All multiples of $3$ are multiples of $6$" is false: $9$ is a multiple of $3$ and not of $6$.
2. With this false premise, Barbara still gives a valid argument, but the argument does not prove its conclusion.
3. For example, "all multiples of $3$ are multiples of $6$, all odd multiples of $3$ are multiples of $3$, so all odd multiples of $3$ are multiples of $6$" is valid in form.
4. Its conclusion is false, since an odd number is never a multiple of $6$.

The typical use of the chain is to break a long inclusion into short steps, each easy to check.
The trap is to reverse a link: "all multiples of $6$ are multiples of $3$" does not give "all multiples of $3$ are multiples of $6$".
