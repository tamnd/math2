## Statement

Let $\mathcal{M}$, $\mathcal{N}_1$ and $\mathcal{N}_2$ be $L$-[[structures|983GZBQYT4]].

1. Elementary amalgamation: let $f_1 \colon \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{N}_1$ and $f_2 \colon \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{N}_2$ be [[elementary embeddings|CGH8C8EA9N]]. Then there are an $L$-structure $\mathcal{N}$ and elementary embeddings $g_1 \colon \mathcal{N}_1 \to \mathcal{N}$ and $g_2 \colon \mathcal{N}_2 \to \mathcal{N}$ with $g_1 \circ f_1 = g_2 \circ f_2$.
2. Joint embedding: if $\mathcal{N}_1 \equiv \mathcal{N}_2$, then there are an $L$-structure $\mathcal{N}$ and elementary embeddings $g_1 \colon \mathcal{N}_1 \to \mathcal{N}$ and $g_2 \colon \mathcal{N}_2 \to \mathcal{N}$.

In both parts we can choose $\mathcal{N}$ with $\abs{N} \le \abs{N_1} + \abs{N_2} + \abs{L} + \aleph_0$, and with $g_1$ an inclusion, so that $\mathcal{N}_1 \preceq \mathcal{N}$.
Here $\equiv$ is [[elementary equivalence|P3B1V182N4]].
A proof is in [[the proof by compactness and diagrams|~ae08988f]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The maps $f_1$ and $f_2$ must be elementary, not only embeddings.
Let $\mathcal{M} = (\N_{>0}, <)$, let $f_1$ be its inclusion into $\mathcal{N}_1 = (\N, <)$, and let $f_2$ be the identity of $\mathcal{N}_2 = (\N_{>0}, <)$.
If $g_1$ and $g_2$ were elementary with $g_1(1) = g_2(1)$, then this element would be least in $\mathcal{N}$, because $1$ is least in $\mathcal{N}_2$.
It would also have an element below it in $\mathcal{N}$, because $0 < 1$ in $\mathcal{N}_1$; so no amalgam exists.
In part 2, elementary equivalence is needed: if $\mathcal{N}$ contains elementary copies of both, then $\mathcal{N}_1 \equiv \mathcal{N} \equiv \mathcal{N}_2$.

### Consequences

- Any two models of a [[complete theory|655K8Y7VS8]] embed elementarily into a common model.
- The models of a complete theory with elementary embeddings have the [[amalgamation property|XSCSX39GEZ]] and the joint embedding property. This is the reason why a large saturated "monster model" can contain all small models of the theory.
- [[Robinson's joint consistency theorem|R6AN38DNBD]] has a proof of the same kind, with compactness and two diagrams.

### Converse

The correct form of the converse uses the expansions by names for $M$.
Elementary maps $g_1$ and $g_2$ with $g_1 \circ f_1 = g_2 \circ f_2$ exist if and only if $(\mathcal{N}_1, f_1(m))_{m \in M} \equiv (\mathcal{N}_2, f_2(m))_{m \in M}$.
Elementarity of $f_1$ and $f_2$ is one way to obtain this, since both expansions are then elementarily equivalent to $(\mathcal{M}, m)_{m \in M}$.

### Generalizations

Any family $(\mathcal{N}_i)_{i \in I}$ of elementary extensions of $\mathcal{M}$ has a common elementary extension over $\mathcal{M}$, by the same proof with one diagram for each $i$.
For substructures in place of elementary substructures, amalgamation is a property of a class of structures, studied in Fraïssé theory; it holds for some classes and fails for others.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose a structure $\mathcal{M}$ has grown in two different ways, into $\mathcal{N}_1$ and into $\mathcal{N}_2$, and neither growth changed any first-order fact about $\mathcal{M}$.
The theorem says that the two growths can be put together in one larger structure that keeps every fact about $\mathcal{N}_1$ and about $\mathcal{N}_2$.
For example, the rational numbers grow into the real numbers in two copies, and both copies fit together in one larger ordered set.

### L2 human

We build an explicit amalgam for $\mathcal{M} = (\Q, <)$ and $\mathcal{N}_1 = \mathcal{N}_2 = (\R, <)$, with inclusions $f_1 = f_2$.

1. $(\Q, <) \preceq (\R, <)$, by [[this example|~89a66bd3]], so the hypotheses hold.
2. Let $\mathcal{N} = \R \times \Q$, ordered by $(x, p) < (y, q)$ iff $x < y$, or $x = y$ and $p < q$.
3. $\mathcal{N}$ is a [[dense linear order|2W4S2H4VYA]] without endpoints. Between $(x, p) < (y, q)$ lies $(x, p + 1)$ when $x < y$, and $(x, (p + q)/2)$ when $x = y$.
4. Let $g_1(x) = (x, 0)$. Let $g_2(x) = (x, 0)$ for $x \in \Q$, and $g_2(x) = (x, 1)$ for $x \notin \Q$. Both maps preserve the order, and $g_1 = g_2$ on $\Q$.
5. The theory of dense linear orders without endpoints has [[quantifier elimination|7FDDXV3JK7]], so every embedding between its models is elementary. So $g_1$ and $g_2$ are elementary.
6. In this amalgam the two copies of $\sqrt{2}$ are different points, while $g_1 = g_2 = \mathrm{id}$ into $\R$ gives an amalgam in which they are equal.

The typical use is to combine two extensions without losing information about either.
A trap is to think that the amalgam is unique; step 6 gives two different amalgams of the same data.

### L3 human

The theorem says that, for a complete theory $T$, the category of models of $T$ with elementary embeddings has amalgamation and joint embedding.
With the [[elementary chain theorem|~26b49e10]] it gives directed colimits, and these are the properties used to build universal and homogeneous models, and the monster model of a complete theory.
For other classes of structures and embeddings, amalgamation becomes a hypothesis: Fraïssé theory and the theory of abstract elementary classes assume it.
In a stable theory one can choose $\mathcal{N}_1$ and $\mathcal{N}_2$ independent over $\mathcal{M}$ in the sense of forking, and this independent amalgam is unique up to isomorphism, because types over models are stationary.
