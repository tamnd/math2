## Summary

A [[theory|92SWXST1ZM]] $T$ in a signature $L$ is *inductive* if the union of every chain of [[models|MVCH6N9SNW]] of $T$ is again a model of $T$.
Here a chain is a family of models indexed by a total order, each a [[substructure|676H7EGTKJ]] of the later ones.
By the Chang-Łoś-Suszko theorem, this holds exactly when $T$ is equivalent to a set of $\forall\exists$ sentences; the formal page is [[this definition|~ae02f1e0]].
Inductive theories are the setting of the theory of [[existentially closed models|~524465b6]] and model companions: chain constructions stay inside the class of models.

## Motivation

To make a model "closed", one adds solutions of equations step by step, and new elements bring new equations.
The construction only ends after infinitely many steps, with the union of a chain.
For the union to be a model again, the theory must be preserved under unions of chains.
This is the condition of inductivity, and it is the reason why fields have algebraic closures and linear orders have dense extensions inside the same first-order framework.

## Notation

Many books define "inductive" by $\forall\exists$ axioms and call the chain condition a theorem.
Other names are $\forall\exists$ theory, $\forall_2$ theory and $\Pi_2$ theory, and a class of structures closed under unions of chains is an *inductive class*.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The theory of fields|~0501a063]]: its only non-universal axiom, the existence of inverses, is $\forall\exists$.
- Every universal theory, for example the theories of linear orders, of graphs, and of groups in the signature $\set{\cdot, ^{-1}, 1}$.
- Every [[model complete theory|YR6HCMABTE]], for example $\mathrm{ACF}$ and $\mathrm{DLO}$; see [[this example|~6530a618]].
- The theory of infinite sets: each axiom "there are at least $n$ elements" is existential.
- The theory of groups in the signature $\set{\cdot}$ alone. Its usual axioms state the existence of an identity first, but it is equivalent to the $\forall\exists$ axioms of a nonempty associative operation in which $a x = b$ and $y a = b$ are always solvable.

### Non-examples

- [[Discrete linear orders without endpoints|~e18beae4]]: the chain $2^{-n} \Z$ has the dense union of the dyadic rationals, so the axiom "every element has an immediate successor" is lost.
- Dense linear orders with endpoints: the chain $[0, n] \cap \Q$ has the union $[0, \infty) \cap \Q$, which has no greatest element.

### Basic properties

- A theory axiomatized by $\forall\exists$ sentences is inductive, by part 2 of the [[elementary chain theorem|NHCRZ74JHH]]. The converse is the Chang-Łoś-Suszko theorem.
- Every model complete theory is inductive, since a chain of its models is an elementary chain.
- Every model of an inductive theory embeds into an [[existentially closed model|~71e6cae7]] of it.
- If $T$ is inductive and $T^*$ is its [[model companion|~7269c0e3]], then every model of $T^*$ is a model of $T$, and the models of $T^*$ are the e.c. models of $T$.

### History

The characterization by $\forall\exists$ sentences is named after Chen Chung Chang, Jerzy Łoś and Roman Suszko.
It is one of the classical preservation theorems of model theory, together with the Łoś-Tarski theorem for universal sentences.

### Common errors

- Reading inductivity from the given axioms only. A theory can be inductive although its axioms are not $\forall\exists$, as for groups in the signature $\set{\cdot}$.
- Confusing unions of chains with unions of elementary chains. Every theory is preserved under unions of elementary chains, so that property says nothing.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A theory is inductive when growing models stay models.
If each structure in an increasing sequence obeys the axioms, the union of the sequence obeys them too.
Fields are an example: a union of a growing sequence of fields is a field.
Orders where each element has a next element are not an example: the union of finer and finer such orders can become dense.

### L2 human

We see why the inverse axiom of fields survives unions of chains, while the successor axiom of discrete orders does not.

1. In a chain of fields, an element $a \neq 0$ has an inverse $b$ at the stage where $a$ first appears. The equation $a b = 1$ still holds in the union.
2. In the chain $\Z \subseteq \frac{1}{2} \Z \subseteq \frac{1}{4} \Z \subseteq \cdots$, the element $0$ has the successor $1$, then $\frac 1 2$, then $\frac 1 4$.
3. Each witness is destroyed at the next stage, because the axiom "$y$ is the successor of $x$" also says that nothing lies between $x$ and $y$.
4. In the union, $0$ has no successor.

The typical use is to check inductivity before building e.c. models by chains.
A trap is that a $\forall\exists\forall$ axiom, like the successor axiom, can fail in the union even if it holds at every stage.

### L3 human

Inductive theories are exactly the theories whose class of models is closed under direct limits along chains of embeddings.
For such a theory, the e.c. models always exist, and the theory has a model companion if and only if the class of e.c. models is elementary.
A model companion of an inductive theory contains it, so it is a strengthening by new $\forall\exists$ axioms, like "every polynomial has a root" for fields.
This is the starting point of [[Robinson forcing|~357e6442]], which studies the e.c. models of an inductive theory even when they do not form an elementary class.
