## Example

Let $p$, $q$ and $r$ be propositional variables.
We show that the two formulas
$$(p \land q) \to r \qquad \text{and} \qquad p \to (q \to r)$$
are [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]].
We compute both columns of a [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]] with the $2^3 = 8$ rows in the standard order.

1. An [[implication|0NMG7W7Q11]] $a \to b$ is false only when $a$ is true and $b$ is false.
2. So $(p \land q) \to r$ is false only when $p \land q$ is true and $r$ is false. By the definition of [[conjunction|025YZN3VJW]], this is the single row $p = \mathrm{T}$, $q = \mathrm{T}$, $r = \mathrm{F}$.
3. The formula $p \to (q \to r)$ is false only when $p$ is true and $q \to r$ is false.
4. The formula $q \to r$ is false only when $q$ is true and $r$ is false. So, by step 3, $p \to (q \to r)$ is false only in the row $p = \mathrm{T}$, $q = \mathrm{T}$, $r = \mathrm{F}$.
5. By steps 2 and 4, both formulas are false in the same single row and true in the other seven rows.

| $p$ | $q$ | $r$ | $p \land q$ | $(p \land q) \to r$ | $q \to r$ | $p \to (q \to r)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | T | T | T | T |
| T | T | F | T | F | F | F |
| T | F | T | F | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | F | T | T | T |
| F | T | T | F | T | T | T |
| F | T | F | F | T | F | T |
| F | F | T | F | T | T | T |
| F | F | F | F | T | T | T |

6. The fifth and the seventh columns agree in every row, so $(p \land q) \to r \equiv p \to (q \to r)$.

## Remarks

The equivalence is called exportation, because the hypothesis $p$ is moved out of the conjunction.
It explains a habit of mathematical writing.
A theorem "if $p$ and $q$, then $r$" is often proved in the form "assume $p$; now, if $q$, then $r$".
In type theory and in Lean, exportation is currying: a function of a pair corresponds to a function that returns a function.

The equivalence fails when the conjunction becomes a disjunction.
The formulas $(p \lor q) \to r$ and $p \to (q \to r)$ differ in the row $p = \mathrm{T}$, $q = \mathrm{F}$, $r = \mathrm{F}$: the first formula is false and the second is true.

The implication is not associative.
The formula $(p \to q) \to r$ is not equivalent to $p \to (q \to r)$: in the row $p = \mathrm{F}$, $q = \mathrm{F}$, $r = \mathrm{F}$, the first formula is false and the second is true.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"If it is Saturday and it is sunny, we go to the beach" says the same as "if it is Saturday, then: if it is sunny, we go to the beach".
Both promises are broken in exactly one situation: a sunny Saturday without a trip to the beach.
In all other situations both promises are kept.
So the two sentences are logically equivalent.

### L2 human

We use exportation to organize a proof of "if $n$ is even and $n$ is a multiple of $3$, then $n$ is a multiple of $6$", for an integer $n$.

1. By exportation, it is enough to prove "if $n$ is even, then (if $3$ divides $n$, then $6$ divides $n$)".
2. Assume that $n$ is even, so $n = 2k$ for an integer $k$.
3. Now assume that $3$ divides $n = 2k$. Since $3$ is prime and $3$ does not divide $2$, the number $3$ divides $k$, so $k = 3m$ for an integer $m$.
4. Then $n = 6m$, so $6$ divides $n$.

The typical use is in the reading of theorems with several hypotheses, which a proof introduces one at a time.
A trap is the wrong bracketing: $(p \to q) \to r$ is a different formula, as the remarks show.
