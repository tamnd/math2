## Example

Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. The matrix $A$ has 2 rows and 3 columns, and $2 \neq 3$, so $A$ is not a [[square matrix|0CP7YY6NAG]].
2. The [[product|076JGP6R3T]] $AB$ is defined only when $B$ has as many rows as $A$ has columns. For $B = A$ this asks $2 = 3$, so $A^2$ is not defined.
3. The product $AA^{\mathsf{T}}$ is defined, with the [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]] $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ of size $3 \times 2$:
$$AA^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 4 + 9 & 4 + 10 + 18 \\ 4 + 10 + 18 & 16 + 25 + 36 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 14 & 32 \\ 32 & 77 \end{pmatrix}.$$
4. The product $A^{\mathsf{T}}A$ is also defined, and it is $3 \times 3$:
$$A^{\mathsf{T}}A = \begin{pmatrix} 17 & 22 & 27 \\ 22 & 29 & 36 \\ 27 & 36 & 45 \end{pmatrix}.$$
5. The products in steps 3 and 4 are square, but they have different sizes. So neither is a "square of $A$".

## Remarks

The entry $(1, 1)$ of $A^{\mathsf{T}}A$ is $1 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 4 = 17$, the squared length of the first column of $A$.
Software that squares entry by entry would return $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 & 9 \\ 16 & 25 & 36 \end{pmatrix}$, which is not a matrix product.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To multiply a matrix by itself, its rows must be as long as its columns.
Here the rows have 3 entries and the columns have 2, so the product does not exist.

### L2 human

The matrices $AA^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $A^{\mathsf{T}}A$ are the nearest replacements for a square of $A$.
They are [[symmetric|0VR671PHE5]], and they appear in least squares problems.
