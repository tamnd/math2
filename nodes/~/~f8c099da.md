## Summary

The method of the minimal counterexample proves that every object $x$ of a set $X$ has a property $P$.
It needs a size function $s \colon X \to \mathbb{N}$ into the [[natural numbers|0MWKJ44V5A]].
The proof assumes, for a [[contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]], that the set $C$ of [[counterexamples|0ED72CTBNP]] is not empty.
The [[well-ordering principle|01BX15HSRR]] gives a least value $m$ of $s$ on $C$, and the proof fixes a counterexample $x_0$ with $s(x_0) = m$.
The proof then derives a contradiction, usually by building a counterexample $x_1$ with $s(x_1) < m$, or by showing that $x_0$ has the property because all smaller objects have it.
The method matters because it lets a proof use every smaller case at once, and it is often the shortest way to write a [[strong induction|0KMVFSHFGZ]].

## Motivation

Many statements about integers, graphs or groups are proved by breaking an object into smaller objects of the same kind.
An induction proof must name the induction variable and state the induction hypothesis for all smaller sizes.
The minimal counterexample packs all of this into one sentence: "let $x_0$ be a counterexample of the least size".
Every object of smaller size then has the property, because $x_0$ has the least size among the counterexamples.
The proof can now study $x_0$ alone, and it often finds a smaller counterexample inside $x_0$.
Euclid's proof that each composite number has a prime divisor has this form.

## Notation

1. The size function is often written $|x|$, $s(x)$ or $n(x)$: the value of an integer, the number of vertices of a graph, the order of a group or the degree of a polynomial.
2. Graph theorists call a minimal counterexample a "minimal criminal" or a "smallest criminal".
3. Some books use "least counterexample" or "smallest counterexample". The words mean the same thing.

## Remarks

### The steps of the method

1. State the claim: every $x$ in $X$ has the property $P$.
2. Choose a size $s(x)$ in $\mathbb{N}$. The choice decides which smaller objects the proof can use.
3. Suppose that the claim is false, and let $x_0$ be a counterexample with the least size $m$.
4. Note the consequence: every $x$ with $s(x) < m$ has the property $P$.
5. Use the structure of $x_0$ to build a smaller counterexample, or to show that $x_0$ has the property. Either gives a contradiction.
6. Conclude that no counterexample exists.

### Examples

1. [[Every integer at least 2 has a prime divisor|~f5eefa58]]: a least integer $m \ge 2$ with no prime divisor is not prime, so $m = ab$ with $1 < a < m$, and a prime divisor of $a$ divides $m$.
2. [[Every graph of maximum degree D has a colouring with D + 1 colours|~8d7f6e53]]: delete one vertex of a counterexample with the fewest vertices, colour the rest, and one colour remains free for the deleted vertex.
3. The [[irrationality of the square root of 2|0DZGSDHD92]]: if $\sqrt{2} = a/b$ with the least positive $b$, then $\sqrt{2} = (2b - a)/(a - b)$ with the smaller denominator $a - b$.
4. The [[fundamental theorem of arithmetic|084HH1NDCB]]: the uniqueness part is often proved from a least integer with two different factorizations.
5. The [[Feit-Thompson theorem|6VK9RSG13Q]] starts from a counterexample of the least order, so each proper subgroup of it is solvable.

### Non-examples

1. The [[false minimal counterexample argument over the positive rationals|~82ba119e]]: the counterexamples to "every positive rational is at least $1$" have no least element, so step 3 of the method fails.
2. A proof that picks "a counterexample" without the word "least" is a plain proof by contradiction. It cannot use the smaller cases.
3. A size function with values in $\mathbb{Z}$ or in the positive reals does not work: these sets have subsets with no least element.

### Basic properties

1. The method is valid because of the [[well-ordering principle|01BX15HSRR]]: the set $s(C)$ is a nonempty subset of $\mathbb{N}$, so it has a least element $m$.
2. The method is [[strong induction|0KMVFSHFGZ]] in contrapositive form. Strong induction proves "if every $x$ with $s(x) < n$ has $P$, then every $x$ with $s(x) = n$ has $P$". The minimal counterexample proves the same implication by contradiction.
3. When step 5 builds a smaller counterexample, the method is the minimal form of [[infinite descent|0EFYCFHYFG]].
4. The size can be any function into a set with a well-founded order, such as pairs of natural numbers in the lexicographic order. This links the method to [[double induction|~2da822d1]].
5. The [[equivalence of induction and well-ordering|~00389da2]] shows that the method proves exactly the statements that induction proves.

### History

Euclid's *Elements*, Book VII, Proposition 31, proves that each composite number has a prime divisor with an argument of this kind.
The method is the standard frame of the proofs of the four colour theorem, where a minimal counterexample is called a "minimal criminal".
Courant and Robbins, in *What Is Mathematics?*, call these the "smallest bad maps".

### Common errors

1. The proof forgets to check that the smaller object is in the set $X$. In a colouring proof, the smaller graph must again satisfy the hypotheses on the degree.
2. The proof uses a size with no least value. Over the [[rational numbers|0NCJ5WXN6Z]] a "least counterexample" need not exist.
3. The proof builds a smaller object but does not show that it is again a counterexample. Then there is no contradiction.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose that a claim about whole numbers is false for some numbers.
Then there is a smallest number for which it is false, because whole numbers cannot go down forever.
Call it the smallest bad number.
Every number below it is good, and we can use that.
If we can now find an even smaller bad number, we have a contradiction, so no bad number exists.
For example, the smallest number from $2$ on with no prime divisor cannot be $6$, because then $2$, which is smaller, would also have no prime divisor.

### L2 human

We prove that every integer $n \ge 1$ is a sum of distinct powers of $2$.

1. Suppose not, and let $m$ be the least integer $m \ge 1$ that is not such a sum.
2. The number $m$ is not $1 = 2^0$, so $m \ge 2$.
3. Let $2^k$ be the largest power of $2$ with $2^k \le m$. Then $m < 2^{k+1}$.
4. If $m = 2^k$, then $m$ is a sum of one power of $2$, which contradicts step 1. So $m > 2^k$.
5. Let $r = m - 2^k$. Then $1 \le r < m$, so $r$ is a sum of distinct powers of $2$ by the minimality of $m$.
6. Also $r < 2^{k+1} - 2^k = 2^k$, so $2^k$ is not one of the powers in the sum for $r$.
7. Then $m = 2^k + r$ is a sum of distinct powers of $2$. This contradicts step 1.

The typical use of the method is a statement in which an object splits into smaller objects of the same kind.
A trap is step 6: without it the sum for $m$ could repeat the power $2^k$, and the claim would not follow.

### L3 human

The method works in every set with a [[well-founded relation|0DQPNJNPYK]], because a well-founded relation is exactly a relation in which each nonempty subset has a minimal element.
So the method is well-founded induction read backward, and it extends to ordinals, to the lexicographic order on tuples and to the subterm order on formulas.
In group theory the size is the order of the group, and the method is the frame of many structure theorems for finite groups.
In graph theory a minimal counterexample is studied as a structure: one proves that it cannot contain certain small configurations, and then that every candidate contains one of them.
The method of [[induction on size|~ad849a99]] is the same idea written as a forward induction.
