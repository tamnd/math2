## Summary

Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over a field $F$.
A left eigenvector of $A$ is a nonzero row vector $y \in F^{1 \times n}$ with $yA = \lambda y$ for some $\lambda \in F$.
Taking transposes, $yA = \lambda y$ holds if and only if $A^{\mathsf{T}} y^{\mathsf{T}} = \lambda y^{\mathsf{T}}$.
So the left eigenvectors of $A$ are the transposes of the ordinary [[eigenvectors|0N6960W0BV]] of $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
The scalars $\lambda$ that occur are the same as the [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $A$.

## Motivation

A Markov chain with transition matrix $P$ acts on probability row vectors by $\pi \mapsto \pi P$.
A stationary distribution is a left eigenvector for the eigenvalue $1$.
Left eigenvectors also give the coordinates of a vector in a basis of right eigenvectors, by biorthogonality.

## Notation

The usual eigenvectors are then called right eigenvectors.
Over $\C$, some authors use the conjugate transpose and write $y^* A = \lambda y^*$ with $y$ a column vector.
In terms of linear maps, a left eigenvector of $A$ is an eigenvector of the [[dual map|0AZ5ARP287]] of $x \mapsto Ax$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[A Markov chain with two states|~c001e66a]]: the stationary distribution $\pi = (5/6, 1/6)$ is a left eigenvector for $1$.
- [[Left and right eigenvectors of a triangular matrix|~4dea89e6]]: they satisfy the biorthogonality relations.
- For a real symmetric matrix, $A^{\mathsf{T}} = A$, so the left eigenvectors are the transposes of the right eigenvectors.

### Non-examples

- [[A right eigenvector that is not a left eigenvector|~1a0caa3d]]: for $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, the row vector $(1, 0)$ is not a left eigenvector.

### Basic properties

- $A$ and $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ have the same [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]], because $\det(tI - A^{\mathsf{T}}) = \det((tI - A)^{\mathsf{T}}) = \det(tI - A)$. So the left and right eigenvalues agree, with the same algebraic multiplicities.
- The geometric multiplicities also agree: $\dim \ker(A - \lambda I) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$, and a matrix and its transpose have the same rank.
- Biorthogonality: if $yA = \mu y$ and $Av = \lambda v$ with $\lambda \ne \mu$, then $yv = 0$. Indeed $\mu (yv) = (yA)v = y(Av) = \lambda (yv)$, so $(\mu - \lambda) yv = 0$.
- If $A$ is [[diagonalisable|07SQCNV233]] as $A = SDS^{-1}$, then the rows of $S^{-1}$ are left eigenvectors and the columns of $S$ are right eigenvectors. Indeed $S^{-1}A = DS^{-1}$.

### History

The terms left and right eigenvector come from the side of the matrix on which the vector is written.

### Common errors

- Assuming that the transpose of a right eigenvector is a left eigenvector. This holds for symmetric matrices, but it fails in general.
- Solving $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$ instead of $y(A - \lambda I) = 0$. The second system has the matrix $A^{\mathsf{T}} - \lambda I$ in column form.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An ordinary eigenvector is a column that the matrix only stretches, when the matrix acts from the left.
A left eigenvector is a row that the matrix only stretches, when the matrix acts from the right.
The stretch factors are the same eigenvalues in both cases, but the vectors are usually different.

### L2 human

We find the left eigenvectors of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $p_A(t) = (t - 1)(t - 2)$, so the eigenvalues are $1$ and $2$.
2. For $\lambda = 1$: $y(A - I) = (y_1, y_2) \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = (0, y_1 + y_2)$. So $y = (1, -1)$.
3. Check: $(1, -1) A = (1, 1 - 2) = (1, -1)$.
4. For $\lambda = 2$: $y(A - 2I) = (y_1, y_2) \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = (-y_1, y_1)$. So $y = (0, 1)$.
5. Check: $(0, 1) A = (0, 2) = 2 (0, 1)$.

The typical use is to find a stationary distribution, or to expand a vector in an eigenbasis.
A trap: a left eigenvector is a row vector that multiplies $A$ from the left, so the system to solve uses the columns of $A - \lambda I$.

### L3 human

A left eigenvector is an eigenvector of the transpose, that is, of the dual map on $(F^n)^*$.
For a simple eigenvalue $\lambda$ with right eigenvector $v$ and left eigenvector $y$, $yv \ne 0$, and $vy/(yv)$ is the spectral projection onto $E_\lambda$.
This projection appears in the first-order perturbation formula $\lambda'(0) = y A'(0) v / (yv)$.
