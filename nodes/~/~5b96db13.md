## Example

Four cards lie on a table, and each card has a letter on one side and a number on the other.
The visible faces are $\text{E}$, $\text{K}$, $4$ and $7$.
The rule is the [[conditional|~38816aeb]] "if a card has a vowel on one side, then it has an even number on the other side".
The task is to turn over exactly the cards that are needed to test whether the rule holds for these four cards.
We decide card by card, using the fact that a conditional fails only when its hypothesis is true and its conclusion is false.

1. Card $\text{E}$: it has a vowel, so the hypothesis is true. An odd number on the back would break the rule, so we must turn it.
2. Card $\text{K}$: it has a consonant, so the hypothesis is false. Any number on the back keeps the rule, so we need not turn it.
3. Card $4$: the conclusion "even number" is true. Any letter on the back keeps the rule, so we need not turn it.
4. Card $7$: the conclusion is false. A vowel on the back would break the rule, so we must turn it.
5. So the answer is $\text{E}$ and $7$: the card that shows the hypothesis, and the card that shows the negation of the conclusion.

## Remarks

The psychologist Peter Wason introduced this task in 1966, and it is one of the most studied problems in the psychology of reasoning.
In the abstract form, only a small minority of people, fewer than about $10$ percent in Wason's studies, choose exactly the two right cards.
The most common answers are $\text{E}$ alone, or $\text{E}$ and $4$.

The answer "$\text{E}$ and $4$" tests the rule and its [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] "if a card has an even number, then it has a vowel".
But the rule says nothing about cards with an even number, so card $4$ cannot break it.
Card $7$ is needed because of the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]]: "if a card has an odd number, then it has a consonant" is equivalent to the rule.

The content of the rule changes the results.
In a version of Griggs and Cox from 1982, the rule is "if a person is drinking beer, then the person is over $19$", and the cards show "beer", "soft drink", "$22$" and "$16$".
Most people then choose "beer" and "$16$", which is the same logical answer.
Explanations of this content effect differ, and one of them says that people reason well about rules of permission and of cheating.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A shop has the rule: "if a child buys a sweet, the child pays $1$ coin".
Four children are at the till.
One bought a sweet, one bought nothing, one paid $1$ coin, and one paid nothing.
To check the rule, we look at the child who bought a sweet, to see the coin, and at the child who paid nothing, to see the shopping bag.
The other two cannot break the rule.

### L2 human

We use the lesson of the task to check the theorem "if $n$ is divisible by $6$, then $n$ is even" on the numbers $12$, $9$, $14$ and $7$.

1. The hypothesis is "divisible by $6$". The number $12$ satisfies it, so we check the conclusion: $12$ is even.
2. The number $9$ fails the hypothesis, so it cannot break the theorem.
3. The number $14$ satisfies the conclusion, so it cannot break the theorem, although $14$ is not divisible by $6$.
4. The number $7$ fails the conclusion, so we check the hypothesis: $7$ is not divisible by $6$.
5. No number has a true hypothesis and a false conclusion, so the check finds no counterexample.

The typical use of this lesson is in the search for a counterexample to "if $P$, then $Q$": look only at cases with $P$ true, or equivalently with $Q$ false.

A trap: in step 3, the number $14$ looks like evidence for the theorem.
It is not, because it does not satisfy the hypothesis.
A case with $Q$ true neither supports nor breaks "if $P$, then $Q$".
