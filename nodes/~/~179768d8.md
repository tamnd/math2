## Summary

Let $U$ and $W$ be [[subspaces|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $\R^n$ with the dot product, with $1 \le p = \dim U \le q = \dim W$.
The *principal angles* $0 \le \theta_1 \le \theta_2 \le \cdots \le \theta_p \le \pi/2$ between $U$ and $W$ are defined one at a time.
For $k = 1, \dots, p$,
$$\cos \theta_k = \max \set{ u \cdot w : u \in U, \ w \in W, \ \norm{u} = \norm{w} = 1, \ u \perp u_1, \dots, u_{k-1}, \ w \perp w_1, \dots, w_{k-1} } = u_k \cdot w_k,$$
where $(u_k, w_k)$ is a pair at which the maximum is attained.
The vectors $u_k$ and $w_k$ are the *principal vectors*.
So $\theta_1$ is the smallest [[angle between two vectors|0ZEHZY85E6]] of $U$ and $W$, and each later angle is the smallest one orthogonal to the earlier pairs.
The [[characterization by singular values|~339ddff3]] shows that the angles do not depend on the choice of the maximizing pairs: $\cos \theta_k$ is the $k$-th largest [[singular value|09VMDSRXWJ]] of $Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2$, where the columns of $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are [[orthonormal bases|0S9GGTR0EX]] of $U$ and $W$.

## Motivation

Two lines through the origin meet at one angle, and two planes in $\R^3$ meet at one dihedral angle.
Two planes in $\R^4$ can meet in more complicated ways: they can share a line and be tilted along a second direction, or share only the origin.
One number does not describe their position, but $p$ angles do.
Principal angles measure how far apart two subspaces are; they are used to compare the column spaces of two matrices and to define distances between subspaces.

## Notation

We write $\theta_k(U, W)$ or $\theta_k$, and we list the angles in increasing order, so the cosines $\sigma_k = \cos \theta_k$ decrease.
Some books call them canonical angles.
The maximum in the definition is at least $0$, because we can replace $w$ by $-w$, and at most $1$, by the [[Cauchy-Schwarz inequality|0RFWW56Y22]]; so $\theta_k \in [0, \pi/2]$.
Over $\C$, the definition uses $\abs{\ip{u}{w}}$, and the matrix $Q_1^* Q_2$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[Two planes in R3|~d2e159b0]]: the planes $x + y = 0$ and $y + z = 0$ have principal angles $0$ and $\pi/3$. The angle $0$ comes from the common line, and $\pi/3$ is the dihedral angle.
2. [[A line and a plane|~a6c922bc]]: the line $\R (1, 1, 1)$ and the plane $z = 0$ have one principal angle $\arccos \sqrt{2/3}$, about $35.26°$. This is the usual angle between a line and a plane.
3. For $U = W$, all principal angles are $0$.
4. For $U \perp W$, all principal angles are $\pi/2$, because every $u \cdot w$ is $0$.

### Non-examples

1. [[An angle between two unit vectors that is not principal|~fc98a5c5]]: in example 1, the vectors $\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1, -1, 0) \in U$ and $(1, 0, 0) \in W$ make an angle of $\pi/4$, which is neither $0$ nor $\pi/3$.
2. The entries of $Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2$ are cosines of angles between basis vectors; they change with the bases and are not the cosines of the principal angles.

### Basic properties

- The number of zero principal angles is $\dim (U \cap W)$.
- For a line $U = \R u$ with $\norm{u} = 1$, the only principal angle satisfies $\cos \theta_1 = \norm{P_W u}$, where $P_W$ is the orthogonal projection onto $W$.
- With $P_U$ and $P_W$ the projections onto $U$ and $W$, $\norm{P_U - P_W}_F^2 = p + q - 2 \sum_{k} \cos^2 \theta_k$ in the Frobenius norm. For $p = q$ this is $2 \sum_k \sin^2 \theta_k$, a distance between subspaces of the same dimension.
- These are parts of the [[characterization by singular values|~339ddff3]].

### History

The Wikipedia article on angles between flats says that Jordan first discussed the generalization of angles to subspaces of any dimension.

### Common errors

- To take the angle between two chosen basis vectors as the angle between the subspaces; see non-example 1.
- To forget that the maximizing pairs must be orthogonal to the earlier pairs, which would give $\theta_1$ again and again.
- To compute singular values of $A^{\mathsf{T}} B$ for bases that are not orthonormal; the columns must be orthonormalized first, for example by a [[QR decomposition|0ERWG4NXQ9]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Principal angles describe how two subspaces sit relative to each other.
The first angle is the smallest angle between a vector of one subspace and a vector of the other.
The next angles repeat this in the directions that are left.

### L2 human

To compute the angles, take orthonormal bases as the columns of $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ and find the singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_p$ of $Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2$.
Then $\theta_k = \arccos \sigma_k$.
For two planes in $\R^3$ that are not equal, one angle is $0$ and the other is the angle between their normals.
The trap is to read the angles off the entries of $Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2$ instead of its singular values.

### L3 human

For subspaces of the same dimension $p$, the chordal distance $\norm{P_U - P_W}_F = \sqrt{2 \sum_k \sin^2 \theta_k}$ is a function of the principal angles, and it is a distance on the [[Grassmannian|CYPA882RB9]] of $p$-dimensional subspaces.
In statistics, the cosines of the principal angles between two column spaces of centred data are the [[canonical correlations|E0WKQ3XBAC]].
