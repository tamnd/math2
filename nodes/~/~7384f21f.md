## Summary

Interval temporal logics are [[temporal logics|8XB117FKKV]] whose formulas are true or false of time intervals rather than of time points.
Over a [[linear order|0FRXBVVR03]] $(T, <)$, an interval is a pair $[a, b]$ with $a < b$, and two intervals stand in exactly one of Allen's thirteen relations.
The logic of Halpern and Shoham (HS) has one modal operator for each of these relations except equality: $\langle A \rangle$ ("meets"), $\langle B \rangle$ ("begins"), $\langle E \rangle$ ("ends"), $\langle D \rangle$, $\langle O \rangle$, $\langle L \rangle$ and their inverses.
HS is undecidable over every class of linear orders with an infinite ascending or descending sequence, while some of its fragments are decidable.
Moszkowski's ITL, with the binary "chop" operator, is the other main interval logic.
Interval logics matter because many properties, such as "the light stays on during the whole meeting", concern durations, not instants.

## Motivation

Events take time: a meeting, a transaction, the rise of a signal.
A point-based logic describes an event by its start and end points and the points between them.
Many statements are more direct about intervals: "during every red phase, no car crosses".
Allen proposed thirteen relations between intervals for planning in artificial intelligence in 1983.
Halpern and Shoham made these relations into the accessibility relations of a [[multimodal logic|ERG3KAQWYF]] in 1986.
The price is high: an interval is a pair of points, so the logic behaves like a two-dimensional modal logic, and the natural logics are undecidable.

## Notation

1. Intervals: $[a, b]$ with $a < b$ (strict semantics), or with $a \le b$ (non-strict semantics, which allows point intervals).
2. Allen's relations of $[a, b]$ to $[c, d]$: before ($b < c$), meets ($b = c$), overlaps ($a < c < b < d$), starts ($a = c$, $b < d$), during ($c < a$, $b < d$), finishes ($c < a$, $b = d$), equal, and the six inverses.
3. HS modalities: $\langle X \rangle \varphi$ is true at $[a, b]$ when $\varphi$ is true at some $[c, d]$ related to $[a, b]$ by $X$. Here $A$ is "after" ($c = b$), $B$ is "begins" ($c = a$, $d < b$), $E$ is "ends" ($a < c$, $d = b$).
4. $\langle \bar X \rangle$ is the modality of the inverse relation, and $[X] = \neg\langle X \rangle\neg$.
5. ITL: $\varphi \mathbin{;} \psi$ ("chop") holds at $[a, b]$ when some $c$ with $a \le c \le b$ has $\varphi$ at $[a, c]$ and $\psi$ at $[c, b]$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The thirteen relations for concrete intervals of integers, and why they are exhaustive and exclusive, are in [[the example|~6792febb]].
2. Over $(\mathbb{N}, <)$, $[B]\bot$ is true exactly at the intervals $[a, a + 1]$ of length one, see [[the example|~ad02e890]].
3. $\langle A \rangle p$ at $[a, b]$ says that $p$ holds on some interval that starts where $[a, b]$ ends. With $p$ = "green", it reads "green starts as soon as this interval ends".
4. $[D]\,\neg\mathit{cross}$ at a red-phase interval says that no crossing interval lies strictly inside it.

### Non-examples

1. Two intervals in a partially ordered time need not stand in any of Allen's relations, see [[the non-example|~4a23b04d]].
2. A point-based formula such as LTL's $Fp$ is not directly an interval formula. It needs a translation that reads $p$ as "true at the left end".

### Basic properties

1. Over a linear order, any two intervals stand in exactly one of the thirteen relations.
2. Every Allen relation is a composition of the relations $A$, $B$, $E$ and their inverses. For example, "during" is $B$ followed by $E$, and "before" is $A$ followed by $A$.
3. So HS can be built from the six modalities $\langle A \rangle$, $\langle B \rangle$, $\langle E \rangle$ and their inverses.
4. HS validity is undecidable over every class of linear orders that contains an order with an infinite ascending or descending sequence, see [[the undecidability theorem|~1fb6205c]].
5. The fragment with only $\langle A \rangle$ and $\langle \bar A \rangle$, called propositional neighbourhood logic, is decidable over the natural numbers and over all linear orders.

### History

James Allen introduced the interval algebra in "Maintaining knowledge about temporal intervals", Communications of the ACM, 1983.
Ben Moszkowski developed ITL with the chop operator in his Stanford thesis of 1983, for hardware verification.
Halpern and Shoham introduced HS in 1986, with the journal version in the Journal of the ACM in 1991.
The decidable and undecidable fragments of HS have since been classified in detail by many authors.

### Common errors

1. Treating intervals as sets of points. Two different intervals can satisfy different formulas even if a formula at points cannot tell them apart.
2. Mixing strict and non-strict semantics. With point intervals, $[B]\bot$ holds at point intervals, not at unit intervals.
3. Reading "during" as "contained or equal". Allen's "during" requires both endpoints to be strictly inside.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Most temporal logics speak about moments: "at 3 o'clock the light is on".
Interval logics speak about stretches of time: "during the whole meeting the light is on".
They have operators like "at some interval that starts when this one ends" or "at some interval inside this one".
Such logics fit planning and hardware design, where things take time.

### L2 human

We evaluate $\langle B \rangle p$ at $[0, 5]$ over $\mathbb{N}$, with $p$ true exactly at the intervals $[a, a + 2]$.

1. $\langle B \rangle p$ asks for an interval $[0, d]$ with $0 < d < 5$ and $p$ true there.
2. The interval $[0, 2]$ qualifies.
3. At $[0, 2]$ itself, $\langle B \rangle p$ fails: the only candidate is $[0, 1]$, of length one.

The typical use is to describe the shape of a behaviour in time, such as "every request interval begins with an acknowledgement interval".
A trap: the strict and non-strict semantics give different logics, so the semantics must be fixed first.

### L3 human

An interval $[a, b]$ is a point $(a, b)$ above the diagonal in $T \times T$, so HS is a two-dimensional modal logic.
Its frames contain grid-like structures, and undecidability follows from [[tiling problems|AJH5HPD2B7]].
The decidable fragments avoid grids, for example by having only modalities that move in one direction.
Venema's logic CDT, built from chop and two related binary operators, extends this view.
