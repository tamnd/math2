## Statement

Let $T$ be a theory, a set of sentences, in a first-order language $L$.
Let $T' = T \cup D$ be an [[extension by definitions|GCK0NN60E4]] of $T$ in the language $L' \supseteq L$.
So $D$ has, for each new relation symbol $R$, an axiom $\forall \bar x \, (R(\bar x) \leftrightarrow \psi_R(\bar x))$, and for each new function symbol $f$, an axiom $\forall \bar x \, \forall y \, (f(\bar x) = y \leftrightarrow \psi_f(\bar x, y))$ with $T \vdash \forall \bar x \, \exists ! y \, \psi_f(\bar x, y)$.
The new symbols are listed in an order in which each definiens uses only symbols of $L$ and earlier new symbols; the condition on $\psi_f$ is then required in the theory built so far.
Then the following hold.

1. Unique expansion: every [[model|MVCH6N9SNW]] of $T$ has exactly one expansion to an $L'$-structure that is a model of $T'$.
2. Conservativity: for every $L$-sentence $\sigma$, $T' \vdash \sigma$ if and only if $T \vdash \sigma$. So $T'$ is a [[conservative extension|N238J43XNE]] of $T$.
3. Eliminability: for every $L'$-formula $\varphi$ there is an $L$-formula $\varphi^*$ with the same free variables such that $T' \vdash \forall \bar z \, (\varphi \leftrightarrow \varphi^*)$, where $\bar z$ lists the free variables of $\varphi$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Existence for function symbols. Over the theory of fields, a symbol $s$ with $\forall x \, (s(x) \cdot s(x) = x)$ gives a [[non-conservative extension|~c70bac1c]], because $2$ has no square root in $\mathbb{Q}$.
- Uniqueness for function symbols. With $T = \set{\exists x \, \exists y \, (x \neq y)}$ in the pure language of equality, the formula $y = y$ has existence but not uniqueness, and the axiom $\forall y \, (c = y \leftrightarrow y = y)$ contradicts $T$.
- The definiens must not use the symbol that it defines. The "definition" $\forall x \, (R(x) \leftrightarrow \lnot R(x))$ is inconsistent, so it proves every $L$-sentence.
- Existence and uniqueness must be provable in $T$, not only true in one model. With $T = \varnothing$ in the language $\set{P}$ and $\psi(y) = P(y)$, the axiom $\forall y \, (c = y \leftrightarrow P(y))$ proves the new $L$-sentence $\exists ! y \, P(y)$.

### Consequences

- A theorem proved with defined symbols has a translation into the original language, and the translation is a theorem of $T$.
- Defined symbols can be used freely in the axioms of a later schema; for example, the induction schema of PA with $\le$ is equivalent to the induction schema without it.
- The definable sets and functions of a model of $T$ do not change when $T$ is replaced by $T'$, by part 3.

### Converse

Part 1 has a converse, by [[Beth's definability theorem|RJBDSA2ETP]].
Let $T'$ be any $L'$-theory that contains $T$, such that every model of $T$ has exactly one expansion to a model of $T'$.
Then each new symbol has an explicit definition in $T'$, and $T'$ has the same models as an extension by definitions of $T$.
Conservativity alone has no such converse: adding a constant with no axioms is conservative, but it is not an extension by definitions.

### Generalizations

- The theorem holds for many-sorted logic, and for new sorts defined as quotients of definable sets by definable equivalence relations, which gives the construction $T^{\mathrm{eq}}$ of model theory; see [[interpretations and imaginaries|SZRM3N6BXW]].

## History

Shoenfield's textbook *Mathematical Logic* of 1967 states the theorem for first-order theories and proves it syntactically.
Beth proved the definability theorem, which gives the converse of part 1, in 1953.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A definition gives a new word for something we can already describe.
The theorem says three things about such new words.
Every situation that obeys the old rules gives the new word exactly one meaning.
The new word proves no new facts in the old words.
Every sentence with the new word can be rewritten in the old words.

### L2 human

Take the theory of groups and define $i(x)$ as the unique $y$ with $x \cdot y = e \land y \cdot x = e$.

1. Part 1: in the group $\mathbb{Z}$ with $+$ and $0$, the only possible interpretation of $i$ is $n \mapsto -n$.
2. Part 3: the sentence $\forall x \, (i(i(x)) = x)$ becomes $\forall x \, \exists y \, (x \cdot y = e \land y \cdot x = e \land y \cdot x = e \land x \cdot y = e)$, as the [[example on group inverses|~96859b6c]] computes.
3. Part 2: the translation is equivalent to the inverse axiom of groups, so it is a theorem of group theory, and so is the original sentence in $T'$.

The typical use is to prove a statement with convenient notation and then to know that it holds in the original theory.
A trap is to apply the theorem to a function symbol before the uniqueness proof is done.

### L3 human

The three parts are the semantic, the proof-theoretic and the syntactic faces of one fact: a defined symbol is determined by the old structure.
The semantic face is unique expansion, the proof-theoretic face is conservativity, and the syntactic face is the translation $\varphi \mapsto \varphi^*$.
The same pattern recurs in [[relative consistency proofs|X3G0RG2GPZ]], where an interpretation translates a theory into another one, and in the notion of definitional equivalence of theories.
