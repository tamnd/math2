## Example

In the language of arithmetic with $<$ and $>$, let $e_1$ be $\varepsilon y\, (y = 2)$ and $e_2$ be $\varepsilon x\, (x > e_1)$.
The term $e_1$ occurs inside $e_2$.
Take the two critical formulas

$$C_a : \; 2 = 2 \to e_1 = 2, \qquad C_b : \; 3 > e_1 \to e_2 > e_1.$$

A substitution gives values to the terms $\varepsilon y\, (y = 2)$ and $\varepsilon x\, (x > n)$ for numerals $n$, and the value $0$ to every term without an assigned value.
To evaluate $e_2$, we first evaluate $e_1$ to a numeral $n$, and then take the value of $\varepsilon x\, (x > n)$.
We run the [[epsilon substitution method|~de0d8cf6]] and repair $C_b$ first.

1. $S_0$ assigns no values. So $e_1$ has the value $0$, and $e_2$ is evaluated as $\varepsilon x\, (x > 0)$, with the value $0$.
2. Under $S_0$, $C_a$ is false: $2 = 2$ is true and $0 = 2$ is false.
3. Under $S_0$, $C_b$ is false: $3 > 0$ is true and $0 > 0$ is false.
4. We repair $C_b$. Its premise shows that $3$ is a witness for $x > 0$, and the least witness is $1$. $S_1$ gives $\varepsilon x\, (x > 0)$ the value $1$.
5. Under $S_1$, $C_b$ is true, since $1 > 0$. $C_a$ is still false, since $e_1$ still has the value $0$.
6. We repair $C_a$. The least witness for $y = 2$ is $2$. $S_2$ adds the value $2$ for $e_1$.
7. Under $S_2$, $e_1$ has the value $2$, so $e_2$ is evaluated as $\varepsilon x\, (x > 2)$, which has no value yet. So $e_2$ has the value $0$.
8. Under $S_2$, $C_a$ is true. $C_b$ is false: $3 > 2$ is true and $0 > 2$ is false.
9. We repair $C_b$ again. The witness is $3$, and the least $m$ with $m > 2$ is $3$. $S_3$ gives $\varepsilon x\, (x > 2)$ the value $3$.
10. Under $S_3$, $e_1$ is $2$ and $e_2$ is $3$. $C_a$ is true, and $C_b$ is true since $3 > 2$.
11. So $S_3$ is a solving substitution, and the process stops after three steps.

## Remarks

Step 7 shows the new feature of nested terms: a change of the inner term $e_1$ changes which term $e_2$ stands for, and the earlier repair of $C_b$ is lost.
The value $1$ of $\varepsilon x\, (x > 0)$ stays in $S_3$, but it is no longer used.
If we repair $C_a$ first, the process takes two steps: $e_1$ gets $2$, and then $\varepsilon x\, (x > 2)$ gets $3$.
In a long proof, such losses can happen many times, and the termination proof must bound them; for arithmetic this needs induction up to $\varepsilon_0$.
Compare the single term of [[this example|~caf02120]], where one step was enough.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Here we have two unknown numbers, and the second one depends on the first.
The first is "the smallest number equal to $2$", and the second is "the smallest number larger than the first".
We guess $0$ for both and then repair the guesses one at a time.
When we change the first number from $0$ to $2$, our answer for the second number no longer fits, and we must repair it again.
At the end the first number is $2$ and the second is $3$.

### L2 human

The worked example is steps 1 to 11.
The typical use is to see why the order of repairs matters for the length of the process, but not for its result here.
A trap is to give $e_2$ one fixed value: its value depends on the value of $e_1$, through the term $\varepsilon x\, (x > n)$ that it stands for.
