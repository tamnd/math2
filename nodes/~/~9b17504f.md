## Example

Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -4 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Both matrices are nonzero.

1. The entry $(1, 1)$ of $AB$ is $1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot (-1) = 0$, and the entry $(1, 2)$ is $1 \cdot (-4) + 2 \cdot 2 = 0$.
2. The entry $(2, 1)$ of $AB$ is $2 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot (-1) = 0$, and the entry $(2, 2)$ is $2 \cdot (-4) + 4 \cdot 2 = 0$.
3. So $AB$ is the [[zero matrix|~d9ded4cd]] $0_{2 \times 2}$, and $A$ is a left [[zero divisor|0NNRDW10RZ]] in $M_2(\mathbb{R})$.
4. In the other order, $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 8 & 4 - 16 \\ -1 + 4 & -2 + 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -6 & -12 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix} \neq 0$.
5. Cancellation fails: $A B = A \cdot 0$, but $B \neq 0$.

## Remarks

The second row of $A$ is twice the first row, and each column of $B$ is a multiple of $(2, -1)^{\mathsf{T}}$, which is orthogonal to the rows of $A$.
In terms of later notions, $A$ is not [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]], and the columns of $B$ lie in the kernel of $A$.
Step 4 shows also that $AB$ and $BA$ can differ even when one of them is zero.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For numbers, a product is zero only when a factor is zero.
For matrices this rule fails: here neither matrix is zero, but their product is.

### L2 human

The product $AB$ is zero exactly when each column of $B$ is a solution of $Ax = 0$.
A nonzero solution exists here because the rows of $A$ are proportional.
