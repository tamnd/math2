## Statement

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$ and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear.
Then there are an integer $r \ge 0$ and nonzero vectors $v_1, \dots, v_r \in V$ such that:

1. $V = Z(v_1; T) \oplus \cdots \oplus Z(v_r; T)$, an [[internal direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] of [[$T$-cyclic subspaces|~33677c91]];
2. the $T$-annihilators $d_i = m_{v_i}$ are nonconstant monic polynomials with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$, where $\mid$ means [[divides|0PG2H7WKGG]].

The number $r$ and the polynomials $d_1, \dots, d_r$ are uniquely determined by $T$: they do not depend on the choice of the $v_i$.
In addition, $d_r = m_T$, the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]], and $d_1 d_2 \cdots d_r = \chi_T$, the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]].
The polynomials $d_1, \dots, d_r$ are the [[invariant factors|~bcfda459]] of $T$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Finite dimension is needed: the multiplication by $x$ on $F[x]$ has no nonzero annihilator, and $F[x]$ is not a sum of finite-dimensional cyclic subspaces.
The divisibility condition is needed for uniqueness.
Without it, $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ has the two decompositions $Fe_1 \oplus Fe_2$, with annihilators $x - 1$ and $x - 2$, and $Z((1, 1))$, with annihilator $(x - 1)(x - 2)$.
The requirement that the $d_i$ are nonconstant excludes summands $Z(0; T) = 0$.

### Consequences

- In the basis made of the bases $v_i, T v_i, \dots$ of the summands, $T$ has the [[rational canonical form|042JWAR3Q0]] $C(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus C(d_r)$.
- $T$ is cyclic if and only if $r = 1$.
- $r$ is the least number of $T$-cyclic subspaces whose sum is $V$, and $r = \max_\lambda \dim \ker(T - \lambda)$ over all $\lambda$ in an algebraic closure.
- $m_T$ and $\chi_T$ have the same irreducible factors, since $d_i \mid d_r$ for each $i$.

### Converse

Conversely, if $V$ is such a direct sum with annihilators $e_1 \mid \cdots \mid e_t$, then $t = r$ and $e_i = d_i$; this is the uniqueness part.
The vectors $v_i$ themselves are not unique.

### Generalizations

The theorem is the structure theorem for finitely generated torsion modules over the principal ideal domain $F[x]$.
The same theorem over $\mathbb{Z}$ is the [[structure theorem for finite abelian groups|0RY0B72XAS]].
Hoffman and Kunze prove a stronger form: each $T$-admissible subspace $W_0$ has a complement of the form $Z(v_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus Z(v_r)$.
They list the annihilators in the opposite order, with $p_1 = m_T$ and $p_{k} \mid p_{k-1}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear map can be complicated on a big space.
The theorem cuts the space into pieces, and on each piece the map has the simplest possible form: it pushes one vector along a chain of vectors.
Each piece has its own polynomial, and the polynomials divide each other.
These polynomials are a complete description of the map up to a change of basis.

### L2 human

Let $A = \operatorname{diag}(2, 2, 3)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

1. $m_A = (x - 2)(x - 3)$ and $\chi_A = (x - 2)^2 (x - 3)$.
2. Take $v_2 = e_1 + e_3$. Its annihilator is $(x - 2)(x - 3) = m_A$, so $d_2 = x^2 - 5x + 6$ and $Z(v_2) = \operatorname{span}\set{e_1, e_3}$.
3. Take $v_1 = e_2$, with annihilator $d_1 = x - 2$ and $Z(v_1) = \mathbb{Q} e_2$.
4. Then $\mathbb{Q}^3 = Z(v_1) \oplus Z(v_2)$, and $d_1 \mid d_2$.
5. Check: $d_1 d_2 = (x - 2)^2 (x - 3) = \chi_A$ and $d_2 = m_A$.

The typical use is to compute the invariant factors and then the rational canonical form.
The trap is to take the decomposition $\mathbb{Q}e_1 \oplus \mathbb{Q}e_2 \oplus \mathbb{Q}e_3$: it is a decomposition into cyclic subspaces, but the annihilators $x - 2, x - 2, x - 3$ do not divide each other.

### L3 human

For the module $V_T$ over the principal ideal domain $F[x]$, the theorem is the invariant factor form of the structure theorem, with no free part.
The elementary divisor form splits each $Z(v_i)$ further into cyclic pieces with annihilators $p^e$, $p$ irreducible, and over an algebraically closed field this gives the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].
The same theorem classifies finite abelian groups, and the analogy between $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$ runs through the whole theory.
