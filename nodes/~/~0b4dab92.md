## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over $F$, and let $f \colon V \to W$ be a linear map.
Let $f^* \colon W^* \to V^*$ be its [[dual map|0C2TQMW5A1]], and write $S^0$ for the [[annihilator|0R6M158SQ9]] of a subspace $S$.

1. $\ker f^* = (\im f)^0$, where $\ker$ is the [[kernel|02YFME3RGW]] and $\im$ is the [[image|0JB228DCWB]].
2. $\im f^* = (\ker f)^0$.
3. $f$ is injective if and only if $f^*$ is surjective. Also, $f$ is surjective if and only if $f^*$ is injective.
4. If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $\operatorname{rank} f^* = \operatorname{rank} f$, where $\operatorname{rank}$ is the [[rank|0JGB7A6GTS]].
5. Let $f_1, \dots, f_k, g \in V^*$. If $\ker f_1 \cap \dots \cap \ker f_k \subseteq \ker g$, then $g$ is a linear combination of $f_1, \dots, f_k$.

Parts 1, 2, 3 and 5 hold in any dimension.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Part 4 needs $\dim V < \infty$. For $V = W$ infinite-dimensional and $f = \id$, both ranks are infinite cardinals, and $\operatorname{rank} f^* = \dim V^*$ is strictly larger than $\operatorname{rank} f = \dim V$, by the [[Erdős-Kaplansky theorem|~ecdd288b]].
- Part 5 needs finitely many forms. On $V = F^{(\N)}$, the coordinate forms $e_i^*$ have common kernel $\{0\}$. The form $g$ with $g(e_i) = 1$ for all $i$ is not a finite linear combination of them.

### Consequences

- In finite dimension, part 4 and the theorem on the [[matrix of the dual map|0ZPB3180AY]] give the equality of row rank and column rank; see the [[proof by duality|~8fe2a51d]] of [[row rank equals column rank|0TZAG60ZYT]].
- Part 5 is the key step in the description of [[linear forms with the same kernel|~577d8228]]: for $k = 1$, $\ker f_1 \subseteq \ker g$ gives $g = c f_1$.
- For a linear system $Ax = b$, part 1 and the dimension formula for annihilators give a solvability test: $b \in \im A$ if and only if $y b = 0$ for every row $y$ with $y A = 0$.

### Converse

Part 5 has a converse: if $g = \sum c_i f_i$, then $g$ vanishes where all $f_i$ vanish.
So $\ker f_1 \cap \dots \cap \ker f_k \subseteq \ker g$ holds if and only if $g \in \operatorname{span}(f_1, \dots, f_k)$.

### Generalizations

- For bounded linear maps between Banach spaces, functional analysis proves analogues with continuous duals and closures, such as $\ker T' = (\im T)^\perp$.
- In finite dimension, the theorem together with the [[double dual|0ZJM9D7NPS]] gives $\ker f = (\im f^*)^0$ inside $V^{**} \cong V$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The dual map turns measurements on $W$ into measurements on $V$.
A measurement on $W$ is sent to zero exactly when it is zero on every output of $f$.
The measurements on $V$ that come from $W$ are exactly those that are zero on the kernel of $f$.
So the dual map sees the image and the kernel of $f$ the other way round.

### L2 human

We check parts 1 and 2 for $f \colon \R^3 \to \R^2$, $f(x, y, z) = (x + y,\ x + y)$.

1. The image of $f$ is the line $\im f = \{(t, t)\}$. Its annihilator is spanned by the form $(a, b) \mapsto a - b$.
2. For $\varphi(a, b) = pa + qb$, the form $f^*(\varphi)$ is $(x, y, z) \mapsto (p + q)(x + y)$.
3. So $f^*(\varphi) = 0$ if and only if $q = -p$, that is, $\varphi$ is a multiple of $a - b$. This is part 1.
4. The kernel of $f$ is $\{(x, y, z) : x + y = 0\}$, spanned by $(1, -1, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 1)$.
5. A form $(x, y, z) \mapsto rx + sy + tz$ vanishes on these vectors if and only if $r = s$ and $t = 0$. These are the multiples of $x + y$.
6. By step 2, $\im f^*$ is also the set of multiples of $x + y$. This is part 2.
7. Both ranks are $1$, as part 4 says.

The typical use is to prove that $f$ is surjective by showing that $f^*$ is injective, which is often easier.
A trap: part 4 is about finite dimension; the image of $f^*$ is a subspace of $V^*$, not of $W^*$.

### L3 human

Parts 1 and 2 say that the dual functor $V \mapsto V^*$ is exact: it sends exact sequences to exact sequences.
Part 2 depends on the fact that every linear form on a subspace extends to the whole space, which uses a basis and so the axiom of choice in infinite dimension.
Over a commutative ring $R$, the functor $\Hom(-, R)$ is only left exact, and part 2 can fail.
For example, multiplication by $2$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ has dual map multiplication by $2$, whose image $2\mathbb{Z}$ is not $(\ker 2)^0 = \Hom(\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z})$.
