## Example

Take the lexicon: "the" has type $np / n$, "dog" has type $n$, and "sleeps" has type $np \backslash s$.
The sequent
$$n, \; np / n, \; np \backslash s \Rightarrow s$$
is not derivable in the [[Lambek calculus|~19f422b8]], so "dog the sleeps" is not a sentence of this grammar, while "the dog sleeps" is.

1. The calculus has cut elimination, by the method of [[cut elimination for the full Lambek calculus|~1050a703]].
   So it suffices to show that there is no cut-free derivation.
2. The right side $s$ is an atom and the antecedent has three formulas, so the sequent is not an axiom, and no right rule applies.
   The last rule is ${/}\mathrm{L}$ on $np / n$ or ${\backslash}\mathrm{L}$ on $np \backslash s$.
3. ${/}\mathrm{L}$ on $np / n$ needs a nonempty list $\Gamma$ right after $np / n$ with $\Gamma \Rightarrow n$.
   The only choice is $\Gamma = np \backslash s$.
4. The sequent $np \backslash s \Rightarrow n$ is not derivable.
   It is not an axiom, and ${\backslash}\mathrm{L}$ needs a nonempty list to the left of $np \backslash s$, which is missing.
5. ${\backslash}\mathrm{L}$ on $np \backslash s$ needs a nonempty list $\Gamma$ right before it with $\Gamma \Rightarrow np$.
   The choices are $\Gamma = np / n$ and $\Gamma = n, np / n$.
6. $np / n \Rightarrow np$ is not derivable: it is not an axiom, and ${/}\mathrm{L}$ needs a nonempty list to the right of $np / n$.
7. $n, np / n \Rightarrow np$ is not derivable: the only possible rule is ${/}\mathrm{L}$ on $np / n$, and it needs a nonempty list to the right of $np / n$.
8. Every possible last rule has a premise that is not derivable, so the sequent is not derivable.

## Remarks

The count check of [[the definition|~19f422b8]] does not detect this failure.
For $n$ the sum is $1 - 1 + 0 = 0$, for $np$ it is $0 + 1 - 1 = 0$, and for $s$ it is $1$, which match the right side $s$.
So the order of the types, and not their number, blocks the derivation.
With exchange, the sequent becomes derivable: exchange turns it into $np / n, n, np \backslash s \Rightarrow s$, which is the parse of "the dog sleeps".
This is why a grammar logic must not have exchange.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"The" needs a noun on its right.
In "dog the sleeps", the noun "dog" is on the left of "the", so "the" cannot use it.
The words have the right types, but in the wrong order.
The Lambek calculus checks the order, so it rejects the string.

### L2 human

We compare the search with the parse of "the dog sleeps".

1. For $np / n, n, np \backslash s \Rightarrow s$, the rule ${/}\mathrm{L}$ on $np / n$ takes $\Gamma = n$, and $n \Rightarrow n$ is an axiom.
2. Its other premise is $np, np \backslash s \Rightarrow s$, which follows from axioms by ${\backslash}\mathrm{L}$.
3. For $n, np / n, np \backslash s \Rightarrow s$, the formula right after $np / n$ is $np \backslash s$, and $np \backslash s \Rightarrow n$ fails.

The typical use of such a search is to show that a grammar rejects a string.
A trap: we must try every possible last rule, and every way to split the antecedent, before we conclude that a sequent is not derivable.
