## Example

Consider Gentzen's calculus [[LK|PZX9J3VEMT]] with sequents $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta$, where $\Gamma$ and $\Delta$ are finite sequences of formulas.
Its structural rules are [[weakening|VHRXX0T5XT]], contraction and [[exchange|YAFD18WYJ8]] on each side.
Gentzen also counts cut as a structural rule, but cut needs a second premise and changes the formulas, so we leave it out here.
Take the sequent
$$p, q \Rightarrow r .$$
The display property of the [[definition of display calculus|~c1a4d015]] would require a sequent $p \Rightarrow W$, obtained from $p, q \Rightarrow r$ by structural rules alone, with the same meaning.
We show that no sequent with antecedent $p$ alone can be reached by structural rules.

**Claim.** Every sequent obtained from $p, q \Rightarrow r$ by structural rules of LK has at least one occurrence of $q$ in its antecedent.

1. The claim holds for $p, q \Rightarrow r$ itself.
2. Weakening adds a formula to the antecedent or to the succedent. It removes nothing, so an occurrence of $q$ stays.
3. Exchange permutes two neighbouring formulas on one side. It keeps every formula on its side, so an occurrence of $q$ stays.
4. Contraction replaces two equal neighbouring formulas $A, A$ on one side by one $A$. If it acts on $q$, one occurrence of $q$ stays; otherwise all occurrences stay.
5. So each structural rule turns a sequent with $q$ in its antecedent into another one, and the claim follows by induction on the number of steps.

**Consequence.**

6. A sequent of the form $p \Rightarrow W$ has antecedent $p$, which has no occurrence of $q$.
7. By the claim, no such sequent arises from $p, q \Rightarrow r$ by structural rules.

So LK cannot display $p$ in this sequent.
In the display calculus, D1 turns $p \circ q \vdash r$ into $p \vdash {*}q \circ r$ in one step.

## Remarks

The condition that fails is the display property, and the reason is a missing structural connective.
LK has a structural conjunction, the comma on the left, and a structural disjunction, the comma on the right.
It has no structural negation, so no structural rule moves $q$ to the other side.
The logical rule ${\to}$R does give $p \Rightarrow q \to r$, but it changes the formulas, so it is not a display step.

The failure is harmless for LK itself, because Gentzen's proof of cut elimination handles each rule by hand.
It matters when we want one cut-elimination proof for a family of calculi, which is Belnap's aim.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In a display calculus, any piece of a sequent can be moved to stand alone, without changing the meaning.
In Gentzen's calculus the bookkeeping rules can add, copy or reorder formulas, but they never move a formula across the arrow.
So in "$p$ and $q$ give $r$", the assumption $q$ always stays on the left.
That means $p$ can never stand alone on the left, and the display property fails.

### L2 human

The argument is an invariant.

1. Choose a property of sequents: "$q$ occurs in the antecedent".
2. Check that the starting sequent has it, and that each structural rule preserves it.
3. Conclude that every reachable sequent has it, so $p$ never stands alone.

The typical use of an invariant is to show that some target can never be reached by a given set of moves.
A trap is to count the logical rule ${\to}$R as a structural move: it builds a new formula $q \to r$.
