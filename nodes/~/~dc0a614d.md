## Example

In classical logic, a [[truth valuation|H31CS3HK5P]] maps each proposition to $0$ or $1$ and turns "and" into minimum and "or" into maximum.
We show that the [[quantum logic|TWEYQ0XAY4]] $\mathcal{L}(\R^2)$ has no such map.

Claim: there is no map $h \colon \mathcal{L}(\R^2) \to \set{0, 1}$ with $h(M \wedge N) = \min(h(M), h(N))$ and $h(M \vee N) = \max(h(M), h(N))$ for all $M, N$, and $h(0) = 0$, $h(\R^2) = 1$.

1. Let $M \neq N$ be lines through the origin. Their intersection is $\set{0}$, and their span is $\R^2$. So $M \wedge N = 0$ and $M \vee N = \R^2$.
2. So $\min(h(M), h(N)) = h(0) = 0$ and $\max(h(M), h(N)) = h(\R^2) = 1$. Hence exactly one of $h(M)$ and $h(N)$ is $1$, and $h(M) + h(N) = 1$.
3. Take three distinct lines $a$, $b$, $c$. By step 2, $h(a) + h(b) = 1$, $h(a) + h(c) = 1$ and $h(b) + h(c) = 1$.
4. Adding the three equations gives $2\,(h(a) + h(b) + h(c)) = 3$. The left side is even and the right side is odd. Contradiction.

So the connectives of [[quantum logic|2E9DMB7A6Q]] are not truth-functional: no assignment of truth values to all propositions respects meet and join.

## Remarks

Every Boolean algebra with $0 \neq 1$ has such a map, by an ultrafilter; so this argument shows again that $\mathcal{L}(\R^2)$ is not Boolean.
The claim does not use the orthocomplement.
A weaker requirement asks only that, for each orthogonal pair of lines, exactly one gets the value $1$.
In $\R^2$ this weaker requirement can be met, because each line is orthogonal to exactly one other line.
In dimension at least $3$ it fails too, by [[the Kochen-Specker theorem|X2RF2WKWV9]], which uses only orthonormal bases inside a finite set of rays.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Try to call each line in the plane "true" or "false", so that "and" and "or" behave as usual.
Any two different lines have "and" equal to the origin, which is false, and "or" equal to the plane, which is true.
So exactly one of any two lines is true, and with three lines that is impossible.

### L2 human

The argument is a parity count over three pairs of lines.
It shows that quantum propositions cannot all have definite truth values that respect meet and join.
The Kochen-Specker theorem is a much stronger form: it keeps the classical rules only inside each orthonormal basis, and it still finds a contradiction in dimension $3$.
A trap is to think that the Kochen-Specker theorem is needed for the claim here; meet and join alone already rule it out.
