## Statement

Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, let $U$ be a subspace of $V$, and let $W$ be a [[complement|04C3BAHV03]] of $U$, so $V = U \oplus W$.
Let $\pi \colon V \to V / U$ be the [[quotient map|0J0SK5FF4M]] onto the [[quotient space|0SVR29SV1V]].
Then the restriction
$$\pi|_W \colon W \to V / U, \qquad w \mapsto w + U,$$
is an [[isomorphism|0XSJ25Z9M4]].
In particular, any two complements of $U$ are isomorphic.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $U \cap W = \{0\}$ is needed for injectivity. If $W = V$, then $U + W = V$ but $\pi|_V = \pi$ has kernel $U$, which is nonzero unless $U = \{0\}$.
- $U + W = V$ is needed for surjectivity. If $W = \{0\}$, then $U \cap W = \{0\}$, but the image of $\pi|_W$ is $\{0\}$, which is not $V / U$ unless $U = V$.

### Consequences

- In finite dimension, $\dim W = \dim(V / U) = \dim V - \dim U$ for every complement $W$, using the [[dimension of a quotient|0SXDQ6TN00]].
- The inverse map $V / U \to W$ picks out, in each coset $v + U$, the unique vector that lies in $W$.
- Since complements exist, every quotient $V / U$ is isomorphic to a subspace of $V$. So $V \cong U \oplus V / U$, as an external direct sum.

### Converse

If $W$ is a subspace such that $\pi|_W$ is an isomorphism, then $W$ is a complement of $U$.
Injectivity gives $U \cap W = \{0\}$, and surjectivity gives $U + W = V$.
So the complements of $U$ are exactly the subspaces that $\pi$ maps isomorphically onto $V / U$.

## History

The identification of a complement with the quotient is implicit in the early treatments of quotient spaces, for example in Halmos's *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces* of 1942.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Each coset $v + U$ meets a complement $W$ in exactly one point.
So the points of $W$ label the cosets one to one, and this labelling respects the operations.

### L2 human

Let $U$ be the $x$-axis in $\R^2$ and $W = \R(1, 1)$.

1. The cosets of $U$ are the horizontal lines $y = c$.
2. The line $y = c$ meets $W$ in the single point $(c, c)$.
3. So $\pi|_W$ sends $(c, c)$ to the line $y = c$, and this is a bijection.
4. Both $W$ and $V / U$ are then identified with $\R$ by $c$.

The trap is to think that the isomorphism depends only on $U$: it depends on the choice of the complement $W$.

### L3 human

The theorem is a special case of the [[first isomorphism theorem|0MTBGVZ4XJ]]: the map $\pi|_W$ is surjective with kernel $U \cap W = \{0\}$.
Equivalently, it says that the short exact sequence $0 \to U \to V \to V / U \to 0$ splits through the inclusion of $W$.
There is no canonical isomorphism between $V / U$ and a subspace of $V$, since the choice of a complement is not canonical.
