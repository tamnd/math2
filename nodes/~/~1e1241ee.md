## Statement

Let $\mathfrak{N}$ be the [[standard structure|WP27CABPA4]] $(\N, 0, S, +, \cdot, <)$, and let $\mathsf{Q}$ be [[Robinson arithmetic|TT8MXRTVBM]].

1. For every $\Delta_0$ sentence $\delta$: if $\mathfrak{N} \models \delta$ then $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \delta$, and if $\mathfrak{N} \not\models \delta$ then $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \neg \delta$.
2. For every $\Sigma_1$ sentence $\varphi$: if $\mathfrak{N} \models \varphi$ then $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \varphi$.

The classes $\Delta_0$ and $\Sigma_1$ are those of the [[definition of the formula classes|~29cc9691]].
Part 2 applies to a $\Sigma_1$ formula $\varphi(x_1, \ldots, x_k)$ through its instances: if $\mathfrak{N} \models \varphi(n_1, \ldots, n_k)$, then $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \varphi(\overline{n_1}, \ldots, \overline{n_k})$.
The same holds for every theory that extends $\mathsf{Q}$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The restriction to $\Sigma_1$ is needed. The $\Pi_1$ sentence $\forall x\,(0 + x = x)$ is true in $\mathfrak{N}$, but it is false in [[a model of Q|~1e15aa46]], so $\mathsf{Q}$ does not prove it, by [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]].
- Truth in $\mathfrak{N}$ is needed. $\mathfrak{N}$ is a model of $\mathsf{Q}$, so $\mathsf{Q}$ proves no sentence that is false in $\mathfrak{N}$.
- Part 1 decides every $\Delta_0$ sentence, but Part 2 does not refute false $\Sigma_1$ sentences: a false $\Sigma_1$ sentence has a true $\Pi_1$ negation, which $\mathsf{Q}$ may fail to prove, as in the first item.

### Converse

For a $\Sigma_1$ sentence $\varphi$, $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \varphi$ implies $\mathfrak{N} \models \varphi$, since $\mathfrak{N} \models \mathsf{Q}$.
So $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \varphi$ if and only if $\mathfrak{N} \models \varphi$, for $\Sigma_1$ sentences $\varphi$.
For an extension $T$ of $\mathsf{Q}$ the converse can fail: a [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]] $T$ may prove a false $\Sigma_1$ sentence.

### Consequences

- A consistent theory $T$ that extends $\mathsf{Q}$ proves no false $\Pi_1$ sentence. If $T \vdash \pi$ with $\pi$ a false $\Pi_1$ sentence, then $\neg \pi$ is equivalent to a true $\Sigma_1$ sentence, which $T$ proves by Part 2, and $T$ is inconsistent.
- The true $\Sigma_1$ sentences are exactly the $\Sigma_1$ theorems of $\mathsf{Q}$, so this set is [[computably enumerable|TN9G24VABN]].
- Part 2 is one half of the representability of computable functions in $\mathsf{Q}$; [[the representability theorem|~d5e5ada1]] uses it for the graph of a function.
- By [[Gödel's first incompleteness theorem|X9WYNYE29C]], a consistent recursively axiomatized extension of $\mathsf{Q}$ leaves some true $\Pi_1$ sentence unproved. Part 2 shows that no true $\Sigma_1$ sentence is such an example.

### Generalizations

The theorem is provable inside stronger theories.
In $\mathsf{PA}$, with a [[provability predicate|NB2BCZR40F]] $\mathrm{Prov}$ for $\mathsf{PA}$, every $\Sigma_1$ sentence $\sigma$ satisfies $\mathsf{PA} \vdash \sigma \to \mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner \sigma \urcorner)$.
This formalized form is the key step in the proof of the second incompleteness theorem.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A $\Sigma_1$ sentence says "there is a number with a property that can be checked by a finite computation".
If such a sentence is true, a witness exists, and the finite check for that witness can be written out as a proof.
The theorem says that the very weak axioms of Robinson arithmetic are enough to write out that proof.
So $\mathsf{Q}$ proves every true statement of the form "this search succeeds".
It may fail to prove true statements of the form "this search never succeeds".

### L2 human

We prove the $\Sigma_1$ sentence $\exists x\,(x \cdot x = \overline{9})$ in $\mathsf{Q}$.

1. The witness is $3$, since $3 \cdot 3 = 9$ in $\N$.
2. $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \overline{3} \cdot \overline{3} = \overline{9}$, by $\mathsf{Q}6$, $\mathsf{Q}7$, $\mathsf{Q}4$ and $\mathsf{Q}5$ applied to numerals.
3. Existential generalization on $\overline{3}$ gives $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \exists x\,(x \cdot x = \overline{9})$.

The typical use of the theorem is to show that a theory extending $\mathsf{Q}$ proves the true instances of a computable relation.
A trap is to conclude that $\mathsf{Q}$ refutes false $\Sigma_1$ sentences: $\exists x\,(0 + x \ne x)$ is false in $\mathfrak{N}$, but $\mathsf{Q}$ does not refute it.

### L3 human

The theorem marks the exact strength of $\mathsf{Q}$: it is complete for $\Sigma_1$ sentences and incomplete for $\Pi_1$ sentences.
A theory is $\Sigma_1$-sound when every $\Sigma_1$ sentence it proves is true; by the theorem, a $\Sigma_1$-sound extension of $\mathsf{Q}$ proves the same $\Sigma_1$ sentences as $\mathsf{Q}$.
Gödel's first theorem in its original form assumes that the theory is $\omega$-consistent, and Rosser's trick weakens this hypothesis to consistency.
The third Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability condition, $\mathsf{PA} \vdash \mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner \sigma \urcorner) \to \mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner \mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner \sigma \urcorner) \urcorner)$, is the formalized form applied to the $\Sigma_1$ sentence $\mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner \sigma \urcorner)$.
