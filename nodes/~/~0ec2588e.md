## Example

Let $(W, R)$ be a Kripke frame with $W$ finite, and let $\mathfrak{g} = (W, R, \mathcal{P}(W))$.
We check the three conditions for a [[descriptive frame|~84a9e3fa]].

1. $\mathfrak{g}$ is a general frame, by [[full frames are general frames|~a5e848aa]].
2. Differentiated: if $u \neq v$, then $a = \set{u}$ contains $u$ and not $v$.
3. Tight: let $u R v$ fail, and let $a = \set{v}$. Then $m_R(a) = \set{w : w R v}$, and $u \notin m_R(a)$.
4. Compact: let $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(W)$ have the finite intersection property. Since $\mathcal{P}(W)$ is finite, $\mathcal{C}$ is finite. Then $\bigcap \mathcal{C}$ is the intersection of a finite subfamily, so it is nonempty.
5. So $\mathfrak{g}$ is differentiated, tight and compact, that is, descriptive.

For a concrete case, take $W = \set{0, 1}$ and $R = \set{(0, 1)}$.
For tightness at the pair $(1, 0)$, the set $a = \set{0}$ has $m_R(a) = \emptyset$, which does not contain $1$.

## Remarks

The example shows that, for finite frames, Kripke semantics and descriptive general frame semantics agree.
It is the reason why finite model property arguments never need general frames.
Algebraically, every finite modal algebra $\mathfrak{B}$ is the complex algebra of the finite Kripke frame of its atoms, and its dual frame is that Kripke frame.
If $W$ is infinite, step 4 fails for the full frame, and the dual of $\mathcal{P}(W)$ is the frame of all ultrafilters of $\mathcal{P}(W)$, which is the ultrafilter extension of $(W, R)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A frame with finitely many points has only finitely many regions.
Every sensible condition on the regions holds automatically, because nothing infinite can go wrong.
So a finite picture of points and arrows is already the most well-behaved kind of general frame.

### L2 human

1. Take $W = \set{0, 1, 2}$ with $0 R 1$, $1 R 2$ only, and all subsets admissible.
2. For tightness at the pair $(0, 2)$, take $a = \set{2}$. Then $m_R(a) = \set{1}$, and $0 \notin m_R(a)$.
3. For compactness, any family of subsets of a three-element set with the finite intersection property is finite, so it has a common point.

The typical use is in completeness proofs by finite models, where the finite countermodel is automatically a descriptive frame of the logic.
A trap: the example uses all subsets as admissible; a finite frame with fewer admissible sets need not be differentiated.
