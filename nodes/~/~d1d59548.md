## Example

On $V = \mathbb{F}_3^2$ let $B(x, y) = x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$.
Then $B(v, v) = v_1^2 + v_2^2$.

1. The squares in $\mathbb{F}_3$ are $0$ and $1$, since $1^2 = 2^2 = 1$.
2. If exactly one coordinate of $v$ is nonzero, then $B(v, v) = 1$.
3. If both coordinates are nonzero, then $B(v, v) = 1 + 1 = 2$.
4. So $B(v, v) \neq 0$ for every $v \neq 0$, and $B$ is anisotropic.
5. Another way: $v_1^2 + v_2^2 = 0$ with $v_2 \neq 0$ gives $(v_1 / v_2)^2 = -1 = 2$, but $2$ is not a square in $\mathbb{F}_3$.

## Remarks

Over $\mathbb{F}_5$ the same form is isotropic, since $2^2 = 4 = -1$ gives $B((1, 2), (1, 2)) = 1 + 4 = 0$.
In general, $x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$ with $q$ odd is isotropic exactly when $-1$ is a square, which happens exactly when $q \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.
Adding a third square makes the form isotropic over $\mathbb{F}_3$, as for [[the sum of three squares|~3d3707b4]].
An anisotropic form over a finite field has dimension at most $2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Over the field with three elements, the sum of two squares is never zero, unless both numbers are zero.
So the form $x^2 + y^2$ has no isotropic vector.
It behaves like the dot product on the real plane in this one respect.

### L2 human

To decide whether $a x^2 + b y^2$ is isotropic over a field, with $a, b \neq 0$, test whether $-b/a$ is a square.
Here $a = b = 1$, and $-1 = 2$ is not a square in $\mathbb{F}_3$.
The typical use is as the anisotropic part in the Witt decomposition of forms over $\mathbb{F}_3$.
A trap: anisotropic over $\mathbb{F}_3$ does not mean positive, since $\mathbb{F}_3$ has no order.
