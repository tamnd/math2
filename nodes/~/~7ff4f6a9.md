## Example

We work in the first-order language of groups, as in the [[elementary theory of groups|~0757d1ee]].
Let $T_{\mathrm{grp}}$ be the set of the three group axioms.
We show that the class of finite groups is not an [[elementary class|MEC3ZMC4F1]]: no set $\Sigma$ of sentences has as its models exactly the finite groups.

1. For each $n \ge 1$, let $\lambda_n$ be the sentence $\exists x_1 \cdots \exists x_n \bigwedge_{1 \le i < j \le n} x_i \ne x_j$. A group satisfies $\lambda_n$ if and only if it has at least $n$ elements.
2. Suppose, for a contradiction, that $\Sigma$ is a set of sentences such that a group satisfies $\Sigma$ if and only if it is finite.
3. Let $\Gamma = T_{\mathrm{grp}} \cup \Sigma \cup \{\lambda_n : n \ge 1\}$. Let $\Gamma_0$ be a finite subset of $\Gamma$, and let $N$ be the largest $n$ with $\lambda_n \in \Gamma_0$, or $N = 1$ if there is none.
4. The group $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$ is finite, so it satisfies $T_{\mathrm{grp}}$ and, by step 2, $\Sigma$. It has $N$ elements, so it satisfies $\lambda_n$ for $n \le N$. Thus it is a model of $\Gamma_0$.
5. By the [[compactness theorem|1NR3HAWG9D]], the set $\Gamma$ has a model $G$.
6. The group $G$ satisfies $\lambda_n$ for every $n$, so $G$ is infinite. It also satisfies $\Sigma$, so $G$ is finite by step 2. This is a contradiction, so no such $\Sigma$ exists.

## Remarks

For each fixed $n$, the property "$G$ has at most $n$ elements" is one sentence, namely $\neg \lambda_{n+1}$.
The proof shows that the conjunction of infinitely many such bounds, with no fixed $n$, is not expressible.
The class of infinite groups is elementary, because the set $T_{\mathrm{grp}} \cup \{\lambda_n : n \ge 1\}$ axiomatizes it.
It is not finitely axiomatizable: if one sentence $\psi$ axiomatized it, then $\neg \psi$ together with $T_{\mathrm{grp}}$ would axiomatize the finite groups, which contradicts the example.
The same proof applies to any class of structures with finite models of every large size, such as finite fields or finite sets.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The sentences of the language of groups can say "there are at least $5$ elements" or "there are at most $5$ elements".
They cannot say "there are only finitely many elements" without a fixed number.
The reason is a general fact of logic, the compactness theorem.
If some list of sentences were true in exactly the finite groups, then there would also be a group that satisfies the list and has infinitely many elements.

### L2 human

We see the key step for a concrete finite part of $\Gamma$.

1. Take $\Gamma_0 = T_{\mathrm{grp}} \cup \{\sigma\} \cup \{\lambda_3, \lambda_7\}$, where $\sigma$ is one sentence of $\Sigma$.
2. Here $N = 7$, and the group $\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$ has the $7$ distinct elements $0, 1, \ldots, 6$.
3. So $\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$ satisfies $\lambda_3$ and $\lambda_7$, and it satisfies $\sigma$ because it is finite.

Every finite part of $\Gamma$ has a model in this way, but the models get larger and larger, and in the limit compactness gives an infinite model.
The typical use of this argument is to show that a property is not first-order.
A trap is to think that the argument shows that a finite group cannot be described by sentences.
One finite group is described up to isomorphism by one sentence, its full multiplication table, as in the [[elementary theory of groups|~0757d1ee]].
