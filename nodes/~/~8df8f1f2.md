## Example

Consider a [[Hilbert system|1MGMQX43B8]] for implication, with modus ponens as its only rule and the two axiom schemas

- (A1) $\varphi \to (\psi \to \varphi)$,
- (A2) $(\varphi \to (\psi \to \chi)) \to ((\varphi \to \psi) \to (\varphi \to \chi))$.

We derive $p \to r$ from the hypotheses $p \to q$ and $q \to r$, so [[hypothetical syllogism|7MT8CPCEM4]] is a derived rule of the system.

1. $q \to r$. Reason: hypothesis.
2. $(q \to r) \to (p \to (q \to r))$. Reason: (A1) with $\varphi = q \to r$ and $\psi = p$.
3. $p \to (q \to r)$. Reason: [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] on lines 1 and 2.
4. $(p \to (q \to r)) \to ((p \to q) \to (p \to r))$. Reason: (A2) with $\varphi = p$, $\psi = q$, $\chi = r$.
5. $(p \to q) \to (p \to r)$. Reason: modus ponens on lines 3 and 4.
6. $p \to q$. Reason: hypothesis.
7. $p \to r$. Reason: modus ponens on lines 6 and 5.

## Remarks

The derivation uses each axiom schema once and modus ponens three times.
Since $p$, $q$, $r$ can be replaced by any formulas, the same seven lines derive $\varphi \to \chi$ from $\varphi \to \psi$ and $\psi \to \chi$.
After this lemma, the rule can be used in later derivations as if it were primitive.
By the [[deduction theorem|9X08XK5RV2]] applied twice, the formula $(q \to r) \to ((p \to q) \to (p \to r))$ is a theorem of the system.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A Hilbert system has very few rules: some fixed formulas called axioms, and one rule that removes an "if".
Even the step "if A gives B and B gives C, then A gives C" has to be built from these.
The seven lines above build it once, so that it never needs to be built again.

### L2 human

We read the derivation as a strategy.

1. Line 2 uses (A1) to put the hypothesis $q \to r$ under the extra condition $p$.
2. Line 4 uses (A2) to move the condition $p$ through the arrow: from "under $p$, $q$ gives $r$" to "if $p$ gives $q$, then $p$ gives $r$".
3. Lines 5 and 7 detach the conclusion by modus ponens.

The typical use is to shorten later derivations, which cite the lemma instead of repeating seven lines.
A trap is the order of the premises in modus ponens: line 7 uses the antecedent $p \to q$ from line 6 and the implication from line 5, not the other way round.
