## Proof

By the [[singular value decomposition|000NPTMX6J]], $A = U \Sigma V^*$ with $U$, $V$ [[unitary|0AR68JTMDT]], so $U^* U = I_m$ and $V^* V = I_n$.
By part 2 of that theorem, $\sigma_i > 0$ for $i \le r$, and $\Sigma_{ii} = 0$ for $i > r$.
Let $X = V \Sigma^+ U^*$.

*Part 1.*

1. Let $E_m = \Sigma \Sigma^+$ and $E_n = \Sigma^+ \Sigma$. Both are products of matrices with nonzero entries only on the diagonal. So $E_m$ is the $m \times m$ diagonal matrix with entries $\sigma_i \cdot \sigma_i^{-1} = 1$ for $i \le r$ and $0$ otherwise. In the same way, $E_n$ is $n \times n$ diagonal with $1$ at $i \le r$ and $0$ otherwise.
2. The nonzero entries of $\Sigma$ lie in rows $1, \dots, r$, so $E_m \Sigma = \Sigma$. The nonzero entries of $\Sigma^+$ lie in rows $1, \dots, r$, so $E_n \Sigma^+ = \Sigma^+$.
3. $A X = U \Sigma V^* V \Sigma^+ U^* = U E_m U^*$, and $X A = V \Sigma^+ U^* U \Sigma V^* = V E_n V^*$, by $V^* V = I$ and $U^* U = I$.
4. Condition 1: $A X A = U E_m U^* U \Sigma V^* = U E_m \Sigma V^* = U \Sigma V^* = A$, by step 3 and step 2.
5. Condition 2: $X A X = V E_n V^* V \Sigma^+ U^* = V E_n \Sigma^+ U^* = V \Sigma^+ U^* = X$, by step 3 and step 2.
6. Conditions 3 and 4: by the rule $(YZW)^* = W^* Z^* Y^*$ from the [[transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]], $(U E_m U^*)^* = U E_m^* U^* = U E_m U^*$. Here $E_m^* = E_m$, since $E_m$ is real and diagonal. The same computation applies to $V E_n V^*$.

*Part 2.*

7. Let $X$ and $Y$ both satisfy the four conditions. By conditions 1 and 3 for $Y$, $A^* = (A Y A)^* = A^* (A Y)^* = A^* A Y$.
8. By conditions 1 and 4 for $X$, $A^* = (A X A)^* = (X A)^* A^* = X A A^*$.
9. By conditions 2 and 3 for $X$ and step 7, $X = X A X = X (A X)^* = X X^* A^* = X X^* A^* A Y$.
10. Since $X^* A^* = (A X)^* = A X$ by condition 3, step 9 gives $X = X A X A Y = X A Y$, using condition 2.
11. By conditions 2 and 4 for $Y$ and step 8, $Y = Y A Y = (Y A)^* Y = A^* Y^* Y = X A A^* Y^* Y$.
12. Since $A^* Y^* = (Y A)^* = Y A$ by condition 4, step 11 gives $Y = X A Y A Y = X A Y$, using condition 2.
13. By steps 10 and 12, $X = Y$. So at most one matrix satisfies the conditions, and by part 1, $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^*$ for every SVD of $A$.

*Part 3.*

14. Since $E_m = \sum_{i \le r} e_i e_i^T$, step 3 gives $A A^+ = \sum_{i \le r} (U e_i)(U e_i)^* = \sum_{i \le r} u_i u_i^*$. In the same way $A^+ A = \sum_{i \le r} v_i v_i^*$.
15. Let $y \in \C^m$ and $p = \sum_{i \le r} (u_i^* y) u_i$. Then $p$ lies in the column space of $A$, which has the orthonormal basis $u_1, \dots, u_r$ by part 3 of the SVD theorem.
16. For $j \le r$, $u_j^* (y - p) = u_j^* y - u_j^* y = 0$, by orthonormality. So $y - p$ is orthogonal to the column space, and $p$ is the [[orthogonal projection|0Q8HZTJC7G]] of $y$. The case of $A^+ A$ uses the basis $v_1, \dots, v_r$ of the column space of $A^*$.

*Part 4.*

17. For every $z$, $\norm{U z}^2 = z^* U^* U z = \norm{z}^2$. The same holds for $V$ and for $U^*$, since $U^*$ is unitary too.
18. Let $x \in \C^n$, $y = V^* x$ and $c = U^* b$. Then $Ax - b = U(\Sigma y - c)$, since $U U^* = I_m$. By step 17, $\norm{Ax - b} = \norm{\Sigma y - c}$ and $\norm{x} = \norm{y}$.
19. The entries of $\Sigma y$ are $\sigma_i y_i$ for $i \le r$ and $0$ for $i > r$. So $\norm{\Sigma y - c}^2 = \sum_{i \le r} \abs{\sigma_i y_i - c_i}^2 + \sum_{i = r+1}^m \abs{c_i}^2$.
20. The second sum does not depend on $x$. So $x$ minimizes $\norm{Ax - b}$ if and only if $y_i = c_i / \sigma_i$ for all $i \le r$. The entries $y_{r+1}, \dots, y_n$ are free.
21. For a minimizer, $\norm{x}^2 = \norm{y}^2 = \sum_{i \le r} \abs{c_i / \sigma_i}^2 + \sum_{i > r} \abs{y_i}^2$. This is smallest exactly when $y_i = 0$ for all $i > r$.
22. So there is exactly one minimizer of least norm, with $y = \Sigma^+ c$. It is $x = V \Sigma^+ U^* b = A^+ b$, by step 13.

*Part 5.*

23. If $A$ is real, part 4 of the SVD theorem gives real orthogonal $U$ and $V$. Then $\Sigma^+$, $U^*$ and $V$ are real, so $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$ is real.

## Idea

In the singular bases, $A$ is the diagonal matrix $\Sigma$, and the best inverse of a diagonal matrix inverts its nonzero entries.
The four conditions are then products of diagonal matrices.
Uniqueness is a short computation with the four equations alone.
For least squares, the unitary changes of coordinates keep all norms, and the problem splits into one scalar problem per coordinate.

## Variants

- Existence can also use a rank factorization $A = F G$, with $F$ of full column rank and $G$ of full row rank. Then $A^+ = G^* (G G^*)^{-1} (F^* F)^{-1} F^*$.
- Part 4 can use the normal equations: the least-squares solutions are $x_0 + \operatorname{Nul}(A)$, and the shortest one lies in the column space of $A^*$, as in the [[minimum-norm solution theorem|QM2Y84VDX8]].
