## Example

The [[Fermat numbers|0R03B54V4Z]] are the numbers $F_n = 2^{2^n} + 1$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$.
Pierre de Fermat conjectured that each $F_n$ is [[prime|0SN0MAEKYX]].
We check the evidence that he had, and then the counterexample that Leonhard Euler found in 1732.

1. $F_0 = 2^1 + 1 = 3$, $F_1 = 2^2 + 1 = 5$ and $F_2 = 2^4 + 1 = 17$. These are prime.
2. $F_3 = 2^8 + 1 = 257$. It is prime, because no prime $p$ with $p^2 \leq 257$ divides it: the candidates are $2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13$, and $17^2 = 289 > 257$.
3. $F_4 = 2^{16} + 1 = 65537$. It is also prime; a check by trial division with the primes up to $256$ confirms this, because $256^2 = 65536 < 65537 < 257^2$.
4. $F_5 = 2^{32} + 1$. Since $2^{32} = 4294967296$, we get $F_5 = 4294967297$.
5. Euler found the factor $641$. We check the product $641 \cdot 6700417$.
6. $6700417 \cdot 600 = 4020250200$.
7. $6700417 \cdot 41 = 6700417 \cdot 40 + 6700417 = 268016680 + 6700417 = 274717097$.
8. $4020250200 + 274717097 = 4294967297 = F_5$.
9. So $F_5 = 641 \cdot 6700417$ with $1 < 641 < F_5$, and $F_5$ is not prime. It is a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]], and the conjecture is false.

Steps 5 to 8 check the factorization, but they do not show how to find $641$.
A short argument shows why $641$ divides $F_5$ without the long multiplication.

10. $641 = 5 \cdot 2^7 + 1$, because $5 \cdot 128 = 640$. So $5 \cdot 2^7 \equiv -1 \pmod{641}$.
11. Raise both sides of step 10 to the fourth power: $5^4 \cdot 2^{28} \equiv 1 \pmod{641}$.
12. $641 = 5^4 + 2^4$, because $625 + 16 = 641$. So $5^4 \equiv -2^4 \pmod{641}$.
13. Put step 12 into step 11: $-2^4 \cdot 2^{28} \equiv 1$, that is, $2^{32} \equiv -1 \pmod{641}$.
14. So $641$ divides $2^{32} + 1 = F_5$.

## Remarks

The example shows a [[conjecture|~3c997250]] that five cases supported and that failed at the sixth case.
The five confirming cases $F_0$ to $F_4$ gave evidence, but they gave no proof.
A [[Fermat prime|0N9E4G2P97]] is a Fermat number that is prime.
No Fermat prime larger than $F_4$ is known.
So the evidence now points the other way, but the statement "only finitely many $F_n$ are prime" is itself an open conjecture.
The numbers grow very fast: $F_6 = 2^{64} + 1$ has $20$ digits, so each new case is much harder to test than the last.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Fermat looked at the numbers $3$, $5$, $17$, $257$ and $65537$.
Each one is $1$ more than a power of $2$, and each one is prime.
He guessed that the next numbers in the list are also prime.
The next number is $4294967297$, which is too big to test easily by hand.
Many years later, Euler showed that it equals $641$ times $6700417$, so the guess was wrong.

### L2 human

We check the claim that $F_6 = 2^{64} + 1$ has $20$ digits.

1. $2^{10} = 1024 > 10^3$, so $2^{60} = (2^{10})^6 > 10^{18}$, and $2^{64} = 16 \cdot 2^{60} > 1.6 \cdot 10^{19}$.
2. $2^{10} = 1024 < 1.03 \cdot 10^3$, so $2^{60} < 1.03^6 \cdot 10^{18} < 1.2 \cdot 10^{18}$, and $2^{64} < 16 \cdot 1.2 \cdot 10^{18} < 2 \cdot 10^{19}$.
3. So $10^{19} < F_6 < 10^{20}$, and $F_6$ has $20$ digits.

The typical use of such an example is to warn against a conjecture that rests only on small cases.
A trap is to read the five primes $F_0$ to $F_4$ as a proof of the pattern.
Five cases are evidence, and the sixth case is a counterexample.
