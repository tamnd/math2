## Proof

We use these facts of [[ordinal arithmetic|KAEDCQ8RM6]], for ordinals $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ and natural numbers $c, d$.

- (F1) Addition is associative.
- (F2) Addition is strictly increasing in its right argument: $\beta < \gamma$ implies $\alpha + \beta < \alpha + \gamma$.
- (F3) $\alpha + \beta \ge \alpha$, since $\beta \ge 0$ and (F2) holds with $\le$.
- (F4) $\omega^\beta \cdot (c + 1) = \omega^\beta \cdot c + \omega^\beta$, by the definition of multiplication.
- (F5) Multiplication by $\omega^\beta > 0$ on the left is strictly increasing in its right argument. So $c < d$ gives $\omega^\beta c < \omega^\beta d$, and $c < \omega$ gives $\omega^\beta c < \omega^\beta \omega$.
- (F6) $\omega^\beta \cdot \omega = \omega^{\beta + 1}$, and $\beta + 1 \le \delta$ gives $\omega^{\beta + 1} \le \omega^\delta$.

Two lemmas.

1. **Lemma 1.** If $\beta < \delta$ and $c$ is a natural number, then $\omega^\beta c < \omega^\delta$. Reason: by (F5) and (F6), $\omega^\beta c < \omega^\beta \omega = \omega^{\beta + 1} \le \omega^\delta$, since $\beta + 1 \le \delta$.
2. **Lemma 2.** If $\sigma$ is a normal form whose exponents are all less than $\delta$, then $\sigma < \omega^\delta$. We prove this by [[induction|0ASJVEFA9J]] on the number $k$ of terms of $\sigma$.
3. For $k = 0$: $\sigma = 0 < \omega^\delta$, since $\omega^\delta \ge 1$.
4. For $k \ge 1$: write $\sigma = \omega^{\beta_1} c_1 + t$, where $t$ is the normal form of the other $k - 1$ terms. The exponents of $t$ are less than $\beta_1$, so $t < \omega^{\beta_1}$ by the induction hypothesis.
5. By (F2) and (F4), $\sigma < \omega^{\beta_1} c_1 + \omega^{\beta_1} = \omega^{\beta_1}(c_1 + 1)$. By Lemma 1 with $\beta_1 < \delta$, this is less than $\omega^\delta$.

A lower bound.

6. **Lemma 3.** If $\sigma$ is a normal form with leading pair $(\beta_1, c_1)$, then $\sigma \ge \omega^{\beta_1} c_1 \ge \omega^{\beta_1} \ge 1$. Reason: (F3) and (F5).

The main argument.
Let $\alpha$ and $\alpha'$ have the term lists $(\beta_i, c_i)_{i \le k}$ and $(\beta'_i, c'_i)_{i \le m}$.

7. Suppose the term lists are equal. Then $\alpha$ and $\alpha'$ are the same sum, so $\alpha = \alpha'$.
8. Suppose the lists differ. Let $j$ be the least index such that $j > k$, or $j > m$, or $(\beta_j, c_j) \ne (\beta'_j, c'_j)$. Let $\pi$ be the sum of the common terms with index less than $j$.
9. By (F1), $\alpha = \pi + \rho$ and $\alpha' = \pi + \rho'$, where $\rho$ and $\rho'$ are the normal forms of the terms with index at least $j$.
10. By (F2), $\rho < \rho'$ gives $\alpha < \alpha'$, and $\rho' < \rho$ gives $\alpha' < \alpha$. So it is enough to compare $\rho$ and $\rho'$.
11. Case A: $j > k$ and $j \le m$, so that condition 1 of the theorem holds. Then $\rho = 0$, and $\rho' \ge 1$ by Lemma 3. So $\alpha < \alpha'$.
12. Case B: $j \le \min(k, m)$ and $\beta_j < \beta'_j$. All exponents of $\rho$ are at most $\beta_j < \beta'_j$, so Lemma 2 gives $\rho < \omega^{\beta'_j}$, and Lemma 3 gives $\omega^{\beta'_j} \le \rho'$. So $\alpha < \alpha'$.
13. Case C: $j \le \min(k, m)$, $\beta_j = \beta'_j = \beta$ and $c_j < c'_j$. Write $\rho = \omega^\beta c_j + t$. The exponents of $t$ are less than $\beta$, so $t < \omega^\beta$ by Lemma 2.
14. By (F2), (F4) and (F5), $\rho < \omega^\beta c_j + \omega^\beta = \omega^\beta (c_j + 1) \le \omega^\beta c'_j$. By Lemma 3, $\omega^\beta c'_j \le \rho'$. So $\alpha < \alpha'$.
15. Cases B and C are exactly condition 2 of the theorem. So each of conditions 1 and 2 gives $\alpha < \alpha'$.
16. The remaining cases of step 8 are the cases A, B and C with the roles of $\alpha$ and $\alpha'$ exchanged. In each of them, steps 11 to 14 give $\alpha' < \alpha$.
17. Suppose $\alpha < \alpha'$. Then the lists are not equal by step 7, and by step 16 the reversed cases do not hold, since they give $\alpha' < \alpha$. So one of the cases A, B or C holds, and condition 1 or 2 is true.

Steps 15 and 17 prove the equivalence.

18. Corollary: if the term lists differ, steps 15 and 16 give $\alpha < \alpha'$ or $\alpha' < \alpha$. So $\alpha \ne \alpha'$. $\blacksquare$

## Idea

Split off the common leading terms, which do not change the comparison because addition is strictly increasing on the right.
At the first difference, the whole rest of the smaller side is bounded by one power of $\omega$, by Lemma 2.
The larger side reaches that power with its first term alone.
So the first difference decides the order.

## Variants

- One can prove uniqueness of the normal form first, by division with remainder, and then derive the comparison rule. The proof above goes the other way and gives uniqueness as a corollary.
- The proof also works for normal forms to any base $\beta \ge 2$ in place of $\omega$, with coefficients $0 < c_i < \beta$; Lemma 1 then uses $\beta^\gamma c < \beta^{\gamma + 1}$ for $c < \beta$.
