## Summary

Let $n \ge 1$.
The *unitary group* $U(n)$ is the set of complex $n \times n$ [[unitary matrices|0AR68JTMDT]], $U(n) = \set{U \in M_n(\C) : U^*U = I_n}$, with matrix multiplication.
It is a [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] of the [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] $GL_n(\C)$.
By [[the properties of unitary matrices|~2220a569]], it is the group of [[linear isometries|07SJXTWVQW]] of $\C^n$ with the standard inner product.
It contains the [[orthogonal group|~dc22fe40]] $O(n)$ as its real matrices, and the [[special unitary group|~f67e0a03]] $SU(n)$ as the kernel of the determinant.

## Motivation

Complex vector spaces with an inner product appear in Fourier analysis and in quantum mechanics.
Their symmetries must keep the complex inner product.
These symmetries form the unitary group, which plays for $\C^n$ the role that $O(n)$ plays for $\R^n$.

## Notation

- Books write $U(n)$, $U_n$ or $U(n, \C)$. For a complex inner product space $V$, the group of its unitary operators is $U(V)$.
- Mathlib writes `Matrix.unitaryGroup (Fin n) ℂ`. For a general star monoid, `unitary` is the submonoid of unitary elements.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[$U(1)$ is the circle group|~75396a62]]: a $1 \times 1$ matrix $(z)$ is unitary exactly when $\abs{z} = 1$.
- The diagonal matrices $\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\theta_1}, \dots, e^{i\theta_n})$ form a commutative subgroup of $U(n)$, the maximal torus.
- [[A unitary matrix with determinant −i|~24dc021c]] lies in $U(2)$ but not in $SU(2)$.
- The $n \times n$ permutation matrices form a finite subgroup of $U(n)$.
- $O(n)$ is the subgroup of real matrices in $U(n)$.

### Non-examples

- [[A matrix with determinant i that is not unitary|~b46dc950]] shows that $\abs{\det A} = 1$ does not imply $A \in U(n)$.
- The complex orthogonal group $O(2, \C) = \set{A \in M_2(\C) : A^TA = I}$ is not contained in $U(2)$, see [[this example|~83b36d34]].

### Basic properties

- $U(n)$ is a subgroup of $GL_n(\C)$: $(UW)^*(UW) = W^*U^*UW = I_n$, and $(U^{-1})^*U^{-1} = UU^* = I_n$.
- $\det \colon U(n) \to U(1)$ is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $SU(n)$. It is surjective because $\det\operatorname{diag}(z, 1, \dots, 1) = z$.
- Each $U \in U(n)$ is $\operatorname{diag}(\det U, 1, \dots, 1)$ times an element of $SU(n)$.
- $U(n)$ is compact and connected, because each element is $V\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\theta_k})V^*$, see [[the properties|~2220a569]].
- The center of $U(n)$ is the group of scalar matrices $zI_n$ with $\abs{z} = 1$.
- $U(n)$ is a compact [[Lie group|7JBRGCVJRB]] of real dimension $n^2$, one of the [[classical groups|75H4HY10Z4]].

### Common errors

- $U(n)$ is connected, unlike $O(n)$. A path $t \mapsto V\operatorname{diag}(e^{it\theta_k})V^*$ joins $I_n$ to each element.
- $U(n)$ is not the set of matrices with $\abs{\det U} = 1$. That set is much larger and not compact.
- $U(1)$ is commutative, but $U(n)$ is not commutative for $n \ge 2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The unitary group collects all the ways to move complex space without changing lengths.
Two such moves done one after the other give another such move, and each move can be undone.
For complex numbers alone, the moves are multiplications by numbers of size $1$, the turns of the complex plane.

### L2 human

Worked example: we check that $U(2)$ is not commutative.

1. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \operatorname{diag}(i, 1)$. Both are unitary.
2. $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. So $AB \neq BA$.
5. $\det(AB) = \det(BA) = -i$, as $\det$ is a homomorphism to a commutative group.

The typical use: a change between two orthonormal bases of $\C^n$ is an element of $U(n)$.
A trap: $U(n)$ is connected, so there is no analogue of the two components of $O(n)$.

### L3 human

$U(n)$ is a compact connected Lie group of real dimension $n^2$, and its Lie algebra is the space of skew-Hermitian matrices.
It is a maximal compact subgroup of $GL_n(\C)$, and $GL_n(\C)$ deformation retracts onto it, by the [[polar decomposition|0J2C7BDJQA]].
The map $U(1) \times SU(n) \to U(n)$, $(z, A) \mapsto zA$, is surjective with kernel of order $n$.
So the fundamental group of $U(n)$ is $\Z$, detected by the determinant.
The Lie theory of $U(n)$ belongs to the area of Lie groups.
