## Summary

Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
The scalar triple product of $a$, $b$ and $c$ is the real number
$$[a, b, c] = a \cdot (b \times c),$$
the [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]] of $a$ with the [[cross product|0N5N15HWZ7]] $b \times c$.
It is the $3 \times 3$ determinant with the rows $a$, $b$ and $c$, by the [[determinant formula|059Q2XWJDC]].
Its absolute value is the volume of the [[parallelepiped|0SQV2VJTGY]] with the edges $a$, $b$ and $c$.
It is $0$ if and only if $a$, $b$ and $c$ lie in one plane through $0$, by the [[coplanarity test|~15dbbcb4]].
So one number answers two questions about three vectors: how much space they span, and whether they span space at all.

## Motivation

Three edges $a$, $b$, $c$ from one corner span a slanted box, a parallelepiped.
Its volume is the area of the base times the height.
The base on $b$ and $c$ has the area $\|b \times c\|$, and $b \times c$ is perpendicular to the base.
The height is the part of $a$ along this perpendicular, so it is $|a \cdot (b \times c)| / \|b \times c\|$.
The product of area and height is $|a \cdot (b \times c)|$, and the lengths cancel.
A volume of $0$ means a flat box, so the test for coplanarity comes free.

## Notation

- Books write $a \cdot (b \times c)$, $[a, b, c]$, $[a\,b\,c]$ or $(a, b, c)$.
- The parentheses in $a \cdot (b \times c)$ are often dropped: $a \cdot b \times c$ can only mean $a \cdot (b \times c)$, since $(a \cdot b) \times c$ is undefined.
- In coordinates, $[a, b, c] = \det \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{pmatrix}$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- $[\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}] = \mathbf{i} \cdot (\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{k}) = \mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{i} = 1$, the volume of the unit cube.
- $a = (1, 2, 3)$, $b = (0, 1, 4)$, $c = (5, 6, 0)$: $b \times c = (-24, 20, -5)$ and $[a, b, c] = -24 + 40 - 15 = 1$. The [[worked example|~39747bd6]] gives the details.
- $a = (1, 2, 3)$, $b = (4, 5, 6)$, $c = (7, 8, 9)$: $[a, b, c] = 0$, because $c = 2b - a$. The [[coplanar example|~7c6ad63b]] shows this.
- $a = (2, 0, 0)$, $b = (0, 3, 0)$, $c = (0, 0, 4)$: the box is a cuboid, and $[a, b, c] = 24 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4$.
- $[a, a, c] = 0$ for all $a$ and $c$, since $a \times c$ is perpendicular to $a$.

### Non-examples

- The product of the lengths $\|a\| \, \|b\| \, \|c\|$ is not the volume unless the edges are perpendicular. The [[non-example|~446c6e14]] gets $\sqrt{2}$ for a box of volume $1$.
- The expression $(a \cdot b) \, c$ is a vector, not a scalar triple product.
- The expression $a \times (b \times c)$ is the vector triple product, a vector; [[its own identity|03FTB26QYD]] treats it.

### Basic properties

- Cyclic symmetry: $[a, b, c] = [b, c, a] = [c, a, b]$.
- A swap of two vectors changes the sign: $[b, a, c] = -[a, b, c]$.
- Exchange of dot and cross: $a \cdot (b \times c) = (a \times b) \cdot c$.
- Linearity in each slot: $[a + a', b, c] = [a, b, c] + [a', b, c]$ and $[ta, b, c] = t[a, b, c]$.
- Volume: $|[a, b, c]|$ is the volume of the parallelepiped on $a$, $b$, $c$; the tetrahedron on the same edges has the volume $\tfrac{1}{6} |[a, b, c]|$.
- Coplanarity: $[a, b, c] = 0$ if and only if $a$, $b$, $c$ are linearly dependent, by the [[coplanarity test|~15dbbcb4]].
- Sign: $[a, b, c] > 0$ exactly when $(a, b, c)$ is a right-handed triple, the same [[orientation|0QJJAQ6ZYZ]] as $(\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k})$.

### History

Lagrange used the dot and cross products in coordinates in 1773, to study the volume of a tetrahedron.
The name and the vector form $a \cdot (b \times c)$ come from the vector analysis of Gibbs and Heaviside in the 1880s.

### Common errors

- Students take the absolute value too early and lose the orientation; the sign of $[a, b, c]$ carries information.
- Students forget the factor $\tfrac{1}{6}$ for a tetrahedron, or use $\tfrac{1}{3}$ or $\tfrac{1}{2}$ instead.
- Students think that $[a, b, c] = 0$ means that two of the vectors are parallel. The vectors $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 1, 0)$ and $(1, 1, 0)$ have $[a, b, c] = 0$ and no parallel pair.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Three arrows from one corner make a slanted box.
The scalar triple product is a single number whose size is the volume of this box.
For the three unit arrows along the axes, the box is a cube with volume $1$.
If the three arrows lie flat in one plane, the box is flat, and the number is $0$.

### L2 human

Worked example: the tetrahedron with the vertices $P = (0, 0, 0)$, $A = (1, 2, 3)$, $B = (0, 1, 4)$ and $C = (5, 6, 0)$.

1. The edges from $P$ are $a = (1, 2, 3)$, $b = (0, 1, 4)$ and $c = (5, 6, 0)$.
2. $b \times c = (1 \cdot 0 - 4 \cdot 6, \; 4 \cdot 5 - 0 \cdot 0, \; 0 \cdot 6 - 1 \cdot 5) = (-24, 20, -5)$.
3. $[a, b, c] = 1 \cdot (-24) + 2 \cdot 20 + 3 \cdot (-5) = 1$.
4. The parallelepiped has the volume $1$, and the tetrahedron has the volume $\tfrac{1}{6}$.

The typical uses are volumes, the test whether four points lie in one plane, and the orientation of a triple of vectors.
A trap: for four points $P$, $A$, $B$, $C$, take the three edges from one vertex, such as $A - P$, $B - P$ and $C - P$.

### L3 human

The map $(a, b, c) \mapsto [a, b, c]$ is the unique alternating trilinear form on $\mathbb{R}^3$ with $[\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}] = 1$, so it is the determinant.
The sub-area on [[determinants|DTBK11SR77]] proves that $|\det A|$ is the volume of the image of the unit cube, which is the [[signed volume|04X3PR72VJ]] view in every dimension.
In the [[exterior algebra|0MDMZ965VE]], $a \wedge b \wedge c = [a, b, c] \, e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge e_3$.
The cross product is defined by the triple product: $b \times c$ is the only vector $w$ with $a \cdot w = [a, b, c]$ for all $a$.
