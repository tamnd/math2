## Example

The argument is the form AAA in the second figure:

- Major premise: all $P$ are $M$.
- Minor premise: all $S$ are $M$.
- Conclusion: all $S$ are $P$.

A mathematical instance: all squares are parallelograms, all rectangles are parallelograms, so all rectangles are squares.
We show that it has the shape of a categorical syllogism but is not a valid one.

1. The conclusion has subject $S$ (rectangles) and predicate $P$ (squares), and the middle term $M$ (parallelograms) occurs in both premises. So the argument has exactly three [[terms|KSJ9W29KXQ]] and the shape of a syllogism.
2. $M$ is the predicate of both premises, so the [[figure|S4BTTGFR6J]] is the second, and the mood is AAA.
3. Distribution: the predicate of an A proposition is not distributed. Both premises are A propositions with $M$ as predicate, so $M$ is never distributed, and the first rule of validity fails.
4. A countermodel: take the rectangle $R$ with sides $1$ and $2$.
5. Every square is a parallelogram and every rectangle is a parallelogram, so both premises are true.
6. $R$ is a rectangle, and $R$ is not a square because its sides differ. So "all rectangles are squares" is false.
7. A [[Venn diagram|0KCT6DPXJK]] shows the gap. The premises shade $S \bar M P$, $\bar S \bar M P$ and $S \bar M \bar P$. The conclusion needs $SM\bar P$ to be empty, and no premise shades it; the rectangle $R$ lies in that region.
8. The premises are true and the conclusion is false in one instance, so the conclusion does not follow from the premises.

By Aristotle's own definition, a syllogism is an argument whose conclusion follows of necessity from its premises.
So this argument is not a syllogism in that sense, and it is not a valid categorical syllogism in the modern sense.

## Remarks

The two premises only say that $S$ and $P$ both lie inside $M$.
Two sets inside a common set can stand in any relation to each other: they can be equal, nested or disjoint.
The error is close to [[affirming the consequent|J7KFCFQRBT]]: for an object $x$ in $S$ we know "$x$ is in $M$" and "if $x$ is in $P$ then $x$ is in $M$", and the argument concludes "$x$ is in $P$".
If the major premise is reversed to "all $M$ are $P$", the form becomes [[Barbara|AC6XQ4KRNT]], which is valid.
If the minor premise is replaced by "no $S$ is $M$", the form becomes Camestres, AEE-2, which is valid with the conclusion "no $S$ is $P$".

## Explanations

### L1 human

"All cats have four legs, and all dogs have four legs, so all dogs are cats" is wrong, and everybody sees it.
The same mistake is harder to see with true-sounding sentences: "all squares are parallelograms, all rectangles are parallelograms, so all rectangles are squares".
Both premises only say that squares and rectangles belong to the big family of parallelograms.
Being in the same family does not make one group part of the other.
A rectangle that is twice as long as it is wide shows that the conclusion is false.

### L2 human

The fallacy often appears in proofs as a step about numbers.

1. Claim made by a student: every number divisible by $6$ is divisible by $4$.
2. "Argument": every number divisible by $4$ is even, and every number divisible by $6$ is even.
3. The middle term "even numbers" is the predicate of both premises, so the form is AAA-2 and the middle term is undistributed.
4. Countermodel: $6$ is divisible by $6$ and is even, but $6$ is not divisible by $4$.

The typical use of the distribution rule is a quick test before a longer check: if the middle term is never distributed, the form is invalid.
The trap is that the conclusion of an invalid form can be true in a single instance, as for "all multiples of $12$ are multiples of $4$", which follows the same pattern with a true conclusion.
