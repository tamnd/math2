## Example

The claim is: "in each triangle $ABC$, the [[altitude|0C9MZY2RNA]] from $C$ meets the side $AB$ at its [[midpoint|0S706JBZBG]]".
We first test the claim on an equilateral triangle, then on a triangle with no special features.

The special test uses $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (2, 0)$ and $C = (1, \sqrt{3})$.

1. The side lengths are $|AB| = 2$, $|AC| = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$ and $|BC| = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$. So the triangle is equilateral.
2. The side $AB$ lies on the $x$-axis. The altitude from $C$ is the vertical line $x = 1$, and its foot is $(1, 0)$.
3. The midpoint of $AB$ is $\left(\frac{0 + 2}{2}, 0\right) = (1, 0)$.
4. The foot and the midpoint agree. The special test supports the claim.

The generic test uses $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (4, 0)$ and $C = (1, 3)$.

5. The side lengths are $|AB| = 4$, $|AC| = \sqrt{1 + 9} = \sqrt{10}$ and $|BC| = \sqrt{9 + 9} = \sqrt{18}$. No two are equal.
6. The side $AB$ lies on the $x$-axis, so the altitude from $C$ is the vertical line $x = 1$. Its foot is $(1, 0)$.
7. The midpoint of $AB$ is $\left(\frac{0 + 4}{2}, 0\right) = (2, 0)$.
8. The foot $(1, 0)$ is not the midpoint $(2, 0)$. So this triangle is a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]], and the claim is false.

## Remarks

The equilateral triangle is a non-example of a generic example: it has extra symmetry, and the claim uses exactly that symmetry.
In fact the foot of the altitude from $C$ is the midpoint of $AB$ exactly when $|CA| = |CB|$.
The reason is that the points at equal distance from $A$ and $B$ form the perpendicular bisector of $AB$.
So the claim holds for each isosceles triangle with apex $C$, and it fails for each other triangle.

A generic triangle for a test has three different side lengths and no right angle.
The triangle of steps 5 to 8 has these properties: its sides are $4$, $\sqrt{10}$ and $\sqrt{18}$, and $10 + 18 \neq 16$, $16 + 10 \neq 18$, $16 + 18 \neq 10$, so it has no right angle.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Draw a triangle with three equal sides, and drop a line straight down from the top corner.
The line hits the base exactly in the middle.
It is tempting to say that this happens in each triangle.
Now draw a lopsided triangle, with the top corner far to the left.
The straight-down line hits the base to the left of the middle.
The first picture was too neat to be a fair test.

### L2 human

We find all the triangles with $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (4, 0)$ and $C = (x, y)$, $y > 0$, for which the claim holds.

1. The foot of the altitude from $C$ is $(x, 0)$, because $AB$ lies on the $x$-axis.
2. The midpoint of $AB$ is $(2, 0)$.
3. The two points agree exactly when $x = 2$.
4. For $x = 2$ we get $|CA|^2 = 4 + y^2 = |CB|^2$. So these are exactly the triangles with $|CA| = |CB|$.

The typical use of this lesson is in geometry problems: draw a figure with no equal sides and no right angles before guessing a property.
A trap is that a neat figure is easier to draw and to read.
That is the reason that students choose it, and it is also the reason that it can mislead.
