## Proof

1. By the [[rational canonical form theorem|042JWAR3Q0]] over $K$, there are $P_A \in GL_n(K)$ and monic nonconstant $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ in $K[x]$ with $A = P_A R P_A^{-1}$ and $R = C(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus C(d_r)$. These $d_i$ are the [[invariant factors|~bcfda459]] of $A$ over $K$.
2. The polynomials $d_i$ are also monic and nonconstant in $L[x]$. If $d_i e = d_{i+1}$ with $e \in K[x]$, then also $e \in L[x]$. So $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ in $L[x]$.
3. The [[companion matrices|0VH1TC1PWF]] $C(d_i)$ depend only on the coefficients of $d_i$, so $R$ is a rational canonical form in $M_n(L)$.
4. Since $P_A \in GL_n(K) \subseteq GL_n(L)$, $A = P_A R P_A^{-1}$ also holds over $L$.
5. By the uniqueness part of the rational canonical form theorem over $L$, $R$ is the rational canonical form of $A$ over $L$, and the invariant factors of $A$ over $L$ are $d_1, \dots, d_r$. This proves part 1.
6. Part 3. By part 2 of the [[theorem on companion matrices|~dd227218]], the block $C(d_i)$ has minimal polynomial $d_i$. A polynomial kills $R$ if and only if it kills each block, so $\mu_R = \operatorname{lcm}(d_1, \dots, d_r) = d_r$. Similar matrices have the same minimal polynomial, so $\mu_A = d_r$ over $K$ and over $L$, by step 5.
7. Part 2. Apply step 1 to $B$: $B = P_B R' P_B^{-1}$ with $P_B \in GL_n(K)$ and $R'$ the rational canonical form of $B$ over $K$. By step 5, $R'$ is also the rational canonical form of $B$ over $L$.
8. Similar matrices have the same rational canonical form, by uniqueness, since [[similarity|027KDW2K72]] is an equivalence relation. As $A$ and $B$ are similar over $L$, $R = R'$.
9. So $B = P_B R P_B^{-1} = P_B P_A^{-1} A P_A P_B^{-1}$. Put $P = P_A P_B^{-1} \in GL_n(K)$. Then $B = P^{-1} A P$.

## Idea

The rational canonical form is computed by operations inside $K$, and it is unique over every field.
So the form over $K$ is already the form over $L$, and equal forms over $L$ are equal forms over $K$.

## Variants

The [[theorem on the characteristic matrix|~57adc122]] gives a second proof.
The determinantal divisors are gcds of polynomials in $K[x]$, and a gcd computed by the Euclidean algorithm in $K[x]$ is also the gcd in $L[x]$.
For infinite $K$ there is a proof without canonical forms.
Let $X_1, \dots, X_m$ be a $K$-basis of the solutions of $AX = XB$.
Gaussian elimination shows that they are also an $L$-basis of the solutions over $L$.
Then $f(t) = \det(t_1 X_1 + \cdots + t_m X_m)$ is a polynomial with coefficients in $K$ that is nonzero at some point of $L^m$, so it is a nonzero polynomial.
A nonzero polynomial over an infinite field has a nonzero value at some point of $K^m$, which gives an invertible $X \in M_n(K)$ with $AX = XB$.
This argument fails for finite $K$, where a nonzero polynomial can vanish at every point of $K^m$.
