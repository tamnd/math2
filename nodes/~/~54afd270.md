## Summary

A weak theory of arithmetic is a first-order theory, much weaker than [[Peano arithmetic|~b3b2d1f3]], that still carries the incompleteness and undecidability phenomena.
The precise property is essential undecidability: the theory is consistent, and every consistent extension of it in the same language is [[undecidable|HWC687ES0Q]].
The standard examples are [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] $\mathsf{Q}$, the theory $\mathsf{R}$ of Tarski, Mostowski and Robinson, and Grzegorczyk's theory of concatenation $\mathsf{TC}$.
These theories have no induction, and $\mathsf{R}$ is not even finitely axiomatizable.
Their study asks how little a theory needs before Gödel's theorems apply to it.

## Motivation

The [[incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]] seem to depend on arithmetic: numbers code formulas, and the theory must prove facts about the codes.
One may ask whether induction, or multiplication, or infinitely many axioms are essential.
$\mathsf{Q}$ shows that induction is not needed.
$\mathsf{R}$ shows that no universally quantified law is needed: its axioms state only numerical facts and bounded facts about numerals.
$\mathsf{TC}$ shows that numbers are not needed: strings with concatenation suffice.
Each weak theory gives a cheap way to prove undecidability: if a consistent theory $T$ interprets an essentially undecidable theory, then $T$ is undecidable.

## Notation

We write $\mathsf{Q}$, $\mathsf{R}$ and $\mathsf{TC}$ for the three standard theories.
For theories in the same language, $T \subseteq T'$ means that every theorem of $T$ is a theorem of $T'$.
A theory $T$ is essentially undecidable when $T$ is consistent and every consistent theory $T' \supseteq T$ in the language of $T$ is undecidable.

## Remarks

### Examples

- $\mathsf{Q}$ is essentially undecidable and finitely axiomatized; [[its page|3K1AKGXF8A]] gives the details.
- $\mathsf{R}$ has five axiom schemas about numerals and no universal law; [[this example|~d4bb21bb]] states it, derives it from $\mathsf{Q}$ and shows that it is not finitely axiomatizable.
- $\mathsf{TC}$ speaks about strings over a two-letter alphabet, with concatenation as its only operation; [[this example|~7fc1d73f]] states its axioms and checks its standard model.

### Non-examples

- [[Presburger arithmetic|B66JYY3K58]] is weak in the sense that it has no multiplication, but it is decidable, so it is not essentially undecidable; [[this non-example|~1c73febc]] explains why.
- The theory of groups is undecidable, but it is not essentially undecidable, because its extension by commutativity is decidable; the L2 explanation below gives the details.

### Basic properties

- Every consistent theory that contains an essentially undecidable theory in the same language is essentially undecidable.
- An essentially undecidable theory is incomplete, and so is each consistent recursively axiomatizable extension of it. A complete recursively axiomatizable theory is decidable, so it cannot be a consistent extension.
- If a consistent theory $T$ interprets an essentially undecidable theory, then $T$ is undecidable. This is the method of Tarski, Mostowski and Robinson.
- If a finitely axiomatized theory $T$ is essentially undecidable, then every theory compatible with $T$ in a common language is undecidable. Here $T'$ is compatible with $T$ when $T \cup T'$ is consistent.
- $\mathsf{R} \subseteq \mathsf{Q} \subseteq \mathsf{PA}$, when $\le$ is read as $x < y \lor x = y$.
- All three of $\mathsf{Q}$, $\mathsf{R}$ and $\mathsf{TC}$ are essentially undecidable. For $\mathsf{Q}$ and $\mathsf{R}$ this follows from the [[representability of recursive functions|~d5e5ada1]].

### History

Tarski, Mostowski and Robinson developed the method of essentially undecidable theories and interpretations in *Undecidable Theories* (1953), with $\mathsf{Q}$ and $\mathsf{R}$ as their main tools.
Grzegorczyk introduced $\mathsf{TC}$ in 2005, under the slogan of undecidability without arithmetization, and proved it undecidable.
Grzegorczyk and Zdanowski then proved that $\mathsf{TC}$ is essentially undecidable.

### Common errors

- To think that a weak theory must be decidable. $\mathsf{R}$ has no universal axioms, and it is still essentially undecidable.
- To confuse undecidable with essentially undecidable. The theory of groups is undecidable, but the theory of abelian groups extends it and is decidable.
- To think that interpreting $\mathsf{Q}$ makes a theory complete or strong. It only makes it undecidable, and incomplete when it is recursively axiomatizable.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Gödel showed that strong theories of arithmetic always leave some questions open.
Logicians then asked how weak such a theory can be and still have this problem.
The answer is: very weak.
A theory that only knows facts such as $2 + 3 = 5$, $2 \cdot 3 = 6$ and "every number at most $3$ is $0$, $1$, $2$ or $3$" already has the problem.
Even a theory about strings of letters, with no numbers at all, has it.

### L2 human

We show that the theory of groups is undecidable but not essentially undecidable.

1. Tarski proved that the theory of groups is undecidable.
2. The theory of abelian groups is a consistent extension of it in the same language.
3. Szmielew proved that the theory of abelian groups is decidable.
4. So the theory of groups has a consistent decidable extension, and it is not essentially undecidable.

The typical use of weak theories is as a target for interpretations: to show a theory undecidable, interpret $\mathsf{Q}$ or $\mathsf{R}$ in it.
A trap is the language: essential undecidability is about extensions in the same language, and a definitional expansion does not change it.

### L3 human

Current research compares weak theories by interpretability, which preorders theories by strength.
$\mathsf{Q}$ and $\mathsf{TC}$ are mutually interpretable, so they have the same strength in this sense.
$\mathsf{R}$ is strictly weaker than $\mathsf{Q}$ in this order.
An interpretation of $\mathsf{Q}$ in $\mathsf{R}$ would use finitely many axioms of $\mathsf{R}$, and these have a finite model.
The interpretation would then give a finite model of $\mathsf{Q}$, and $\mathsf{Q}$ has none.
Weak theories also measure the cost of coding.
In $\mathsf{Q}$ the coding of sequences needs the [[β-function|BM6P0C2AC2]], while in $\mathsf{TC}$ strings are themselves sequences.
Another line of work studies weak set theories, such as adjunctive set theory, which also interpret $\mathsf{Q}$.
