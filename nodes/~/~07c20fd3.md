## Summary

Let a theorem state a bound $f(x) \le C\, g(x)$ for every $x$ in a class $X$, where $g(x) > 0$ and $C$ is a constant.
The constant $C$ is *sharp*, or *best possible*, if no smaller constant works: for every $C' < C$ there is an $x \in X$ with $f(x) > C'\, g(x)$.
Equivalently, $C$ is the [[supremum|0R3H5PANRQ]] of the ratio $f(x)/g(x)$ over $X$.
The bound is *attained* if $f(x_0) = C\, g(x_0)$ for some $x_0 \in X$, and such an $x_0$ is an *equality case* or an *extremal case*.
In the same way, a hypothesis of a theorem is sharp if the theorem fails when the hypothesis is weakened, and a lower bound is sharp if no larger bound works.
A sharp result tells the reader that the theorem cannot be improved in the stated form, so further work must change the form of the bound.

## Motivation

A theorem that gives an [[upper bound|0S60DVMVPN]] leaves a question open: is the bound the truth, or only a safe estimate?
For example, the inequality $x^2 + 1 \ge 0$ holds for every real $x$, but the stronger inequality $x^2 + 1 \ge 1$ also holds.
So the first inequality wastes information.
The word "sharp" marks the bounds that waste nothing.
A proof of sharpness has two parts: a proof of the bound, and an example (or a sequence of examples) that shows that no better bound of the same form holds.
The second part is a kind of [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]]: it refutes each improved bound.

## Notation

There is no special symbol.
Authors write "the constant $C$ is sharp", "the bound is best possible", "the inequality is optimal", or "the estimate is tight".
The word "tight" is common in computer science, optimization and combinatorics.
The phrase "sharp up to a constant" means that the order of growth cannot be improved, although the constant factor may not be the best one.
Some authors write $f \lesssim g$ for $f \le C g$ with an unnamed constant $C$; a sharp result of this kind says that $g$ cannot be replaced by a smaller order of growth.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The AM-GM inequality for two numbers is sharp|~06323013]]. For $a, b \ge 0$, $\sqrt{ab} \le \tfrac{1}{2}(a + b)$, and the constant $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is best possible, because $a = b = 1$ gives equality.
2. [[The triangle inequality is sharp|~14aff70f]]. The bound $|a + b| \le |a| + |b|$ is attained whenever $a b \ge 0$, so no constant smaller than $1$ in front of $|a| + |b|$ works.
3. The [[four colour theorem|0M406JC8J1]] says that every finite planar graph has chromatic number at most $4$. The bound $4$ is sharp, because the complete graph $K_4$ is planar and needs $4$ colours.
4. For $x > 0$ we have $x/(1 + x) < 1$. The bound $1$ is sharp but not attained: for $C' < 1$, every $x > C'/(1 - C')$ gives $x/(1 + x) > C'$.
5. [[Bernoulli's inequality|0M1MX28VXK]] $(1 + x)^n \ge 1 + n x$ holds for all integers $n \ge 1$ and all real $x \ge -2$, and the threshold $-2$ is sharp. Basic property 5 proves both parts.

### Non-examples

1. [[The bound $x^2 + 1 \ge 0$ is true but not sharp|~b87ed440]]. The lower bound $0$ can be replaced by the larger bound $1$, which is attained at $x = 0$.
2. The bound $\pi(x) \le x$ on the [[prime-counting function|0RQSNVTFVZ]] is true for $x \ge 1$, but it is far from sharp. The [[prime number theorem|0EHBY5Y84N]] gives $\pi(x)/x \to 0$, so for each $c > 0$ we have $\pi(x) \le c x$ for all large $x$.
3. The usual hypothesis $x \ge -1$ in Bernoulli's inequality is not a sharp hypothesis, because the inequality also holds for $-2 \le x < -1$ (example 5).

### Basic properties

1. Attained implies sharp. Suppose $f(x_0) = C\, g(x_0)$ with $g(x_0) > 0$. For $C' < C$ we get $f(x_0) = C\, g(x_0) > C'\, g(x_0)$, so the bound with $C'$ fails at $x_0$.
2. The sharp constant is a supremum. The bound $f \le C g$ holds on $X$ exactly when $C \ge f(x)/g(x)$ for all $x \in X$. So the least such $C$ is $\sup_{x \in X} f(x)/g(x)$, when this supremum is finite.
3. Sharp does not imply attained. Example 4 has the sharp constant $1 = \sup_{x > 0} x/(1 + x)$, but no $x > 0$ gives $x/(1 + x) = 1$. A sequence $x_k \to \infty$ shows sharpness instead of one equality case.
4. Sharpness is relative to a form. The triangle inequality is sharp among bounds $C(|a| + |b|)$, but for $a = 1$, $b = -1$ the exact value $|a + b| = 0$ is much smaller than the bound. A sharp result can be far from the truth for many single inputs.
5. Bernoulli's inequality holds for $x \ge -2$, and $-2$ is the sharp threshold. The proof follows this list.

We prove property 5 for $-2 \le x < -1$, since the case $x \ge -1$ is the usual form of Bernoulli's inequality.
For $n = 1$, both sides equal $1 + x$.
For $n \ge 2$, put $y = 1 + x$, so $-1 \le y < 0$ and $|y^n| \le 1$, which gives $(1 + x)^n \ge -1$.
Since $x < -1$, we have $1 + n x < 1 - n \le -1$, so $(1 + x)^n > 1 + n x$.
Now let $x = -2 - t$ with $t > 0$, and let $n$ be odd with $n > 4/t^2$.
The binomial theorem gives $(1 + t)^n \ge 1 + n t + \tfrac{1}{2} n (n - 1) t^2$.
Since $n > 4/t^2$, we have $\tfrac{1}{2} n (n - 1) t^2 > 2(n - 1)$, so $(1 + t)^n > 2n - 1 + n t$.
Since $n$ is odd, $(1 + x)^n = -(1 + t)^n < 1 - 2n - n t = 1 + n x$, so the inequality fails.

### History

The study of best constants and of the cases of equality is a main theme of the book *Inequalities* by Hardy, Littlewood and Pólya, first published in 1934.
The constant in [[Hardy's inequality|S7A8YFGG8X]] is a standard case of a best possible constant that no nonzero sequence attains.
In computer science and optimization, the word "tight" is used with the same meaning as "sharp".

### Common errors

1. A student confuses "sharp" with "attained". A bound can be sharp and never attained, as in example 4; then a sequence of examples, not one example, proves sharpness.
2. A student shows an equality case for a different inequality. The equality case must satisfy every hypothesis of the theorem; for example, $a = b = -1$ is not allowed in the AM-GM inequality, which needs $a, b \ge 0$.
3. A student reads "the bound is sharp" as "the bound is close to the true value for every input". Sharpness only says that the bound cannot be improved for the whole class.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A bound is sharp when it cannot be made any better.
Every rectangle with perimeter $4$ has area at most $1$.
This bound is sharp, because the square with side $1$ has perimeter $4$ and area exactly $1$.
If someone claims "the area is at most $0.9$", the square shows that the claim is false.
So the number $1$ is the best bound of this kind.
A bound such as "the area is at most $100$" is also true, but it is not sharp.

### L2 human

We find the best constant $C$ such that $2ab \le C(a^2 + b^2)$ for all real numbers $a$ and $b$.

1. For all real $a, b$, we have $(a - b)^2 \ge 0$, because a square of a real number is not negative.
2. We expand: $a^2 - 2ab + b^2 \ge 0$, so $2ab \le a^2 + b^2$. So $C = 1$ works.
3. We test a smaller constant $C' < 1$ at $a = b = 1$. The left side is $2$, and the right side is $2C' < 2$.
4. So no $C' < 1$ works, and $C = 1$ is the sharp constant.
5. The equality cases are the pairs with $a = b$, because $(a - b)^2 = 0$ exactly when $a = b$.

The typical use of sharpness is in two steps: prove the bound, then exhibit an equality case or a sequence that approaches the bound.
A trap: a test with many random numbers can suggest that a bound is not sharp, because random inputs are rarely near the equality case.
The proof of sharpness must use the right input, here $a = b$.

### L3 human

The sharp constant in an inequality $f \le C g$ is the norm of an operator or the value of an optimization problem, so a proof of sharpness is a proof of a lower bound for that value.
In analysis, sharp constants in functional inequalities, such as the [[isoperimetric inequality|KBX8RBZ626]] and Hardy's inequality, lead to the study of extremal functions and of stability: how close is a function to an extremal one when the inequality is almost an equality.
In combinatorics, an upper bound is often matched by a construction, and the result is "sharp" or "tight" when the construction meets the bound; when only the order of growth matches, the result is "sharp up to a constant".
The same idea applies to hypotheses: a counterexample that satisfies all hypotheses but a weakened one shows that the hypothesis is sharp.
This is the logical dual of [[stronger and weaker statements|~f7d8f0b6]]: a sharp result is a statement that has no stronger statement of the same form.
