## Example

Let $\mathcal{T}$ be the class of finite triangle-free [[graphs|00C0BR7D7H]].
We show that $\mathcal{T}$ has a [[Fraïssé limit|HJVRBDQSGG]], the [[Henson graph|TZT25G2YB3]] $G_3$, and that $G_3$ is universal for countable triangle-free graphs.

1. HP: an induced subgraph of a triangle-free graph is triangle-free.
2. AP: let $B, C \in \mathcal{T}$ meet in $A$, and take the [[free amalgam|~dade84ce]] $D$.
3. A triangle in $D$ has its three vertices in $B$ or in $C$, since no edge joins $B \setminus A$ to $C \setminus A$.
4. So $D$ is triangle-free, and $\mathcal{T}$ has [[AP|XSCSX39GEZ]]. JEP is AP over the empty graph.
5. There are finitely many graphs on each finite vertex set, so [[Fraïssé's theorem|~8123841a]] gives a unique limit $G_3$.
6. Let $H$ be a countable triangle-free graph with vertices $h_0, h_1, \dots$. Embed them one by one.
7. At step $k$, let $U$ be the images of the neighbors of $h_k$ among $h_0, \dots, h_{k-1}$, and $V$ the images of the others.
8. $U$ is independent, since $H$ has no triangle; so the extension property of $G_3$ gives a new vertex for $h_k$.

## Remarks

Step 8 needs the extension property in the form: for disjoint finite $U, V$ with $U$ independent, some vertex is joined to all of $U$ and none of $V$.
This holds in $G_3$, since the graph $U \cup V \cup \set{z}$ with those edges is triangle-free, hence in the age.
The same argument with $K_n$ in place of the triangle gives $G_n$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Gluing two triangle-free graphs along a common part, without new edges, never creates a triangle.
That is why a single generic triangle-free countable graph exists.
Any countable triangle-free graph can be copied into it vertex by vertex.

### L2 human

We embed the $5$-cycle $h_0 h_1 h_2 h_3 h_4$ into $G_3$.

1. Pick any vertex for $h_0$, then a neighbor for $h_1$.
2. $h_2$ needs a neighbor of $h_1$ that is not adjacent to $h_0$. Use $U = \set{h_1}$, $V = \set{h_0}$.
3. $h_3$: $U = \set{h_2}$, $V = \set{h_0, h_1}$.
4. $h_4$: $U = \set{h_0, h_3}$, which is independent, and $V = \set{h_1, h_2}$.

The typical use is to see a Fraïssé limit defined by a forbidden configuration.
A trap is to forbid a configuration that is not a clique; for some such classes amalgamation fails.
