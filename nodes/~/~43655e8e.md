## Example

Let $a_m = 33\ldots31$ be the number with $m - 1$ digits $3$ followed by a digit $1$, so $a_m = (10^m - 7)/3$.
The first terms are [[prime|0SN0MAEKYX]], and the pattern ends, as the [[strong law of small numbers|~c43ae046]] warns.

1. The data. The numbers $a_2 = 31$, $a_3 = 331$, $a_4 = 3331$, $a_5 = 33331$, $a_6 = 333331$, $a_7 = 3333331$ and $a_8 = 33333331$ are all prime.
2. The guess. Seven primes in a row suggest the conjecture "every $a_m$ with $m \geq 2$ is prime".
3. The failure. $a_9 = 333333331 = 17 \cdot 19607843$.
4. More failures. $a_{10} = 3333333331 = 673 \cdot 4952947$ and $a_{11} = 33333333331 = 307 \cdot 108577633$.

So the conjecture of step 2 is false, and $m = 9$ is a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]].

## Remarks

The run of primes has a reason, and the reason is not a law that makes every $a_m$ prime.
No $a_m$ is divisible by $2$ or $5$, because it ends in $1$.
No $a_m$ is divisible by $3$, because its digit sum $3(m-1) + 1$ is not.
A prime $p \geq 7$ divides $a_m$ exactly when $10^m \equiv 7 \pmod p$.
For $p = 7$ this is impossible, and the powers of $10$ modulo $11$ and modulo $13$ never equal $7$.
So the smallest prime that can divide some $a_m$ is $17$, and $10^9 \equiv 7 \pmod{17}$ is its first chance.
Large numbers without small factors are prime more often than random numbers, and that explains the long run.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The numbers $31$, $331$, $3331$, $33331$, $333331$, $3333331$ and $33333331$ are all prime.
It is tempting to believe that adding another $3$ in front always gives a prime.
But $333333331$ is $17 \times 19607843$, so the pattern breaks.
Seven good cases were a coincidence helped by the absence of small factors, not a rule.

### L2 human

We find which $a_m$ are divisible by $17$.

1. $17$ divides $a_m$ exactly when $17$ divides $10^m - 7$, because $3$ is invertible modulo $17$.
2. The powers of $10$ modulo $17$ are $10, 15, 14, 4, 6, 9, 5, 16, 7$ for $m = 1, \ldots, 9$.
3. The first power equal to $7$ is $10^9$, so $17$ divides $a_9$.
4. The powers of $10$ modulo $17$ repeat with period $16$, so $17$ also divides $a_{25}$, $a_{41}$ and so on.

The typical use: to explain a run of primes, find the primes that can never divide the terms.
A trap: a long run of primes does not show that the terms are always prime; it often shows only that small primes cannot divide them.
