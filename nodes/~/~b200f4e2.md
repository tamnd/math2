## Summary

Proof comprehension is the understanding of a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] that a reader takes to be correct.
Proof validation is the process by which a reader decides whether an argument is a correct proof.
Research in mathematics education studies how students and mathematicians do both, and how teaching and tests can assess and improve them.
The reader's methods are on the pages [[reading proofs|40GCSM2G56]] and [[checking a proof|4M98ZZNK0E]]; this page treats the research behind them.
The field matters because students spend more time reading proofs than writing them, and most tests in proof-based courses ask only for writing.

## Motivation

In a lecture course, a student hears or reads many proofs and writes few.
If the student does not understand the proofs that the lecturer gives, most of the course is lost.
For a long time, tests of proof comprehension were rare: tests asked students to reproduce a proof, which a student can do from memory.
Researchers asked what it means to understand a proof, how to test it, and how experts and novices differ when they read and check proofs.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. A comprehension question on Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many primes asks: "Why is the number $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1$ not divisible by $p_1$?". The question tests the justification of one step.
2. A comprehension question on the same proof asks: "Summarize the main idea in two sentences." The question tests the holistic understanding of the proof.
3. A validation task gives four arguments for the same theorem, one of them with a wrong step, and asks which arguments are proofs. Selden and Selden used tasks of this kind.
4. A study records the eye movements of students and of mathematicians while they check a proof, as in the work of Inglis and Alcock.

### Non-examples

1. A test that asks students to "state and prove the theorem of the lecture" tests memory of a proof, not its comprehension. A student can reproduce a proof that the student does not understand.
2. A computer that checks a formal proof in a [[proof assistant|~03ceb7ae]] validates the proof, but this is not the human validation that this research studies.

### Research findings

1. Annie Selden and John Selden (2003) used the name validation for the reading and checking of proofs. Students of a transition-to-proof course at first often could not tell valid from invalid arguments, and their judgements improved as they reflected during the interview.
2. Juan Pablo Mejía-Ramos and coauthors (2012) proposed a model for the assessment of proof comprehension. Its local part asks about the meaning of terms, the logical status of statements, and the justification of claims. Its holistic part asks for a summary, the modular structure, the transfer of ideas to other problems, and illustration by examples.
3. Mejía-Ramos, Kristen Lew, Jimmy de la Torre and Keith Weber (2017) built proof comprehension tests on this model, and they described how they checked the quality of the tests.
4. Earlier, Jurie Conradie and John Frith (2000) proposed comprehension tests on proofs as a form of assessment in university courses.
5. Weber (2008) observed mathematicians who checked proofs. They used examples to test claims, as well as deductive reasoning.
6. Matthew Inglis and Lara Alcock (2012) used eye tracking. Students spent more time on surface features, and mathematicians moved their attention between lines more often, which suggests that they inferred unstated warrants. Mathematicians sometimes disagreed on whether a proof was valid.
7. Inglis, Mejía-Ramos, Weber and Alcock (2013) asked many mathematicians to judge one proof, and the mathematicians did not agree on its validity.
8. Mark Hodds, Alcock and Inglis (2014) found that a short training in self-explanation improved the proof comprehension of undergraduate students.

### History

Research on the reading of proofs grew in the 2000s, after earlier research had studied mostly the writing of proofs.
The 2003 study of the Seldens started a series of studies on validation.
The assessment model of 2012 started a series on comprehension tests.
Eye tracking and experiments with large groups of mathematicians came in the 2010s.

### Common errors

1. A teacher assumes that a student who can reproduce a proof understands it. Comprehension tests often show the contrary.
2. A teacher assumes that mathematicians agree on each judgement of validity. The 2013 study shows disagreement even on short proofs.
3. A student checks only that each line looks right. The research shows that experts ask what justifies each line, and that they test claims with examples.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Reading a proof and understanding it are two different things.
A student can read each line and still not know why the proof works.
Researchers ask students questions such as "why is this step true?" and "what is the main idea?".
They also ask students to find the error in a wrong proof.
Students often do less well than they expect, and short training in careful reading can help.

### L2 human

We build a short comprehension test for the proof that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, with the model of Mejía-Ramos and coauthors.
The proof assumes $\sqrt{2} = a/b$ with integers $a, b$ and $b \neq 0$, in lowest terms, derives $a^2 = 2b^2$, concludes that $a$ and $b$ are both even, and gets a contradiction.

1. Meaning of terms: "What does 'in lowest terms' mean?" Answer: $a$ and $b$ have no common factor greater than $1$.
2. Logical status: "What does the proof assume, and what does it prove?" Answer: it assumes that $\sqrt{2}$ is rational and derives a contradiction.
3. Justification: "Why does $a^2 = 2b^2$ imply that $a$ is even?" Answer: if $a$ were odd, $a^2$ would be odd.
4. Summary: "State the idea in one sentence." Answer: a fraction for $\sqrt{2}$ could always be reduced further, which is impossible.
5. Transfer: "Does the same proof show that $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational?" Answer: yes, with "divisible by $3$" in place of "even".
6. Example: "Why does the proof fail for $\sqrt{4}$?" Answer: $a^2 = 4b^2$ has the solution $a = 2$, $b = 1$, in lowest terms, so no contradiction appears.

The typical use of such a test is in a proof-based course, in place of "reproduce the proof".
A trap is question 5: a student who answers "yes" without a reason has not shown transfer.

### L3 human

Comprehension and validation are two aims of reading, and they use the same acts: finding the reason for each step and testing claims.
The research links the cognitive study of individual readers with the social study of standards of proof.
The disagreement of mathematicians on validity supports the view that the acceptance of a proof depends on a community, as in the work of Hanna on the [[philosophy of mathematics in education|~5b9b7014]].
The [[Toulmin model|~889806ec]] gives a language for the unstated warrants that expert readers infer.
