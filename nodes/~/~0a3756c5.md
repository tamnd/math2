## Summary

Linear algebra studies [[vector spaces|000AYPA9AJ]], the [[linear maps|0NRYHJPQG2]] between them, and the [[systems of linear equations|0GT4WWGWH2]] that these maps describe.
A vector space is a set with an addition and a multiplication by numbers that obey the usual rules of arithmetic.
A linear map $T$ respects these operations: $T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v)$ and $T(c v) = c \, T(v)$.
In finite dimension, a choice of bases turns each linear map into a [[matrix|0VD9ZSWWJG]], and each linear system into a matrix equation $A x = b$.
The subject matters because most of applied mathematics reduces a hard problem to a linear one, and linear problems can be solved completely.

## Motivation

Many laws of nature and of economics are linear, at least for small changes: twice the cause gives twice the effect.
A system of linear equations with many unknowns appears in circuits, in balances of chemical reactions, in the fitting of data and in the discretization of differential equations.
Three views of the same problem make it tractable.

1. Equations: $x + 2y = 5$ and $3x + 4y = 6$.
2. Matrices: $A x = b$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $b = (5, 6)$.
3. Linear maps: find the vector that the map $T(x, y) = (x + 2y, \; 3x + 4y)$ sends to $(5, 6)$.

All three views give $x = -4$ and $y = \tfrac{9}{2}$.
Check: $-4 + 9 = 5$ and $-12 + 18 = 6$.
The equations view gives algorithms, the matrix view gives compact notation and computation, and the map view gives the theory.

## Notation

- Vectors are written $u, v, w$ or $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$, and scalars $a, b, c$ or $\lambda$.
- $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $F^n$ are the spaces of columns of length $n$; $V$, $W$ are general vector spaces over a field $F$.
- $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ or $\operatorname{Hom}(V, W)$ is the space of linear maps, and $M_{m \times n}(F)$ is the space of $m \times n$ matrices.
- Books differ on columns versus rows: most books treat vectors as columns, and some older books use rows.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the rotation by $90^\circ$, $T(x, y) = (-y, x)$: a linear map whose matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
- The polynomials of degree at most $2$ with the derivative $p \mapsto p'$: a linear map on a space of dimension $3$.
- The solutions of $y'' + y = 0$: a vector space of dimension $2$ with the basis $\cos t$, $\sin t$.
- The fitting of a line to data points by [[least squares|0BYTQ4X1HC]]: a projection in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- A [[Markov chain|0G48PYM96Q]]: its long-run distribution is an eigenvector of a matrix with the eigenvalue $1$.

### Non-examples

- The system $x^2 + y = 1$, $x + y = 0$ is not linear; it belongs to algebraic geometry and numerical analysis.
- The map $x \mapsto x + 1$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is not linear, since it sends $0$ to $1$. It is affine.
- The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ with the usual addition are not a vector space over a field; they form a module over the ring $\mathbb{Z}$.

### Basic properties

- Every vector space has a basis, and in finite dimension all bases have the same size, the [[dimension|03GSZ8RTZJ]].
- A linear map is determined by its values on a basis.
- The [[rank-nullity theorem|0W4FSW1TVB]]: for $T : V \to W$ with $V$ of finite dimension, $\dim V = \dim \ker T + \dim \operatorname{im} T$.
- A square system $A x = b$ has exactly one solution for every $b$ if and only if $A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]], if and only if $\det A \neq 0$.

### History

The method of elimination for linear systems appears in the Chinese text "The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art".
Cramer published his rule for square systems in 1750, and Sylvester introduced the word "matrix" in 1850.
Cayley defined the algebra of matrices in 1858, Grassmann studied vector spaces in 1844, and Peano gave the axioms of a vector space in 1888.

### Common errors

- Students treat a matrix as a table of numbers only, and lose the map that it represents. The essay on [[common errors|~5aa23dfb]] lists more.
- Students compute without the view that explains the result, such as a determinant without its meaning as a volume.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Linear algebra is the mathematics of straight lines, flat planes and their analogues in more dimensions.
It solves many equations at once, such as $x + 2y = 5$ and $3x + 4y = 6$, which have the solution $x = -4$, $y = 4.5$.
It also describes motions that keep lines straight, such as rotations, reflections and stretches.
Computers use it to draw pictures, to rank web pages and to recognize faces.

### L2 human

A first course follows a path through the three views.

1. Solve systems by elimination, and describe the set of all solutions.
2. Write the systems as $A x = b$, and learn matrix products and inverses.
3. Abstract to vector spaces and linear maps, with bases and dimension.
4. Study one map in detail: eigenvalues, eigenvectors and diagonalization.
5. Add lengths and angles: inner products, orthogonality and least squares.

The typical use is to turn a problem into $A x = b$ or $A v = \lambda v$ and solve it.
A trap: a calculation in coordinates depends on the basis, and only some answers, such as the rank or the eigenvalues, are the same in every basis.

### L3 human

Linear algebra is the study of modules over a field, and many of its results hold over a principal ideal domain with small changes.
The classification of a linear map on a finite-dimensional space up to change of basis is the structure theorem for modules over the polynomial ring $F[x]$.
In category theory, finite-dimensional vector spaces with linear maps form a basic example of an abelian category with duality.
Representation theory, differential geometry and quantum mechanics are built on the linear structure of their objects.
