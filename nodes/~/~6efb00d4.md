## Statement

Let $V$ be an [[inner product space|01EK923SKP]] over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and let $v_1, \dots, v_k \in V$.
Let $G = G(v_1, \dots, v_k)$ be their [[Gram matrix|0HX8HZ1WTQ]], and let $\det$ be the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].

1. Let $v_1, \dots, v_k$ be [[linearly independent|0EVHJFTG9Z]], and let $u_1, \dots, u_k$ be the orthogonal output of the [[Gram-Schmidt process|~53ebde52]]. Then
$$\det G = \norm{u_1}^2 \norm{u_2}^2 \cdots \norm{u_k}^2 > 0.$$
2. If $v_1, \dots, v_k$ are dependent, then $\det G = 0$. So $\det G \geq 0$ always, with equality exactly for dependent families.
3. Define the $k$-dimensional volume of the parallelepiped spanned by an independent family as $\operatorname{vol}(v_1, \dots, v_k) = \norm{u_1} \cdots \norm{u_k}$, and as $0$ for a dependent family. Then $\det G = \operatorname{vol}(v_1, \dots, v_k)^2$.
4. Let $k = n$, let $V = \mathbb{K}^n$ with the standard inner product, and let $A$ have the columns $v_1, \dots, v_n$. Then $\det G = |\det A|^2$, so $\operatorname{vol}(v_1, \dots, v_n) = |\det A|$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Part 1 needs independence, since the Gram-Schmidt process is defined only for independent families. For $(1, 2)$, $(2, 4)$ the process gives $u_2 = 0$, and part 2 covers this case.
- Positive definiteness is needed for the sign. For the [[Minkowski form|TFGECQ5C32]] $\beta(x, y) = x_1 y_1 - x_2 y_2$, the matrix of $\beta$ on $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ is $\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$, with determinant $-1$.
- Part 4 needs $k = n$, since $\det A$ is defined only for a square matrix. For $k < n$, part 3 still gives the volume.

### Consequences

- For $k = 2$, $\det G = \norm{x}^2\norm{y}^2 - |\ip{x}{y}|^2 \geq 0$. This is the [[Cauchy-Schwarz inequality|0RFWW56Y22]] with its equality case.
- In $\mathbb{R}^3$, two vectors have $\det G(x, y) = \norm{x \times y}^2$, the squared area of their parallelogram. The [[example in $\mathbb{R}^3$|~d04ddcaf]] checks this.
- Hadamard's inequality: $\det G \leq \norm{v_1}^2 \cdots \norm{v_k}^2$, since $\norm{u_j} \leq \norm{v_j}$ for each $j$. Equality holds for orthogonal families of nonzero vectors.
- The volume does not depend on the order of the vectors, since reordering the $v_i$ conjugates $G$ by a permutation matrix.

### Converse

The converse of part 2 is part of the statement: $\det G = 0$ only for dependent families.
Part 4 has a converse for $k = n$ only: the absolute value of $\det A$ is determined by the Gram matrix, but its sign is not.

### Generalizations

- Each $\norm{u_j}$ is the distance from $v_j$ to the span of $v_1, \dots, v_{j-1}$. So part 3 is the rule "base times height", repeated.
- Part 4 over $\mathbb{R}$ agrees with the [[determinant as a volume scaling factor|RC5BTSG8T2]], which uses Lebesgue measure.
- For $k < n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the [[Cauchy-Binet formula|08WE2W7DYS]] gives $\det(A^T A)$ as the sum of the squares of the $k \times k$ minors of $A$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The determinant of the Gram matrix measures how much space the vectors fill.
For two vectors it is the squared area of their parallelogram, and for three it is the squared volume of their box.
It is zero exactly when the vectors are flat, that is, dependent.

### L2 human

Worked example: three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

1. Let $v_1 = (1, 0, 0)$, $v_2 = (1, 1, 0)$ and $v_3 = (1, 1, 1)$.
2. The Gram matrix is $G = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. Subtract row 1 from rows 2 and 3, then row 2 from row 3. This gives an upper triangular matrix with diagonal $1, 1, 1$, so $\det G = 1$.
4. The Gram-Schmidt process gives $u_1 = (1, 0, 0)$, $u_2 = (0, 1, 0)$ and $u_3 = (0, 0, 1)$, so $\prod_j \norm{u_j}^2 = 1$.
5. The matrix $A$ with the columns $v_j$ is upper triangular with diagonal $1, 1, 1$, so $\det A = 1$ and $|\det A|^2 = 1$.

All three values agree, as the theorem says.
A trap: $\det G$ is the square of the volume.
The volume is $\sqrt{\det G}$.

### L3 human

The theorem gives a definition of $k$-dimensional volume inside a space of any dimension, using only the inner product.
This is the volume element of a $k$-dimensional surface in $\mathbb{R}^n$: the area formula uses $\sqrt{\det(J^T J)}$, with $J$ the Jacobian matrix.
In Riemannian geometry, the same expression $\sqrt{\det g}$ is the volume form of the metric $g$.
