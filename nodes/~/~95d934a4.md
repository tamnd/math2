## Example

Let $F$ be a field.
Let $F^{(\N)}$ be the space of sequences $(x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ in $F$ with only finitely many nonzero terms, and let $F^{\N}$ be the space of all sequences; both are [[spaces of sequences|0GZB2SN0PH]].
We show that the [[dual space|0M856Q1AMZ]] of $F^{(\N)}$ is isomorphic to $F^{\N}$.

1. Let $e_k$ be the sequence with $1$ at place $k$ and $0$ elsewhere. The vectors $e_0, e_1, e_2, \dots$ form a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] of $F^{(\N)}$, because each $x$ is the finite sum $\sum_k x_k e_k$ in exactly one way.
2. Define $\Phi \colon (F^{(\N)})^* \to F^{\N}$ by $\Phi(f) = (f(e_0), f(e_1), f(e_2), \dots)$.
3. $\Phi$ is linear, since $(f + g)(e_k) = f(e_k) + g(e_k)$ and $(af)(e_k) = a f(e_k)$.
4. $\Phi$ is injective. If $\Phi(f) = 0$, then $f$ is $0$ on a basis, so $f = 0$.
5. $\Phi$ is surjective. Let $c = (c_0, c_1, \dots) \in F^{\N}$. Put $f(x) = \sum_k c_k x_k$. The sum is finite, because only finitely many $x_k$ are nonzero.
6. The map $f$ is linear, and $f(e_k) = c_k$. So $\Phi(f) = c$.
7. By steps 3 to 6, $\Phi$ is a [[linear isomorphism|0B8T10CE7N]]. This is the case $B = \N$ of the [[theorem on maps determined by a basis|0R8X9XA83A]].
8. Example values: the form with $c = (1, 1, 1, \dots)$ sends $x$ to the sum of its terms. At $x = (2, 0, 5, 0, 0, \dots)$, it gives $2 + 5 = 7$.
9. The form with $c = (1, 2, 4, 8, \dots)$, $c_k = 2^k$, sends $x$ to $\sum_k 2^k x_k$. At the same $x$, it gives $2 + 4 \cdot 5 = 22$.

## Remarks

- The form with $c = (1, 1, 1, \dots)$ is not a finite combination of the coordinate forms $e_k^*$. So the $e_k^*$ do [[not span the dual|~d17873c6]].
- The space $F^{(\N)}$ has countable dimension. For $F = \Q$, the dual $\Q^{\N}$ is uncountable, so its dimension over $\Q$ is uncountable. Hence $F^{(\N)}$ and its dual are not isomorphic, a case of the [[Erdős-Kaplansky theorem|~ecdd288b]].
- For $F = \R$, the space $F^{(\N)}$ is isomorphic to the [[space of polynomials|0JGZCDT5DN]] $\R[x]$, by $(x_k) \mapsto \sum_k x_k t^k$. So $\R[x]^* \cong \R^{\N}$: a form on polynomials is any choice of values on $1, t, t^2, \dots$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A finitely supported sequence is a list of numbers that is zero after some point.
A linear form on such lists is fixed by its value on each "unit list" $e_k$.
These values can be any infinite list of numbers, with no restriction.
So the dual space is the space of all infinite lists, which is much larger than the space of finitely supported lists.

### L2 human

The method is to evaluate a form on a basis, as in finite dimension.

1. A basis of the space is $e_0, e_1, \dots$.
2. A form is free to take any value $c_k$ at $e_k$.
3. The form is then $x \mapsto \sum_k c_k x_k$, a finite sum for each $x$.

The typical use is to see why duals of infinite-dimensional spaces are larger than the spaces.
A trap: the coordinate forms $e_k^*$ span only the finitely supported sequences inside $F^{\N}$, not all of $F^{\N}$.
