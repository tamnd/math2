## Example

Let $U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which is upper triangular, and $L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which is lower triangular.

1. By the [[product rule|076JGP6R3T]], $UL = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 1 & 0 + 1 \\ 0 + 1 & 0 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. The entry $(2, 1)$ of $UL$ is $1 \neq 0$, so $UL$ is not upper [[triangular|0MKABWDRNC]].
3. The entry $(1, 2)$ is $1 \neq 0$, so $UL$ is not lower triangular either.
4. The diagonal of $UL$ is $(2, 1)$, not the product $(1, 1)$ of the diagonals.
5. In the other order, $LU = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, also not triangular.

## Remarks

The product theorem needs both factors upper triangular.
In the other direction, many matrices factor as $A = LU$; for instance $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = LU$ here.
This is the LU factorisation of the topic on [[invertible matrices and factorizations|P8J6QR66TJ]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Mixing an upper and a lower triangular matrix destroys both patterns of zeros.
The product has nonzero entries on both sides of the diagonal.

### L2 human

The upper matrix keeps the flag $\langle e_1 \rangle \subset \langle e_1, e_2 \rangle$, and the lower one keeps the opposite flag.
A composite of maps that keep different flags keeps neither.
