## Example

Let $\circ$ be the one-point reflexive frame and $\bullet$ the one-point irreflexive frame.
We show that every consistent normal modal logic $L$ is contained in $\mathrm{Log}(\circ)$ or in $\mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$.
So these two logics are the only coatoms of the [[lattice of normal modal logics|~d43c867f]].

The [[canonical model|TVE4F04KME]] of $L$ has as worlds the maximal $L$-consistent sets, with $\Gamma R \Delta$ when $\square \varphi \in \Gamma$ implies $\varphi \in \Delta$, and $V(p) = \set{\Gamma : p \in \Gamma}$.
It is nonempty by [[Lindenbaum's lemma|SC0BSJ3GT6]], and by the [[truth lemma|NM4CB7QAYT]] every theorem of $L$ is true at every world.

1. For $\varphi \in L$ and a map $s$ from variables to $\set{\top, \bot}$, let $\varphi^s$ be the result of the substitution. Then $\varphi^s \in L$ by closure under substitution, and $\varphi^s$ has no variables.
2. Case 1: some world $\Gamma$ of the canonical model has no successor. By induction on formulas, a variable-free formula $\chi$ is true at $\Gamma$ exactly when it is true in $\bullet$, since $\square \psi$ is true at both and $\lozenge \psi$ false at both.
3. Each $\varphi^s$ is true at $\Gamma$ by step 1, so it is true in $\bullet$. The valuations on $\bullet$ are exactly the maps $s$, so $\varphi$ is valid on $\bullet$. So $L \subseteq \mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$.
4. Case 2: every world has a successor, so the model is [[serial|NRNKV2YYWJ]]. By induction, every variable-free $\chi$ is either true at all worlds or false at all worlds, and its value is its value in $\circ$.
5. The induction step for $\square \chi$: if $\chi$ is true everywhere, so is $\square \chi$. If $\chi$ is false everywhere, $\square \chi$ is false everywhere, since every world has a successor. The case $\lozenge \chi$ is dual.
6. As in step 3, each $\varphi^s$ is true in the model, so it is true in $\circ$, and $L \subseteq \mathrm{Log}(\circ)$.
7. Both logics are consistent, since $\bot$ is false on a one-point frame. Neither contains the other: $\square \bot \in \mathrm{Log}(\bullet) \setminus \mathrm{Log}(\circ)$ and $\square p \to p \in \mathrm{Log}(\circ) \setminus \mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$.
8. So the consistent logics are exactly the logics below one of the two, and the two are maximal consistent logics, that is, coatoms.

## Remarks

The example shows that the top of the lattice is simple, while the bottom near $\mathsf{K}$ is very complex.
David Makinson proved the theorem in 1971.
$\mathrm{Log}(\circ)$ is $\mathsf{K} \oplus (\square p \leftrightarrow p)$, often called $\mathsf{Triv}$, and $\mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$ is $\mathsf{K} \oplus \square \bot$, often called $\mathsf{Ver}$.
A consequence: every consistent normal modal logic is valid on a one-point frame, so it has at least one Kripke frame, though it can still be Kripke incomplete.
Case 2 of the proof also shows that every logic not contained in $\mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$ proves $\lozenge \top$, which is the [[splitting of the lattice by the irreflexive point|~f95097d2]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Strengthen a modal logic step by step without making it contradictory.
At the end one reaches one of exactly two logics.
In one of them, "necessarily" means "true"; in the other, everything is necessary.
These are the logics of a single point that sees itself and of a single point that sees nothing.

### L2 human

1. Take $L = \mathsf{S4}$. Its canonical model is reflexive, so every world has a successor, and Case 2 applies.
2. So $\mathsf{S4} \subseteq \mathrm{Log}(\circ)$; indeed $\circ$ is reflexive and transitive.
3. Take $L = \mathsf{GL}$. The axiom $\square(\square p \to p) \to \square p$ with $p = \bot$ gives $\square \lozenge \top \to \square \bot$, and $\circ$ refutes it. So $\mathsf{GL} \not\subseteq \mathrm{Log}(\circ)$, and $\mathsf{GL} \subseteq \mathrm{Log}(\bullet)$.

The typical use is to show that a logic is contained in a known one by a substitution argument with constants.
A trap: the theorem uses the canonical model only as a set of worlds; it does not need $L$ to be complete.
