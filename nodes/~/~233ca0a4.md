## Statement

For $\theta, \alpha \in \R$, let
$$R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S(\alpha) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\alpha & \sin 2\alpha \\ \sin 2\alpha & -\cos 2\alpha \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. Each real $2 \times 2$ [[orthogonal matrix|0QRBVFJ50X]] is $R(\theta)$ for some $\theta$, or $S(\alpha)$ for some $\alpha$. The matrices $R(\theta)$ have [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]] $1$, and the matrices $S(\alpha)$ have determinant $-1$.
2. $S(\alpha)$ is the [[reflection|025158VEYZ]] in the line through $0$ and $(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)$.
3. For all $\theta, \varphi, \alpha, \beta \in \R$:
$$R(\theta)R(\varphi) = R(\theta + \varphi), \quad S(\alpha)S(\beta) = R(2(\alpha - \beta)), \quad R(\theta)S(\alpha) = S\left(\alpha + \tfrac{\theta}{2}\right), \quad S(\alpha)R(\theta) = S\left(\alpha - \tfrac{\theta}{2}\right).$$

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Part 1 needs orthogonality. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ times $2$ has the same shape, but it is not orthogonal for any $\theta$.
- Part 1 fails in dimension $3$: there, an orthogonal matrix with determinant $-1$ need not be a reflection, for example $-I_3$.

### Consequences

- The product of two reflections in lines at the angle $\alpha - \beta$ is the rotation by $2(\alpha - \beta)$, twice the angle between the lines.
- Each rotation $R(\theta)$ is the product of two reflections: $R(\theta) = S(\theta/2)S(0)$. This is the case $n = 2$ of the [[Cartan-Dieudonné theorem|0VMWGYY35Q]].
- $S(\alpha)R(\theta)S(\alpha)^{-1} = R(-\theta)$. So conjugation by a reflection reverses the direction of a rotation.
- $O(2)$ is not commutative: $S(\alpha)S(\beta) = R(2(\alpha - \beta))$ and $S(\beta)S(\alpha) = R(2(\beta - \alpha))$ differ unless $2(\alpha - \beta)$ is a multiple of $\pi$.

### Converse

Each matrix $R(\theta)$ and each matrix $S(\alpha)$ is orthogonal, because its columns are unit vectors and their dot product is $0$.
So the two families together are exactly $O(2)$.

### Generalizations

- In dimension $n$, each orthogonal matrix is a block diagonal matrix of blocks $R(\theta)$, $1$ and $-1$ in a suitable orthonormal basis, by [[the normal form|~ff85d703]].
- In dimension $3$, each rotation turns about an axis, by [[Euler's rotation theorem|2ZVG43GSKA]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

In the plane, every map that keeps lengths and the origin is a turn or a mirror image.
A turn is given by its angle, and a mirror image by its mirror line.
Two mirror images, one after the other, give a turn.
The angle of the turn is twice the angle between the two mirror lines.

### L2 human

Worked example: the reflection in the line $y = x$.

1. The line $y = x$ passes through $(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)$ for $\alpha = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
2. $S(\pi/4) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\frac{\pi}{2} & \sin\frac{\pi}{2} \\ \sin\frac{\pi}{2} & -\cos\frac{\pi}{2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. This matrix swaps the two coordinates, which is the mirror image in the line $y = x$.
4. Its determinant is $-1$, as part 1 says.

The typical use: to recognize an orthogonal $2 \times 2$ matrix, compute its determinant and read the angle from the first column.
A trap: the matrix $S(\alpha)$ contains the angle $2\alpha$, not $\alpha$.

### L3 human

The theorem says that $O(2)$ is the semidirect product $SO(2) \rtimes \set{I, S(0)}$, with $S(0)$ acting on $SO(2) \cong U(1)$ by inversion.
The finite subgroups of $O(2)$ are the cyclic groups of rotations and the dihedral groups.
The reflections form one connected component, a circle of mirror lines, and the rotations form the other.
