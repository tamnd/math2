## Example

Problem: find a formula for the roots of $x^5 + a x^4 + b x^3 + c x^2 + d x + e = 0$ in terms of the coefficients, using only the field operations and roots of any order.
We show how [[generalization|~478f3b5e]] misleads here.

1. The known cases. The [[quadratic formula|0KYZXYDGQZ]] gives the roots of a quadratic with one square root.
2. The [[cubic formula|FB6J5WAT5V]] of the sixteenth century gives the roots of a cubic with square roots and cube roots.
3. The [[quartic formula|4CDFMKKZ01]] of Ferrari reduces a quartic to a cubic and gives its roots by radicals.
4. Generalize: the three cases suggest the general problem "for every degree $n$, find a formula by radicals", and suggest that the method for degree $n$ reduces it to degree $n - 1$.
5. Try the method. For degree $5$, the reductions that work for degrees $3$ and $4$ do not lead to an equation of lower degree.
6. The general problem has no solution. The [[Abel–Ruffini theorem|163A2WQQJX]] states that for every $n \ge 5$ the general polynomial of degree $n$ is not [[solvable by radicals|J7YS0DA3HQ]].
7. Even single equations fail. The roots of $x^5 - 4x + 2$, which has rational coefficients, cannot be written by radicals.

So the generalization from degrees $2$, $3$ and $4$ led to a false expectation.
Mathematicians spent more than two centuries on the general formula before Ruffini (1799) and Abel (1824) showed that it does not exist.

## Remarks

The generalization failed because the property that the method used does not carry over.
In Galois theory, an equation is solvable by radicals when its Galois group is solvable, and the symmetric groups $S_2$, $S_3$, $S_4$ are solvable, while $S_5$ is not.
For $x^5 - 4x + 2$ the Galois group is $S_5$: the polynomial is irreducible by Eisenstein's criterion at $2$, and it has exactly three real roots, which forces the group $S_5$ for a polynomial of prime degree.

The failure was still productive.
The attempts at a general formula led Lagrange to study permutations of the roots, and this led to group theory and to Galois theory.
Some quintics are solvable by radicals, for example $x^5 - 2$, whose roots are $\sqrt[5]{2}$ times the fifth roots of unity.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For an equation like $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$, there is a formula that gives the answers $2$ and $3$, using a square root.
Mathematicians found similar but longer formulas for equations with $x^3$ and with $x^4$.
So it seemed natural that equations with $x^5$ have a formula too.
They do not: it was proved that no formula of this kind exists for $x^5$ and higher powers.
Three successes in a row did not mean that the fourth would come.

### L2 human

We check the three real roots of $f(x) = x^5 - 4x + 2$, which the remarks use.

1. Values: $f(-2) = -22$, $f(-1) = 5$, $f(0) = 2$, $f(1) = -1$, $f(2) = 26$.
2. The signs change three times, so $f$ has a root in each of $(-2, -1)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$.
3. The derivative $f'(x) = 5x^4 - 4$ has exactly two real zeros, so $f$ has at most three real roots.
4. So $f$ has exactly three real roots and two nonreal roots.

The typical lesson: a pattern in the degree is a conjecture about all degrees, and it needs the same test as any other conjecture.

A trap: the theorem does not say that the roots of a quintic cannot be computed.
They can be found to any accuracy by numerical methods, and they can be written with functions other than radicals.
