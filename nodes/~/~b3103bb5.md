## Statement

Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$.
A [[bilinear form|0P799D6AMN]] $B$ on $V$ is *reflexive* when, for all $u, v \in V$, $B(u, v) = 0$ implies $B(v, u) = 0$.
So $B$ is reflexive exactly when orthogonality for $B$ is a symmetric relation.

1. $B$ is reflexive if and only if $B$ is [[symmetric|0744CN4MDA]] or [[alternating|08ZQAKBKHF]].

Now let $V$ be a complex vector space, and let $h$ be a [[sesquilinear form|0W44K2VNBD]] on $V$, linear in the first slot and conjugate-linear in the second.
Call $h$ reflexive when $h(u, v) = 0$ implies $h(v, u) = 0$.

2. $h$ is reflexive if and only if $ch$ is [[Hermitian|8NJ7XY6F1S]] for some $c \in \C$ with $\abs{c} = 1$.

The [[proof|~f9eacaad]] compares the two linear forms $B(u, \cdot)$ and $B(\cdot, u)$, which have the same kernel.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Reflexivity is needed in part 1. The [[form with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$|~08303f03]] is neither symmetric nor alternating, and $B(e_2, e_1) = 0$ while $B(e_1, e_2) = 2$.
- In part 1, "alternating" cannot be replaced by "skew-symmetric" in general, and "symmetric" cannot be dropped. In characteristic $2$ the dot product is reflexive and not alternating.
- In part 2 the factor $c$ is needed. The form $h = ih_0$ with $h_0$ the [[standard inner product on $\C^n$|TFZ8DYVJ1J]] is reflexive and skew-Hermitian, but not Hermitian.

### Consequences

- For a reflexive form there is one notion of [[orthogonal complement|01TXN7ETH2]] $W^\perp$, and one radical. For other forms we must distinguish left and right complements.
- So the theory of orthogonality, isotropy and Witt's theorem treats exactly the symmetric and the alternating forms, and the Hermitian forms.
- If $\operatorname{char} F \neq 2$, a reflexive form is symmetric or skew-symmetric, by the [[relation of alternating and skew-symmetric forms|~205a4a20]].

### Converse

Both converses are part of the statement.
A symmetric form is reflexive, since $B(v, u) = B(u, v)$.
An alternating form is reflexive, since it is skew-symmetric and $B(v, u) = -B(u, v)$.

### Generalizations

Let $K$ be a division ring with an anti-automorphism $\sigma$, and let $h$ be a $\sigma$-sesquilinear form.
The Wikipedia article on sesquilinear forms states that $h$ is reflexive exactly when $h(v, u) = \varepsilon \sigma(h(u, v))$ for some fixed $\varepsilon$.
Part 1 is the case $\sigma = \id$, where $\varepsilon = \pm 1$.
Over $\C$ the factor $i$ turns skew-Hermitian forms into Hermitian forms, so only the Hermitian case remains in part 2.

## History

Birkhoff and von Neumann related the correlations of projective geometries to nondegenerate sesquilinear forms in 1936, according to the Wikipedia article on sesquilinear forms.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For the dot product, "$u$ is at a right angle to $v$" means the same as "$v$ is at a right angle to $u$".
For a general form this can fail: the form can give $0$ in one order and $2$ in the other order.
The theorem says when the order never matters for the value $0$.
This happens only for symmetric forms and for alternating forms.

### L2 human

We test the form $B(x, y) = x^T A y$ on $\Q^2$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $A$ is not symmetric, since $A_{12} = 1 \neq -1 = A_{21}$.
2. $B$ is not alternating, since $B(e_1, e_1) = 1$.
3. By part 1, $B$ is not reflexive. We look for a pair that shows this.
4. Let $u = e_1$ and $v = e_1 - e_2$. Then $B(u, v) = A_{11} - A_{12} = 0$.
5. In the other order, $B(v, u) = A_{11} - A_{21} = 1 + 1 = 2 \neq 0$.
6. So $v$ is orthogonal to $u$ in one order only, as part 1 predicts.

The typical use is to restrict the general theory to symmetric, alternating and Hermitian forms.
A trap: one pair with $B(u, v) = B(v, u) = 0$ proves nothing.
Reflexivity is a condition on all pairs.

### L3 human

The theorem explains why the classical groups come from exactly three kinds of forms: symmetric, alternating and Hermitian.
These give the [[orthogonal groups|0TKYAEJ8N7]], the [[symplectic groups|X12FS2JAP0]] and the [[unitary groups|0R9R0Y1HDK]].
In the theory of polar spaces and buildings, the same result classifies the polarities of a projective space that come from forms.
The proof is a small case of a general principle: two linear forms with the same kernel are proportional.
