## Statement

Let $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Sat}}$ be the language of arithmetic with a binary predicate $\mathrm{Sat}$, with a fixed [[Gödel numbering|MFJXPAF0R0]].
Then the set of all instances of the [[naive satisfaction schema|~8614bcb6]] is inconsistent in first-order logic.
In particular, it has no model, and no base theory of arithmetic is needed for the contradiction.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. The instance used is for $\varphi(v) := \neg \mathrm{Sat}(v, v)$, which contains $\mathrm{Sat}$. Without such instances the schema is consistent.
2. The term $\ulcorner \varphi \urcorner$ is a numeral, so it is a closed term of the language. The universal instance at this term needs no arithmetic axiom.

### Consequences

1. No formula $\mathrm{Sat}(x, y)$ of arithmetic satisfies the naive satisfaction schema for arithmetic in the standard model. This is a form of [[Tarski's undefinability theorem|YRV6SYK2BY]].
2. The [[Grelling-Nelson paradox|~1ca05504]] is the informal form of this proposition.

### Converse

The converse question asks which parts of the schema are consistent.
The schema for formulas without $\mathrm{Sat}$ is consistent, and compositional axioms for satisfaction give the theory [[CT|~5af95d4a]].

### Generalizations

The same proof works in any language that can name its own formulas by closed terms and has a binary predicate for satisfaction.
With sets and membership in place of formulas and satisfaction, it is the proof of [[Russell's paradox|4CVPENYR0S]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The proposition says that no predicate can tell, for every formula including those that use it, which things the formula is true of.
The bad formula is "$v$ is a formula that is not true of itself".
Ask whether this formula is true of itself, and both answers fail.

### L2 human

1. Let $h$ be the code of $\neg \mathrm{Sat}(v, v)$.
2. The schema gives $\mathrm{Sat}(\underline{h}, \underline{h}) \leftrightarrow \neg \mathrm{Sat}(\underline{h}, \underline{h})$.
3. A biconditional of the form $p \leftrightarrow \neg p$ is false under every valuation, so the schema has no model.

The typical use is as the shortest proof that semantic closure fails.
The trap is to think that the proof needs the diagonal lemma: the self-application in the formula does the work.

### L3 human

The proposition is a direct diagonal argument, like the proof that a set has fewer elements than its power set.
The [[liar paradox|~005ac188]] needs the diagonal lemma because truth applies only to sentences, which have no free variable to apply to a code.
