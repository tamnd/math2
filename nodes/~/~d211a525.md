## Summary

A parity argument is a proof that uses only whether certain integers are even or odd.
Its usual form has three parts: a quantity $Q$ with integer values, a fact that fixes the parity of $Q$, and a target that needs the other parity.
The fact comes from a second way to compute $Q$, or from the rule that each allowed move keeps the parity of $Q$, or changes it each time.
Then the target is impossible, because an integer cannot be both even and odd.
In the language of [[congruences|0WAY5WX8NY]], a parity argument computes $Q$ modulo $2$.
It is the simplest kind of [[invariant|~6e8e1c09]], and it is often the first tool to try when a problem asks whether something is possible.

## Motivation

Many questions ask whether a configuration can exist or whether a target can be reached.
A search through all cases is often too long, and for an infinite family of cases it is not possible at all.
A parity argument replaces the search by one computation in the two-element set $\{\text{even}, \text{odd}\}$.
For example, a board with $25$ cells has no tiling by dominoes, because each domino covers $2$ cells and $25$ is odd.
No case analysis of the positions of the dominoes is needed.

## Notation

We write $n \equiv 0 \pmod 2$ for "$n$ is even" and $n \equiv 1 \pmod 2$ for "$n$ is odd".
The rules of parity are the rules of addition and multiplication modulo $2$: even plus odd is odd, odd plus odd is even, and a product is odd only when both factors are odd.
Some books write $(-1)^n$ for the parity of $n$, which is $1$ for even $n$ and $-1$ for odd $n$; this is the form used for the [[sign of a permutation|02DKV69ZH0]].

## Remarks

### Examples

- Domino tilings: a board with an odd number of cells has no [[domino tiling|83PJCPB9VA]], since $k$ dominoes cover $2k$ cells, an even number. So the $5 \times 5$ and $5 \times 7$ boards have no tiling.
- Graphs: no [[graph|00C0BR7D7H]] has nine vertices that all have [[degree|0KS0KRDV4Y]] $3$. The sum of the degrees would be $27$, but the [[handshaking lemma|0XAP8WHNES]] says that this sum is $2|E|$, an even number. The [[full example|~f8e6b063]] gives the details.
- Euler's bridges: the [[Seven Bridges of Königsberg|0YCW781Q9D]] have no walk that crosses each bridge once. A walk enters and leaves each inner vertex, so it uses an even number of edge ends there, and at most two vertices can have odd degree. All four land areas have odd degree.
- Signed sums: no choice of signs makes $\pm 1 \pm 2 \pm \cdots \pm 10$ equal to $0$. The sum $1 + 2 + \cdots + 10 = 55$ is odd, and a change of the sign of $k$ changes the sum by $2k$. The [[full example|~ddc1d33b]] gives the details.
- Cups: seven cups stand upright, and a move turns over exactly two cups. The number of upside-down cups changes by $-2$, $0$ or $2$, so it stays even, and it can never be $7$.
- Permutations: a product of an odd number of transpositions is never the identity permutation, because the [[sign|02DKV69ZH0]] of such a product is $-1$. This fact proves that half of the positions of the 15 puzzle cannot be solved.

### Non-examples

- An even count does not prove existence. The $4 \times 4$ board without two opposite corners has $14$ cells, an even number, and still has no domino tiling. Parity gives no information here, as [[this example|~ab06afef]] shows, and a [[coloring proof|~6e95dc63]] is needed.
- A quantity that changes its parity in an irregular way gives no argument. If a move can add $1$ or $2$ to a counter, the parity of the counter can take both values, and parity alone decides nothing.
- A problem whose answer depends on a residue modulo $3$ or modulo $4$ is beyond parity. An example is the tiling of the $10 \times 10$ board by $1 \times 4$ bars: the number of cells is $100$, an even number and a multiple of $4$, and a coloring with four colors is needed.

### Basic properties

- Parity respects addition and multiplication: the map $n \mapsto n \bmod 2$ from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\{0, 1\}$ turns sums into sums modulo $2$ and products into products modulo $2$.
- A sum of integers is odd exactly when it has an odd number of odd terms. This is the step behind the [[number of odd-degree vertices is even|0QQQQCPZA8]].
- The number $0$ is even, as the page on the [[parity of zero|HFFN6YCTAB]] explains. Many parity proofs need this fact at the start state.
- A parity argument proves only impossibility, or the need for a condition. It never proves that an object exists.

### The steps

1. Choose an integer quantity $Q$ that the problem determines: a count of cells, a sum of degrees, a number of inversions.
2. Find the parity of $Q$ for every allowed configuration, or show that each allowed move keeps the parity of $Q$.
3. Compute the parity of $Q$ for the target.
4. If the two parities differ, the target is impossible. If they agree, the argument decides nothing, and another method is needed.

### History

Leonhard Euler used a parity argument in 1736 to show that the Königsberg bridge walk does not exist.
In the same paper he proved that the degrees of a graph add up to twice the number of edges, which is now the handshaking lemma.
William Woolsey Johnson and William Edward Story used a parity argument in 1879 to show that half of the starting positions of the 15 puzzle cannot be solved.

### Common errors

- To read "the parities agree" as a proof that the target can be reached. Parity gives a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.
- To forget a kind of move. If one allowed move changes the parity, the argument fails, so each kind of move must be checked.
- To mix the two meanings of "odd": the parity of a number and the parity of a [[permutation|05RWFKVTJ7]]. A permutation is odd when its sign is $-1$, which is a different count.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every whole number is even or odd, and never both.
A parity argument uses this simple fact to show that something cannot happen.
Take a board with $5$ rows and $5$ columns, so it has $25$ squares.
Each domino covers exactly $2$ squares, so any number of dominoes covers an even number of squares.
Since $25$ is odd, no set of dominoes covers this board exactly.
We did not try any placements: one check of even and odd was enough.

### L2 human

We show that a knight on a chessboard cannot return to its start square after an odd number of moves.

1. Give the square in row $r$ and column $c$ the number $r + c$, with rows and columns numbered $1$ to $8$.
2. A knight move changes one coordinate by $1$ and the other by $2$, so it changes $r + c$ by $\pm 1 \pm 2$, which is $-3$, $-1$, $1$ or $3$.
3. Each of these four changes is odd, so each move changes the parity of $r + c$.
4. After $k$ moves the parity of $r + c$ has changed $k$ times. For odd $k$ the final parity differs from the start parity.
5. The start square has one parity, so for odd $k$ the knight is on another square. This proves the claim.

The typical use is to rule out a target with a short invariant, before any search starts.
The trap is the converse: the parity check in step 4 does not show that the knight can return after every even number of moves.
For two moves it can return, but that fact needs its own construction, such as a move and its reverse.

### L3 human

A parity argument is a homomorphism to the group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
The quantity $Q$ takes values in an abelian group, and the allowed moves act by adding elements of a subgroup $H$.
The target is impossible when it lies outside the coset of the start modulo $H$.
Parity is the case where the group is $\mathbb{Z}$ and the obstruction is detected in the quotient $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
Larger quotients, such as $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and the colorings of [[coloring proofs|~6e95dc63]], detect finer obstructions.

The same pattern appears in many fields.
The sign homomorphism from the symmetric group to $\{1, -1\}$ gives the alternating group and the theory of determinants.
In topology, the mod $2$ degree of a map and the Euler characteristic modulo $2$ are parity invariants.
In coding theory, a parity bit detects one error in a word.
The general [[invariants|~6e8e1c09]] of this sub-area keep the idea and replace the two-element group by richer structures.
