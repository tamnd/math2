## Proof

We compute both sides for $2n = 2$ and $2n = 4$.
For general $n$, we cite the proof in the Pfaffian article listed in the sources.

1. Let $2n = 2$ and $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & a \\ -a & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. The only perfect matching of $\{1, 2\}$ is $\{\{1, 2\}\}$, with $\pi_M = \operatorname{id}$. So the [[definition|C9JJD2AVJR]] gives $\operatorname{pf}(A) = a$.
3. Directly, $\det A = 0 \cdot 0 - a \cdot (-a) = a^2 = \operatorname{pf}(A)^2$.
4. Let $2n = 4$. Write $a = a_{12}$, $b = a_{13}$, $c = a_{14}$, $d = a_{23}$, $e = a_{24}$, $f = a_{34}$.
5. Then $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & a & b & c \\ -a & 0 & d & e \\ -b & -d & 0 & f \\ -c & -e & -f & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
6. The three matchings give $\operatorname{pf}(A) = af - be + cd$, by the definition. Call this $P$.
7. By the [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]] along row 1, $\det A = a C_{12} + b C_{13} + c C_{14}$, with the [[cofactors|0W2QAVJQ1W]] $C_{1j}$.
8. The minor $M_{12}$ has rows $(-a, d, e)$, $(-b, 0, f)$, $(-c, -f, 0)$.
9. Expansion along its first row gives $M_{12} = -a f^2 - d c f + e b f = -f P$. So $C_{12} = -M_{12} = f P$.
10. The minor $M_{13}$ has rows $(-a, 0, e)$, $(-b, -d, f)$, $(-c, -e, 0)$.
11. Expansion gives $M_{13} = -a e f + e (be - cd) = -e P$. So $C_{13} = M_{13} = -e P$.
12. The minor $M_{14}$ has rows $(-a, 0, d)$, $(-b, -d, 0)$, $(-c, -e, -f)$.
13. Expansion gives $M_{14} = -a d f + d (be - cd) = -d P$. So $C_{14} = -M_{14} = d P$.
14. By steps 7, 9, 11 and 13, $\det A = a f P - b e P + c d P = P^2$.

## Idea

For size $4$, each cofactor in the first row is the Pfaffian times one entry of the opposite pair.
The Laplace expansion along row 1 then rebuilds the Pfaffian, so the determinant is its square.

## Variants

The general case follows from the identity $\operatorname{pf}(B A B^T) = \det(B) \operatorname{pf}(A)$.
Over a field, a nonsingular alternating $A$ equals $B J B^T$ for a symplectic basis matrix $B$, so both sides equal $(\det B)^2$.
A singular alternating $A$ equals $B J' B^T$, where $J'$ has fewer than $n$ such blocks and zeros elsewhere, so both sides are $0$.
The identity of polynomials over $\mathbb{Z}$ then gives the case of a commutative ring.
