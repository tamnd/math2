## Statement

Let $A(p)$ be a modal formula in which the variable $p$ is modalized: every occurrence of $p$ lies in the scope of some $\square$.
$A$ may contain other variables $q_1, \dots, q_k$.
For a formula $H$, $A(H)$ is the result of replacing every $p$ by $H$.
Write $\boxdot X := X \wedge \square X$, and let $r$ be a variable that does not occur in $A$.

1. Existence: there is a formula $H$ whose variables are among $q_1, \dots, q_k$ such that $\mathsf{GL} \vdash H \leftrightarrow A(H)$.
2. Uniqueness: $\mathsf{GL} \vdash \boxdot(p \leftrightarrow A(p)) \wedge \boxdot(r \leftrightarrow A(r)) \to (p \leftrightarrow r)$. Hence, if $\mathsf{GL} \vdash H \leftrightarrow A(H)$ and $\mathsf{GL} \vdash H' \leftrightarrow A(H')$, then $\mathsf{GL} \vdash H \leftrightarrow H'$.
3. Explicit form for one box: if $A(p) = B(\square C(p))$, where $B(x)$ is a formula in a new variable $x$ in which $p$ does not occur and $C(p)$ is any formula, then $H = B(\square C(B(\top)))$ is a fixed point.
4. Arithmetical form: let $*$ be a realization of $q_1, \dots, q_k$ for $\mathsf{PA}$, and write $A^*(\sigma)$ for the realization of $A$ with $p^* = \sigma$. If $\mathsf{PA} \vdash \sigma \leftrightarrow A^*(\sigma)$, then $\mathsf{PA} \vdash \sigma \leftrightarrow H^*$.

Here $\mathsf{GL}$ is the logic of the [[definition of provability logic|61THBNS8WE]].
The proof is in [[the proof of the de Jongh-Sambin theorem|~fd4f24eb]]; it proves parts 2, 3 and 4 in full and cites the reduction of part 1 to part 3.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. $p$ must be modalized. For $A(p) = \neg p$, no formula $H$ has $\mathsf{GL} \vdash H \leftrightarrow \neg H$, since $\mathsf{GL}$ is consistent. For $A(p) = p$, every formula is a fixed point, so uniqueness fails.
2. Löb's axiom is needed. In $\mathsf{K4}$, no formula $H$ has $\mathsf{K4} \vdash H \leftrightarrow \square \neg H$. On the one-world frame with $a R a$, which validates $\mathsf{K4}$, $\square \neg H$ is true at $a$ exactly when $\neg H$ is, so $H \leftrightarrow \square \neg H$ fails at $a$.
3. The boxes in uniqueness. The formula $(p \leftrightarrow A(p)) \wedge (r \leftrightarrow A(r)) \to (p \leftrightarrow r)$, without $\boxdot$, is not a theorem for $A(p) = \square p$. Take worlds $1 R 2$ with $p$ true at both and $r$ false at both. At $1$, both $p \leftrightarrow \square p$ and $r \leftrightarrow \square r$ hold, but $p \leftrightarrow r$ fails.

### Consequences

1. Gödel sentences: $A(p) = \neg \square p$ has the fixed point $\neg \square \bot$. So every sentence $G$ with $\mathsf{PA} \vdash G \leftrightarrow \neg \mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner G \urcorner)$ is equivalent in $\mathsf{PA}$ to the [[consistency statement|4NBAX3VRQ7]].
2. [[Henkin sentences|SBMQ5QHG9W]]: $A(p) = \square p$ has the fixed point $\top$. So every sentence equivalent to its own provability is provable, which is Löb's answer to Henkin.
3. $A(p) = \square \neg p$ has the fixed point $\square \bot$. A sentence that says "my negation is provable" is equivalent to the inconsistency of $\mathsf{PA}$.
4. $A(p) = \square p \to q$ has the fixed point $\square q \to q$. This is the sentence of the usual proof of [[Löb's theorem|3HD30YG3DG]].
5. The [[diagonal lemma|3PRW76471J]] gives fixed points in arithmetic, but they are not explicit; part 4 shows that, for modal $A$, they are all equivalent to one explicit sentence.

### Converse

The converse of part 2 holds: if $\mathsf{GL} \vdash H \leftrightarrow H'$ and $H$ is a fixed point, then so is $H'$, by replacement of equivalents.
So the fixed points of $A$ are exactly the formulas equivalent to $H$.

### Generalizations

1. In arithmetic, part 4 holds for every theory to which [[Solovay's theorem|~5cc205a1]] applies, since the proof uses only realizations of theorems of $\mathsf{GL}$.
2. The theorem holds for the extensions of $\mathsf{GL}$ that are closed under the same rules, since the proof uses only theorems of $\mathsf{GL}$.
3. Algebraically, it says that in a [[Magari algebra|~90876855]] every term in which the variable is under the operator has a unique fixed point, given by a term.

## History

Dick de Jongh and Giovanni Sambin proved the existence of explicit fixed points independently.
The uniqueness was proved independently by Claudio Bernardi, de Jongh and Sambin.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Many famous sentences of logic talk about themselves: "I am not provable" or "I am provable".
The theorem says that such a self-referential sentence, built from provability, can be rewritten without self-reference.
For example, "I am not provable" is equivalent to "arithmetic is consistent".
It also says that two sentences with the same self-referential description are equivalent.

### L2 human

We find the fixed point of $A(p) = \neg \square p$ with part 3 and check it.

1. Write $A(p) = B(\square C(p))$ with $B(x) = \neg x$ and $C(p) = p$.
2. Then $B(\top) = \neg \top$, and $H = \neg \square \neg \top$, which is equivalent to $\neg \square \bot$.
3. Check: we need $\mathsf{GL} \vdash \neg \square \bot \leftrightarrow \neg \square \neg \square \bot$, that is, $\square \bot \leftrightarrow \square \neg \square \bot$.
4. From left to right: $\bot \to \neg \square \bot$ is a tautology, so $\square \bot \to \square \neg \square \bot$.
5. From right to left: $\neg \square \bot$ is $\square \bot \to \bot$, so $\square \neg \square \bot$ is Löb's antecedent $\square(\square \bot \to \bot)$, and Löb's axiom gives $\square \bot$.

The typical use is to compute what a self-referential sentence says.
A trap: the theorem needs $p$ under a box; "I am false" has no fixed point.

### L3 human

The theorem shows that self-reference through provability adds no expressive power: every such fixed point is already a Boolean and modal combination of the parameters.
It is related to the Beth definability property: part 2 says that $\boxdot(p \leftrightarrow A(p))$ defines $p$ implicitly and part 1 gives an explicit definition.
Löb's axiom enters through Löb's rule in both halves, and this is why the theorem fails for $\mathsf{K4}$.
