## Statement

Let $p$, $q$ and $r$ be propositional variables.
Let $\top$ be a formula that is true under every valuation, and let $\bot$ be a formula that is false under every valuation.
The following [[logical equivalences|0JMR2HM2N2]] hold, where $\land$ is [[conjunction|025YZN3VJW]] and $\lor$ is [[disjunction|0F72FK8EKB]].

1. Commutative laws: $p \land q \equiv q \land p$ and $p \lor q \equiv q \lor p$.
2. Associative laws: $(p \land q) \land r \equiv p \land (q \land r)$ and $(p \lor q) \lor r \equiv p \lor (q \lor r)$.
3. Distributive laws: $p \land (q \lor r) \equiv (p \land q) \lor (p \land r)$ and $p \lor (q \land r) \equiv (p \lor q) \land (p \lor r)$.
4. Absorption laws: $p \land (p \lor q) \equiv p$ and $p \lor (p \land q) \equiv p$.
5. Idempotent laws: $p \land p \equiv p$ and $p \lor p \equiv p$.
6. Identity laws: $p \land \top \equiv p$ and $p \lor \bot \equiv p$.
7. Domination laws: $p \lor \top \equiv \top$ and $p \land \bot \equiv \bot$.
8. Duality principle. Let $\varphi$ and $\psi$ be formulas built from propositional variables, $\top$ and $\bot$ with the connectives $\neg$, $\land$ and $\lor$ only. The dual $\varphi^d$ is the formula that we get from $\varphi$ when we exchange $\land$ with $\lor$ and $\top$ with $\bot$ at every place. If $\varphi \equiv \psi$, then $\varphi^d \equiv \psi^d$.

By the substitution property of logical equivalence, laws 1 to 7 stay true when $p$, $q$ and $r$ are replaced by any formulas $\varphi$, $\psi$ and $\chi$.
The proof is on the page [[proof of the laws of propositional logic by truth tables|~47008b41]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Laws 1 to 7 have no hypotheses: they hold for all propositions $p$, $q$ and $r$.
The duality principle has one hypothesis, the restriction to the connectives $\neg$, $\land$ and $\lor$.
Instead of dropping hypotheses, we show that close variants of the laws fail, each by one row of a [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]].

1. Mixed brackets are not associative: $(p \land q) \lor r \not\equiv p \land (q \lor r)$. In the row $p = \mathrm{F}$, $q = \mathrm{F}$, $r = \mathrm{T}$, the left side is T and the right side is F.
2. [[Negation|029JBBF693]] does not distribute over conjunction: $\neg(p \land q) \not\equiv \neg p \land \neg q$. In the row $p = \mathrm{T}$, $q = \mathrm{F}$, the left side is T and the right side is F. The correct law is [[De Morgan's law|09PKJKA6J0]].
3. Absorption needs two different connectives: $p \land (p \land q) \not\equiv p$. In the row $p = \mathrm{T}$, $q = \mathrm{F}$, the left side is F and the right side is T.
4. [[Material implication|0NMG7W7Q11]] is neither commutative nor associative. In the row $p = \mathrm{T}$, $q = \mathrm{F}$, $p \to q$ is F and $q \to p$ is T. In the row where $p$, $q$ and $r$ are all F, $(p \to q) \to r$ is F and $p \to (q \to r)$ is T.
5. The duality principle needs the restriction to $\neg$, $\land$ and $\lor$. The equivalence $p \to p \equiv \top$ is correct, but the exchange of $\top$ and $\bot$ gives $p \to p \equiv \bot$, which fails in the row $p = \mathrm{T}$. The true dual is found only after $p \to p$ is rewritten as $\neg p \lor p$, and the dual $\neg p \land p \equiv \bot$ is correct.

### Consequences

1. By the associative and commutative laws, brackets and order do not matter in a long conjunction or disjunction. So we write $p_1 \land p_2 \land \cdots \land p_n$ and $p_1 \lor p_2 \lor \cdots \lor p_n$ without brackets.
2. The laws simplify formulas. For example, $(p \lor q) \land (p \lor \neg q) \equiv p \lor (q \land \neg q) \equiv p \lor \bot \equiv p$, by the distributive law, the [[law of noncontradiction|~f0f20560]] and the identity law.
3. Each law in the list comes with its dual, and the duality principle turns one member of each pair into the other.
4. Together with the complement laws $p \lor \neg p \equiv \top$ and $p \land \neg p \equiv \bot$, laws 1, 3 and 6 are the axioms of a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]]. Each correct equivalence between formulas in $\neg$, $\land$, $\lor$, $\top$ and $\bot$ then follows from these laws by replacement of equivalents.
5. The laws, De Morgan's laws and the [[double negation law|0NFAGWH84K]] turn each formula into an equivalent formula in [[disjunctive normal form|0GVDFAY4KW]] or in [[conjunctive normal form|0B3D4C8YF2]].
6. The laws of sets follow. For subsets $A$, $B$, $C$ of a universal set $U$, we have $x \in A \cap B$ exactly when $x \in A$ and $x \in B$, and $x \in A \cup B$ exactly when $x \in A$ or $x \in B$. So each law of logic gives a law of the [[algebra of sets|PFMGZ7988V]], as in the table below.

| Logic | Sets |
|---|---|
| $\land$, $\lor$, $\neg$ | [[intersection|0ZNMMA9TF5]] $\cap$, [[union|0X1GSWNBN1]] $\cup$, [[complement|0RKF1ZT5JJ]] $A^c$ |
| $\top$, $\bot$ | the [[universal set|0JNXSS9TJ9]] $U$, the [[empty set|0ECVK59Y03]] $\varnothing$ |
| $\equiv$ | $=$ |
| $p \land (q \lor r) \equiv (p \land q) \lor (p \land r)$ | $A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$ |
| $p \lor (p \land q) \equiv p$ | $A \cup (A \cap B) = A$ |
| $p \land \bot \equiv \bot$ | $A \cap \varnothing = \varnothing$ |

See also the [[distributive laws for sets|08N6EVA7DV]], [[De Morgan's laws for sets|0VK0W4D5Y5]] and [[Venn diagrams|0KCT6DPXJK]].

### Converse

Each of laws 1 to 7 is an equivalence, so it already contains both directions: each side is a logical consequence of the other.
The converse of the duality principle is also true: if $\varphi^d \equiv \psi^d$, then $\varphi \equiv \psi$.
The reason is that the dual of $\varphi^d$ is $\varphi$ again, so we can apply the principle to $\varphi^d$ and $\psi^d$.

### Generalizations

1. The distributive laws extend to $n$ terms: $p \land (q_1 \lor \cdots \lor q_n) \equiv (p \land q_1) \lor \cdots \lor (p \land q_n)$, and dually. The proof is by induction on $n$.
2. Laws 1 to 5 hold in every [[distributive lattice|0HM0HQCK6N]], and laws 1 to 7 hold in every bounded distributive lattice.
3. Laws 1 to 7 are also valid in [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], because each [[Heyting algebra|NG943XNQWH]] is a bounded distributive lattice. The duality principle fails there: $p \land \neg p \equiv \bot$ holds intuitionistically, but its dual $p \lor \neg p \equiv \top$, the [[law of excluded middle|0RYYK8PG4S]], does not.
4. In a [[Boolean ring|0RCV1ZFP8G]], conjunction becomes multiplication and [[exclusive or|~777e424f]] becomes addition. The idempotent law $p \land p \equiv p$ becomes $x^2 = x$.

## History

George Boole introduced an algebra of logic in The Mathematical Analysis of Logic of 1847 and in The Laws of Thought of 1854.
In his algebra the law $xx = x$ holds, and this idempotent law separates his algebra from the algebra of numbers.
Augustus De Morgan stated his laws for the negation of a conjunction and of a disjunction in Formal Logic of 1847.
Truth tables, which give a mechanical proof of each law, were used by Ludwig Wittgenstein and by Emil Post in 1921.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The laws of logic are rules for rewriting a sentence without a change of meaning.
"It is cold and it rains" says the same as "it rains and it is cold": this is the commutative law.
"It is cold, or it is cold and it rains" says the same as "it is cold": this is the absorption law.
"It rains or it rains" says no more than "it rains": this is the idempotent law.
Each law has a twin, with "and" and "or" exchanged.
The same rules hold for collections of objects, with "and" as the common part of two collections and "or" as their union.

### L2 human

We simplify $\varphi = (p \lor q) \land (p \lor \neg q) \land (p \lor r)$.

1. By the distributive law, read from right to left, $(p \lor q) \land (p \lor \neg q) \equiv p \lor (q \land \neg q)$.
2. By the law of noncontradiction, $q \land \neg q \equiv \bot$, so step 1 gives $p \lor \bot$.
3. By the identity law, $p \lor \bot \equiv p$.
4. By replacement of equivalents, $\varphi \equiv p \land (p \lor r)$.
5. By the absorption law, $p \land (p \lor r) \equiv p$. So $\varphi \equiv p$.
6. A truth table with $8$ rows confirms this: $\varphi$ is true in the four rows with $p$ true and false in the four rows with $p$ false.

The typical use is the simplification of a condition, of a logic circuit or of a set expression.
A trap is a wrong distributive law such as $p \land (q \lor r) \equiv (p \land q) \lor r$, which fails in the row $p = \mathrm{F}$, $q = \mathrm{F}$, $r = \mathrm{T}$.

### L3 human

Laws 1 to 7, with the complement laws, say that the classes of formulas modulo logical equivalence form a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]], the [[Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra|QC9M3Q8B8Q]].
Every Boolean algebra satisfies the same equations as the two-element algebra $\{\mathrm{F}, \mathrm{T}\}$, so a law of logic is the same thing as an identity of Boolean algebras.
For this reason a law checked by a truth table also holds for sets, for events in probability, and for switching circuits.

The duality principle is the order reversal of [[duality in order theory|MRYFDAG5G8]]: the dual of a lattice is the same set with the order turned around.
In classical logic, negation is an isomorphism from the Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra to its dual, and this is why De Morgan's laws prove the principle.
In intuitionistic logic, negation is not such an isomorphism, laws 1 to 7 survive, and the duality principle fails.
The page [[propositional logic|09K9MWD2QQ]] treats the laws as part of a formal system.
