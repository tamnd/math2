## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $v_1, \dots, v_m \in V$ with $m \ge 1$.

1. If the family $(v_1, \dots, v_m)$ is [[linearly dependent|0EVHJFTG9Z]], then there is an index $k$ with $v_k \in \operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_{k-1})$; for $k = 1$ this means $v_1 = 0$.
2. For such an index $k$, removing $v_k$ does not change the [[span|0HR573CHNM]]: $\operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, \widehat{v_k}, \dots, v_m) = \operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_m)$.
3. The family is linearly dependent if and only if some $v_j$ lies in the span of the other vectors $v_i$, $i \ne j$.
4. If $(v_1, \dots, v_m)$ is independent and $v \notin \operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_m)$, then $(v_1, \dots, v_m, v)$ is independent.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The field matters in part 3: we divide by a nonzero coefficient.
Over the ring $\Z$, the family $(2, 3)$ is dependent, but neither entry lies in the span of the other.
In part 4, the condition $v \notin \operatorname{span}$ is needed: $(1, 0)$ is independent, and $(1, 0), (2, 0)$ is not.

### Consequences

Part 2 shows that each finite spanning list contains a basis: remove dependent vectors one at a time; see [[spanning sets contain bases|06MRNP4BNT]].
Part 4 shows that an independent list in a finite-dimensional space extends to a basis; see [[extension to a basis|0M4SXE5WW3]].
The [[Steinitz exchange lemma|08ZDRN6H41]] applies parts 1 and 2 at each step.

### Converse

Part 3 is an equivalence.
The converse of part 4 also holds: if $(v_1, \dots, v_m, v)$ is independent, then $v \notin \operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_m)$, by part 3.

### Generalizations

Parts 1 to 4 hold for infinite families, with finite combinations: the proof uses only one finite relation at a time.
For modules over a ring that is not a field, parts 1 and 3 fail, as the example with $\Z$ shows.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If some vectors are dependent, then one of them is built from the earlier ones.
We can throw this vector away, and the span stays the same.
If a new vector is not built from an independent list, adding it keeps the list independent.

### L2 human

Example: $v_1 = (1, 0)$, $v_2 = (0, 1)$, $v_3 = (2, 3)$ in $\R^2$.
The relation $2 v_1 + 3 v_2 - v_3 = 0$ shows dependence.
The largest index with a nonzero coefficient is $k = 3$, and indeed $v_3 = 2 v_1 + 3 v_2$.
Removing $v_3$ leaves $(1, 0), (0, 1)$, which still span $\R^2$.
