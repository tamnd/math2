## Example

Let $G = S_3$ be the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] on $\{1, 2, 3\}$, and compose permutations from right to left.
Let $H = \{e, (1\,2)\}$, a [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] of order $2$.
We find the $(H, H)$ [[double cosets|~d8f44326]] $HgH$.

1. The double coset of $e$ is $HeH = H$, because $H$ is closed under products.
2. The double coset of $(1\,3)$ is the union of the [[left cosets|03DEH43F4H]] $(1\,3)H$ and $(1\,2)(1\,3)H$.
3. We have $(1\,3)H = \{(1\,3), (1\,2\,3)\}$, because $(1\,3)(1\,2) = (1\,2\,3)$.
4. We have $(1\,2)(1\,3) = (1\,3\,2)$ and $(1\,3\,2)(1\,2) = (2\,3)$, so $(1\,2)(1\,3)H = \{(1\,3\,2), (2\,3)\}$.
5. Thus $H(1\,3)H = \{(1\,3), (2\,3), (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$.
6. The two sets in steps 1 and 5 are disjoint, and they cover all $6$ elements of $S_3$.

Thus $H \backslash S_3 / H$ has exactly two elements, $H$ and $S_3 \setminus H$.
They have $2$ and $4$ elements, and $4$ does not divide $6$.

## Remarks

The size formula agrees with the count.
The [[conjugate|0H11ZPX4ND]] $(1\,3)H(1\,3)^{-1}$ is $\{e, (2\,3)\}$, and it meets $H$ only in $e$.
Thus $|H(1\,3)H| = 2 \cdot 2 / 1 = 4$.
For $g = e$, the formula gives $2 \cdot 2 / 2 = 2$.

The analogue of [[Lagrange's theorem|3C53TD]] fails for double cosets.
The sizes $2$ and $4$ differ, and $4$ is not a divisor of $|S_3| = 6$.

The group $S_3$ acts on the $3$ left cosets of $H$, and this is its action on $\{1, 2, 3\}$.
The two double cosets match the two orbits of $S_3$ on ordered pairs of points: pairs of equal points and pairs of different points.
This agrees with the fact that $S_3$ is [[$2$-transitive|BDJ1AX4XBB]] on $\{1, 2, 3\}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of the six ways to arrange three cards.
The subgroup has two moves: do nothing, or swap the first two cards.
We may use these moves before and after a given arrangement.
From "do nothing" we reach only the two moves of the subgroup.
From any other arrangement we reach the four other arrangements.
So the six arrangements fall into two pieces, of sizes $2$ and $4$.

### L2 human

We check the result with the right cosets.

1. The right cosets of $H$ are $H$, $H(1\,3) = \{(1\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$ and $H(2\,3) = \{(2\,3), (1\,2\,3)\}$.
2. The double coset $H(1\,3)H$ is the union $H(1\,3) \cup H(1\,3)(1\,2)$.
3. We have $(1\,3)(1\,2) = (1\,2\,3)$, and $H(1\,2\,3) = H(2\,3)$.
4. Thus $H(1\,3)H = H(1\,3) \cup H(2\,3)$, the same set as before.

The typical use is a small test case for double coset formulas.
A trap is to assume that $H(1\,3)H$ is the left coset $(1\,3)H$.
The double coset is twice as large.
