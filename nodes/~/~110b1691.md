## Summary

*Deep inference* is a way of building proof systems in which every rule has the form $S\{A\} / S\{B\}$: the rule rewrites a subformula $A$ into $B$ inside an arbitrary context $S\{\ \}$, at any depth.
The context is a formula with one hole, and the hole must lie in a positive position, so that $S\{A\} \to S\{B\}$ is valid whenever $A \to B$ is valid.
The *calculus of structures* of Guglielmi is the main formalism of deep inference, and its system SKS for classical [[propositional logic|09K9MWD2QQ]] is given in the Notation below.
A derivation is a sequence of formulas, each obtained from the previous one by one rule instance or by the equations, and a proof is a derivation from the unit $\mathsf{t}$.
Deep inference gives proof systems whose rules are all local, unlike the shallow rules of a [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]].

## Motivation

In a sequent calculus a rule acts only on the main connective of a formula in the sequent, so rules are shallow.
This restriction forces some rules to copy or erase whole formulas, such as contraction and weakening on a formula $A$ of any size.
Such a rule is not local: to check it, we must compare or copy an unbounded amount of data.
If rules may act anywhere inside a formula, these steps can be broken into atomic pieces, and each rule touches a bounded amount of data.
Guglielmi also wanted a proof system for a logic BV with a self-dual non-commutative connective, and deep rules gave one.

## Notation

Formulas of SKS are built from atoms $a, b, \dots$, their negations $\bar{a}, \bar{b}, \dots$, and the units $\mathsf{t}$ and $\mathsf{f}$, with $\vee$ and $\wedge$.
Negation of a compound formula is defined by the De Morgan laws, so negation never occurs above $\vee$ or $\wedge$ and every context is positive.
Other presentations write $\top$ and $\bot$ for the units, and some use bracket notation for $\vee$ and $\wedge$.
Formulas are taken modulo the equations: $\vee$ and $\wedge$ are associative and commutative, $A \vee \mathsf{f} = A$, $A \wedge \mathsf{t} = A$, $\mathsf{t} \vee \mathsf{t} = \mathsf{t}$ and $\mathsf{f} \wedge \mathsf{f} = \mathsf{f}$.
The rules of SKS are listed with premise first and conclusion second; each one applies inside any context $S\{\ \}$.
In the atomic rules, $a$ stands for an atom or a negated atom, and $\bar{\bar{a}} = a$.

- Atomic identity $\mathsf{ai}{\downarrow}$: $\mathsf{t}$ to $a \vee \bar{a}$. Atomic cut $\mathsf{ai}{\uparrow}$: $a \wedge \bar{a}$ to $\mathsf{f}$.
- Atomic weakening $\mathsf{aw}{\downarrow}$: $\mathsf{f}$ to $a$. Atomic coweakening $\mathsf{aw}{\uparrow}$: $a$ to $\mathsf{t}$.
- Atomic contraction $\mathsf{ac}{\downarrow}$: $a \vee a$ to $a$. Atomic cocontraction $\mathsf{ac}{\uparrow}$: $a$ to $a \wedge a$.
- Switch $\mathsf{s}$: $A \wedge (B \vee C)$ to $(A \wedge B) \vee C$.
- Medial $\mathsf{m}$: $(A \wedge B) \vee (C \wedge D)$ to $(A \vee C) \wedge (B \vee D)$.

The rules marked ${\downarrow}$ are the down rules, and those marked ${\uparrow}$ are the up rules; each up rule is the dual of a down rule.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The formula $(a \wedge b) \vee \bar{a} \vee \bar{b}$, an instance of excluded middle for $a \wedge b$, has a proof with two atomic identities and two switches. The [[example of identity for a conjunction|~1fb74cff]] gives each step.
- Contraction on $a \wedge b$ reduces to atomic contractions with one medial: $(a \wedge b) \vee (a \wedge b)$ becomes $(a \vee a) \wedge (b \vee b)$ and then $a \wedge b$. The [[example of contraction through medial|~e160e178]] checks each step.
- The general identity rule, from $\mathsf{t}$ to $A \vee \bar{A}$, is derivable from $\mathsf{ai}{\downarrow}$ and switch, by induction on $A$.
- The rule $\mathsf{s}$ applied deep: from $a \vee (b \wedge (c \vee d))$ it gives $a \vee ((b \wedge c) \vee d)$, with context $a \vee \{\ \}$.
- The system BV of Guglielmi extends multiplicative linear logic with a self-dual non-commutative connective, and it is presented in the calculus of structures.

### Non-examples

- A step that applies switch inside a negation is not a sound deep inference step. The context $\neg\{\ \}$ is negative, and the [[non-example|~f4b38806]] gives truth values that make the premise true and the conclusion false.
- A rule of [[LK|PZX9J3VEMT]], such as ${\wedge}$R, acts on the main connective of formulas in a sequent. It is a shallow rule, and it cannot be applied inside a subformula.
- Distributivity, from $A \wedge (B \vee C)$ to $(A \wedge B) \vee (A \wedge C)$, is valid but is not a rule of SKS. It copies $A$, so it is not local.

### Basic properties

- Soundness: each rule of SKS has a valid implication from premise to conclusion, and every context is positive, so every derivation is sound.
- Completeness and cut admissibility: the down fragment $\{\mathsf{ai}{\downarrow}, \mathsf{aw}{\downarrow}, \mathsf{ac}{\downarrow}, \mathsf{s}, \mathsf{m}\}$ proves every tautology, so the up rules, including the atomic cut, are admissible. We cite this theorem without proof.
- Locality: identity, cut, weakening and contraction are restricted to atoms, and switch and medial only rearrange subformulas without copying or erasing them.
- General contraction from $A \vee A$ to $A$ is derivable from $\mathsf{ac}{\downarrow}$, medial and the equations, by induction on $A$.
- The calculus has top-down symmetry: reversing a derivation and negating every formula gives a derivation that uses the dual rules.

### History

Guglielmi introduced the calculus of structures in the paper "A System of Interaction and Structure", to give a proof system for the logic BV.
The system SKS for classical propositional logic was developed later in the same formalism, with identity, cut, weakening and contraction on atoms only.
The calculus of structures has since been applied to linear, classical and modal logic and to process calculi.

### Common errors

- Applying a rule inside a negated subformula. In SKS negation is only on atoms, so every context is positive.
- Confusing switch with distributivity. Switch keeps one copy of $A$ and moves it next to $B$, while distributivity copies $A$.
- Thinking that cut is needed because the up rules are present. The up rules are admissible, so every tautology has a proof with down rules only.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In most proof systems, a rule looks only at the outermost symbol of a formula.
Deep inference allows a rule to rewrite any part of a formula, even a part deep inside it.
For example, in "$a$ or ($b$ and ($c$ or $d$))", a rule can rewrite the inner part "$b$ and ($c$ or $d$)" without touching $a$.
Because rules can act anywhere, big steps can be cut into very small steps on single letters.
So each step of a proof does only a small, fixed amount of work.

### L2 human

We prove $(a \wedge b) \vee \bar{a} \vee \bar{b}$ in SKS.

1. Start from $\mathsf{t}$, which equals $\mathsf{t} \wedge \mathsf{t}$.
2. Apply $\mathsf{ai}{\downarrow}$ twice to get $(a \vee \bar{a}) \wedge (b \vee \bar{b})$.
3. Switch with $A = a \vee \bar{a}$, $B = b$, $C = \bar{b}$ gives $((a \vee \bar{a}) \wedge b) \vee \bar{b}$.
4. Switch inside the left disjunct, on $b \wedge (a \vee \bar{a})$, gives $((b \wedge a) \vee \bar{a}) \vee \bar{b}$.
5. The equations rewrite this as $(a \wedge b) \vee \bar{a} \vee \bar{b}$.

The typical use is to build identity and contraction for big formulas from their atomic versions.
A trap is to use step 4 inside a negation; the formula language of SKS prevents that by allowing negation only on atoms.

### L3 human

Deep inference views a proof as a rewriting system on formulas, and the context $S\{\ \}$ plays the role that the sequent plays in Gentzen systems.
The up and down rules are dual, so a derivation can be read from top to bottom or, after negation, from bottom to top.
Deep inference systems exist for classical, linear and modal logics, and for BV with its non-commutative connective.
Like proof nets, deep inference derivations are used to study when two proofs are the same up to rule permutations.
Together with [[display calculi|~eb0eba64]] and [[hypersequent calculi|~908a51bf]], deep inference is one of the main proof formats of structural proof theory beyond the sequent.
