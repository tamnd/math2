## Statement

Let $\mathbb{H}$ be the [[quaternions|AM07TESJED]], with basis $1, i, j, k$ over $\R$ and $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$.
For $q = a + bi + cj + dk$, let $\overline{q} = a - bi - cj - dk$ and $\abs{q}^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2$.
Define
$$\phi(a + bi + cj + dk) = \begin{pmatrix} a + bi & c + di \\ -c + di & a - bi \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. $\phi \colon \mathbb{H} \to M_2(\C)$ is an injective $\R$-linear map with $\phi(1) = I_2$ and $\phi(pq) = \phi(p)\phi(q)$ for all $p, q \in \mathbb{H}$.
2. $\phi(\overline{q}) = \phi(q)^*$, the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]], and $\det\phi(q) = \abs{q}^2$.
3. $\phi$ restricts to a group isomorphism from the unit quaternions $\set{q \in \mathbb{H} : \abs{q} = 1}$ onto $SU(2)$.
4. So $SU(2) = \set{\begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ -\overline{\beta} & \overline{\alpha} \end{pmatrix} : \alpha, \beta \in \C, \ \abs{\alpha}^2 + \abs{\beta}^2 = 1}$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Part 3 needs $\abs{q} = 1$. For $q = 2$, $\phi(q) = 2I_2$ has determinant $4$, and it is not unitary.
- Part 4 needs determinant $1$. The unitary matrix $\operatorname{diag}(i, i)$ has determinant $-1$, and it is not of this form, because its diagonal entries $i$ and $i$ are not conjugates.

### Consequences

- $SU(2)$ is homeomorphic to the $3$-sphere $S^3 \subseteq \R^4$, by $(a, b, c, d) \mapsto \phi(a + bi + cj + dk)$. So it is compact and connected.
- The image $\phi(\mathbb{H})$ is the set of complex matrices $\begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ -\overline{\beta} & \overline{\alpha} \end{pmatrix}$, a real subalgebra of $M_2(\C)$ of dimension $4$.
- $\abs{pq} = \abs{p}\abs{q}$ for all quaternions, because $\det$ is multiplicative.

### Converse

Each element of $SU(2)$ comes from exactly one unit quaternion, by part 3.
So the formulas can be used in both directions: a unit quaternion gives a matrix, and a matrix in $SU(2)$ gives its quaternion $a + bi + cj + dk$ with $\alpha = a + bi$ and $\beta = c + di$.

### Generalizations

- The unit quaternions act on the pure quaternions $bi + cj + dk \cong \R^3$ by $v \mapsto qv\overline{q}$. This gives a surjective homomorphism $SU(2) \to SO(3)$ with kernel $\set{\pm 1}$, see [[quaternions and spatial rotation|EAK8KT5AMD]].
- In the same way, $\mathbb{H}^n$ with the quaternionic inner product gives the compact symplectic group $Sp(n)$, and $Sp(1) = SU(2)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Quaternions are numbers with four real parts, and their sizes multiply like the sizes of complex numbers.
Each quaternion can be written as a $2 \times 2$ complex matrix.
Multiplication of quaternions becomes multiplication of matrices.
The quaternions of size $1$ become exactly the unitary matrices with determinant $1$.

### L2 human

Worked example: the images of $i$, $j$ and $k$.

1. $\phi(i) = \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{pmatrix}$, $\phi(j) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\phi(k) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. $\phi(i)\phi(j) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \phi(k)$, as $ij = k$ says.
3. $\phi(k)^2 = \begin{pmatrix} i \cdot i & 0 \\ 0 & i \cdot i \end{pmatrix} = -I_2$, as $k^2 = -1$ says.
4. Each of the three matrices has determinant $1$, and each is unitary. So $i$, $j$ and $k$ are unit quaternions.

The typical use: compute with unit quaternions as $2 \times 2$ matrices, or the other way.
A trap: $\phi(i)$ is not the scalar matrix $iI_2$.
The complex unit $i$ and the quaternion unit $i$ are different objects here.

### L3 human

The theorem identifies the compact Lie group $SU(2)$ with $S^3$, so $SU(2)$ is simply connected.
The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ is the space of pure quaternions with the commutator bracket, and it is isomorphic to $(\R^3, \times)$.
The homomorphism $SU(2) \to SO(3)$ is the universal cover of $SO(3)$, so $SU(2) = \operatorname{Spin}(3)$.
The Lie theory of these groups belongs to the area of Lie groups.
