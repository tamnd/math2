## Example

Statement: if a right triangle has legs of lengths $a$ and $b$, hypotenuse of length $c$, and area $\frac{c^2}{4}$, then the triangle is isosceles.
We find the proof by the [[forward-backward method|~f8b8bc06]].

1. Backward. The goal is "the triangle is isosceles". The hypotenuse is longer than each leg, so the two equal sides must be the legs. New goal: $a = b$.
2. Backward. How can two numbers be shown equal? One way: show $a - b = 0$, and for that it suffices to show $(a - b)^2 = 0$. New goal: $a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = 0$.
3. Forward. The [[area of a triangle|065JWMZW86]] with legs $a$ and $b$ at the right angle is $\frac{ab}{2}$. With the hypothesis, $\frac{ab}{2} = \frac{c^2}{4}$, so $2ab = c^2$.
4. Forward. By the [[Pythagorean theorem|0VP3A1290K]], $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$. With step 3, $2ab = a^2 + b^2$.
5. The chains meet: step 4 gives $a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = 0$, the goal of step 2.

Written forward, the proof reads: the area gives $2ab = c^2$, Pythagoras gives $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, so $(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = 0$, so $a = b$, and the triangle is isosceles.

## Remarks

The backward steps chose which equation to aim for.
Without them, the forward process could produce many true equations, such as $c^4 = 4a^2b^2$, that do not lead to the goal.

The converse also holds: an isosceles right triangle with legs $a = b$ has $c^2 = 2a^2$ and area $\frac{a^2}{2} = \frac{c^2}{4}$.
For a right triangle with legs $3$ and $4$, the hypotenuse is $5$, the area is $6$, and $\frac{c^2}{4} = 6.25$, so the hypothesis fails, as it must for a triangle that is not isosceles.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Fold a square sheet of paper along a diagonal to get a right triangle with two equal sides.
Its area is half of the square, and a little calculation shows that it is a quarter of the square of the long side.
The statement says the reverse: a right triangle with exactly this area must have two equal sides.
To find the proof, we asked what we need at the end, two equal sides, and what the facts give us at the start, and we met in the middle.

### L2 human

We use the same plan for a related statement: if a right triangle has legs $a$, $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, then $a + b \leq c\sqrt{2}$.

1. Backward. Both sides are positive, so it suffices to show $(a + b)^2 \leq 2c^2$.
2. Forward. By Pythagoras, $2c^2 = 2a^2 + 2b^2$.
3. So the goal becomes $a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \leq 2a^2 + 2b^2$, that is, $0 \leq a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = (a - b)^2$.
4. This is true, so the statement holds, with equality exactly when $a = b$.

The typical use: square a goal with positive sides, then use a known identity in the forward direction.

A trap: in step 1, squaring keeps the direction of the inequality only because both sides are positive.
The final write-up must start from $(a - b)^2 \geq 0$ and go forward to the claim.
