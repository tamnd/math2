## Summary

*Combining logics* is the study of methods that build one logic from two or more given logics.
The combined logic has the connectives of all the components, so it has mixed formulas such as $\square_1(\square_2 p \to p)$.
The main methods are fusion, products and fibring.
The main questions are which properties transfer to the combination, and when the combination is a conservative extension of each component.
The *collapse problem* is the case where the combination identifies connectives that should stay different.

## Motivation

Many applications need more than one modality at a time.
An agent can have knowledge and obligations, and a program can have time and action.
A logic of "is" and "ought" needs an alethic box and a deontic box in one language, to state that "ought" does not follow from "is".
So we need a general way to put two logics together, and to know what we get.
Knowledge representation combines temporal reasoning with reasoning in description logics; description logics are treated in the [[theory of computation|52TNMMSXD3]].
The reverse direction is also useful: a complex logic can be split into simpler fragments.

## Notation

Let $\mathcal{L}_1$ and $\mathcal{L}_2$ be logics with [[consequence relations|J78Y1M4GND]] $\vdash_1$ and $\vdash_2$.
A combination is a logic $\mathcal{L}$ over a language that contains both languages.
It *extends* $\mathcal{L}_i$ if $\Gamma \vdash_i \varphi$ implies $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, and it is *conservative* over $\mathcal{L}_i$ if the converse holds for formulas in the language of $\mathcal{L}_i$.
We write $\mathcal{L}_1 \otimes \mathcal{L}_2$ for the fusion of two normal modal logics and $\mathcal{L}_1 \oplus \mathcal{L}_2$ for the unconstrained fibring.

## Remarks

### Examples

- Fusion. Let $\mathcal{L}_1$ and $\mathcal{L}_2$ be normal [[modal logics|C3RZY20ETK]] with boxes $\square_1$ and $\square_2$. Their fusion is the smallest normal bimodal logic that contains both, see [[multimodal logic|ERG3KAQWYF]]. Its axioms are the axioms of both logics; its frames are triples $(W, R_1, R_2)$ such that $(W, R_1)$ is an $\mathcal{L}_1$-frame and $(W, R_2)$ is an $\mathcal{L}_2$-frame.
- [[Mixed theorems of the fusion of K and T|~7e3f3838]]: the fusion proves $\square_1(\square_2 p \to p)$ but not $\square_2(\square_1 p \to p)$.
- Products. A product frame has the worlds $W_1 \times W_2$; $R_1$ moves the first coordinate and $R_2$ moves the second. Products prove interaction formulas such as commutativity, see [[products of modal logics|JA2EBWVW8A]].
- Fibring by functions (Gabbay). A model of $\mathcal{L}_1$ evaluates a formula $\square_2 \varphi$ at a world $w$ by a map $h_1$ that sends $w$ to a pointed model of $\mathcal{L}_2$. A second map $h_2$ does the same in the other direction.
- Algebraic fibring (Sernadas and others). The unconstrained fibring $\mathcal{L}_1 \oplus \mathcal{L}_2$ is the least logic over the disjoint union of the signatures that extends both logics; in category terms it is a coproduct. Constrained fibring also identifies shared connectives.
- [[Collapse of classical and intuitionistic implication|~24dd4ee2]]: a combination in which both implications satisfy the deduction theorem makes intuitionistic implication classical.
- [[Two copies of classical conjunction collapse|~9e83f3de]]: in the fibring of two copies of the logic of conjunction, the two conjunctions are interderivable.

### Non-examples

- Adding axioms to one logic, as in going from $\mathsf{K}$ to $\mathsf{S4}$, is an extension, not a combination; it adds no new connective.
- The fusion is not the product: [[the fusion of K with K does not prove commutativity|X2J9XQAP89]], but every product frame validates it.

### Basic properties

- Transfer for fusions. Fine and Schurz (1996) and Kracht and Wolter (1991) studied the transfer of completeness, the finite model property, decidability and interpolation from two normal modal logics to their fusion.
- Gabbay's criterion. The combination should be the smallest logic in the combined language that is a conservative extension of both components.
- Products behave worse. The product $\mathsf{S5} \times \mathsf{S5}$ has a finite axiomatization, but the $n$-dimensional product $\mathsf{S5}^n$ for $n \geq 3$ does not.
- Transfer for fibring. Unconstrained algebraic fibring preserves decidability. Marcelino and Caleiro showed that the decision problem of the fibring reduces in polynomial time to the harder of the two component problems.
- Collapse. If both components have an implication with the deduction theorem, and the combination keeps both deduction theorems, then the two implications are interderivable. Fibring of [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]] with classical logic then collapses into classical logic.
- Solutions of the collapse problem change the method: modulated fibring, cryptofibring and the meet combination of logics all avoid it.
- A related effect: if we join the sequent rules for classical conjunction and for classical disjunction, the result proves distributivity. Algebraic fibring of the two logics gives the logic of lattices instead, which is not distributive.

### History

Segerberg (1973) and Šehtman (1978) introduced products of modal logics.
Thomason (1984) introduced fusion, and Fitting (1969) had given early examples of fusions.
Gabbay (1996) introduced fibring, and in the same year he identified the collapse problem for classical and intuitionistic logic.
Del Cerro and Herzig (1996) gave an explicit collapse for a combination $\mathsf{C} + \mathsf{J}$, and repaired it with a modified weakening axiom.
Sernadas, Sernadas and Caleiro (1999) gave the categorial definition of fibring.

### Common errors

- To assume that the union of two axiom systems always gives the intended combination. The schemas now range over mixed formulas, and the result can prove new formulas in the old languages.
- To assume that a fusion has interaction between its boxes. Each interaction formula must be added as an axiom.
- To assume that transfer results for fusions hold for products. Products can lose finite axiomatizability and decidability.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Sometimes one logic is not enough.
We may want to talk about time and about knowledge in the same sentence.
Combining logics gives general recipes to join two logics into one.
The hard part is to make sure that the join does not change what each old logic says.

### L2 human

Fusion joins two modal logics with no link between the boxes; a frame has one relation for each box.
Products put two frames side by side, as rows and columns, and so the boxes commute.
Fibring is a general method: a model of one logic calls a model of the other when it meets the other connective.
The main risk is collapse, where shared structural rules force two different connectives to coincide.

### L3 human

Algebraic fibring makes the combination a universal construction, the least logic over the combined signature that extends both components.
Transfer theorems then ask which metaproperties pass to this least logic: completeness, interpolation, decidability and complexity.
For fusions of normal modal logics, the answers are mostly positive, by the work of Fine, Schurz, Kracht and Wolter.
The collapse problem shows that a minimal combination can still be too strong, when the presentation forces the deduction theorem for both implications.
