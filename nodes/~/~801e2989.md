## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, and let $M = \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix}$ be a [[block matrix|01AXG9KZQF]] over $F$ with $A$ of size $p \times p$ and $D$ of size $q \times q$.
Let $A$ be [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]], and let $S = M/A = D - CA^{-1}B$ be the [[Schur complement|CA1J41NWGS]] of $A$ in $M$.

1. (Block LDU factorization.)
$$M = \begin{pmatrix} I_p & 0 \\ CA^{-1} & I_q \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} A & 0 \\ 0 & S \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} I_p & A^{-1}B \\ 0 & I_q \end{pmatrix}.$$
2. $M$ is invertible if and only if $S$ is invertible.
3. If $S$ is invertible, then
$$M^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} A^{-1} + A^{-1} B S^{-1} C A^{-1} & -A^{-1} B S^{-1} \\ -S^{-1} C A^{-1} & S^{-1} \end{pmatrix}.$$
4. In the same way, let $D$ be invertible and let $T = M/D = A - BD^{-1}C$. Then $M$ is invertible if and only if $T$ is invertible, and in that case
$$M^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} T^{-1} & -T^{-1} B D^{-1} \\ -D^{-1} C T^{-1} & D^{-1} + D^{-1} C T^{-1} B D^{-1} \end{pmatrix}.$$

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The formula needs $A^{-1}$. For the [[exchange matrix|~74c14e5a]] $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ with $A = (0)$, $M$ is invertible, but parts 1 and 3 do not apply. Part 4 does not apply either, since $D = (0)$.
- Part 3 needs $S$ invertible. For $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $A = (1)$, we get $S = 1 - 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 0$, and $M$ is singular, as part 2 predicts.
- The blocks $A$ and $D$ must be square, so that $A^{-1}$ and $S^{-1}$ make sense; $B$ and $C$ can be rectangular.

### Consequences

- Comparing the two formulas for $M^{-1}$ when $A$, $D$, $S$ and $T$ are invertible gives $T^{-1} = A^{-1} + A^{-1} B S^{-1} C A^{-1}$. This is the [[Woodbury formula|~e4e78e97]] for $(A - BD^{-1}C)^{-1}$.
- The lower right block of $M^{-1}$ is $S^{-1}$. So the inverse of a Schur complement is a block of an inverse.
- With [[determinants|0KA7A4ENVJ]], part 1 gives $\det M = \det A \det S$.
- For $p = n - 1$ and $q = 1$, the last pivot of the [[LU decomposition|0RKG5YW66Y]] is the number $S$.

### Converse

Part 2 is an equivalence.
If $M$ is invertible and $A$ is singular, the formula does not apply, but $D$ or another block may still be invertible.

### Generalizations

- The formulas hold for matrices over any ring when $A$ and $S$ are units, because the proof only multiplies blocks.
- The quotient formula $M/A = (M/E)/(A/E)$ of Crabtree and Haynsworth holds when $E$ is an invertible leading block of $A$.
- For a singular $A$, generalized inverses give a version of the formula; this belongs to matrix theory.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To invert a matrix made of four blocks, invert the upper left block first.
Then form the Schur complement and invert it too.
The four blocks of the inverse are built from these two inverses.

### L2 human

Check the case $p = q = 1$, with $M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ and $a \ne 0$.

1. $S = d - cb/a = (ad - bc)/a$, so $S^{-1} = a/(ad - bc)$.
2. The lower right entry of $M^{-1}$ is $S^{-1} = a/(ad - bc)$.
3. The upper left entry is $1/a + (1/a)\, b\, S^{-1}\, c\, (1/a) = 1/a + bc/(a(ad - bc)) = d/(ad - bc)$.
4. The off diagonal entries are $-b/(ad - bc)$ and $-c/(ad - bc)$.
5. This is the [[formula for the inverse of a $2 \times 2$ matrix|8XWC7RKNN1]].

The [[worked $3 \times 3$ example|~25fc1b6c]] uses a $2 \times 2$ block $A$.

### L3 human

Part 1 says that $M$ is in the double coset of $\operatorname{diag}(A, S)$ for the unipotent block triangular groups.
The formula is the block version of $LDU$, and it is the starting point of recursive inversion algorithms, such as the Strassen algorithm for inversion.
Iterating over a chain of leading blocks recovers the [[existence of the LU decomposition|~8a5f1cba]].
