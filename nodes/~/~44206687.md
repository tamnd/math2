## Statement

Fix a whole number $N \ge 2$ and a [[sorites series|PDFBXAN9CC]] $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_N$ for a predicate $\Phi$.
In the standard example, $\alpha_n$ is a collection of $n$ grains of wheat, and $\Phi$ is "is a heap".
Write $H(n)$ for the sentence "$n$ grains of wheat make a heap".
The *tolerance principle* for "heap" is the following set of $N - 1$ conditionals:
$$H(n + 1) \to H(n) \qquad \text{for each } n \text{ with } 1 \le n \le N - 1.$$
In words: if $n + 1$ grains make a heap, then $n$ grains make a heap.
Its universal form is the single sentence $\forall n\, (1 \le n < N \to (H(n + 1) \to H(n)))$.
For a general predicate, the principle says that $\Phi$ applies equally to neighbors in the series.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. The neighbors must differ only by a small step. For a series with steps of $1000$ grains, the principle is not plausible.
2. The predicate must be vague. For the sharp predicate "$n \ge 500$", the conditional for $n = 499$ is false. So the principle fails, and no paradox arises.
3. The series must be finite and must link a clear case to a clear non-case. Otherwise the principle leads to no conflict.

### Consequences

1. With the two clear cases $H(N)$ and $\neg H(1)$, the principle is inconsistent; see the [[sorites theorem|~15bcecd7]].
2. So the principle, as an axiom, has a [[counterexample|~13b952b1]] in classical logic: some $n$ has $H(n + 1)$ and $\neg H(n)$.

### Converse

The converse conditionals $H(n) \to H(n + 1)$ also look true for "heap": adding one grain to a heap gives a heap.
They are consistent with the clear cases, since "$H(n)$ iff $n \ge 500$" satisfies all of them.
So the paradox comes from the tolerance direction alone, together with the clear cases.

### Generalizations

1. The same principle can be stated for any vague predicate with a sorites series: "tall" on heights, "bald" on numbers of hairs, "old" on ages.
2. The SEP entry calls the property of applying across incremental differences the tolerance of a vague term, following Wright.

## History

The SEP entry states that the name "tolerance" for this property follows Wright (1975, 1976).
The heap example goes back to the original paradox, which the SEP entry credits to Eubulides.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take a heap of sand and remove one grain.
It is still a heap: one grain cannot make the difference.
This rule is the tolerance principle.
It seems true for every heap, large or small.

### L2 human

Take $N = 5$ for a small case.

1. The principle has $4$ conditionals: $H(2) \to H(1)$, $H(3) \to H(2)$, $H(4) \to H(3)$, $H(5) \to H(4)$.
2. Suppose $H(5)$ and $\neg H(1)$.
3. Then not all conditionals hold, by the [[sorites theorem|~15bcecd7]].

The typical use of the principle is as the major premise of the sorites argument.
The trap is to accept each conditional and then to deny that their conjunction can fail.
In classical logic, the conjunction of all of them, with the two clear cases, is false.

### L3 human

Tolerance is the source of the [[sorites paradox|~13b952b1]].
Each response to the paradox gives a different status to the principle.
[[Epistemic vagueness|37ACYXP8QN]] makes one conditional false at an unknown place.
A [[supervaluational semantics|QQPVKJSAX1]] makes the universal form false but no single conditional false.
A degree theory makes each conditional almost, but not fully, true; see [[the sorites in Łukasiewicz logic|EP8J937EQC]].
