## Statement

Let $R$ be a ring with identity, and let $n \ge 1$.
The [[centre|MVT64B3B6H]] of $M_n(R)$ is
$$Z(M_n(R)) = \{\, cI_n : c \in Z(R) \,\}.$$
In particular, if $R$ is commutative, for example a field, the centre of $M_n(R)$ is the set of [[scalar matrices|0FRM62SG7D]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The identity of $R$ is used to form the [[matrix units|~2c685e2c]] $E_{ij}$, which test the commutation.
- Without commutativity of $R$, a scalar matrix $cI$ with $c \notin Z(R)$ is not central. Over the quaternions, $iI$ does not commute with $jI$.

### Consequences

- For $n \ge 2$ and $R \neq 0$, the centre is a proper subring, so $M_n(R)$ is not commutative.
- Two matrix rings $M_n(F)$ and $M_m(K)$ over fields can be isomorphic only if $F \cong K$, since the centre recovers the field.
- A linear map of $F^n$ that commutes with every linear map is a multiple of the identity.

### Converse

- Every $cI$ with $c \in Z(R)$ is central, because $(cI)B$ and $B(cI)$ have the entries $c\,b_{ij}$ and $b_{ij}\,c$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Most pairs of matrices do not commute.
The matrices that commute with every other matrix are only the multiples of the identity matrix.

### L2 human

The [[proof|~ef387ba9]] tests a matrix $A$ against the matrix units $E_{ij}$, which have a single $1$.
The [[lemma on matrix units|~30ae7883]] shows that this test already forces $A = cI$.
Commuting with the scalar matrices $bI$ then forces $c$ to commute with every $b$.

### L3 human

The centre of $M_n(R)$ is isomorphic to $Z(R)$ by $c \mapsto cI$.
This is one instance of a general fact: Morita equivalent rings have isomorphic centres.
For a field $F$, $M_n(F)$ is a central simple $F$-algebra.
