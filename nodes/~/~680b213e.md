## Statement

Let $V$ be an inner product space over $\mathbb{K} = \R$ or $\C$, and let $U$ be a finite-dimensional subspace of $V$.
Let $P = P_U$ be the [[orthogonal projection|0Q8HZTJC7G]] onto $U$, and let $U^\perp$ be the [[orthogonal complement|079D68S3AW]].
Then:

1. $P$ is linear, $P^2 = P$, the image of $P$ is $U$, and the kernel of $P$ is $U^\perp$.
2. If $e_1, \dots, e_k$ is an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of $U$, then $P v = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \ip{v}{e_i} e_i$ for all $v \in V$.
3. If $u_1, \dots, u_k$ is a basis of $U$ of nonzero, pairwise orthogonal vectors, then $P v = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\ip{v}{u_i}}{\ip{u_i}{u_i}} u_i$.
4. Let $V = \mathbb{K}^n$ with $\ip{x}{y} = \sum_j x_j \overline{y_j}$. Let $A$ be an $n \times k$ matrix whose columns are [[linearly independent|0EVHJFTG9Z]] and span $U$, so that $U$ is the [[column space|0F0RPPJ2TT]] of $A$. Then $A^* A$ is invertible, and $P$ has the matrix $A (A^* A)^{-1} A^*$, with $A^*$ the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]]. Over $\R$ this is $A (A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
5. In part 4, if the columns of $A = Q$ are orthonormal, then $Q^* Q = I$ and $P = Q Q^*$.

The [[proof|~0dff636a]] checks each formula against the unique splitting $v = Pv + (v - Pv)$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Orthonormality in part 2: for the basis $(1, 1, 0), (0, 1, 1)$ of a plane and $v = (1, 2, 3)$, the sum $\sum_i \ip{v}{u_i} u_i$ is $(3, 8, 5)$. The correct projection is $(1/3, 8/3, 7/3)$, as in [[the projection onto a plane|~33fbc74c]].
- Independent columns in part 4: for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, $A^{\mathsf{T}} A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 4 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $0$, so the formula has no meaning. Then one must first drop dependent columns.

### Consequences

- The projection onto a line $\mathbb{K} a$ with $a \ne 0$ is $\frac{a a^*}{a^* a}$. In $\R^n$ this is $\frac{a a^{\mathsf{T}}}{a^{\mathsf{T}} a}$.
- $I - P$ is the projection onto $U^\perp$ along $U$, and $\norm{v}^2 = \norm{Pv}^2 + \norm{v - Pv}^2$ by the [[Pythagorean theorem|0BVTDM872V]].
- If $V$ is finite-dimensional, the trace of $P$ is $\dim U$. For $P = Q Q^*$ this follows from $\tr(Q Q^*) = \tr(Q^* Q) = \tr(I_k) = k$.
- The [[expansion in an orthogonal set|Q6DE7QVZ24]] is part 3 for $v \in U$, since then $Pv = v$. Part 3 alone is the node [[projection formula for an orthogonal basis|6ARG1JYE34]].

### Generalizations

For a closed subspace $U$ of a Hilbert space with an orthonormal basis $(e_i)$, part 2 holds with an infinite sum that converges in norm.
For a [[Fourier series|G12KXDHPPS]], the partial sum of degree $N$ is the projection onto the trigonometric polynomials of degree at most $N$; see [[the polynomial and Fourier example|~5c06ec2d]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

To project onto a subspace with perpendicular unit axes, project onto each axis and add the results.
If the axes are not perpendicular, this simple rule fails, and the matrix formula corrects for the angles between the axes.

### L2 human

We find the matrix of the projection onto the line through $a = (1, 2, 2)$ in $\R^3$.

1. $a^{\mathsf{T}} a = 1 + 4 + 4 = 9$.
2. $P = \frac{1}{9} a a^{\mathsf{T}} = \frac{1}{9} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. For $v = (1, 1, 1)$, $a^{\mathsf{T}} v = 5$, so $P v = \frac{5}{9} (1, 2, 2)$.

The full check is in [[the line example|~69c2d599]].
The typical use of part 4 is least squares: $A (A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}} b$ is the point of the column space closest to $b$.
The trap is to cancel $A (A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}}$ to $I$; that is valid only when $A$ is square and invertible, and then $U$ is the whole space.

### L3 human

The matrix $A (A^* A)^{-1} A^*$ depends only on $U$, not on the basis: if $A' = A G$ with $G$ invertible, then $A' (A'^* A')^{-1} A'^* = A G G^{-1} (A^* A)^{-1} (G^*)^{-1} G^* A^* = A (A^* A)^{-1} A^*$.
The factor $(A^* A)^{-1}$ is the inverse of the [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]] of the columns, and it corrects for their lack of orthonormality.
With the [[Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse|0YY8VX28JR]] $A^+$, the projection onto the column space of any $A$ is $A A^+$, with no condition on the columns.
