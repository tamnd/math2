## Summary

A *hypersequent* is a finite [[multiset|MYJ8Y050J0]] of sequents $\Gamma_1 \Rightarrow \Delta_1 \mid \cdots \mid \Gamma_n \Rightarrow \Delta_n$, read as the disjunction of its components.
A *hypersequent calculus* is a proof system whose lines are hypersequents.
Its rules are the rules of a [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]], each applied to one component with an unchanged side hypersequent, together with external weakening, external contraction and extra structural rules that act on several components.
The main example is Avron's calculus HLC for Gödel-Dummett logic LC, whose extra rule is the communication rule; the [[definition|~b56e1b30]] states it in full.
Hypersequents give cut-free calculi with the subformula property for many logics that have no such ordinary sequent calculus.

## Motivation

Gödel-Dummett logic LC is [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]] with the extra axiom $(A \to B) \vee (B \to A)$.
In the calculus [[LJ|PZX9J3VEMT]], a cut-free derivation of $\Rightarrow A \vee B$ ends with the rule ${\vee}$R, so it contains a derivation of $\Rightarrow A$ or of $\Rightarrow B$.
LC does not have this disjunction property.
The formula $(p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$ is in LC, but neither disjunct is in LC.
A one-world model with $p$ true and $q$ false refutes $p \to q$, and the model with the values swapped refutes $q \to p$.
So no calculus in which every cut-free proof of a disjunction ends with ${\vee}$R can be complete for LC.
A hypersequent keeps several alternatives open at the same time.
The communication rule then lets two alternatives exchange formulas, which is the step that LC needs.
The same problem arises for the modal logic S5, and hypersequents solve it in the same way.

## Notation

Components are separated by a vertical bar $\mid$, and $G$, $H$ or $\mathcal{G}$ denote side hypersequents.
For intermediate logics the components are single-conclusion sequents $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Pi$, with $\Pi$ empty or one formula.
For classical, modal and substructural logics the components are often multi-conclusion sequents $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta$.
The interpretation of the bar depends on the logic.
For LC a hypersequent means the disjunction of the implications $\bigwedge \Gamma_i \to \bigvee \Delta_i$.
For S5 it means the disjunction of the formulas $\Box(\bigwedge \Gamma_i \to \bigvee \Delta_i)$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The derivation of $(p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$ in HLC uses one application of the communication rule to two axioms. It is worked out in the [[example of the prelinearity axiom|~01080d03]].
- The weak excluded middle $\neg p \vee \neg\neg p$ is derivable in HLC with the communication rule and internal contraction. It is worked out in the [[example of the weak excluded middle|~4d8ede92]].
- Avron's calculus for S5 has multi-conclusion components, the rules of [[LK|PZX9J3VEMT]] in each component, and three modal rules. The rule $\Box$R infers $G \mid \Box\Gamma \Rightarrow \Box A$ from $G \mid \Box\Gamma \Rightarrow A$, and $\Box$L infers $G \mid \Gamma, \Box A \Rightarrow \Delta$ from $G \mid \Gamma, A \Rightarrow \Delta$. The modal splitting rule infers $G \mid \Box\Gamma \Rightarrow \Box\Delta \mid \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$ from $G \mid \Box\Gamma, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Box\Delta, \Pi$.
- In that S5 calculus, $\Rightarrow p, \Box\neg\Box p$ has a cut-free derivation. From $p \Rightarrow p$, $\Box$L gives $\Box p \Rightarrow p$. Splitting gives $\Box p \Rightarrow \; \mid \; \Rightarrow p$, then ${\neg}$R and $\Box$R give $\Rightarrow \Box\neg\Box p \mid \Rightarrow p$. Internal weakening in both components and external contraction give $\Rightarrow p, \Box\neg\Box p$.
- A hypersequent with one component is an ordinary sequent. So every derivation of LJ is a derivation of HLC in which all hypersequents have one component.

### Non-examples

- The calculus HLJ is HLC without the communication rule. It has no rule beyond EW and EC that acts on several components, so it is only LJ written with bars. A hypersequent is derivable in HLJ only if one component is derivable in LJ, as the [[non-example of HLJ|~d22732b9]] proves.
- The list of premises of a rule is a list of sequents, but it is not a hypersequent: the premises are all asserted, so the list is read as a conjunction. For instance, $p \Rightarrow q$ and $q \Rightarrow p$ together are not valid in LC, while the hypersequent $p \Rightarrow q \mid q \Rightarrow p$ is.
- A nested sequent is a tree of sequents, in which a sequent can contain other sequents. A nested sequent with a sequent inside a sequent inside a sequent is not a hypersequent, because the components of a hypersequent contain formulas only.

### Basic properties

- Every rule except the extra structural rules copies the side hypersequent $G$, so these rules act on one component at a time.
- HLC is sound and complete for LC: $\Rightarrow A$ is derivable if and only if $A \in \mathrm{LC}$. This and the next property are stated in the [[definition|~b56e1b30]].
- HLC has cut elimination, and a cut-free derivation has the [[subformula property|~694ac127]].
- The bar interacts with the logical rules only through the structural rules, so one set of logical rules serves for many logics.

### History

Mints used structures of this kind in 1971, under the name "cortege", to give a calculus for S5.
Pottinger independently used them in 1983 for modal logics.
Avron's influential 1996 survey introduced the term "hypersequent" and treated modal, intermediate and substructural logics.
Dummett introduced the logic LC in 1959.

### Common errors

- Reading the bar as a conjunction. The bar is a disjunction, so a hypersequent is weaker than each of its components.
- Applying external contraction to two components that are not equal. External contraction removes one of two identical components only.
- Treating the communication rule as an intuitionistic rule. It is sound on linearly ordered Kripke models, and it fails on a model with two incomparable worlds.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An ordinary sequent is one claim of the form "these assumptions give this conclusion".
A hypersequent is a list of such claims, and it says that at least one of them holds.
For example, "$p$ gives $q$, or $q$ gives $p$" is a hypersequent with two claims.
The proof rules change one claim at a time, as in an ordinary proof.
One special rule, the communication rule, lets two claims exchange assumptions.
With this rule we can prove facts that hold when all situations are arranged in one line, such as "$p$ gives $q$, or $q$ gives $p$".

### L2 human

We derive $(p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$ in HLC.

1. $q \Rightarrow q$ and $p \Rightarrow p$ are axioms.
2. The communication rule with these two premises gives $p \Rightarrow q \mid q \Rightarrow p$.
3. The rule ${\to}$R in the first component gives $\Rightarrow p \to q \mid q \Rightarrow p$.
4. The rule ${\to}$R in the second component gives $\Rightarrow p \to q \mid \Rightarrow q \to p$.
5. The rule ${\vee}$R in each component gives two equal components $\Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$.
6. External contraction gives $\Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$.

The typical use is proof search: we read the rules from the conclusion upward and stop at axioms.
Because the calculus is cut-free, every formula in the search is a subformula of the goal.
A trap is to forget that step 2 changes the antecedents: the conclusion $p \Rightarrow q$ comes from the premise $q \Rightarrow q$ with $p$ moved in.

### L3 human

Hypersequents are a first step in a hierarchy of richer structures for proof systems.
A hypersequent is a disjunction of sequents, a nested sequent is a tree of sequents, and a [[labelled sequent calculus|~fddc9de5]] names the worlds of a Kripke model explicitly.
[[Display calculi|~eb0eba64]] and [[deep inference|~110b1691]] take other routes to the same goal of analytic calculi.
Hypersequent calculi exist for fuzzy logics such as Gödel logic, for S5 and other modal logics, and for substructural logics.
The bar can be seen as a structural disjunction, as the comma on the right of an LK sequent is a structural disjunction of formulas.
