## Example

Let $L$ be any signature, and let $\lambda_3$ be the sentence
$$\exists x \, \exists y \, \exists z \, (\neg x = y \wedge \neg x = z \wedge \neg y = z),$$
which uses only the logical symbol $=$.
We show that $\lambda_3$ is [[true|~58a589d1]] in an $L$-structure $\mathcal{M}$ if and only if the domain $M$ has at least three elements.

1. Suppose $M$ has three different elements $a$, $b$ and $c$. Take the witnesses $x \mapsto a$, $y \mapsto b$ and $z \mapsto c$.
2. Under this assignment, $x = y$ fails because $a \neq b$, so $\neg x = y$ holds, by the clauses for equality and negation of [[satisfaction|~cf18e0d7]]. In the same way, $\neg x = z$ and $\neg y = z$ hold.
3. By the clause for $\wedge$, the conjunction holds, and by three uses of the clause for $\exists$, $\mathcal{M} \models \lambda_3$.
4. Conversely, suppose $\mathcal{M} \models \lambda_3$. By the clause for $\exists$, used three times, there are $a, b, c \in M$ that satisfy the conjunction.
5. By the clauses for $\wedge$, $\neg$ and $=$, we get $a \neq b$, $a \neq c$ and $b \neq c$. So $M$ has at least three elements.

For example, $\lambda_3$ is true in $(\set{0, 1, 2}, <)$, in $(\N, <)$ and in the group $\Z / 4\Z$, and false in $(\set{0, 1}, <)$ and in the trivial group.

## Remarks

The example shows that first-order sentences can count elements, as long as the count is a fixed finite number.
The same pattern gives, for each $n \geq 1$, a sentence $\lambda_n$ with $n$ variables that is true exactly in the structures with at least $n$ elements.
Then $\lambda_n \wedge \neg \lambda_{n+1}$ is true exactly in the structures with exactly $n$ elements.
The set $\set{\lambda_n : n \geq 1}$ has as models exactly the infinite structures.
No single sentence is true exactly in the infinite structures, and no set of sentences is true exactly in the finite ones; these facts follow from the [[compactness theorem|1NR3HAWG9D]].
The example also shows that $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$ always records the size of a finite domain.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The sentence says "there are three things that are all different from each other".
It is true in any world with three or more objects: just pick three different ones.
It is false in a world with only two objects, because any three picks must repeat one of them.
So a sentence can say "there are at least three objects" without any symbols except "equals".

### L2 human

We check that $\lambda_3$ is false in $M = \set{0, 1}$, whatever the signature.

1. Any choice of values for $x$, $y$ and $z$ picks three elements from $\set{0, 1}$.
2. Three picks from two elements repeat an element, by the pigeonhole principle. So two of the values are equal.
3. If the values of $x$ and $y$ are equal, then $\neg x = y$ fails; the cases $x, z$ and $y, z$ are the same.
4. So no choice satisfies the conjunction, and $\lambda_3$ is false.

The typical use is to fix the size of the models of a theory, or to require infinite models.
A trap is to write $\exists x \, \exists y \, \exists z \, \top$: without the inequalities, the witnesses may coincide, and the sentence is true in every structure.
