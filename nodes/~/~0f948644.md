## Statement

Let $R$ be a ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $N$ be a strictly [[upper triangular|0MKABWDRNC]] $n \times n$ matrix over $R$.
Then $N^n = 0$, so $N$ is [[nilpotent|0419GJC84P]].
More precisely, $(N^k)_{ij} = 0$ whenever $j - i < k$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The diagonal must be zero. The upper triangular $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has $k$-th power $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which is never $0$.
- The exponent $n$ cannot be lowered in general: the shift matrix $E_{12} + E_{23} + \dots + E_{n-1,n}$ has $N^{n-1} = E_{1n} \neq 0$.

### Consequences

- $I - N$ is invertible with inverse $I + N + \dots + N^{n-1}$, by the [[geometric series for nilpotent matrices|~9c73b141]].
- A unitriangular matrix $I + N$ is invertible, and its inverse is unitriangular.
- The same holds for strictly lower triangular matrices, by transposition.

### Converse

- Over a field, every nilpotent matrix is [[similar|0E89HQATM8]] to a strictly upper triangular one. This uses [[triangularisation|0363JMQ42Y]] and the fact that $0$ is the only eigenvalue; it belongs to a later topic.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A strictly upper triangular matrix has zeros on and below the diagonal.
Each multiplication by it pushes the nonzero entries one step further up and to the right.
After $n$ steps, nothing is left.

### L2 human

As a map, $N$ sends $e_j$ into $\langle e_1, \dots, e_{j-1} \rangle$, one level down the flag.
Applying $N$ $n$ times takes every basis vector below $e_1$, to $0$; the [[proof|~66e14576]] gives the entry version.
