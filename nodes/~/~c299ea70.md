## Example

Let $\mathcal{A} = (\Z, <)$, and let $\mathcal{B} = (\Z + \Z, <)$ be the [[ordinal sum|NJY30DQG3H]] of two copies of $\Z$: the set $\set{0, 1} \times \Z$ with $(i, m) < (j, n)$ when $i < j$, or $i = j$ and $m < n$.
We show that no [[back-and-forth system|~95b1c1d3]] from $\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{B}$ exists.

1. Suppose that $I$ is a back-and-forth system, and choose some $p_0 \in I$. It exists, since $I$ is not empty.
2. Back for $b = (0, 0)$ gives $p_1 \in I$ with $p_1 \supseteq p_0$ and $(0, 0)$ in its range.
3. Back for $b' = (1, 0)$ gives $p_2 \in I$ with $p_2 \supseteq p_1$ and $(1, 0)$ in its range.
4. Let $x = p_2^{-1}(0, 0)$ and $y = p_2^{-1}(1, 0)$. Since $(0, 0) < (1, 0)$ and $p_2$ reflects $<$, we have $x < y$ in $\Z$. Let $d = y - x \ge 1$.
5. Exactly $d - 1$ integers lie strictly between $x$ and $y$.
6. Apply back $d$ times, to the elements $(0, 1), (0, 2), \dots, (0, d)$ of $\mathcal{B}$. Each lies strictly between $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$. We get $q \in I$ with $q \supseteq p_2$ and these $d$ elements in its range.
7. Their preimages under $q$ are $d$ distinct integers, and each lies strictly between $x$ and $y$, because $q$ preserves and reflects $<$.
8. This contradicts step 5, so no back-and-forth system exists, and $\mathcal{A} \not\cong_p \mathcal{B}$.

## Remarks

The two orders are [[elementarily equivalent|P3B1V182N4]]: Duplicator wins every finite Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game on them; see [[this example|~4a7a973a]].
So the example separates elementary equivalence from partial isomorphism.
By [[Karp's theorem|~ca02717b]] some sentence of $L_{\infty\omega}$ separates them, and one is easy to write:
$$\forall x \, \forall y \, \bigvee_{n \ge 0} \text{"at most } n \text{ elements lie between } x \text{ and } y\text{"}.$$
Each disjunct is a first-order formula, so the sentence lies in $L_{\omega_1\omega}$.
It holds in $\Z$ and fails in $\Z + \Z$ for $x = (0, 0)$ and $y = (1, 0)$.
The proof above follows this sentence: step 6 is Spoiler's answer to the disjunct for $n = d - 1$.

Both orders are countable, so a back-and-forth system would give an isomorphism; the example also shows directly that $\Z \not\cong \Z + \Z$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The integers form one infinite line of steps, and any two of them are a finite number of steps apart.
Two copies of the integers, one after the other, contain two points that are infinitely far apart: one in the first copy and one in the second.
A matching that tries to copy those two points into the integers must put them a finite distance apart.
Then we ask for more points between them than the integers have room for, and the matching cannot grow.

### L2 human

We run the proof with an actual choice of $p_2$.

1. Suppose $p_2 = \set{(0, (0, 0)), (3, (1, 0))}$, so $x = 0$, $y = 3$ and $d = 3$.
2. Between $0$ and $3$ lie exactly the two integers $1$ and $2$.
3. Back must give preimages to $(0, 1)$, $(0, 2)$, $(0, 3)$, three elements between $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$.
4. Their preimages must be three distinct integers between $0$ and $3$, and there are only two. So back fails.

The typical use of this example is to show that partial isomorphism is strictly finer than elementary equivalence.
A trap is to stop after one back step: one step does not fail, and the failure needs $d$ steps, which depends on the earlier moves.
