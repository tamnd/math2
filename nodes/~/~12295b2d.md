## Statement

Let $F$ be a field and let $n \ge 1$.

1. [[Congruence|NZJDNC6EP6]] of $n \times n$ matrices over $F$ is an [[equivalence relation|0SGTMBEWRF]].

Let $A' = P^T A P$ with $P$ invertible.
Then:

2. $\rank A' = \rank A$.
3. If $A$ is [[symmetric|0VR671PHE5]], then $A'$ is symmetric. If $A$ is [[skew-symmetric|0PBY4E9GQF]], then $A'$ is skew-symmetric.
4. If $x^T A x = 0$ for all $x \in F^n$, then $x^T A' x = 0$ for all $x \in F^n$.
5. $\det A' = (\det P)^2 \det A$.

The [[proof|~82c9fd77]] uses the rules for transposes and products.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $P$ must be invertible. For $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $A = I_2$, $P^T A P = P$ has rank $1$, and the relation is not symmetric.
- The relation uses $P^T$ and $P$ together. With $P^T A Q$ for two independent invertible matrices, symmetry is not kept: $I_2$ becomes $Q$ when $P = I_2$, and $Q$ can be any invertible matrix.

### Consequences

- The rank of a [[bilinear form|0SQZMXB6GP]] is well defined, and so are its symmetry and skew-symmetry.
- A symmetric matrix is never congruent to a nonsymmetric matrix.
- Over $\R$, the sign of the determinant is a congruence invariant.

### Converse

The converse of part 2 is false: matrices of equal rank need not be congruent.
Over $\R$, $I_2$ and $-I_2$ both have rank $2$, but they are not congruent, as the [[concept page|WJWNYSQKK8]] shows.

### Generalizations

The same proof works for $*$-congruence $A' = P^* A P$ over $\C$, which keeps rank and the property of being [[Hermitian|0P17NGQQMQ]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Congruence behaves like equality in three ways.
Each matrix is congruent to itself.
If one matrix is congruent to a second, the second is congruent to the first.
If a first is congruent to a second and the second to a third, the first is congruent to the third.
Congruence also keeps the number of independent rows and the mirror symmetry of a matrix.

### L2 human

We check the transitivity on an example over $\Q$.

1. $A = I_2$ and $P = \operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$ give $B = P^T A P = \operatorname{diag}(4, 1)$.
2. $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $C = Q^T B Q = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. $PQ = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $(PQ)^T I_2 (PQ) = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = C$.
4. All three matrices are symmetric with rank $2$, and the determinants $1$, $4$, $4$ differ by squares.

The typical use is to show that two matrices are not congruent: find an invariant that differs.
A trap: equal determinants do not prove congruence, and different determinants do not disprove it over $\Q$.

### L3 human

Part 1 holds because congruence is the orbit relation of the action $P \cdot A = (P^{-1})^T A P^{-1}$ of the group $GL_n(F)$.
Every orbit relation of a group action is an equivalence relation.
Parts 2 to 5 say that rank, symmetry, skew-symmetry, the alternating property and the square class of the determinant are invariants of this action.
