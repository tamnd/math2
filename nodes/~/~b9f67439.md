## Statement

Let $A$ be a real $m \times n$ matrix with [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]] $A^{\mathsf{T}}$, and let $b \in \R^m$.
Then:

1. A vector $\hat x \in \R^n$ is a [[least-squares solution|0BYTQ4X1HC]] of $Ax = b$ if and only if it solves the *normal equations* $A^{\mathsf{T}} A \hat x = A^{\mathsf{T}} b$.
2. Equivalently, $\hat x$ is a least-squares solution if and only if the residual $b - A \hat x$ is orthogonal to every column of $A$.
3. The normal equations always have a solution.
4. $\operatorname{Nul}(A^{\mathsf{T}} A) = \operatorname{Nul}(A)$, where $\operatorname{Nul}$ is the [[null space|0PQ2GGY1QV]]. So $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ and $A$ have the same rank.
5. The $n \times n$ matrix $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] if and only if the columns of $A$ are [[linearly independent|0CRMGPAH83]]. Then $\hat x = (A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}} b$ is the unique least-squares solution.

Over $\C$ the same holds with the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]] $A^*$ in place of $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
The [[proof|~7c8b8bfc]] turns the orthogonality condition of best approximation into a linear system.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Independent columns in part 5: for $A$ with columns $(1, 1, 1)$ and $(2, 2, 2)$, $A^{\mathsf{T}} A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 12 \end{pmatrix}$ is singular, and the normal equations have a line of solutions; see [[the example with dependent columns|~4c571e93]].
- The transpose, not the inverse: $A$ is usually not square, so "$\hat x = A^{-1} b$" has no meaning. Multiplying by $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ first gives a square system.

### Consequences

- For a line fit $y = c + s t$ to points $(t_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, m$, the normal equations are $m c + (\sum t_i) s = \sum y_i$ and $(\sum t_i) c + (\sum t_i^2) s = \sum t_i y_i$; see [[the four-point example|~942c5c13]].
- The projection matrix onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is $A (A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}}$ for independent columns, as in the [[projection formulas|~680b213e]].

### Numerical trouble

The normal equations square the condition number.
For $A$ with independent columns, the 2-norm [[condition number|AB3S6MHJ8X]] is $\kappa_2(A) = \sigma_{\max} / \sigma_{\min}$, the ratio of the largest and smallest [[singular values|09VMDSRXWJ]].
With the [[singular value decomposition|000NPTMX6J]] $A = U \Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}}$, we get $A^{\mathsf{T}} A = V \Sigma^{\mathsf{T}} \Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}}$, so the singular values of $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ are the squares $\sigma_i^2$.
Hence $\kappa_2(A^{\mathsf{T}} A) = \kappa_2(A)^2$.
When $A$ is ill-conditioned, forming and solving $A^{\mathsf{T}} A \hat x = A^{\mathsf{T}} b$ in floating point can lose about twice as many digits as a method that works with $A$ itself.
For this reason the [[QR decomposition|0ERWG4NXQ9]] is preferred in computation; see [[numerical methods for least squares|AWQA2Y30N4]].

### Converse

Part 1 is an equivalence.
A related claim is false: a solution of $A^{\mathsf{T}} A x = A^{\mathsf{T}} b$ need not solve $Ax = b$, because $Ax = b$ may have no solution at all.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The normal equations turn a minimization into an ordinary square linear system.
The word "normal" means "perpendicular": the equations say that the error is perpendicular to the columns of $A$.

### L2 human

For the four points $(0, 1)$, $(1, 2)$, $(2, 2)$, $(3, 4)$ and the line $y = c + s t$:

1. $m = 4$, $\sum t_i = 6$, $\sum t_i^2 = 14$, $\sum y_i = 9$ and $\sum t_i y_i = 18$.
2. The normal equations are $4c + 6s = 9$ and $6c + 14s = 18$.
3. The solution is $c = s = 9/10$.

The typical use is a small fit by hand or a fit with well-conditioned data.
The trap is to use the normal equations for a nearly dependent set of columns, where the squared condition number destroys accuracy.

### L3 human

The normal equations are the first-order condition for the convex quadratic $f(x) = \norm{Ax - b}^2$, whose gradient is $2 A^{\mathsf{T}} (A x - b)$.
The matrix $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ is the [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]] of the columns of $A$, and it is positive definite exactly when the columns are independent.
In statistics, $(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}} b$ is the ordinary least-squares estimator; this belongs to [[linear regression|41FVC4P5JB]].
