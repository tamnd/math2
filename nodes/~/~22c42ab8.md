## Example

Each year, $10\%$ of the people in a city move to its suburbs, and $20\%$ of the people in the suburbs move to the city.
Let $x_k = (c_k, s_k)$ be the fractions of the population in the city and in the suburbs after $k$ years, with $x_0 = (1, 0)$.
Then $x_{k+1} = A x_k$ with
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. $p_A = (t - 0.9)(t - 0.8) - 0.02 = t^2 - 1.7 t + 0.7 = (t - 1)(t - 0.7)$.
2. The eigenvalues $1$ and $0.7$ are distinct, so $A$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] by [[distinct eigenvalues imply diagonalizable|0Y95Z7MT02]].
3. Eigenvectors: $A(2, 1) = (1.8 + 0.2, 0.2 + 0.8) = (2, 1)$ and $A(1, -1) = (0.7, -0.7)$.
4. $P = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\det P = -3$, so $P^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$.
5. $A^k = P \operatorname{diag}(1, 0.7^k) P^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 + 0.7^k & 2 - 2 \cdot 0.7^k \\ 1 - 0.7^k & 1 + 2 \cdot 0.7^k \end{pmatrix}$. For $k = 1$, this is $A$.
6. The coordinates of $x_0$ are $P^{-1} x_0 = (\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})$. So $x_k = \tfrac{1}{3}(2, 1) + \tfrac{1}{3} \, 0.7^k (1, -1)$.
7. In coordinates, $x_k = \big(\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{3} 0.7^k, \ \tfrac{1}{3} - \tfrac{1}{3} 0.7^k\big)$.
8. Check: the formula gives $x_1 = (0.9, 0.1)$, $x_2 = (0.83, 0.17)$ and $x_3 = (0.781, 0.219)$. Direct multiplication gives $A(0.9, 0.1) = (0.83, 0.17)$ and $A(0.83, 0.17) = (0.781, 0.219)$.
9. Since $0.7^k \to 0$, $x_k \to (\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})$, and $A^k \to \tfrac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

## Remarks

- The limit $(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})$ is the eigenvector for $1$ whose entries add up to $1$. It is the stationary distribution of the [[Markov chain|0G48PYM96Q]] with transition matrix $A$.
- Every start $x_0$ with $c_0 + s_0 = 1$ gives the same limit, since its coordinate along $(2, 1)$ is $\tfrac{1}{3}(c_0 + s_0) = \tfrac{1}{3}$.
- The distance to the limit is multiplied by $0.7$ each year. So the second eigenvalue sets the speed of convergence.
- The columns of $A$ add up to $1$. So $(1, 1) A = (1, 1)$, and $1$ is an eigenvalue of every such matrix.

## Explanations

### L1 human

People move between a city and its suburbs every year with fixed rates.
The matrix tells us how the shares change in one year.
One special direction is not changed at all: this is the balance where as many people move in as move out.
The other direction shrinks by $30\%$ each year, so the shares approach the balance.

### L2 human

Start instead with $x_0 = (0.5, 0.5)$.

1. $P^{-1} x_0 = \tfrac{1}{3}(0.5 + 0.5, \ 0.5 - 1) = (\tfrac{1}{3}, -\tfrac{1}{6})$.
2. So $x_k = \tfrac{1}{3}(2, 1) - \tfrac{1}{6} \, 0.7^k (1, -1) = \big(\tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{1}{6} 0.7^k, \ \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{6} 0.7^k\big)$.
3. For $k = 1$, this is $(0.55, 0.45)$, and $A(0.5, 0.5) = (0.45 + 0.1, 0.05 + 0.4) = (0.55, 0.45)$.
4. The limit is again $(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})$.

The typical use is to find the steady state and the speed of approach from the two eigenvalues.
The trap is to use the rows of $A$ as the "from" states: here column $j$ lists where the people of place $j$ go.

### L3 human

For a column-stochastic matrix with positive entries, the Perron-Frobenius theorem gives a simple eigenvalue $1$ with a positive eigenvector, and all other eigenvalues have absolute value less than $1$.
Then $A^k$ converges to the rank-one projection onto the stationary distribution, even when $A$ is not diagonalizable.
Diagonalization makes the rate explicit: the error decays like $|\lambda_2|^k$, where $\lambda_2$ is the second largest eigenvalue in absolute value.
