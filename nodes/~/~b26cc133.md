## Statement

Let $R$ be a commutative ring and let $A$ be an [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] $n \times n$ matrix over $R$.

1. $A^{-1}$ is invertible, and $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$.
2. The [[transpose|09DXWYRD43]] $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ is invertible, and $(A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1} = (A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}}$.
3. If $c$ is a unit of $R$, then $cA$ is invertible, and $(cA)^{-1} = c^{-1} A^{-1}$.
4. For each integer $k \ge 0$, the power $A^k$ is invertible, and $(A^k)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^k$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

In part 3 the scalar $c$ must be a unit.
For $c = 0$ the matrix $cA$ is the zero matrix, which is singular.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the matrix $2 I_2$ is singular, because $\tfrac{1}{2} I_2$ has entries outside $\mathbb{Z}$.
Over a field, part 3 holds for each $c \ne 0$.

### Consequences

- By part 4, the notation $A^{-k} = (A^{-1})^k = (A^k)^{-1}$ has no ambiguity.
- With part 4, the law $A^j A^k = A^{j+k}$ holds for all integers $j$ and $k$.
- By part 2, the transpose of a symmetric invertible matrix has a symmetric inverse: if $A^{\mathsf{T}} = A$, then $(A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} = A^{-1}$.

### Converse

Part 2 has a converse: if $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ is invertible, then $A = (A^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}$ is invertible, by part 2 applied to $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
Part 4 has a converse for $k \ge 1$: if $A^k$ is invertible, then $A$ is invertible, by part 2 of the [[inverse of a product|0S4WTRE9GG]].

### Generalizations

Parts 1 and 4 hold in each monoid.
Part 2 holds for each ring with an involution that reverses products, for example the conjugate transpose $A^{*}$ of a complex matrix: $(A^{*})^{-1} = (A^{-1})^{*}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Undoing the undo gives back the original matrix.
Flipping a matrix over its diagonal and then inverting gives the same result as inverting first and then flipping.
Scaling a matrix by $3$ is undone by the inverse matrix scaled by $\tfrac{1}{3}$.

### L2 human

Worked example: let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, with $A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $(3A)^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{3} A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/3 & -1/3 \\ -1/3 & 2/3 \end{pmatrix}$. Check: $(3A)\cdot\tfrac{1}{3}A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I_2$.
2. $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $(A^{-1})^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. Check part 4: $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -3 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 - 9 & -15 + 15 \\ 6 - 6 & -9 + 10 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$.

A trap: $(cA)^{-1}$ is $c^{-1} A^{-1}$ and not $c A^{-1}$.

### L3 human

Parts 1, 3 and 4 say that $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ is a group that contains the scalar units $R^{\times} I_n$ in its centre.
Part 2 says that $A \mapsto (A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}$ is a group homomorphism of $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ to itself, since both the transpose and the inverse reverse the order of products.
This map is an automorphism of order $2$.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ it is not an inner automorphism: conjugation keeps the trace, but $\operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$ goes to $\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac{1}{2}, 1)$, which has a different trace.
