## Example

For a real number $c$, let $A_c = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ c & -1 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $A_c^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot c & 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot (-1) \\ c \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot c & c \cdot 0 + (-1)(-1) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. So $A_c$ is [[involutory|~cc833adb]] for every $c$.
3. $A_c (2, c) = (2, 2c - c) = (2, c)$, so $(2, c)$ is fixed.
4. $A_c (0, 1) = (0, -1)$, so $(0, 1)$ changes sign.
5. So $A_c$ is the reflection that fixes the line spanned by $(2, c)$ and reverses the line spanned by $(0, 1)$.

## Remarks

For $c \neq 0$ the two lines are not perpendicular, so $A_c$ is an oblique reflection and not an orthogonal matrix.
There are infinitely many involutory $2 \times 2$ real matrices, while the only real numbers with $x^2 = 1$ are $\pm 1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The square of each of these matrices is the identity, whatever the number $c$ is.
So the equation $A^2 = I$ has infinitely many solutions among matrices.

### L2 human

A reflection of the plane is determined by its two eigenlines, and these can be any two different lines through $0$.
Here the $-1$ line is the $y$-axis and the $+1$ line turns with $c$.
