## Example

Let $V = \mathbb{C}$, as a vector space of dimension $1$ over $\mathbb{C}$.
This is a non-example: the [[definition|0Q8BZXVT1Z]] of orientation does not apply.

1. The bases of $V$ are the families $(w)$ with $w \ne 0$.
2. The change of basis matrix from $(1)$ to $(i)$ is the $1 \times 1$ matrix $(i)$, with determinant $i$.
3. The number $i$ is neither positive nor negative, so the rule "same orientation when the determinant is positive" has no meaning.
4. Check by paths: $w(t) = e^{i \pi t}$ for $t \in [0, 1]$ is a path of bases from $(1)$ to $(-1)$.
5. Over $\mathbb{R}$, the bases $(1)$ and $(-1)$ of $\mathbb{R}$ have opposite orientations, but over $\mathbb{C}$ they are joined by a path. So no two classes arise.

## Remarks

The same set $\mathbb{C}$, as a real vector space of dimension $2$ with basis $(1, i)$, has two orientations.
Multiplication by $w = a + bi \ne 0$ has the real matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $a^2 + b^2 > 0$.
So every complex-linear isomorphism keeps the real orientation, and every complex space has a canonical real orientation.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Orientation needs a sign, positive or negative.
A complex number such as $i$ has no sign, so complex bases do not fall into two classes.

### L2 human

The path $e^{i \pi t}$ turns the basis $(1)$ into $(-1)$ without passing through $0$.
Over the real numbers, such a path from $1$ to $-1$ must pass through $0$.
A trap: a complex space still has an orientation when we view it as a real space of twice the dimension.
