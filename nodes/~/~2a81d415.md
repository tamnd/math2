## Example

Starting point: the [[Pythagorean theorem|0VP3A1290K]].
In a right triangle with legs $a$, $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, the squares on the sides satisfy $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.
We pose new problems by [[problem posing|~71594150]], with the question "what if not squares?".

1. Attributes: the triangle has a right angle; the figures on the sides are squares; the figures are built outward; the relation is between areas.
2. "What if not squares, but equilateral triangles?" New problem: is the area of the equilateral triangle on the hypotenuse the sum of the areas on the legs?
3. An equilateral triangle of side $s$ has area $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} s^2$. So the question is whether $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} a^2 + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} b^2 = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} c^2$, and this follows from $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ on multiplying by $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}$.
4. Check with the $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle: $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(9 + 16) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} \cdot 25$.
5. "What if not equilateral triangles, but semicircles?" A semicircle on a side $s$ has area $\frac{\pi}{8} s^2$, and the same argument gives $\frac{\pi}{8} a^2 + \frac{\pi}{8} b^2 = \frac{\pi}{8} c^2$.
6. The pattern poses a general problem: for which figures does the relation hold? Answer: for any three [[similar|0YF6QXXWB5]] figures placed on the three sides with the sides as corresponding segments. Similar figures with corresponding lengths $a$, $b$, $c$ have areas $k a^2$, $k b^2$, $k c^2$ for one constant $k$, and $k a^2 + k b^2 = k c^2$.

## Remarks

The general version is Proposition 31 of Book VI of Euclid's Elements.
It also gives a proof of the original theorem, by a choice of figure.
Drop the altitude from the right angle to the hypotenuse; it cuts the triangle into two triangles similar to the whole, with hypotenuses $a$ and $b$.
These two triangles are similar figures on the sides $a$ and $b$, the whole triangle is the similar figure on $c$, and the two parts fill the whole.
So the relation holds for this one family of similar figures, hence $k a^2 + k b^2 = k c^2$ for its constant $k > 0$, and so $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.
The general problem is easier to prove than the special one, an instance of the [[inventor's paradox|~aaa3fffe]].
"What if not a right angle?" poses a different problem, answered by the [[law of cosines|060ZVZBV3E]]: $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab \cos C$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Pythagorean theorem is often drawn with squares on the three sides of a right triangle.
The big square has the same area as the two small squares together.
Now ask: what if we draw triangles with equal sides instead of squares?
Or half-circles?
The same rule holds: the big shape has the same area as the two small ones.
In fact, it works for any shape, as long as the three shapes are copies of one another at different sizes.

### L2 human

We test the general claim on the $5$-$12$-$13$ right triangle with semicircles.

1. The check $25 + 144 = 169 = 13^2$ confirms that the triangle is right-angled.
2. Semicircles on the legs: $\frac{\pi}{8} \cdot 25$ and $\frac{\pi}{8} \cdot 144$.
3. Their sum is $\frac{\pi}{8} \cdot 169$.
4. The semicircle on the hypotenuse has area $\frac{\pi}{8} \cdot 13^2 = \frac{\pi}{8} \cdot 169$. The areas agree.
5. Non-similar figures fail: squares on the legs and an equilateral triangle on the hypotenuse give $25 + 144 = 169$ against $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} \cdot 169 \approx 73.2$.

The typical use: after a theorem, vary one attribute, solve the variant, and look for the general statement that covers all variants.

A trap: the three figures must be similar to one another with the sides as corresponding lengths; three figures of the same type but different shapes, such as rectangles of different proportions, do not satisfy the relation.
