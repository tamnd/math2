## Example

Agent $a$ may form a plan $p$ to attack, and then sends it to agent $b$.
Every delivered message is acknowledged by the receiver, and the acknowledgements alternate: message 1 goes from $a$ to $b$, message 2 from $b$ to $a$, and so on.
Each message may be lost, and after a loss nobody sends again.

Worlds.
World $0$: there is no plan and no message.
World $k \ge 1$: there is a plan, and messages $1, \dots, k - 1$ arrive while message $k$ is lost.
So $p$ is true exactly at the worlds $k \ge 1$.

Information.
Agent $a$ knows whether there is a plan, and how many messages she received, which is $\lfloor (k - 1)/2 \rfloor$ at world $k \ge 1$.
Agent $b$ knows how many messages he received: $0$ at world $0$, and $\lfloor k/2 \rfloor$ at world $k \ge 1$.
So the relations are [[equivalence relations|0G8VEZ3WTK]] with classes

- agent $a$: $\set{0}$, $\set{1, 2}$, $\set{3, 4}$, $\set{5, 6}$, and so on;
- agent $b$: $\set{0, 1}$, $\set{2, 3}$, $\set{4, 5}$, and so on.

Claim: for $j \ge 0$, $E_G^j p$ is true at world $k$ exactly when $k \ge j + 1$, with $E_G^0 p = p$ and $G = \set{a, b}$.

1. For $j = 0$ the claim is the definition of $p$.
2. Suppose the claim holds for $j$. Then $E_G^{j+1} p$ holds at $k$ iff both classes of $k$ lie in $\set{j + 1, j + 2, \dots}$.
3. At $k = 0$, $a$'s class is $\set{0}$, which fails.
4. For $k \ge 1$, one class of $k$ starts at $k - 1$ and the other starts at $k$. For odd $k$, $b$'s class starts at $k - 1$; for even $k$, $a$'s class does.
5. So both classes lie above $j$ iff $k - 1 \ge j + 1$, that is, $k \ge j + 2$. This proves the claim for $j + 1$.
6. At world $k \ge 1$, $E_G^{k-1} p$ is true and $E_G^k p$ is false.
7. In particular $C_G p$ is false at every world, by the definition of [[common knowledge|RRRHWH9F6Y]].

Every delivered message adds one level of "everyone knows", and no finite number of messages gives common knowledge.

## Remarks

The worlds form one chain $0 - 1 - 2 - 3 - \cdots$ under the two relations, so every world reaches world $0$, where $p$ is false.
That is the reason for step 7, by [[the reachability clause|~2e0d432a]].
Simultaneous attack needs common knowledge of the plan, so it cannot be guaranteed over this channel.
Halpern and Moses proved a general form: when communication is not guaranteed, sending messages cannot create common knowledge.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two generals must attack together, and they talk by messengers who can be caught.
General A sends "attack at dawn".
General B gets it, but A does not know that, so B sends a reply.
Now A knows B knows, but B does not know that A got the reply.
Every reply adds one level, and the last messenger is always in doubt, so they never reach common knowledge.

### L2 human

We read off the depth of knowledge at world $4$: messages 1, 2 and 3 arrived, and message 4 was lost.

1. $E_G^3 p$ holds at $4$, since $4 \ge 3 + 1$.
2. $E_G^4 p$ fails at $4$, since $4 < 4 + 1$.
3. Directly: $b$ sent message 4 and cannot tell world $4$ from world $5$, where message 4 arrived.

The typical use is to explain why protocols with lossy channels cannot achieve simultaneous action.
A trap: the knowledge depth grows with the number of messages, but common knowledge is not a limit that is ever reached.

### L3 human

The model is a chain in which the two partitions interleave, so their meet has a single block.
Rubinstein's electronic mail game uses the same chain to show that common knowledge, not high-order mutual knowledge, drives equilibrium behaviour.
