## Example

Let $N \colon \R^2 \to \R^2$ be $N(x, y) = (y, 0)$, with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
We check that $N$ is not a [[projection|0RH4E3CAV9]], and that the conclusion of the [[theorem on projections|~d025ab24]] fails for it.

1. $N$ is linear, since it is given by a matrix.
2. $N^2(x, y) = N(y, 0) = (0, 0)$. So $N^2 = 0$.
3. $N(0, 1) = (1, 0) \ne (0, 0)$, so $N \ne 0 = N^2$. Hence $N$ is not a projection.
4. The [[image|0JB228DCWB]] of $N$ is $\{(y, 0)\}$, the $x$-axis.
5. The [[kernel|02YFME3RGW]] of $N$ is $\{(x, y) : y = 0\}$, also the $x$-axis.
6. So $\operatorname{im} N \cap \ker N$ is the $x$-axis, and $\operatorname{im} N + \ker N$ is the $x$-axis, not $\R^2$.
7. Hence $\R^2 \ne \operatorname{im} N \oplus \ker N$, and both conditions of a direct sum fail.

## Remarks

$N$ is not the zero map, but $N$ maps its own image to $0$.
A projection does the opposite: it fixes its image.
The only eigenvalue of $N$ is $0$, while a nonzero projection has the eigenvalue $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The map $N$ moves the vertical direction onto the horizontal one and then kills it.
Applying it twice gives zero, while a projection gives the same result twice.

### L2 human

For a linear map in finite dimension, $\dim \operatorname{im} + \dim \ker = \dim V$ always holds.
Here $1 + 1 = 2$, so the dimensions add up, and yet the sum is not direct.
The dimension count alone cannot tell a projection apart from other maps: the overlap of image and kernel is the real test.
