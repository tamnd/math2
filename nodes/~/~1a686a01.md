## Summary

Existential graphs are a notation for logic in which formulas are drawings on a plane, the sheet of assertion, and proofs are changes of the drawings by a few rules.
A graph drawn on the sheet is asserted, two graphs drawn side by side are asserted together ([[conjunction|025YZN3VJW]]), and a closed curve called a cut around a graph denies it ([[negation|029JBBF693]]).
The alpha system uses letters for propositions, side by side placement and cuts, and it is equivalent to classical [[propositional logic|09K9MWD2QQ]].
The beta system adds lines of identity, heavy lines that say "something exists" and connect predicates to it, and it is equivalent to [[first-order logic|086QKPF0A6]] with identity.
The gamma system adds modal notions and graphs about graphs, and it was left unfinished.
Proofs use five [[rules of inference|QPD3X471Q1]]: erasure, insertion, iteration, deiteration and the double cut.
The graphs matter as a complete proof system for first-order logic in which each rule is a local change of a picture, controlled only by the number of cuts around the change.

## Motivation

Algebraic notations for logic hide the structure of a formula in brackets and in the order of symbols.
Peirce wanted a notation in which the logical form can be seen at once, and in which a proof is a sequence of visible steps.
Two operations are enough for classical propositional logic: "and" and "not".
So it is enough to have one way to put assertions together, side by side, and one way to deny an assertion, a cut around it.
For quantifiers one needs a way to say that an individual exists and to connect several statements about it.
A line drawn between predicates does this, and it also shows identity: two predicates on one line are about the same individual.

## Notation

In text we write a cut as a pair of square brackets, so $[P]$ is a cut around $P$, and juxtaposition as a space.
The area of a cut is the part of the plane inside it and outside every cut inside it.
The depth of an area is the number of cuts that enclose it: the sheet has depth $0$.
An area of even depth is a positive area, and an area of odd depth is a negative area.

An alpha graph translates into a formula $\tau(G)$ of propositional logic by these clauses.

| Graph $G$ | Formula $\tau(G)$ |
|---|---|
| the empty graph | $\top$ (true) |
| a letter $P$ | $P$ |
| $G \ H$ (side by side) | $\tau(G) \land \tau(H)$ |
| $[G]$ | $\neg \tau(G)$ |

So $[P \ [Q]]$ is $\neg(P \land \neg Q)$, which is equivalent to $P \to Q$; Peirce called this shape a scroll.
The graph $[[P] \ [Q]]$ is $\neg(\neg P \land \neg Q)$, which is equivalent to $P \lor Q$.
An empty cut $[\ ]$ is $\neg \top$, that is, false.

In a beta graph, a predicate letter is written at the end of a line, and a line of identity stands for an individual.
A line whose outermost part lies in an area of depth $d$ is read as an existential quantifier at that depth.
So a line from $P$ on the sheet reads $\exists x\, P(x)$, and the same line inside a cut reads $\neg \exists x\, P(x)$.
Take a cut that contains $S$ and a second cut, and $P$ inside the second cut.
A line that lies in the first cut, starts at $S$ and passes into the second cut to $P$ reads $\neg \exists x\,(S(x) \land \neg P(x))$, that is, $\forall x\,(S(x) \to P(x))$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[Modus ponens in the alpha graphs|~e19f353d]]: from $P$ and $[P \ [Q]]$ the rules give $Q$ in three steps, by deiteration, double cut and erasure.
- [[The syllogism Darii in the beta graphs|~a50862bf]]: from "every $M$ is $P$" and "some $S$ is $M$" the beta rules give "some $S$ is $P$". The proof joins lines of identity and removes a double cut.
- The law of excluded middle $[[P] \ [[P]]]$ is a theorem of alpha: it comes from the empty sheet by a double cut, an insertion and an iteration (see L2).
- A line of identity on the sheet with $P$ at one end and $Q$ at the other reads $\exists x\,(P(x) \land Q(x))$. Two separate lines, one from $P$ and one from $Q$, read $\exists x\, P(x) \land \exists y\, Q(y)$.

### Non-examples

- [[An Euler diagram is not an existential graph|~da9ffb32]]: it shows classes as regions and "all $S$ are $P$" as a region inside a region. It has no cut, no line of identity and no rules of transformation.
- A [[Venn diagram|0KCT6DPXJK]] with shaded regions is not an existential graph. Shading says that a region is empty, which is a statement about classes, and the diagram cannot express a relation between two individuals.
- A truth table for $P \to Q$ is not an existential graph. It lists valuations; it does not assert a graph on a sheet and it has no derivations.

### Basic properties

- The rules depend only on the depth of the area where they act. Erasure works in positive areas and insertion in negative areas; iteration, deiteration and the double cut work in any area.
- The rules are sound: each rule turns a graph into a graph that the first one implies, read through $\tau$. For erasure the reason is that $A \land B$ implies $A$, and a negative area reverses the direction of implication, which is why insertion is allowed there.
- A theorem is a graph derived from the empty sheet. Every theorem of alpha is a tautology, and every tautology is a theorem of alpha; so alpha is complete for classical propositional logic.
- Beta with the same five rules, extended to lines of identity, is sound and complete for first-order logic with identity in a language of relation symbols.
- Each rule except erasure and insertion has an inverse rule: iteration is undone by deiteration, and the double cut can be drawn and removed.

### History

Charles Sanders Peirce developed the existential graphs in the late 1890s and the first years of the twentieth century.
He divided them into the alpha, beta and gamma parts and stated the rules of inference for them.
He left the gamma part unfinished, and parts of it were later related to systems of modal logic.
The equivalence of alpha with propositional logic and of beta with first-order logic with identity was proved by later logicians, who also turned Peirce's rules into exact definitions.
Peirce also worked on extensions of the diagrams of Euler and Venn.

### Common errors

- Erasing in a negative area. From $[P \ Q]$ (not both $P$ and $Q$) one may not erase $Q$, because $[P]$ (not $P$) does not follow.
- Removing two cuts with a graph between them. The double cut rule only applies when nothing lies in the area between the two cuts; $[P \ [Q]]$ is not equivalent to $P \ Q$.
- Reading every line of identity as "for all". A line is an existential quantifier at the depth of its outermost part; "for all" arises from a line inside a cut whose end lies in a second cut.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Imagine a large sheet of paper on which we write only things that we claim are true.
If we write "it rains" and "it is cold" next to each other, we claim both.
If we draw a loop around "it rains", we claim that it does not rain.
A big loop that contains "it rains" and a smaller loop around "it is cold" says "if it rains, then it is cold".
Proofs are made by a few moves, such as erasing something that is not inside any loop, or removing two loops that sit directly one inside the other.
Peirce designed these moves so that a true drawing can only become another true drawing.

### L2 human

We derive the law of excluded middle $P \lor \neg P$ from the empty sheet.

1. Start with the empty sheet, which is the axiom of the system.
2. Draw a double cut around the empty graph: $[[\ ]]$. The double cut rule allows this in any area.
3. The area between the two cuts has depth $1$, so it is negative. By insertion we draw the graph $[P]$ there: $[[P] \ [\ ]]$.
4. The inner cut lies in the same area of depth $1$ as $[P]$. By iteration we copy $[P]$ into the inner cut: $[[P] \ [[P]]]$.
5. By the translation, $[[P] \ [[P]]]$ is $\neg(\neg P \land \neg\neg P)$, which is equivalent to $P \lor \neg P$.

The typical use of the rules is this pattern: a double cut makes room, insertion in a negative area adds what we need, and iteration copies it to where it is used.
The trap is step 3: insertion of $[P]$ on the sheet itself would be an unsound step, since the sheet has depth $0$ and is positive.

### L3 human

The alpha rules resemble the modern systems of deep inference: a rule may act at any depth inside a formula, and its direction depends on the polarity of the position.
Erasure and insertion express that conjunction can be weakened in a positive position and strengthened in a negative one, and iteration and deiteration express the contraction and the reuse of hypotheses that sequent calculi handle with structural rules.
Because all rules act inside the graph, existential graphs relate more closely to deep inference systems than to [[Hilbert systems|1MGMQX43B8]] or to [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]].

The beta system shows that lines can do the work of variables.
A line of identity is a variable together with its quantifier, and the branching of lines is the sharing of a variable between atomic formulas.
This idea returns in conceptual graphs in computer science and in string diagrams for relations in category theory, where the same pictures are read in a category of relations.
The comparison with Venn and Euler diagrams shows the limit of class diagrams: they capture [[syllogistic|~566128b7]] and other reasoning with one-place predicates, while the beta graphs reach full first-order logic with relations of any arity.
