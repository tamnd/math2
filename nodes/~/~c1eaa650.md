## Example

Let $S_3$ be the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] on $\{1, 2, 3\}$, with permutations composed from right to left.
Its [[opposite group|D33V8HE0KY]] $S_3^{\mathrm{op}}$ is the same set of six permutations with the product $\sigma \ast \tau = \tau \sigma$.
We compute in $S_3^{\mathrm{op}}$, and we check that the inversion map $\iota(\sigma) = \sigma^{-1}$ is an [[isomorphism|08MG7H8JW5]] from $S_3$ to $S_3^{\mathrm{op}}$.

1. In $S_3$, $(1\ 2)(1\ 3) = (1\ 3\ 2)$: the point $1$ goes to $3$ and then stays at $3$, the point $3$ goes to $1$ and then to $2$, and the point $2$ stays at $2$ and then goes to $1$.
2. In $S_3^{\mathrm{op}}$, $(1\ 2) \ast (1\ 3) = (1\ 3)(1\ 2) = (1\ 2\ 3)$: the point $1$ goes to $2$ and then stays at $2$, the point $2$ goes to $1$ and then to $3$, and the point $3$ stays and then goes to $1$.
3. So the products of steps 1 and 2 differ, and $S_3^{\mathrm{op}}$ has a different operation from $S_3$.
4. The map $\iota$ fixes the identity and the three transpositions, because each of them is its own inverse. It swaps $(1\ 2\ 3)$ and $(1\ 3\ 2)$, because $(1\ 2\ 3)(1\ 3\ 2) = e$.
5. Check one product: $\iota\bigl((1\ 2)(1\ 3)\bigr) = \iota\bigl((1\ 3\ 2)\bigr) = (1\ 2\ 3)$, and $\iota((1\ 2)) \ast \iota((1\ 3)) = (1\ 2) \ast (1\ 3) = (1\ 2\ 3)$ by step 2.
6. In general, $\iota(\sigma\tau) = (\sigma\tau)^{-1} = \tau^{-1}\sigma^{-1} = \iota(\sigma) \ast \iota(\tau)$ for all $\sigma, \tau \in S_3$. So $\iota$ is a homomorphism.
7. Since $\iota(\iota(\sigma)) = \sigma$, the map $\iota$ is a bijection, and so it is an isomorphism.

## Remarks

The example shows that $S_3^{\mathrm{op}}$ is a different operation on the same set, but an isomorphic group.
The [[Cayley table|0YB2NRP0T5]] of $S_3^{\mathrm{op}}$ is the Cayley table of $S_3$ reflected in its main diagonal, because the entry in row $\sigma$ and column $\tau$ is $\tau\sigma$.
The identity map is not an isomorphism here, as [[the identity map from S3 to its opposite group is not a homomorphism|~4235ecda]] shows.
For the [[cyclic subgroup|08ABT85339]] $A_3 = \{e, (1\ 2\ 3), (1\ 3\ 2)\}$, which is abelian, the opposite operation is the same as the old one.
If we compose permutations from left to right instead, we get exactly the operation of $S_3^{\mathrm{op}}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of the six ways to rearrange three cards.
In $S_3$, "$\sigma$ times $\tau$" means: do $\tau$ first, then $\sigma$.
In the opposite group, "$\sigma$ times $\tau$" means: do $\sigma$ first, then $\tau$.
The two rules give different answers for some pairs of moves.
Still, the two groups have the same shape, and the match between them sends each move to the move that undoes it.

### L2 human

We fill one row of the Cayley table of $S_3^{\mathrm{op}}$, the row of $a = (1\ 2\ 3)$.

1. $a \ast e = e a = (1\ 2\ 3)$.
2. $a \ast (1\ 2) = (1\ 2)(1\ 2\ 3)$. The point $1$ goes to $2$ and then to $1$, and $2$ goes to $3$ and stays, and $3$ goes to $1$ and then to $2$. So the product is $(2\ 3)$.
3. $a \ast (1\ 3) = (1\ 3)(1\ 2\ 3)$. The point $1$ goes to $2$ and stays, $2$ goes to $3$ and then to $1$, and $3$ goes to $1$ and then back to $3$. So the product is $(1\ 2)$.
4. $a \ast (2\ 3) = (2\ 3)(1\ 2\ 3)$. The point $1$ goes to $2$ and then to $3$, $3$ goes to $1$ and stays, and $2$ goes to $3$ and then to $2$. So the product is $(1\ 3)$.
5. $a \ast a = a a = (1\ 3\ 2)$, and $a \ast (1\ 3\ 2) = e$.

In $S_3$ itself, $(1\ 2\ 3)(1\ 2) = (1\ 3)$, which differs from the entry $(2\ 3)$ of step 2.
The typical use is to see a concrete group where $G$ and $G^{\mathrm{op}}$ differ as operations.
A trap is to read the row and the column of the table in the wrong order: in $S_3^{\mathrm{op}}$ the row element acts first, and in $S_3$ it acts second.
