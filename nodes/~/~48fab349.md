## Summary

Teaching linear algebra is the part of mathematics education, MSC class 97H60, that studies how students learn the subject of the [[overview|~0a3756c5]] and how courses present it.
The main choices are the order of the ideas, the role of proofs, and the role of computation by software.
A matrix-first course starts with linear systems and matrices in $\mathbb{R}^n$; an abstract-first course starts with vector spaces and linear maps.
The question matters because linear algebra is the first course with abstract structure for many students, and it serves engineering, science, economics and computing.

## Motivation

A first course in linear algebra has students from mathematics, physics, engineering, economics and computer science.
They need different things: algorithms and matrix skills, geometric intuition, or a first contact with axioms and proofs.
Research in mathematics education found that many students can row reduce a matrix but cannot say what a span or a basis means.
Course design tries to connect the computations with the concepts behind them.

## Notation

- MSC2020 97H60 is the class for linear algebra in mathematics education.
- LACSG is the Linear Algebra Curriculum Study Group.
- CAS means computer algebra system; numerical software works with floating-point numbers instead of exact numbers.

## Remarks

### Examples

- Matrix-first: Lay starts with linear systems, row reduction and the equation $A x = b$, and reaches abstract vector spaces in chapter 4.
- Matrix-first with geometry: Strang starts with vectors, dot products and the column picture of $A x = b$, and builds on the four fundamental subspaces.
- Abstract-first: Axler starts with vector spaces over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ and puts determinants near the end; the essay on [[linear algebra without determinants|~2e5ccd05]] discusses this.
- Proof-based and free: Hefferon starts with linear systems, and proves the results from the beginning.
- Activity-based and free: Austin builds each section around activities with computation cells.

### Non-examples

- A course in numerical linear algebra is not a first course: it assumes the theory and studies the errors and the cost of algorithms.
- A course on matrix computations only, with no subspaces and no linear maps, does not meet the conceptual goals described here.

### Basic properties

- The LACSG met in 1990 and published its recommendations in the College Mathematics Journal in 1993. The authors were Carlson, Johnson, Lay and Porter.
- The recommendations: the first course should be matrix-oriented and should serve the client disciplines.
- They also asked faculty to consider the needs and interests of students, to use technology, and to offer a second course in matrix theory.
- In many curricula the first course is the first contact with definitions and proofs. Some courses add a separate transition course for proofs.
- Software for teaching includes MATLAB and Octave, Python with NumPy, and Sage. Moler first wrote MATLAB in the 1970s for students of numerical linear algebra.

### History

Linear algebra became a standard first-year or second-year course in the second half of the 20th century, often after calculus.
The LACSG recommendations of 1993 moved many American courses towards a matrix-oriented first course.
Research groups, among them those of Dorier and Sierpinska, studied the difficulties of students with the formal concepts.

### Common errors

- Courses teach the algorithms without the meaning: students compute a row echelon form but cannot say what it tells about the [[column space|03AJKXH1AV]].
- Courses start with axioms without examples, and students learn the words without the objects. The essay on [[common errors|~5aa23dfb]] lists typical student errors.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Linear algebra can be taught in two main orders.
One order starts with sets of equations and tables of numbers, and later reaches the general ideas.
The other order starts with the general ideas, and uses the tables as examples.
Most first courses for engineers and scientists use the first order.

### L2 human

A typical matrix-first syllabus, in order:

1. Linear systems, row reduction and the solution sets.
2. Matrix operations, inverses and the invertible matrix theorem.
3. Determinants.
4. Vector spaces, subspaces, bases, dimension and rank.
5. Eigenvalues, eigenvectors and diagonalization.
6. Orthogonality, projections and least squares.

The typical use of software in such a course is the row reduction of large matrices, and the eigenvalues of matrices with no simple roots.
A trap: a computer gives decimal answers, and a computed rank or zero can be wrong by rounding; a small pivot can stand for an exact $0$.

### L3 human

Sierpinska described three modes of thinking in linear algebra: synthetic-geometric, analytic-arithmetic and analytic-structural.
A student who moves between the picture of a plane, its equation, and the notion of a two-dimensional subspace uses all three.
Dorier described the "obstacle of formalism": students use the formal language without its meaning.
Designs that respond to this start from a problem, such as a linear system or a geometric transformation, and introduce the general notion as the tool that solves it.
The school side of the subject, vectors in the plane and in space, belongs to the [[school curricula|9AHB6RQVDE]] area.
