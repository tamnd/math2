## Example

Let $R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the [[rotation|0B9QNFGTHM]] of $\R^2$ by a right angle.

1. The line $\R e_1$ is not [[invariant|0ACX5QT8TE]], since $R e_1 = e_2$ is not a multiple of $e_1$.
2. The [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]] is $p_R(t) = t^2 + 1$, which has no real root. So $R$ has no real [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]], by the [[roots of the characteristic polynomial|053QVHF6N7]].
3. A line $\R v$ with $v \ne 0$ is invariant only if $R v = \lambda v$ for a real $\lambda$. By step 2, no line through $0$ is invariant, and the invariant subspaces are $0$ and $\R^2$.
4. By step 2, $R$ has no eigenvector in $\R^2$. So $R$ is not [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] over $\R$.
5. Over $\C$, the roots are $i$ and $-i$. We have $R(1, -i) = (i, 1) = i(1, -i)$ and $R(1, i) = (-i, 1) = -i(1, i)$.
6. Let $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -i & i \end{pmatrix}$. Then $\det P = 2i$ and $P^{-1} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ 1 & -i \end{pmatrix}$.
7. Multiplying back, $P \operatorname{diag}(i, -i) = \begin{pmatrix} i & -i \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and this times $P^{-1}$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = R$. So $R$ is diagonalizable over $\C$.
8. The map $R$ is [[semisimple|08VRX3Q4PB]] over $\R$. By step 3, the only invariant subspaces are $0$ and $\R^2$, and each is an invariant complement of the other.

## Remarks

The example shows that diagonalizability depends on the field.
The minimal polynomial $t^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\R$, so it has no repeated irreducible factor, which is the criterion for semisimplicity.
For a rotation by an angle $\theta$ that is not a multiple of $\pi$, the same argument works with the eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\theta}$.
For $\theta = \pi$, the rotation is $-I$, and every line is invariant.
We also have $R^4 = I$, so $R$ is a matrix of finite order, and such matrices are diagonalizable over $\C$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Turning the plane by a quarter turn moves every line through the center to a different line.
So no line stays in place, and there is no direction that the turn only stretches.
A diagonal matrix only stretches along the axes, so the turn cannot be written as a diagonal matrix with real numbers.
With complex numbers, the turn becomes multiplication by $i$ and by $-i$ in two complex directions.

### L2 human

We find the complex eigenvectors step by step.

1. For $\lambda = i$, the matrix $R - iI = \begin{pmatrix} -i & -1 \\ 1 & -i \end{pmatrix}$ has rank $1$.
2. Its first row gives $-i x - y = 0$, so $y = -ix$, and $x = 1$ gives $v = (1, -i)$.
3. For $\lambda = -i$, the same work gives $v = (1, i)$.
4. These vectors are the columns of $P$ in step 6 of the example, and $P^{-1} R P = \operatorname{diag}(i, -i)$.

The trap is to say that $R$ is "not diagonalizable" without naming the field.
