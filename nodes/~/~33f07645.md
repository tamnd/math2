## Example

Let $T$ be a theory in a first-order language $L$, and let $c$ be a constant symbol that is not in $L$.
Let $T'$ be the same set of sentences $T$, now read as a theory in the language $L' = L \cup \set{c}$.
We show that $T'$ is a [[conservative extension|N238J43XNE]] of $T$.

1. $T'$ is an extension of $T$: a derivation from $T$ in $L$ is also a derivation from $T'$ in $L'$.
2. Let $M$ be a [[model|MVCH6N9SNW]] of $T$. A [[structure|983GZBQYT4]] has a nonempty domain, so we can choose an element $a$ of $M$.
3. Let $(M, a)$ be the $L'$-structure that agrees with $M$ on $L$ and interprets $c$ as $a$.
4. The truth of an $L$-sentence in $(M, a)$ does not depend on the interpretation of $c$, so every sentence of $T$ is true in $(M, a)$. So $(M, a)$ is a model of $T'$.
5. Let $\sigma$ be an $L$-sentence with $T' \vdash \sigma$. By soundness, $\sigma$ is true in $(M, a)$, and by the remark in step 4 it is true in $M$.
6. Since $M$ was an arbitrary model of $T$, $\sigma$ is true in every model of $T$, and [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]] gives $T \vdash \sigma$.

## Remarks

There is also a purely syntactic proof: in a derivation of $\sigma$ from $T'$, replace each occurrence of $c$ by a variable that occurs nowhere in the derivation.
The result is a derivation of $\sigma$ from $T$, because $c$ does not occur in $\sigma$ or in $T$.
The example changes when axioms about $c$ are added.
If $T \vdash \exists x \, P(x)$, then $T \cup \set{P(c)}$ is still conservative over $T$, because $c$ can name a witness; this is the step that Henkin's proof of the completeness theorem uses many times.
If $T \nvdash \exists x \, P(x)$, then $T \cup \set{P(c)}$ proves the new $L$-sentence $\exists x \, P(x)$, so it is not conservative.
For example, in the theory of fields the axiom $c \cdot c = 1 + 1$ prove $\exists x \, (x \cdot x = 1 + 1)$, which fails in $\mathbb{Q}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A theory about numbers does not change when we give a name, say "Bob", to one of the numbers and say nothing else about Bob.
Whatever we prove about numbers using the name Bob, we could have proved with "some number" instead.
The name is only a label, and labels add no facts.

### L2 human

Let $T$ be the theory of groups in the language $\set{\cdot, e}$, and add a constant $c$ with no axioms.

1. In $T'$ we prove $c \cdot e = c$, an instance of the identity axiom. This sentence uses $c$, so it is allowed to be new.
2. Replace $c$ by a new variable $x$ in this derivation: it becomes a derivation of $x \cdot e = x$ from the group axioms.
3. Generalization gives $\forall x \, (x \cdot e = x)$, a sentence of the old language, and it is a theorem of $T$.

The typical use is in proofs that "fix an arbitrary element $c$"; the conservativity of new constants is what makes this step safe.
A trap is to assume a fact about $c$ that is not provable for an arbitrary element, such as $c \neq e$ in a theory that has the trivial group as a model.
