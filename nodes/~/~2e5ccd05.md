## Summary

Linear algebra without determinants is an approach to the theory of [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]] that proves the main structure theorems before the [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] is defined.
Axler set it out in the article "Down with determinants!" in the American Mathematical Monthly 102 (1995), and in his textbook "Linear Algebra Done Right".
Eigenvalues come from polynomials in the operator, in later editions from the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]], not from the characteristic polynomial $\det(A - \lambda I)$.
The approach matters because it shows why eigenvalues exist, while the determinant route only computes them.

## Motivation

In a determinant-first course, $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ when $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.
This criterion is quick, but its proof uses the full theory of determinants, which many students find opaque.
Axler's question was whether the eigenvalue theory needs determinants at all.
The answer is no: one dimension count and the fundamental theorem of algebra prove the existence of an eigenvalue.

## Notation

- $\mathcal{L}(V)$ is the space of linear operators on $V$.
- For a polynomial $p(z) = a_0 + a_1 z + \dots + a_m z^m$, the operator $p(T)$ is $a_0 I + a_1 T + \dots + a_m T^m$.
- The minimal polynomial of $T$ is the monic polynomial $p$ of least degree with $p(T) = 0$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- Existence of an eigenvalue: let $V \neq 0$ be a complex vector space of dimension $n$, let $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$, and let $v \neq 0$.
    1. The $n + 1$ vectors $v, Tv, \dots, T^n v$ are dependent, so $p(T) v = 0$ for a nonzero polynomial $p$.
    2. The polynomial $p$ is not constant, since $a_0 v = 0$ forces $a_0 = 0$.
    3. By the [[fundamental theorem of algebra|0JP0W07Y9A]], $p(z) = c(z - \lambda_1) \cdots (z - \lambda_m)$. So $c (T - \lambda_1 I) \cdots (T - \lambda_m I) v = 0$, and some factor $T - \lambda_j I$ is not injective.
- Numbers: for $T(x, y) = (-y, x)$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ and $v = (1, 0)$, we get $Tv = (0, 1)$ and $T^2 v = -v$. So $p(z) = z^2 + 1 = (z - i)(z + i)$.
- Then $(T + iI) v = (i, 1)$, and $T(i, 1) = (-1, i) = i \, (i, 1)$. So $i$ is an eigenvalue with the eigenvector $(i, 1)$.
- Upper triangular form: the same method proves that every operator on a complex space has an [[upper triangular|0N4XY7Z3NN]] matrix in some basis.

### Non-examples

- The proof fails over $\mathbb{R}$: the rotation $T(x, y) = (-y, x)$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$ has no real eigenvalue, because $z^2 + 1$ has no real root.
- Determinant-free does not mean that determinants are absent. Axler defines them later, in chapter 9 of the fourth edition, and uses them for volume.

### Basic properties

- The approach proves, without determinants: existence of eigenvalues over $\mathbb{C}$, upper triangular form, the [[spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]], and the [[Jordan form|085HQ5G5TE]].
- The eigenvalues of $T$ are exactly the zeros of the minimal polynomial.
- The [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]] is defined from the multiplicities of the eigenvalues, and it agrees with $\det(zI - T)$ later.
- Older books, such as Hoffman and Kunze or Friedberg, Insel and Spence, treat determinants first and eigenvalues after. Their eigenvalue chapters use $\det(A - \lambda I)$.

### What is gained and what is lost

- Gained: a proof that shows why an eigenvalue exists, proofs that work for operators and not only for matrices, and less dependence on one long computation.
- Lost: the quick test $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ for small matrices, and [[Cramer's rule|0977KZ6PQB]].
- Lost early: the meaning of the determinant as a signed volume, and its role in the change of variables of integrals.
- In numerical practice neither route is used directly: eigenvalues of large matrices are computed by iterative methods such as the QR algorithm, not by roots of a polynomial.

### History

Determinants are older than matrices: Leibniz and Seki used them in the 17th century, and Cramer published his rule in 1750.
For a long time, books defined the eigenvalues through determinants.
Axler's article of 1995 and his book made the determinant-free route popular in proof-based courses.

### Common errors

- Students think that the determinant-free approach avoids all polynomials. It uses polynomials in $T$ in an essential way.
- Students think that the approach works over every field. The existence of an eigenvalue needs an algebraically closed field such as $\mathbb{C}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An eigenvector of a transformation is a direction that it only stretches, without a turn.
Many books find these directions with a special number, the determinant.
Axler found a way to prove that such directions exist without this number.
The proof uses the fact that every polynomial equation has a complex solution.

### L2 human

Worked comparison for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. Determinant route: $\det(A - \lambda I) = (2 - \lambda)^2 - 1 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 3)$, so the eigenvalues are $1$ and $3$.
2. Polynomial route: with $v = (1, 0)$, $A v = (2, 1)$ and $A^2 v = (5, 4)$.
3. Then $A^2 v - 4 A v + 3 v = (5 - 8 + 3, \; 4 - 4 + 0) = (0, 0)$, so $p(z) = z^2 - 4z + 3 = (z - 1)(z - 3)$.
4. Both routes give $1$ and $3$.

The typical use of the polynomial route is in proofs about operators; the typical use of the determinant route is the computation for a small matrix.
A trap: in step 3 the polynomial depends on $v$; for an eigenvector $v$ it has degree $1$ only.

### L3 human

The polynomial route is the module view: $V$ is a module over $\mathbb{C}[z]$, with $z$ acting as $T$, and the minimal polynomial generates its annihilator.
The structure theorem for modules over a principal ideal domain then gives the canonical forms, as in the sub-area on [[canonical forms|GEBYZKYV3D]].
The determinant remains the right tool for volume, orientation and the exterior algebra, as in the sub-area on [[determinants|DTBK11SR77]].
