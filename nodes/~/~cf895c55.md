## Statement

Let $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^3$, let $a \times b$ be the [[cross product|0N5N15HWZ7]], let $a \cdot b$ be the [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]], and let $\|\cdot\|$ be the [[length|0XKBKGTCPC]].
Then
$$\|a \times b\|^2 = \|a\|^2 \, \|b\|^2 - (a \cdot b)^2.$$

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The identity holds for all $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^3$, with no further hypothesis.
The restriction to $\mathbb{R}^3$ is needed only because the cross product is defined there; the right side makes sense in every $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### Consequences

- The [[length formula|~bafd22bd]] $\|a \times b\| = \|a\| \, \|b\| \sin \theta$, the area of the parallelogram.
- The [[Cauchy-Schwarz inequality|0RFWW56Y22]] in $\mathbb{R}^3$: the left side is $\geq 0$, so $(a \cdot b)^2 \leq \|a\|^2 \|b\|^2$.
- Equality in Cauchy-Schwarz holds if and only if $a \times b = 0$, that is, if and only if $a$ and $b$ are parallel.
- For unit vectors $a$ and $b$, $\|a \times b\|^2 + (a \cdot b)^2 = 1$, which is $\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta = 1$.

### Converse

The identity determines the length of $a \times b$, but not its direction.
The vectors $a \times b$ and $-(a \times b)$ both satisfy it; perpendicularity and the right-hand rule fix the direction.

### Generalizations

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, Lagrange's identity reads
$$\Big(\sum_i a_i^2\Big)\Big(\sum_i b_i^2\Big) - \Big(\sum_i a_i b_i\Big)^2 = \sum_{i < j} (a_i b_j - a_j b_i)^2.$$
It proves the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for $\mathbb{R}^n$ at once, since the right side is a sum of squares.
The right side is the squared area of the parallelogram spanned by $a$ and $b$, and it equals the Gram determinant $\det \begin{pmatrix} a \cdot a & a \cdot b \\ b \cdot a & b \cdot b \end{pmatrix}$.
The case $n = 2$ is the Brahmagupta-Fibonacci identity $(a_1^2 + a_2^2)(b_1^2 + b_2^2) = (a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2)^2 + (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)^2$.

## History

The identity is named after Lagrange.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The dot product measures how much two arrows agree in direction, and the cross product measures how much they disagree.
Lagrange's identity says that together they account for everything.
For $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(1, 1, 0)$: the dot product is $1$, the cross product is $(0, 0, 1)$ with length $1$, and the lengths are $1$ and $\sqrt{2}$.
Then $1^2 = 1 \cdot 2 - 1^2$, as the identity says.

### L2 human

Worked check with $a = (1, 2, 3)$ and $b = (4, 5, 6)$.

1. $a \times b = (-3, 6, -3)$, so $\|a \times b\|^2 = 9 + 36 + 9 = 54$.
2. $\|a\|^2 = 14$, $\|b\|^2 = 77$ and $a \cdot b = 32$.
3. $14 \cdot 77 - 32^2 = 1078 - 1024 = 54$.

The typical use is to compute the area of a parallelogram without the cross product, from dot products alone.
A trap: the identity is about squares; $\|a \times b\| = \|a\| \, \|b\| - |a \cdot b|$ is false.

### L3 human

The identity says that the map $(a, b) \mapsto (a \cdot b, a \times b)$ from $\mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathbb{R}^3$ to $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^3 = \mathbb{R}^4$ multiplies lengths.
This is the multiplicativity of the norm of quaternions: for pure quaternions $a$ and $b$, $ab = -a \cdot b + a \times b$, and $\|ab\| = \|a\| \, \|b\|$.
A bilinear product on $\mathbb{R}^n$ with perpendicularity and this identity exists only for $n = 3$ and $n = 7$, which is the content of the [[Hurwitz theorem on composition algebras|WZ81H3S4KK]] in this setting.
