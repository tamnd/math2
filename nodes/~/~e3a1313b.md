## Example

Problem: find the sum of the interior angles of a convex polygon with $n$ sides, where $n \geq 3$.
We know the answer for one shape only: the [[angle sum of a triangle|05MDKQBEAF]] is $180^\circ$.
So we [[decompose|~8b9406d8]] the polygon into triangles, use the known result on each piece, and recombine.

1. Name the vertices $V_1, V_2, \ldots, V_n$ in order around the polygon.
2. Draw the diagonals from $V_1$ to $V_3, V_4, \ldots, V_{n-1}$. There are $n - 3$ of them.
3. The polygon is convex, so each diagonal lies inside it, and the diagonals cut it into $n - 2$ triangles: $V_1 V_2 V_3$, $V_1 V_3 V_4$, up to $V_1 V_{n-1} V_n$.
4. Each triangle has angle sum $180^\circ$, so all the triangles together have angle sum $(n - 2) \cdot 180^\circ$.
5. Recombine: every angle of a triangle lies at a vertex of the polygon and is part of the polygon's angle there. At $V_1$, the angles of the $n - 2$ triangles fill the polygon's angle exactly; at every other vertex one or two triangle angles fill it.
6. So the angles of the triangles add up to the angles of the polygon, and the angle sum of the polygon is $(n - 2) \cdot 180^\circ$.

Checks: for $n = 3$ the formula gives $180^\circ$; for $n = 4$ it gives $360^\circ$; for $n = 5$ it gives $540^\circ$; for $n = 6$ it gives $720^\circ$.
A regular hexagon has six angles of $120^\circ$, and $6 \cdot 120^\circ = 720^\circ$.

## Remarks

Step 5 is the recombination, and it is where the argument could fail.
It needs two facts: no triangle angle lies outside the polygon's angles, and no part of a polygon angle is missed.
Both hold because the triangles fit together without overlap and fill the polygon.

A different decomposition gives the same answer.
Join a point $O$ inside the polygon to all $n$ vertices; this gives $n$ triangles with total angle sum $n \cdot 180^\circ$.
The angles at $O$ form a full turn of $360^\circ$ and are not angles of the polygon, so the angle sum of the polygon is $n \cdot 180^\circ - 360^\circ = (n - 2) \cdot 180^\circ$.
Here the recombination has to remove the extra angles at $O$.

The formula also holds for simple polygons that are not convex, but the diagonals from $V_1$ may then leave the polygon.
A [[triangulation|Q6KKBP3RRP]] into $n - 2$ triangles still exists, by a different argument.

## Explanations

### L1 human

We know that the angles of a triangle add up to $180^\circ$.
To find the angle sum of a pentagon, cut it into triangles by lines from one corner.
A pentagon is cut into $3$ triangles, and their angles together make up exactly the angles of the pentagon.
So the angle sum of a pentagon is $3 \cdot 180^\circ = 540^\circ$.
In general, a shape with $n$ sides cuts into $n - 2$ triangles.

### L2 human

We give the same proof as an [[induction|0ASJVEFA9J]] on $n$, where each step cuts off one triangle.

1. For $n = 3$ the polygon is a triangle, and the angle sum is $180^\circ = (3 - 2) \cdot 180^\circ$.
2. Let $n \geq 4$ and let $P = V_1 V_2 \cdots V_n$ be convex. The diagonal $V_1 V_{n-1}$ lies inside $P$ and cuts it into the triangle $T = V_1 V_{n-1} V_n$ and the polygon $Q = V_1 V_2 \cdots V_{n-1}$.
3. The polygon $Q$ is convex with $n - 1$ sides, so by the induction hypothesis its angle sum is $(n - 3) \cdot 180^\circ$.
4. At $V_1$ and at $V_{n-1}$, the angle of $P$ is the sum of the angles of $Q$ and $T$ there. At $V_n$, the angle of $P$ is the angle of $T$. All other angles of $P$ are angles of $Q$.
5. So the angle sum of $P$ is $(n - 3) \cdot 180^\circ + 180^\circ = (n - 2) \cdot 180^\circ$.

The typical use: a figure with an unknown property is cut into pieces with a known property.

A trap: step 2 uses convexity, since in a polygon that is not convex, a diagonal can pass outside the polygon, and then step 4 fails.
