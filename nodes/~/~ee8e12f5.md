## Example

Let $U = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
We check that $U$ is [[unitary|0D6RMJ10M9]], and we diagonalize it by a unitary matrix.

1. The [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]] is $U^* = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. $U^*U = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 + (-i)(i) & i + (-i) \\ -i + i & (-i)(i) + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$. So $U$ is unitary.
3. $\det U = \frac{1}{2}(1 - i^2) = 1$. So $U$ is in the [[special unitary group|~f67e0a03]] $SU(2)$.
4. $\tr U = \sqrt{2}$, so the characteristic polynomial is $t^2 - \sqrt{2}\,t + 1$.
5. Its roots are $\lambda_\pm = \frac{\sqrt{2} \pm \sqrt{2 - 4}}{2} = \frac{1 \pm i}{\sqrt{2}} = e^{\pm i\pi/4}$. Both have absolute value $1$.
6. $U(1, 1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1 + i, 1 + i) = \lambda_+(1, 1)$, and $U(1, -1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1 - i, -1 + i) = \lambda_-(1, -1)$.
7. The eigenvectors are orthogonal: $\ip{(1, 1)}{(1, -1)} = 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (-1) = 0$.
8. With $V = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, we get $U = V\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\pi/4}, e^{-i\pi/4})V^*$.

## Remarks

- The example illustrates [[the properties of unitary matrices|~2220a569]]: the determinant and the eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, and the eigenvectors are orthogonal.
- $U$ has the form $\begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ -\overline{\beta} & \overline{\alpha} \end{pmatrix}$ with $\alpha = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $\beta = \frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}$. By [[the quaternion theorem|~b83f5358]], it matches the unit quaternion $\frac{1 + k}{\sqrt{2}}$.
- $U^2 = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2i \\ 2i & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and $U^8 = I_2$, because $\lambda_\pm^8 = 1$.
- $U$ is symmetric, $U^T = U$, but not Hermitian, because $U^* \neq U$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

This matrix keeps all lengths in the complex plane of two variables.
It has two special directions, $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$.
In each direction, it multiplies by a complex number of size $1$.
These two numbers are an eighth turn forward and an eighth turn back on the unit circle.

### L2 human

The worked computation is in the Example.
The typical use: this pattern of steps diagonalizes any unitary matrix.
A trap: the eigenvalues are not real, although the trace $\sqrt{2}$ and the determinant $1$ are real.
