## Example

Let $L = \set{\cdot, ^{-1}, 1}$ and let $T$ be the theory of [[groups|G5S1M9]] in $L$.
We show that $T$ has no [[model companion|~c265fb1d]].
The theory $T$ is universal, so it is [[inductive|~719ccde0]], and its [[existentially closed models|~524465b6]] are the [[algebraically closed groups|4SPKGZBTTT]].

### The class of e.c. groups is not elementary

1. Conjugacy. Let $G$ be e.c. and let $a, b \in G$ have the same [[order|0JPYSD5E0B]]. Then $a^k \mapsto b^k$ is an isomorphism $\langle a \rangle \to \langle b \rangle$. The [[HNN extension|YEYERRY37K]] for it is a group $H \supseteq G$ with an element $t$ such that $t a t^{-1} = b$. The existential formula $\exists t \, (t a t^{-1} = b)$ holds in $H$, so it holds in $G$.
2. Elements of each order. For $n \ge 1$, the group $G \times \Z/n\Z$ contains $G$ and an element of order $n$. Having an element of order $n$ is an existential sentence, so $G$ has one.
3. A set of formulas. Let $\Sigma(x, y)$ consist of $x^n \neq 1$ and $y^n \neq 1$ for all $n \ge 1$, and of $\forall t \, (t x t^{-1} \neq y)$.
4. Finite satisfiability. A finite part of $\Sigma$ mentions only exponents $n \le N$. Choose primes $p \neq q$ larger than $N$, and elements $x$ of order $p$ and $y$ of order $q$ in an e.c. group $G$. Conjugate elements have the same order, so $x$ and $y$ are not conjugate, and the finite part holds.
5. Compactness. Suppose the e.c. groups were the models of a theory $T'$. By steps 2 to 4 and [[compactness|1NR3HAWG9D]], some model of $T'$ has elements $x, y$ that realize $\Sigma$. Both have infinite order, so by step 1 they are conjugate, which contradicts the last formula of $\Sigma$.

### Conclusion

For an inductive theory, a model companion exists exactly when the class of e.c. models is elementary, and then it is the theory of that class.
By the first part this class is not elementary, so $T$ has no model companion, although every group embeds into an e.c. group.

## Remarks

The example shows that the existence of e.c. models does not give a model companion: the e.c. groups exist but have no first-order description.
Other classes behave in the same way, or differently, as follows.

- The theory of commutative rings also has no model companion.
- The theory of abelian groups has a model companion, a theory of divisible abelian groups, so the commutative case behaves differently.
- The theory of fields with one automorphism has a model companion, $\mathrm{ACFA}$, while the theory of fields with two commuting automorphisms has none.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For many classes of structures there is a first-order theory that describes their "closed" members, like the algebraically closed fields among the fields.
For groups this fails.
Closed groups exist, but no list of first-order axioms describes exactly them.
The reason is that in a closed group, elements of the same order are conjugate, and "having infinite order" cannot be expressed by one formula.

### L2 human

We see step 1 for $a$ and $b$ of order $3$ in an e.c. group $G$.

1. The map $a \mapsto b$, $a^2 \mapsto b^2$, $1 \mapsto 1$ is an isomorphism of cyclic groups of order $3$.
2. The HNN extension adds a new element $t$ with the relation $t a t^{-1} = b$, and $G$ embeds into it.
3. So $a$ and $b$ are conjugate in a larger group, and since $G$ is e.c., they are already conjugate in $G$.

Step 4 then uses orders $p$ and $q$ that are large primes, and compactness lets them grow to infinity.
The typical use of the example is as a warning: inductivity gives e.c. models, not a model companion.
A trap is to think that the failure comes from the lack of the amalgamation property; the theory of groups has amalgamation, and the failure is the non-elementary class of e.c. models.
