## Counterexample

Claim to refute: $\det(A + B) = \det A + \det B$ for all $2 \times 2$ real matrices, that is, $\det \colon M_2(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$ is a [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]].
Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. By the [[2 × 2 formula|0ZN2J42SK7]], $\det A = 1 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 0 = 0$.
2. In the same way, $\det B = 0 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0 = 0$.
3. $A + B = I_2$, and $\det I_2 = 1$.
4. So $\det(A + B) = 1 \neq 0 = \det A + \det B$.
5. A second failure, of homogeneity: $\det(2 I_2) = 2 \cdot 2 - 0 = 4$, but $2 \det I_2 = 2$.

## Remarks

The determinant is linear in each row separately, by [[multilinearity|0PPG793642]], but not in the whole matrix.
For $n \times n$ matrices, $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$, so $\det$ is homogeneous of degree $n$, not of degree $1$.
For $n = 1$ the determinant is the identity map $K \to K$, which is linear; the failure starts at $n = 2$.
A correct formula for a sum exists in special cases, for example the [[matrix determinant lemma|0BC3Q6K92H]] for a rank one change.

## Explanations

### L1 human

One might hope that the determinant of a sum is the sum of the determinants.
Here two matrices each have determinant $0$, but their sum is the identity, with determinant $1$.
So the hope fails.

### L2 human

To expand $\det(A + B)$ correctly, use multilinearity row by row: for $2 \times 2$ matrices
$$\det(A + B) = \det A + \det B + \det(a_1; b_2) + \det(b_1; a_2),$$
where $(a_1; b_2)$ is the matrix with row $1$ of $A$ and row $2$ of $B$.
In the example, $\det(a_1; b_2) = \det I_2 = 1$ and $\det(b_1; a_2) = 0$, which gives $1$.
A trap: in exams, students often write $\det(A + B) = \det A + \det B$ in a proof; it is wrong for $n \ge 2$.
