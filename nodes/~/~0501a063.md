## Example

Let $L = \set{+, -, \cdot, 0, 1}$ and let $T$ be the theory of [[fields|04C5ZBG1WT]] in $L$.
We show that $T$ is [[inductive|~ae02f1e0]].
Let $(K_i)_{i \in I}$ be a chain of fields, indexed by a nonempty total order, and let $K$ be its union.

1. The axioms of commutative rings with $0 \neq 1$ are universal sentences. Take one of them, say $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x \cdot y = y \cdot x)$, and $a, b \in K$.
2. Since the index set is totally ordered, $a$ and $b$ lie in one $K_j$. The operations of $K$ restrict to those of $K_j$, because $K_j$ is a [[substructure|676H7EGTKJ]] of $K$. So $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ holds in $K$. The other universal axioms, and $0 \neq 1$, are checked in the same way.
3. The inverse axiom is $\forall x \, \exists y \, (x = 0 \lor x \cdot y = 1)$. Let $a \in K$ with $a \neq 0$, and let $a \in K_j$.
4. Since $K_j$ is a field, there is $b \in K_j$ with $a \cdot b = 1$ in $K_j$. This equation also holds in $K$.

So $K$ is a field, and $T$ is inductive.
This is part 2 of the [[elementary chain theorem|NHCRZ74JHH]] in a concrete case.

## Remarks

The example shows the mechanism of inductivity: a universal axiom is checked in one stage, and an existential witness found in one stage survives in the union.
For instance, the union of the chain $\Q \subseteq \Q(\sqrt 2) \subseteq \Q(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3) \subseteq \cdots$, over the square roots of all primes, is a field.

When the theory changes, the result changes in this way.

- Fields of a fixed characteristic: still inductive, since "$1 + \cdots + 1 = 0$" and its negation are quantifier-free.
- Algebraically closed fields: still inductive, since "every monic polynomial of degree $n$ has a root" is a $\forall\exists$ sentence for each $n$.
- Fields with an element that is not a square: not inductive. The sentence $\exists a \, \forall x \, (x \cdot x \neq a)$ is $\exists\forall$. Start with the [[rational numbers|0NCJ5WXN6Z]] and adjoin, one at a time, a square root of each element that appears. Each stage is a finite extension of $\Q$, so it has a non-square, but in the union every element is a square.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If you have a growing sequence of number systems, each one a field, then the union of all of them is a field too.
Each sum, product or inverse that you need already exists at some stage, and it does not change later.

### L2 human

We check the inverse of $a = \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3$ in the union of the chain above.

1. The element $a$ lies in the stage $\Q(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3)$.
2. In that field, $(\sqrt 3 + \sqrt 2)(\sqrt 3 - \sqrt 2) = 3 - 2 = 1$, so $a^{-1} = \sqrt 3 - \sqrt 2$.
3. The equation $a \cdot (\sqrt 3 - \sqrt 2) = 1$ holds at that stage, so it holds in every later stage and in the union.

The typical use of this example is the construction of the algebraic closure, and of e.c. fields, as unions of chains.
A trap is to think that every first-order property of the stages passes to the union: the property "has a non-square" does not.
