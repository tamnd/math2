## Example

Let $T_G$ be the [[elementary theory of groups|RX9MB386C5]], the set of consequences of the group axioms in the signature $\set{\cdot, {}^{-1}, e}$.
We check that $T_G$ is not [[essentially undecidable|~c0b3bb9c]].

1. Let $\mathcal{E}$ be the trivial group, with one element $e$, and $e \cdot e = e$ and $e^{-1} = e$.
2. $\mathcal{E}$ satisfies the group axioms, so $T_G \subseteq \operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{E})$.
3. $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{E})$ is consistent, because $\mathcal{E}$ is a model.
4. $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{E})$ is [[decidable|HWC687ES0Q]]. To decide a sentence, we evaluate it in $\mathcal{E}$: each quantifier ranges over the single element $e$, and each term has the value $e$.
5. So $T_G$ has a consistent decidable extension, and the condition of essential undecidability fails.

Yet $T_G$ itself is undecidable, by a theorem of Tarski published in 1953.
So $T_G$ is an undecidable theory that is not essentially undecidable.

## Remarks

The example shows that undecidability does not pass to extensions.
Many other extensions of $T_G$ are decidable: the theory of any finite group, by evaluation, and the theory of abelian groups, by [[Szmielew's theorem|Y0753DF5R9]].
The theory of abelian groups is not complete, so it is a decidable extension that is not of the form $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{E})$.
For contrast, every consistent extension of [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] is undecidable.
Tarski's proof of the undecidability of $T_G$ uses interpretations, the method of the [[transfer theorem|~e4c9e250]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

No computer can decide every statement that follows from the rules of a group.
But if we add the rule "there is only one element", the question becomes easy: a computer just checks the statement on that one element.
So the rules of a group are hard to decide, but not in the strong sense, because some extra rules make them easy.

### L2 human

We decide the sentence $\sigma = \exists x \, (x \ne e)$ in two extensions of $T_G$.

1. In the trivial group, the only element is $e$, so $\sigma$ is false and $\neg \sigma \in \operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{E})$.
2. In the theory of $\Z / 2 \Z$, the element $1$ differs from the identity $0$, so $\sigma$ is true.
3. So $\sigma$ is not decided by $T_G$, and the two extensions decide it in opposite ways.

The typical use of this non-example is to show the difference between "undecidable" and "essentially undecidable".
A trap is to conclude from the decidability of one extension that $T_G$ is decidable; decidability does not pass to subtheories either.
