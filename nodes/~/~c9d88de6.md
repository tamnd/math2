## Example

Let $\sigma = \set{+, \cdot, -, 0, 1}$ be the signature of [[rings|0B1261B5ED]], and let $\approx$ be a binary relation symbol that plays the role of $=$.
Let $\mathcal{M}$ have domain $\Z$, the usual ring operations, and $\approx$ interpreted as $E = \set{(a, b) : a \equiv b \pmod 2}$.
We check that $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies all the equality axioms for $\approx$, and that $E$ is not identity.

1. Reflexivity: $a - a = 0$ is even, so $a \mathrel{E} a$ for all $a$.
2. Substitution for $+$: if $a \mathrel{E} b$ and $c \mathrel{E} d$, then $(a + c) - (b + d) = (a - b) + (c - d)$ is even. So $(a + c) \mathrel{E} (b + d)$.
3. Substitution for $\cdot$: $ac - bd = a(c - d) + d(a - b)$, which is even under the same hypotheses.
4. Substitution for $-$: $(-a) - (-b) = -(a - b)$ is even when $a - b$ is even.
5. The constants $0$ and $1$ need only reflexivity.
6. Substitution for $\approx$: $E$ is an [[equivalence relation|0G8VEZ3WTK]]. If $a \mathrel{E} b$, $c \mathrel{E} d$ and $a \mathrel{E} c$, then $b \mathrel{E} a \mathrel{E} c \mathrel{E} d$, so $b \mathrel{E} d$.
7. So $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies all the equality axioms, as listed for groups in the [[equality axioms example|~2958cf80]].
8. But $0 \mathrel{E} 2$ and $0 \neq 2$. So $E$ is not identity, and $\mathcal{M}$ is not a structure of [[first-order logic with equality|~cc7adc48]] for $\approx$.

So the equality axioms do not force the symbol to mean identity.

## Remarks

In $\mathcal{M}$ the sentence $\forall x \, \forall y \, \forall z \, (x \approx y \lor x \approx z \lor y \approx z)$ is true: of three integers, two have the same parity.
So $\mathcal{M}$ "thinks" it has at most two elements, although $\Z$ is infinite.
The [[quotient|0011MTQMR6]] $\mathcal{M}/E$ is the ring $\Z / 2\Z$, in which $\approx$ is identity, and it satisfies the same sentences as $\mathcal{M}$.
The proof of this fact works for every relation $E$ that satisfies the equality axioms, that is, for every [[congruence|DCSXXT4SER]].

1. Let $\pi \colon \Z \to \Z/2\Z$ be the quotient map, and let $s$ be an assignment in $\Z$.
2. For each term $t$, the value of $t$ in $\mathcal{M}/E$ under $\pi \circ s$ is $\pi$ of its value in $\mathcal{M}$ under $s$. This holds by induction on $t$, since $E$ respects $+$, $\cdot$ and $-$.
3. For atomic $t_1 \approx t_2$: it holds in $\mathcal{M}$ under $s$ exactly when the values are in $E$, that is, when their images under $\pi$ are equal.
4. The connectives commute with this correspondence.
5. For $\exists x \, \varphi$: since $\pi$ is onto, a witness in $\Z/2\Z$ has the form $\pi(a)$, and $a$ is a witness in $\Z$, and conversely.
6. So each formula holds in $\mathcal{M}$ under $s$ exactly when it holds in $\mathcal{M}/E$ under $\pi \circ s$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The rules for equals say: everything equals itself, and equals can replace equals.
The relation "has the same parity" on the integers obeys all these rules.
But $0$ and $2$ have the same parity and are different numbers.
So the rules alone do not make a relation into true equality.

### L2 human

We check one instance of substitution for $\cdot$ with numbers.

1. Take $a = 3$, $b = 7$, $c = 4$ and $d = 10$. Then $a \mathrel{E} b$ and $c \mathrel{E} d$.
2. Then $ac = 12$ and $bd = 70$, and $70 - 12 = 58$ is even.
3. So $ac \mathrel{E} bd$, as step 3 predicts.
4. In $\Z / 2\Z$ this is $\bar{1} \cdot \bar{0} = \bar{0} = \bar{1} \cdot \bar{0}$.

The typical use of this construction is in the proof of the completeness theorem with equality, where the Henkin model is first built with a congruence and then divided by it.
A trap: "satisfies the axioms of equality" and "is equality" differ, and the difference vanishes only after the quotient.
