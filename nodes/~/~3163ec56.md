## Statement

Let $U$, $V$, $W$ be vector spaces over a field $F$, of any dimension.

1. For each [[linear map|0RJF668P1T]] $f \colon V \to W$, the [[dual map|0C2TQMW5A1]] $f^* \colon W^* \to V^*$ is linear.
2. The map $\Hom(V, W) \to \Hom(W^*, V^*)$, $f \mapsto f^*$, is linear: $(af + bg)^* = a f^* + b g^*$.
3. For linear maps $f \colon U \to V$ and $g \colon V \to W$, the dual of the [[composition|00BKRZKMSS]] is $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$.
4. $(\id_V)^* = \id_{V^*}$.
5. If $f$ is an isomorphism, then $f^*$ is an isomorphism, and $(f^*)^{-1} = (f^{-1})^*$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- In part 3, the maps must be composable: $f$ ends where $g$ starts. The dual maps are then composable in the reverse order, $W^* \to V^* \to U^*$.
- No finite dimension is needed for any part.

### Consequences

- For matrices, part 3 and the theorem on the [[matrix of the dual map|0ZPB3180AY]] give $(BA)^{\mathsf{T}} = A^{\mathsf{T}} B^{\mathsf{T}}$, the [[transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]].
- If $p \colon V \to V$ is a projection, $p \circ p = p$, then $p^* \circ p^* = (p \circ p)^* = p^*$. So $p^*$ is again a projection.

### Converse

Part 5 has a converse, in any dimension: if $f^*$ is an isomorphism, then $f$ is an isomorphism.
This follows from the theorem on the [[kernel and image of the dual map|~0b4dab92]].

### Generalizations

Parts 3 and 4 say that $V \mapsto V^*$, $f \mapsto f^*$ is a contravariant functor; see [[contravariant functor|RECR96V97B]].
The same rules hold for $\Hom(-, X)$ for any fixed space $X$ in place of $F$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Doing $f$ and then $g$, and then pulling a measuring rule back, is the same as pulling it back along $g$ first and then along $f$.
So the order of the maps reverses.
This is the same rule as for taking off socks and shoes: the last one put on is the first one taken off.

### L2 human

We check part 3 on $f \colon \R \to \R^2$, $f(t) = (t, 2t)$, and $g \colon \R^2 \to \R$, $g(x, y) = x + y$.

1. $g \circ f$ is $t \mapsto 3t$.
2. Let $\varphi \in \R^*$ be $\varphi(s) = 5s$. Then $(g \circ f)^*(\varphi)(t) = \varphi(3t) = 15t$.
3. On the other side, $g^*(\varphi)(x, y) = 5(x + y)$, and $f^*(g^*(\varphi))(t) = 5(t + 2t) = 15t$.
4. Both sides give $15t$.

The typical use is to compute duals of composite maps one factor at a time.
A trap: writing $(g \circ f)^* = g^* \circ f^*$, which is not even defined unless the spaces match.
