## Example

For an integer $n$, let $D(n)$ be the statement "$n$ is even or $n$ is a multiple of $3$".
In mathematics, "or" is inclusive, as on the page [[connectives in mathematical language|~ba599a3d]].
So $D(n)$ is true when at least one of its two parts is true.
We find the truth value of $D(n)$ for $n = 4, 9, 6, 7$, one case for each pattern of the parts.

1. $n = 4$: $4 = 2 \cdot 2$ is even, and $4$ is not a multiple of $3$. One part is true, so $D(4)$ is true.
2. $n = 9$: $9$ is odd, and $9 = 3 \cdot 3$ is a multiple of $3$. One part is true, so $D(9)$ is true.
3. $n = 6$: $6 = 2 \cdot 3$ is even and a multiple of $3$. Both parts are true, so $D(6)$ is true.
4. $n = 7$: $7$ is odd, and $7$ is not a multiple of $3$, because $7 = 2 \cdot 3 + 1$. Both parts are false, so $D(7)$ is false.

## Remarks

Case 3 is the case where the inclusive and the exclusive "or" differ.
With the exclusive reading, $D(6)$ would be false, because both parts are true.
The inclusive [[disjunction|0F72FK8EKB]] is the reading of every mathematical text unless the text says "but not both".

The set of $n$ in $\{1, 2, \ldots, 12\}$ with $D(n)$ true is $\{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12\}$, which has $8$ elements.
It is the union of the even numbers $\{2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12\}$ and the multiples of $3$, $\{3, 6, 9, 12\}$.
The exclusive reading would remove $6$ and $12$, and it would leave $6$ elements.
This matches the count $6 + 4 - 2 = 8$ for the union.

If we replace "or" by "and", the statement is true exactly for the multiples of $6$.
In $\{1, \ldots, 12\}$, these are $6$ and $12$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take the claim "the number is even or it can be split into $3$ equal whole parts".
For $4$ it is true: $4$ is even.
For $9$ it is true: $9 = 3 + 3 + 3$.
For $6$ both parts hold, and in mathematics the claim is still true.
For $7$ neither part holds, so the claim is false.

### L2 human

We count the integers from $1$ to $30$ for which $D(n)$ is true.

1. There are $15$ even numbers from $1$ to $30$.
2. There are $10$ multiples of $3$ from $1$ to $30$.
3. A number is both even and a multiple of $3$ exactly when it is a multiple of $6$. There are $5$ such numbers.
4. With the inclusive "or", we count the union: $15 + 10 - 5 = 20$.
5. With the exclusive "or", we remove the $5$ numbers of step 3 once more: $20 - 5 = 15$.

The typical use is in counting and in probability: "at least one of two events" is an inclusive "or", and the count of the union needs the subtraction of step 4.

A trap: the answer $15 + 10 = 25$ counts the multiples of $6$ twice.
It is wrong for both readings of "or".
