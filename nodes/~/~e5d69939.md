## Proof

We derive $\neg(p \land \neg p)$ with the rules of [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]].
We read $\neg \varphi$ as "$\varphi$ implies the false statement $\bot$", as in Lean, where `Not p` is `p → False`.
So the rule for proving $\neg\varphi$ is: assume $\varphi$, derive $\bot$, and then discharge the assumption.

1. Assume $p \land \neg p$. Reason: we want to prove the negation of this formula.
2. From step 1, infer $p$. Reason: the elimination rule of [[conjunction|025YZN3VJW]], `And.left`.
3. From step 1, infer $\neg p$. Reason: the elimination rule of conjunction, `And.right`.
4. From steps 2 and 3, infer $\bot$. Reason: $\neg p$ means $p \to \bot$, and modus ponens with $p$ gives $\bot$.
5. Discharge the assumption of step 1, and conclude $\neg(p \land \neg p)$. Reason: the introduction rule of [[negation|029JBBF693]].
6. The steps use only the elimination rule of conjunction, modus ponens and the introduction rule of negation. None of them is the law of excluded middle or double negation elimination. So the derivation is valid in [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], and also in classical logic. $\blacksquare$

## Idea

To refute a statement, we assume it and derive the false statement.
The assumption $p \land \neg p$ hands us both $p$ and a way to turn $p$ into $\bot$.
Applying the second to the first gives $\bot$ at once.

## Variants

In Lean the whole derivation is one term, `fun h => (And.right h) (And.left h)`: given a proof `h` of `p ∧ ¬p`, apply its second part to its first part.
The derivation never uses the rule "from $\bot$ infer anything", so it is also valid in minimal logic.
A [[proof by a truth table|~cd354ce5]] gives the classical result only.
