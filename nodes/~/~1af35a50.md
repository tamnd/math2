## Proof

The proof has two parts.
Part A shows that the graph of every total recursive function is defined in $\mathfrak{N}$ by a $\Sigma_1$ formula; we give it in outline, with the main lemma, the [[β-function lemma|BM6P0C2AC2]], proved separately.
Part B, given in full, turns any $\Sigma_1$ definition of the graph of a function into a $\Sigma_1$ formula that represents the function in $\mathsf{Q}$.
Parts 2 and 3 of the theorem follow at the end.
We write functions of one argument; the case of $k$ arguments is the same with $\vec{x}$ in place of $x$.
Steps 1 to 4 of [[the proof of Σ1-completeness|~e5ce2fd9]] are cited as N1 to N4: N1 computes sums and products of numerals, N2 evaluates closed terms, N3 separates distinct numerals, and N4 is $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \forall x\,(x < \overline{n} \leftrightarrow \bigvee_{i < n} x = \overline{i})$.

Part A, in outline.

1. Let $\beta(c, d, i)$ be the remainder of $c$ on division by $1 + (i + 1) d$. By [[this lemma|2X3BHXYR6D]], its graph $v = \beta(c, d, i)$ is defined by the $\Delta_0$ formula $B(c, d, i, v) := \exists q \le c\,\bigl(c = q \cdot (1 + (i + 1) \cdot d) + v \land v < 1 + (i + 1) \cdot d\bigr)$. By the β-function lemma, every finite sequence $a_0, \ldots, a_n$ equals $\beta(c, d, 0), \ldots, \beta(c, d, n)$ for some $c, d$. Note $\beta(c, d, i) \le c$.
2. The zero, successor and projection functions have the $\Delta_0$ graphs $y = 0$, $y = S x$ and $y = x_j$.
3. Composition. If $h(x) = f(g(x))$ and the graphs of $f$ and $g$ are defined by $\Sigma_1$ formulas $F$ and $G$, then $y = h(x)$ is defined by $\exists z\,(G(x, z) \land F(z, y))$. Moving the existential quantifiers of $G$ and $F$ to the front and merging them as in [[this example|~d54a438d]] gives a $\Sigma_1$ formula. Several inner functions are handled in the same way.
4. Primitive recursion. Let $h(x, 0) = f(x)$ and $h(x, z + 1) = g(x, z, h(x, z))$, with $\Sigma_1$ graphs $F$ and $G$. Then $y = h(x, z)$ holds in $\N$ exactly when there are $c, d$ such that $\beta(c, d, 0) = f(x)$, $\beta(c, d, i + 1) = g(x, i, \beta(c, d, i))$ for all $i < z$, and $\beta(c, d, z) = y$. From left to right, step 1 codes the sequence $h(x, 0), \ldots, h(x, z)$; from right to left, induction on $i \le z$ gives $\beta(c, d, i) = h(x, i)$.
5. Written with $B$, $F$ and $G$, the middle clause is $\forall i < z\, \exists u \le c\, \exists u' \le c\,\bigl(B(c, d, i, u) \land B(c, d, i + 1, u') \land G(x, i, u, u')\bigr)$. The existential quantifier of $G$ can be moved in front of $\exists u \le c\, \exists u' \le c$ by logic. The result has $\forall i < z$ in front of a $\Sigma_1$ formula, and it is equivalent in $\mathfrak{N}$ to a $\Sigma_1$ formula by [[collection of witnesses|~6d00eafb]]. With the outer $\exists c\, \exists d$ and step 3, the graph of $h$ is $\Sigma_1$.
6. Minimization. Let $h(x) = $ the least $z$ with $g(x, z) = 0$, where such a $z$ exists for every $x$, and let $G$ define the graph of $g$. Then $y = h(x)$ is defined by $G(x, y, 0) \land \forall z < y\, \exists u\,(G(x, z, u) \land u \ne 0)$. Since $g$ is total, the second conjunct says that $g(x, z) \ne 0$ for $z < y$. By [[collection|~6d00eafb]] it is $\Sigma_1$ in $\mathfrak{N}$.
7. By induction on the definition of a total [[recursive function|HBK5QHC1GC]] from the basic functions by composition, primitive recursion and minimization, steps 2 to 6 show that its graph is defined in $\mathfrak{N}$ by a $\Sigma_1$ formula. Merging its existential quantifiers as in the example of step 3, this formula can be written $\exists w\, \delta(x, y, w)$ with $\delta \in \Delta_0$.

Part B, in full.
Let $f : \N \to \N$, and let $\exists w\, \delta(x, y, w)$ with $\delta \in \Delta_0$ define the graph of $f$ in $\mathfrak{N}$.

8. Trichotomy: for each $n$, $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \forall x\,(x < \overline{n} \lor x = \overline{n} \lor \overline{n} < x)$. First, for each $m$, $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \forall u\,(S u + \overline{m} = S(u + \overline{m}))$, by induction on $m$: for $m = 0$ both sides equal $S u$ by $\mathsf{Q}4$, and $S u + S\overline{m} = S(S u + \overline{m}) = S S(u + \overline{m}) = S(u + S\overline{m})$ by $\mathsf{Q}5$ and the induction hypothesis.
9. We prove trichotomy by induction on $n$, reasoning in $\mathsf{Q}$ with $x$ arbitrary. For $n = 0$: by $\mathsf{Q}3$, $x = 0$ or $x = S y$; in the second case $y + S 0 = S(y + 0) = S y = x$ by $\mathsf{Q}5$ and $\mathsf{Q}4$, so $0 < x$ by $\mathsf{Q}8$.
10. For $n + 1$, use the three cases of the hypothesis for $\overline{n}$. If $x < \overline{n}$ or $x = \overline{n}$, then $x = \overline{i}$ for some $i \le n$ by N4, and $\overline{i} < \overline{n + 1}$ by N4. If $\overline{n} < x$, then $v + S\overline{n} = x$ for some $v$, and by $\mathsf{Q}3$, $v = 0$ or $v = S u$. If $v = 0$, then $x = 0 + \overline{n + 1} = \overline{n + 1}$ by N1. If $v = S u$, then $x = S u + S\overline{n} = S(u + S\overline{n}) = u + S S\overline{n}$ by step 8 and $\mathsf{Q}5$, so $\overline{n + 1} < x$ by $\mathsf{Q}8$.
11. Define the $\Delta_0$ formulas $\delta^*(x, y, w) := y \le w \land \exists w'' \le w\; \delta(x, y, w'')$ and $\theta(x, y, w) := \delta^*(x, y, w) \land \forall y' \le w\; \forall w' \le w\,\bigl(\delta^*(x, y', w') \to y' = y\bigr)$, and the $\Sigma_1$ formula $\varphi(x, y) := \exists w\; \theta(x, y, w)$.
12. Fix $n$ and put $m = f(n)$. In $\N$, $\delta^*(n, y', w')$ implies $\delta(n, y', w'')$ for some $w''$, so $y' = m$. Choose $w_0$ with $\delta(n, m, w_0)$ true, and put $c = \max(m, w_0)$. Then $\delta^*(n, m, c)$ is true, and so is $\theta(n, m, c)$, since every $y'$ with $\delta^*(n, y', w')$ true equals $m$.
13. By Part 1 of [[Σ1-completeness|~1e1241ee]], $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \theta(\overline{n}, \overline{m}, \overline{c})$ and $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \delta^*(\overline{n}, \overline{m}, \overline{c})$. Hence $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \varphi(\overline{n}, \overline{m})$, which gives $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \forall y\,(y = \overline{m} \to \varphi(\overline{n}, y))$.
14. For the other direction we reason in $\mathsf{Q}$: assume $\theta(\overline{n}, y, w)$, and show $y = \overline{m}$. We split into the cases $w \le \overline{c}$ and $\neg(w \le \overline{c})$.
15. Case $w \le \overline{c}$. By N4, $w = \overline{j}$ for some $j \le c$. The first conjunct of $\theta$ gives $y \le \overline{j}$, so $y = \overline{i}$ for some $i \le j$, by N4. For each such pair $(i, j)$, either $\theta(n, i, j)$ is true in $\N$, and then $\delta^*(n, i, j)$ is true, so $i = m$ by step 12 and $y = \overline{m}$; or it is false, and then $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \neg \theta(\overline{n}, \overline{i}, \overline{j})$ by Part 1 of Σ1-completeness, which contradicts the assumption. In every subcase, $y = \overline{m}$.
16. Case $\neg(w \le \overline{c})$. By trichotomy for $\overline{c}$, $\overline{c} < w$, so $\overline{c} \le w$. By trichotomy for $\overline{m}$, $w < \overline{m}$, $w = \overline{m}$ or $\overline{m} < w$. In the first two cases $w = \overline{j}$ for some $j \le m \le c$ by N4, and $\overline{j} \le \overline{c}$ by N4, against the case hypothesis. So $\overline{m} < w$, and $\overline{m} \le w$.
17. Apply the second conjunct of $\theta(\overline{n}, y, w)$ with $y' = \overline{m}$ and $w' = \overline{c}$. Its hypotheses $\overline{m} \le w$, $\overline{c} \le w$ and $\delta^*(\overline{n}, \overline{m}, \overline{c})$ hold by steps 16 and 13. So $\overline{m} = y$.
18. Steps 14 to 17 give $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \forall y\,(\varphi(\overline{n}, y) \to y = \overline{m})$. With step 13, $\varphi$ represents $f$ in $\mathsf{Q}$, and $\varphi$ is $\Sigma_1$. Any extension of $\mathsf{Q}$ proves the same sentences. This proves Part 1.
19. Part 2. Let $R$ be recursive, with recursive characteristic function $\chi_R$ represented by $\varphi$. Put $\psi(x) := \varphi(x, \overline{1})$. If $n \in R$, then $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \varphi(\overline{n}, \overline{1})$. If $n \notin R$, then $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \forall y\,(\varphi(\overline{n}, y) \leftrightarrow y = 0)$ and $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \overline{1} \ne 0$ by $\mathsf{Q}1$, so $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \neg \psi(\overline{n})$.
20. Part 3. Let $T$ be consistent with a recursive set of axioms, so that its theorems can be listed by a computable procedure. If $\psi$ represents $R$ in $T$, then on input $n$ list the theorems of $T$ until $\psi(\overline{n})$ or $\neg \psi(\overline{n})$ appears. One of them appears by representability, and not both by consistency, so the answer is correct and $R$ is recursive.
21. If $\varphi$ represents $f$ in $T$, then on input $n$ list the theorems of $T$ until $\varphi(\overline{n}, \overline{m})$ appears for some $m$. It appears for $m = f(n)$, and for $m \ne f(n)$ the theory proves $\neg \varphi(\overline{n}, \overline{m})$ by N3, so by consistency it does not prove $\varphi(\overline{n}, \overline{m})$. Hence $f$ is recursive.

## Idea

The graph of a computable function says "there is a computation with this output", which is $\Sigma_1$; the β function lets one number code the whole computation.
A $\Sigma_1$ definition of the graph is not yet a representation, since $\mathsf{Q}$ may not prove that the output is unique.
Part B builds uniqueness into the formula: $\theta$ says that $y$ is the only output with a witness below $w$, and $\mathsf{Q}$ can check all small candidates by Σ1-completeness and compare large ones with numerals by trichotomy.

## Variants

In Part A, if the language has a symbol for every primitive recursive function, as in primitive recursive arithmetic, the β function is not needed.
Gödel's 1931 paper represented the primitive recursive relations in his system, which is enough for the incompleteness theorems; the extension to all recursive functions adds step 6.
Part B uses only N1 to N4 and trichotomy, so the same argument works in every theory that proves these facts, for example the theory $\mathsf{R}$.
