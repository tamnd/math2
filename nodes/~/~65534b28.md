## Proof

Let $L$ be a countable first-order language with equality, and let $\Gamma$ be a [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]] set of [[sentences|DKFGQTRYZE]] of $L$.
We build a model of $\Gamma$ whose universe is countable, by the [[Henkin construction|~95e9f5bb]].
Derivability $\vdash$ is in the Hilbert system $H$ of the [[soundness theorem|~331f6430]], for the language named in each step.
For a formula $\varphi$, write $n(\varphi)$ for the number of occurrences of $\neg$, $\to$ and $\forall$ in $\varphi$.

Step (a): facts about $H$.
We use the facts (P1) to (P5) of the [[proof of Lindenbaum's lemma|~bc83b0fa]] and four more facts, (P6) to (P9).
The proofs of (P6) and (P7) are inductions on derivations in Enderton, Section 2.4.

1. (P6) Generalization theorem: if $\Sigma \vdash \varphi$ and the variable $x$ is not free in any member of $\Sigma$, then $\Sigma \vdash \forall x \, \varphi$. The induction uses the axioms of groups 3 and 4, and the fact that the generalizations of axioms are axioms.
2. (P7) Constant replacement: let $D$ be a derivation of $\varphi$ from $\Sigma$, where the constant $c$ does not occur in $\Sigma$. Let $y$ be a variable that does not occur in $D$. Replace $c$ by $y$ everywhere in $D$. The result is a derivation of $\varphi^c_y$ from $\Sigma$. Each axiom becomes an axiom of the same group, and the members of $\Sigma$ do not change.
3. (P8) Generalization on constants: let $\Sigma$ be a set of sentences, and let $c$ be a constant symbol that occurs neither in $\Sigma$ nor in $\varphi$. If $\Sigma \vdash \varphi^x_c$, then $\Sigma \vdash \forall x \, \varphi$.
4. Proof of (P8). Take a derivation of $\varphi^x_c$ and a variable $y$ that does not occur in it. By (P7), $\Sigma \vdash \varphi^x_y$, since $c$ does not occur in $\varphi$.
5. The members of $\Sigma$ are sentences, so (P6) gives $\Sigma \vdash \forall y \, \varphi^x_y$.
6. The variable $y$ does not occur in $\varphi$, so $(\varphi^x_y)^y_x = \varphi$, and $x$ is substitutable for $y$ in $\varphi^x_y$: the occurrences of $y$ stand where $x$ was free. Group 2 gives the axiom $\forall y \, \varphi^x_y \to \varphi$, and MP gives $\Sigma \vdash \varphi$.
7. Then (P6) gives $\Sigma \vdash \forall x \, \varphi$, since $x$ is not free in the sentences of $\Sigma$.
8. (P9) Equality: for closed terms $t, u, w, t_i, u_i$, an $n$-ary function symbol $f$ and an $n$-ary relation symbol $P$, the following hold. (i) $\vdash t = t$. (ii) $t = u \vdash u = t$. (iii) $t = u, u = w \vdash t = w$. (iv) $t_1 = u_1, \dots, t_n = u_n \vdash f t_1 \dots t_n = f u_1 \dots u_n$. (v) $t_1 = u_1, \dots, t_n = u_n, P t_1 \dots t_n \vdash P u_1 \dots u_n$.
9. In the proof of (P9) we instantiate generalized axioms with closed terms. A closed term has no variables, so it is substitutable for every variable, and group 2 with MP removes one quantifier $\forall x$ at a time.
10. (i): $\forall x \, (x = x)$ is a generalization of an axiom of group 5, so it is an axiom. Instantiate $x$ by $t$.
11. (ii): take the axiom of group 6 with $\alpha = (x = x)$ and $\alpha' = (y = x)$, where the first occurrence of $x$ is replaced. Its generalization over $x, y$, instantiated with $t, u$, gives $\vdash t = u \to (t = t \to u = t)$. With (i) and MP twice, $t = u \vdash u = t$.
12. (iii): take the axiom of group 6 with $\alpha = (x = z)$ and $\alpha' = (y = z)$. Instantiate $x, y, z$ by $u, t, w$: $\vdash u = t \to (u = w \to t = w)$. From $t = u$, (ii) gives $u = t$, and MP twice gives $t = w$.
13. (iv): for $i = 1, \dots, n$, take the axiom of group 6 with $\alpha = (z = f v_1 \dots v_{i-1} x v_{i+1} \dots v_n)$ and $\alpha'$ with this $x$ replaced by $y$. Instantiate $z$ by $f t_1 \dots t_n$, $v_j$ by $u_j$ for $j < i$, $v_j$ by $t_j$ for $j > i$, $x$ by $t_i$ and $y$ by $u_i$.
14. Step 13 turns $f t_1 \dots t_n = f u_1 \dots u_{i-1} t_i \dots t_n$ into $f t_1 \dots t_n = f u_1 \dots u_i t_{i+1} \dots t_n$, by MP with $t_i = u_i$. Start from (i) for $i = 0$ and apply step 13 for $i = 1, \dots, n$; the last formula is (iv).
15. (v): in the same way, the axiom of group 6 with $\alpha = P v_1 \dots v_{i-1} x v_{i+1} \dots v_n$ replaces $t_i$ by $u_i$ in one place. Starting from $P t_1 \dots t_n$, $n$ steps give $P u_1 \dots u_n$.

Step (b): new constants keep consistency.

16. Let $C = \set{c_0, c_1, c_2, \dots}$ be countably many new constant symbols, and let $L^+ = L \cup C$.
17. $\Gamma$ is consistent in $L^+$. Suppose not. Then $\Gamma \vdash \neg \forall x \, (x = x)$ in $L^+$, since an inconsistent set derives every formula by (P3).
18. Take such a derivation $D$. It uses finitely many constants $c_{i_1}, \dots, c_{i_k}$ of $C$. Replace them by distinct variables that do not occur in $D$.
19. The sentences of $\Gamma$ and the sentence $\neg \forall x \, (x = x)$ contain no symbol of $C$. By (P7), used $k$ times, the result is a derivation in $L$ of $\neg \forall x \, (x = x)$ from $\Gamma$.
20. Also $\vdash \forall x \, (x = x)$ in $L$, by step 10. So $\Gamma$ is inconsistent in $L$, against the hypothesis.

Step (c): the witness sentences.

21. $L^+$ is countable, so the pairs $(\varphi, x)$ of a formula $\varphi$ of $L^+$ and a variable $x$ such that $x$ is the only variable that can be free in $\varphi$ form a countable set. List them as $(\varphi_0, x_0), (\varphi_1, x_1), \dots$.
22. Let $d_n$ be the first constant of the list $c_0, c_1, \dots$ that occurs in none of $\varphi_0, \dots, \varphi_n$ and differs from $d_0, \dots, d_{n-1}$. It exists because these finitely many formulas contain finitely many constants of $C$.
23. Let $\theta_n$ be the sentence $\neg \forall x_n \, \varphi_n \to \neg (\varphi_n)^{x_n}_{d_n}$, and let $\Theta = \set{\theta_0, \theta_1, \dots}$.
24. The constant $d_m$ does not occur in $\theta_0, \dots, \theta_{m-1}$. Indeed, $\theta_k$ contains only the symbols of $\varphi_k$ and $d_k$, and step 22 gives $d_m \notin \varphi_k$ and $d_m \ne d_k$ for $k < m$.

Step (d): $\Gamma \cup \Theta$ is consistent in $L^+$.

25. Suppose not. By (P1), some finite part is inconsistent, so by (P2) some set $\Gamma \cup \set{\theta_0, \dots, \theta_m}$ is inconsistent. Take the least such $m$.
26. Put $\Gamma' = \Gamma \cup \set{\theta_0, \dots, \theta_{m-1}}$. It is consistent: for $m = 0$ by Step (b), and for $m > 0$ by the choice of $m$.
27. Write $\varphi, x, d$ for $\varphi_m, x_m, d_m$. Since $\Gamma' \cup \set{\theta_m}$ is inconsistent, (P5) gives $\Gamma' \vdash \neg \theta_m$.
28. Every truth assignment that makes $\neg(A \to B)$ true makes $A$ true and $B$ false. With (P3), $\Gamma' \vdash \neg \forall x \, \varphi$ and $\Gamma' \vdash \neg \neg \varphi^x_d$, and then $\Gamma' \vdash \varphi^x_d$.
29. The constant $d$ occurs neither in $\Gamma$ (whose sentences are in $L$), nor in $\theta_0, \dots, \theta_{m-1}$ (by step 24), nor in $\varphi$ (by step 22). The members of $\Gamma'$ are sentences.
30. By (P8), $\Gamma' \vdash \forall x \, \varphi$. With step 28, $\Gamma'$ is inconsistent, which contradicts step 26.

Step (e): a maximal consistent Henkin set.

31. [[Lindenbaum's lemma|~8ecc8dc7]] for the countable language $L^+$ gives a maximal consistent set $\Delta$ of sentences of $L^+$ with $\Gamma \cup \Theta \subseteq \Delta$.
32. Witness property: let $\neg \forall x \, \varphi \in \Delta$, where $\varphi$ has no free variable other than $x$. Then $(\varphi, x) = (\varphi_n, x_n)$ for some $n$, and $\theta_n \in \Delta$.
33. Property 3 of Lindenbaum's lemma, applied to $\theta_n$, gives $\neg \varphi^x_{d_n} \in \Delta$. So every sentence $\neg \forall x \, \varphi$ in $\Delta$ has a witness constant in $\Delta$.

Step (f): the term model.

34. Let $T$ be the set of closed [[terms|6D3WA7N2W4]] of $L^+$. It is not empty, since $c_0 \in T$. For $t, u \in T$, put $t \sim u$ when $(t = u) \in \Delta$.
35. $\sim$ is an equivalence relation. By (P9)(i), (ii), (iii) and property 1 of Lindenbaum's lemma (deductive closure), $\Delta$ contains $t = t$; it contains $u = t$ when it contains $t = u$; and it contains $t = w$ when it contains $t = u$ and $u = w$.
36. $\sim$ is a congruence. If $t_i \sim u_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$, then (P9)(iv) and deductive closure give $f t_1 \dots t_n \sim f u_1 \dots u_n$.
37. Also, if $t_i \sim u_i$ for all $i$, then $P t_1 \dots t_n \in \Delta$ if and only if $P u_1 \dots u_n \in \Delta$. This follows from (P9)(v), used in both directions with (P9)(ii), and deductive closure.
38. Write $[t]$ for the class of $t$, and let $M = T / {\sim}$. Define a [[structure|983GZBQYT4]] $\mathcal{M}$ for $L^+$ with universe $M$, as follows.
39. For a constant symbol $c$, put $c^{\mathcal{M}} = [c]$. For a function symbol $f$, put $f^{\mathcal{M}}([t_1], \dots, [t_n]) = [f t_1 \dots t_n]$, which does not depend on the representatives by step 36.
40. For a relation symbol $P$, let $P^{\mathcal{M}}$ be the set of tuples $([t_1], \dots, [t_n])$ with $P t_1 \dots t_n \in \Delta$. By step 37 this does not depend on the representatives. The symbol $=$ means identity on $M$, as in every structure.
41. Every closed term $t$ has the value $t^{\mathcal{M}} = [t]$, by induction on $t$. For a constant this is step 39. For $t = f t_1 \dots t_n$, the induction hypothesis and step 39 give $t^{\mathcal{M}} = f^{\mathcal{M}}([t_1], \dots, [t_n]) = [t]$.
42. So every element of $M$ is the value of a closed term.

Step (g): the truth lemma.
For every sentence $\sigma$ of $L^+$: $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$ if and only if $\sigma \in \Delta$.

43. We use [[strong induction|0KMVFSHFGZ]] on $n(\sigma)$, for all sentences at once. The substitution of a closed term does not change $n$: $n(\varphi^x_t) = n(\varphi)$.
44. Case $\sigma = (t = u)$. By step 41 and the definition of satisfaction, $\mathcal{M} \models t = u$ iff $[t] = [u]$ iff $t \sim u$ iff $(t = u) \in \Delta$.
45. Case $\sigma = P t_1 \dots t_n$. By step 41, $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$ iff $([t_1], \dots, [t_n]) \in P^{\mathcal{M}}$ iff $P t_1 \dots t_n \in \Delta$, by step 40.
46. Case $\sigma = \neg \tau$. $\mathcal{M} \models \neg \tau$ iff $\mathcal{M} \not\models \tau$ iff $\tau \notin \Delta$ (induction hypothesis) iff $\neg \tau \in \Delta$ (property 2 of Lindenbaum's lemma).
47. Case $\sigma = (\tau \to \rho)$. $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$ iff $\mathcal{M} \not\models \tau$ or $\mathcal{M} \models \rho$ iff $\tau \notin \Delta$ or $\rho \in \Delta$ (induction hypothesis) iff $\sigma \in \Delta$ (property 3).
48. Case $\sigma = \forall x \, \varphi$, where $x$ is the only variable that can be free in $\varphi$. By [[satisfaction|2RBQPV02AS]], $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$ iff $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi[x \mapsto a]$ for every $a \in M$.
49. By step 42, each $a$ is $t^{\mathcal{M}}$ for a closed $t$. The substitution lemma (see the [[soundness proof|~4b907310]]) gives $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi[x \mapsto t^{\mathcal{M}}]$ iff $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi^x_t$, because a closed $t$ is substitutable.
50. So $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$ iff $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi^x_t$ for every closed $t$. Since $n(\varphi^x_t) < n(\sigma)$, the induction hypothesis turns this into: $\varphi^x_t \in \Delta$ for every closed term $t$.
51. If $\forall x \, \varphi \in \Delta$, then $\varphi^x_t \in \Delta$ for every closed $t$, by property 4 of Lindenbaum's lemma.
52. If $\forall x \, \varphi \notin \Delta$, then $\neg \forall x \, \varphi \in \Delta$ by property 2. The witness property (step 33) gives a constant $d$ with $\neg \varphi^x_d \in \Delta$, so $\varphi^x_d \notin \Delta$ by property 2.
53. Steps 51 and 52 show: $\forall x \, \varphi \in \Delta$ iff $\varphi^x_t \in \Delta$ for every closed $t$. With step 50, $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$ iff $\sigma \in \Delta$. This ends the induction.

Step (h): the conclusion.

54. Let $\mathcal{M}_0$ be the reduct of $\mathcal{M}$ to $L$: the same universe and the same interpretations of the symbols of $L$, with the constants of $C$ forgotten.
55. Satisfaction of an $L$-sentence uses only the symbols of $L$, so $\mathcal{M}_0 \models \sigma$ iff $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$ for every sentence $\sigma$ of $L$. This is an induction on formulas.
56. Every $\gamma \in \Gamma$ lies in $\Delta$, so $\mathcal{M} \models \gamma$ by the truth lemma, and $\mathcal{M}_0 \models \gamma$ by step 55. Hence $\mathcal{M}_0$ is a model of $\Gamma$.
57. Size: $T$ is a set of finite strings over the countable alphabet of $L^+$, so it is [[countable|01TQB88H4C]]. The map $t \mapsto [t]$ sends $T$ onto $M$, so $M$ is countable, that is, finite or countably infinite.
58. Hence every consistent set of sentences of a countable language has a model with a countable universe. This proves the theorem for countable $L$.

## Idea

A model must be built from syntax alone, so its elements are the closed terms, and two terms name the same element when the theory says that they are equal.
For this to work, the theory must decide every sentence (Lindenbaum's lemma) and must name a witness for every existential sentence (the Henkin constants).
Then the truth lemma, an induction on sentences, shows that the term model makes true exactly the sentences of the theory.
The witnesses are used in one place only: the case $\forall x \, \varphi$ of the truth lemma.

## Variants

- Uncountable languages: if $L$ has cardinality $\kappa \ge \aleph_0$, add $\kappa$ new constants, well-order the pairs $(\varphi, x)$ in type $\kappa$, and choose the witnesses by transfinite recursion. Lindenbaum's lemma by [[Zorn's lemma|~08168ab3]] finishes the proof, and the model has at most $\kappa$ elements. This uses the axiom of choice.
- Interleaved form: decide the sentences one at a time, and add a witness $\neg \varphi^x_d$ right after each $\neg \forall x \, \varphi$ that goes in. This form is used in the proof of [[Robinson's joint consistency theorem|~3d4d4be9]].
- Languages without equality: the relation $\sim$ is not needed, and the universe is the set $T$ itself.
- Hasenjaeger gave a simplified form of the argument in 1953, and Rasiowa and Sikorski gave an algebraic form with ultrafilters of the [[Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra|QC9M3Q8B8Q]].
