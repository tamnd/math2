## Example

Let $V = \R^2$ and let $U$ be the line spanned by $u = (1, 1)$.
We compare the [[annihilator|0R6M158SQ9]] $U^0$ with the [[orthogonal complement|0XJXTBCB2T]] $U^\perp$ for two [[inner products|0NB0AWGJBW]].

1. The annihilator: a form $ax + by$ vanishes on $U$ exactly when $a + b = 0$. So $U^0 = \R \cdot (x - y)$, a line in $(\R^2)^*$.
2. For the [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]], $v \perp u$ means $v_1 + v_2 = 0$. So $U^\perp = \R \cdot (1, -1)$, a line in $\R^2$.
3. Take the inner product $\langle v, w \rangle_G = v_1 w_1 + 2 v_2 w_2$, that is, $v^{\mathsf{T}} G w$ with $G = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$.
4. For this inner product, $v \perp u$ means $v_1 + 2 v_2 = 0$. So $U^{\perp_G} = \R \cdot (2, -1)$, a different line in $\R^2$.
5. The annihilator in step 1 did not use any inner product, so it is the same in both cases.
6. Hence the orthogonal complement is not the annihilator: it lies in another space, and it changes with the inner product.

## Remarks

- The two are related by the [[Riesz map|04GYPXDXNH]] $v \mapsto \langle v, \cdot \rangle$, which depends on the inner product. It sends $U^\perp$ onto $U^0$.
- Check for $G$: the vector $(2, -1)$ goes to the form $w \mapsto 2 w_1 + 2 \cdot (-1) w_2 = 2(w_1 - w_2)$, which is $2(x - y) \in U^0$.
- Check for the dot product: $(1, -1)$ goes to the form $x - y \in U^0$.
- The dimension formulas agree: $\dim U^\perp = \dim U^0 = \dim V - \dim U = 1$ in finite dimension.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The orthogonal complement of a line in the plane is the perpendicular line, and "perpendicular" depends on how angles are measured.
The annihilator of the line is the set of linear equations that hold on it, and it needs no angles.
With the usual angles, the two can be matched; with other angles, the match changes.

### L2 human

To pass from the annihilator to an orthogonal complement, choose an inner product and use the Riesz map.

1. Compute $U^0$ from equations: here $x - y$.
2. Find the vector $v$ with $\langle v, w \rangle = w_1 - w_2$ for all $w$: for the dot product $v = (1, -1)$, for $G$ it is $v = (1, -\frac{1}{2})$.
3. These vectors span $U^\perp$ for the chosen inner product.

The typical use is to keep the two notions apart when the space has no preferred inner product.
A trap: writing $U^\perp$ for both, and then treating the annihilator as a subspace of $V$.
