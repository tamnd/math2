## Summary

A probabilistic proof of a non-probabilistic theorem is a proof of a statement without randomness that introduces a random object or a random process.
The proof reads a deterministic quantity as an [[expected value|0WQNRCNY3M]], a probability or a limit of [[random variables|0PNRKMRC55]].
Then a theorem of probability, such as [[Chebyshev's inequality|02HZ1BK3MW]] or the [[strong law of large numbers|0DFWHQ839T]], gives the deterministic result.
The proof is fully rigorous, because a probability space is a measure space and probability theory is a part of analysis.
The approach matters because the language of probability organizes long estimates into a few standard steps.

## Motivation

Some estimates in analysis and number theory are sums with many terms of different signs and sizes.
A direct estimate of such a sum can be long.
Sometimes the sum is the expected value of a random variable with a simple structure, for example a sum of independent terms.
Then the general theorems of probability control the sum at once: the variance bounds the spread, and the law of large numbers gives the limit.
The probabilistic reading also suggests the correct answer, because it says what a typical term does.

## Notation

We write $\mathbb{P}$ for a probability and $\mathbb{E}$ for an expected value.
For $x \in [0, 1]$ and $n \ge 1$, the random variable $S_n$ has the [[binomial distribution|0WCWA1KT3M]] with parameters $n$ and $x$.
It is the number of successes in $n$ independent [[Bernoulli trials|0PHC9FE2V5]] with success probability $x$.
For a function $f$ on $[0, 1]$, the $n$-th [[Bernstein polynomial|0JT92VK307]] of $f$ is
$$B_n f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right) \binom{n}{k} x^k (1 - x)^{n-k} = \mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\frac{S_n}{n}\right)\right].$$

## Remarks

### Examples

- The [[Weierstrass approximation theorem|0JQE130DHW]]: every continuous function on $[0, 1]$ is a uniform limit of polynomials. Bernstein's proof uses the binomial distribution and Chebyshev's inequality, as the section below shows.
- Almost every real number is [[normal|C98T2T5FJS]]. Borel's proof reads the digits of a random number as independent trials and uses the strong law of large numbers, as the section below shows.
- The [[Hardy-Ramanujan theorem|ZJNW10PZKK]]: almost every integer $n$ has about $\ln \ln n$ distinct prime factors. Turán's proof computes the mean and the [[variance|0477R9PYAE]] of the number of prime factors over $n \le N$, and then uses Chebyshev's inequality.
- The [[Erdős-Kac theorem|BY5WRND0J1]] refines this: the number of distinct prime factors of $n$ is approximately normally distributed, with mean and variance $\ln \ln n$.
- The [[Dirichlet problem|8D56GQ4AG8]] for the Laplace equation: [[Kakutani's solution|CCEKM4241P]] gives the value at a point as the expected boundary value at the exit point of a [[Brownian motion|22735FGJV6]] started at that point.
- Lower bounds for [[Ramsey numbers|0K9QVPJYJF]]: a random colouring has no large monochromatic clique with positive probability, as the [[probabilistic lower bound|SAZNT80CG5]] shows.

### Bernstein's proof of the Weierstrass theorem

Let $f \colon [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous, let $M = \max \abs{f}$, and let $\varepsilon > 0$.

1. By [[uniform continuity|0M4JJT780X]], there is $\delta > 0$ with $\abs{f(y) - f(x)} < \varepsilon$ when $\abs{y - x} < \delta$.
2. Fix $x \in [0, 1]$. By the formula in the Notation, $B_n f(x) - f(x) = \mathbb{E}[f(S_n/n) - f(x)]$.
3. On the event $\abs{S_n/n - x} < \delta$, the difference $\abs{f(S_n/n) - f(x)}$ is less than $\varepsilon$, by step 1.
4. On the other event, the difference is at most $2M$.
5. So $\abs{B_n f(x) - f(x)} \le \varepsilon + 2M \cdot \mathbb{P}(\abs{S_n/n - x} \ge \delta)$.
6. The mean of $S_n/n$ is $x$, and its variance is $x(1 - x)/n \le 1/(4n)$.
7. By Chebyshev's inequality, $\mathbb{P}(\abs{S_n/n - x} \ge \delta) \le 1/(4n\delta^2)$.
8. So $\abs{B_n f(x) - f(x)} \le \varepsilon + M/(2n\delta^2)$ for every $x \in [0, 1]$.
9. For $n \ge M/(2\varepsilon\delta^2)$, the error is at most $2\varepsilon$ at every point. So $B_n f \to f$ [[uniformly|08VZ57PT26]].

Each $B_n f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $n$, so the theorem follows.
The probability in step 7 is a finite sum, and step 7 is the identity $\sum_k (k - nx)^2 \binom{n}{k} x^k (1 - x)^{n-k} = nx(1 - x)$ in disguise.
So the proof can be written with no probability at all, but the probabilistic form shows why each step is natural.

### Borel's theorem on normal numbers

A real number $x \in [0, 1)$ is normal in base $b$ if each block of $L$ digits appears in its base $b$ expansion with limit frequency $b^{-L}$.

1. Choose $x$ at random in $[0, 1)$, with the [[Lebesgue measure|2Z2QVWZ262]] as the probability.
2. The binary digits $d_1, d_2, \ldots$ of $x$ are then independent, and each digit is $0$ or $1$ with probability $1/2$.
3. By the strong law of large numbers, $(d_1 + \cdots + d_n)/n \to 1/2$ with probability $1$.
4. So the set of $x$ where the frequency of the digit $1$ is not $1/2$ is a [[null set|VQQM4Y550T]].
5. The same argument works for each base $b$ and each block $w$ of digits. Split the positions into $L$ classes modulo the length $L$ of $w$, so that the blocks in one class are independent.
6. There are countably many pairs $(b, w)$, and a countable union of null sets is a null set.
7. So the set of numbers that are not normal in every base is a null set, and almost every $x$ is normal in every base.

The proof shows that normal numbers exist, and that almost every number is normal.
It names no normal number.

### Non-examples

- A proof of a theorem about random objects, for example a proof of the law of large numbers, is a proof in probability theory. Its statement already has randomness, so it is not in this class.
- A heuristic that treats the primes as random gives predictions, for example on the gaps between primes. It is not a proof, because the primes are a fixed set and the model is not derived from the definition of a prime.
- A Monte Carlo estimate of an integral is not a proof of its value. A random sample gives a value that is probably close, and a proof needs a bound that holds with certainty.

### Basic properties

- Positive probability gives existence. If a random object has a property with probability greater than $0$, then some object has the property.
- Probability $1$ gives "almost all", and not "all". The rational numbers are not normal, and they form a null set.
- The probability can be removed. A proof on a finite or a measure space can be written as a calculation with sums or integrals, as step 7 of Bernstein's proof shows.
- The proofs often give rates. For $f(x) = x^2$, the error of $B_n f$ is exactly $x(1 - x)/n$, at most $1/(4n)$.
- Measure-theoretic existence proofs are non-constructive, like [[counting arguments|~0ba16916]].

### History

Karl Weierstrass proved his approximation theorem in 1885, and Sergei Bernstein gave the probabilistic proof with his polynomials in 1912.
Émile Borel proved in 1909 that almost every real number is normal, and David Champernowne gave an explicit base $10$ normal number in 1933.
Pál Turán gave his variance proof of the Hardy-Ramanujan theorem in 1934, and Shizuo Kakutani connected Brownian motion with the Dirichlet problem in 1944.
Burgess Davis later gave a proof of [[Picard's little theorem|R2PHM118NG]] with Brownian motion.

### Common errors

- To think that a probabilistic proof is only a heuristic. A proof on a probability space is as rigorous as any proof in analysis.
- To read "with probability $1$" as "for every element". Bernstein's proof gives every point, and Borel's proof gives almost every point only.
- To treat a fixed object as random. The digits of $\sqrt{2}$ are fixed numbers, and the independence of digits holds only for a random $x$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some facts about numbers and curves contain no chance at all.
Mathematicians can still prove them with coin flips in the argument.
For example, flip a coin many times: about half of the flips come up heads.
In the same way, a number chosen at random has about half of its binary digits equal to $1$.
So such numbers exist, even though the statement says nothing about chance.

### L2 human

We compute the Bernstein polynomials of $f(x) = x^2$ and check the error bound.

1. By the Notation, $B_n f(x) = \mathbb{E}[(S_n/n)^2]$.
2. For any random variable, $\mathbb{E}[Y^2] = \operatorname{Var}(Y) + (\mathbb{E}[Y])^2$.
3. With $Y = S_n/n$, the mean is $x$ and the variance is $x(1 - x)/n$. So $B_n f(x) = x^2 + x(1 - x)/n$.
4. For $n = 2$ we check by the sum: $B_2 f(x) = 0 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2x(1 - x) + 1 \cdot x^2 = x^2 + x(1 - x)/2$.
5. The error $B_n f(x) - f(x) = x(1 - x)/n$ has its maximum $1/(4n)$ at $x = 1/2$.

The typical use is the proof of uniform approximation, where the variance bound gives the rate.
A trap is to think that $B_n f$ agrees with $f$ at the points $k/n$.
For example, $B_2 f(1/2) = 1/4 + 1/8 = 3/8$, but $f(1/2) = 1/4$.

### L3 human

Probabilistic proofs fall into a few patterns.
In the first pattern, a deterministic quantity is an expected value, and concentration inequalities control it: Bernstein's proof, Turán's proof and the [[probabilistic method|3B55HNS754]] in combinatorics.
In the second pattern, a set of measure $1$ is nonempty: Borel's theorem and many results on typical elements in analysis.
In the third pattern, a stochastic process solves a differential equation: Brownian motion gives harmonic functions, and the heat equation and the Feynman-Kac formula extend this.

The same ideas run through the later areas.
In analytic number theory, probabilistic number theory studies arithmetic functions as sums of almost independent random variables.
In ergodic theory, Furstenberg's correspondence principle turns statements about sets of integers into statements about measure-preserving systems.
In complexity theory, random choices prove the existence of good objects such as expanders and codes, and the search for explicit constructions is a separate problem.
