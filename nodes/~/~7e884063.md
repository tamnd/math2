## Definition

Let $\sigma$ be a [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]].
An *atomic formula* over $\sigma$ is a string of one of the following two forms:

1. $t_1 = t_2$, where $t_1$ and $t_2$ are [[terms|6D3WA7N2W4]] over $\sigma$;
2. $R(t_1, \dots, t_n)$, where $R$ is a relation symbol of $\sigma$ of arity $n$ and $t_1, \dots, t_n$ are terms over $\sigma$.

In first-order logic without equality, only the second form is atomic.
An atomic formula, or the negation of one, is a *literal*.

## Notation

1. A binary relation symbol is usually written infix: $t_1 < t_2$ for $<(t_1, t_2)$, and $t_1 \in t_2$ for $\in(t_1, t_2)$.
2. A relation symbol $R$ of arity $0$, where a book allows it, gives the atomic formula $R$ with no brackets.
3. Some books also count $\top$ and $\bot$ as atomic formulas.

## Remarks

The atomic formulas are the base of the recursive definition of [[formulas|HYJ10AF6RT]].
The relation symbols and the equality sign occur only in atomic formulas, and each atomic formula has exactly one of them.
Example: in the signature of ordered rings, $x \cdot x < y + 1$ is atomic, of form 2 with $R$ equal to $<$.
Non-example: $(x < y \lor x = y)$ is not atomic, since it has the connective $\lor$.
In Mathlib the atomic formulas are those built by `FirstOrder.Language.BoundedFormula.equal` and `FirstOrder.Language.Relations.boundedFormula`, and `IsAtomic` marks them.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An atomic formula says that two terms are equal, or that a relation holds between some terms.
For example, $x + 1 = y$ and $x < y$ are atomic.
It is the smallest kind of formula: no smaller formula sits inside it.

### L2 human

We check that $P(f(x), c)$ is an atomic formula of the signature $\set{c, f, P}$, with arities $0, 1, 2$.

1. $x$ is a term, and $f(x)$ is a term since $f$ has arity $1$.
2. $c$ is a term, as a constant symbol.
3. $P$ is a relation symbol of arity $2$, and it gets the two terms $f(x)$ and $c$.
4. So $P(f(x), c)$ is atomic, by form 2.

The typical use of the definition is the base case of a recursion on formulas.
A trap: $f(x) = P(x, c)$ is not a formula, since $P(x, c)$ is a formula and not a term.
