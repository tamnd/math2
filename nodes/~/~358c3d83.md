## Example

Let $V = C^\infty(\R)$ be the real vector space of smooth functions $\R \to \R$, and let $D f = f'$.

1. $D$ is linear, because $(af + bg)' = af' + bg'$.
2. Let $\lambda \in \R$ and $f(x) = e^{\lambda x}$. Then $Df = \lambda e^{\lambda x} = \lambda f$, and $f \ne 0$.
3. So every real $\lambda$ is an [[eigenvalue|0ZND7NQ970]] of $D$, with [[eigenvector|0B5H4208Z9]] $e^{\lambda x}$.
4. Suppose $f' = \lambda f$. Let $g(x) = f(x)e^{-\lambda x}$. Then $g' = (f' - \lambda f)e^{-\lambda x} = 0$, so $g$ is a constant $c$.
5. So $f = c e^{\lambda x}$, and the [[eigenspace|0AYEPBQMAW]] $E_\lambda$ is the line spanned by $e^{\lambda x}$.
6. Now restrict $D$ to $P_2$, the polynomials of degree at most $2$, with basis $1, x, x^2$.
7. $D1 = 0$, $Dx = 1$ and $Dx^2 = 2x$. So $[D] = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
8. This matrix is strictly upper triangular. By the [[determinant of a triangular matrix|0K1KKMFHYH]], the [[characteristic polynomial|0BZ81AJNWF]] is $p_D(t) = t^3$.
9. Second method: $D^3 p = p''' = 0$ for every $p \in P_2$, and $D^2 x^2 = 2 \ne 0$. So $D$ is nilpotent on $P_2$, and its only eigenvalue is $0$, with eigenvectors the constants.

## Remarks

- On $P_2$, the eigenvalue $0$ has algebraic multiplicity $3$ and geometric multiplicity $1$.
- On $C^\infty(\R)$ the derivative has eigenvalues, but no characteristic polynomial, because the space is infinite-dimensional.
- Over $\C$, the functions $e^{\lambda x}$ with $\lambda \in \C$ give every complex number as an eigenvalue.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The exponential function $e^{\lambda x}$ has derivative $\lambda e^{\lambda x}$.
So it is an eigenvector of the derivative, with eigenvalue $\lambda$.
This is why exponentials solve linear differential equations.

### L2 human

The typical use is to solve $y' = \lambda y$ and systems of such equations.
On the finite-dimensional space $P_2$, the derivative is nilpotent, and $p_D(t) = t^3$.
A trap is to expect polynomials to give nonzero eigenvalues: differentiation lowers the degree.
