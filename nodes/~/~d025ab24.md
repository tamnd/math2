## Statement

Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$.

1. If $P$ is a [[projection|0RH4E3CAV9]] on $V$, then $V = \operatorname{im} P \oplus \ker P$, where $\operatorname{im} P$ is the [[image|0JB228DCWB]] and $\ker P$ the [[kernel|02YFME3RGW]]. Also, $P v = v$ for every $v \in \operatorname{im} P$, and $I - P$ is a projection with image $\ker P$ and kernel $\operatorname{im} P$.
2. If $V = U \oplus W$ is a [[direct sum|041JTZCK9Q]], then the map $P_{U, W}(u + w) = u$, for $u \in U$ and $w \in W$, is a projection with image $U$ and kernel $W$.
3. The maps $P \mapsto (\operatorname{im} P, \ker P)$ and $(U, W) \mapsto P_{U, W}$ are inverse bijections between the set of projections on $V$ and the set of ordered pairs $(U, W)$ of subspaces with $V = U \oplus W$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $P^2 = P$ is needed in part 1. For $N(x, y) = (y, 0)$, image and kernel are both the $x$-axis, so $V \ne \operatorname{im} N \oplus \ker N$, see [[the nilpotent map|~698f8e2d]].
- The sum must be direct in part 2. If $U + W = V$ but $U \cap W \ne \{0\}$, then a vector has several decompositions, and "the $U$-part" has no meaning.
- $P$ must be linear. The map $x \mapsto |x|$ on $\R$ satisfies $f \circ f = f$, but its image $[0, \infty)$ is not a subspace.

### Consequences

- The [[existence of complements|0P9T8WHNMH]] gives, for every subspace $U$, a projection with image $U$.
- A projection is diagonalizable, with eigenvalues in $\{0, 1\}$, and its matrix in a basis adapted to $\operatorname{im} P \oplus \ker P$ is $\begin{pmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
- In finite dimension, $\operatorname{rank} P + \dim \ker P = \dim V$ for a projection, directly from the decomposition.
- More generally, families $P_1, \dots, P_k$ of projections with $P_i P_j = 0$ for $i \ne j$ and $\sum_i P_i = I$ correspond to decompositions $V = U_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_k$.

### Converse

Part 3 is itself a two-way statement.
The image alone does not determine the projection: the projections with image $U$ correspond to the complements of $U$.

## History

The correspondence between idempotents and direct sum decompositions is classical in ring theory, where it goes back to Peirce's decomposition of 1870 for idempotents in associative algebras.
For operators on Hilbert space, it was used by von Neumann and Riesz in the spectral theory of the 1920s and 1930s.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Choosing a projection is the same as choosing two complementary subspaces.
The projection keeps the part in the first subspace and kills the part in the second.

### L2 human

Take $P(x, y) = (x + y, 0)$ on $\R^2$.

1. $P^2(x, y) = P(x + y, 0) = (x + y, 0) = P(x, y)$, so $P$ is a projection.
2. $\operatorname{im} P$ is the $x$-axis, and $\ker P = \R(1, -1)$.
3. The decomposition of $(x, y)$ is $P(x, y) + (I - P)(x, y) = (x + y, 0) + (-y, y)$.
4. $(I - P)(x, y) = (-y, y)$, a projection with image $\R(1, -1)$ and kernel the $x$-axis.

The trap is to think that $P$ is determined by its image: the projection $(x, y) \mapsto (x, 0)$ has the same image and a different kernel.

### L3 human

In the ring $\operatorname{End}(V)$, the theorem identifies idempotents with direct sum decompositions of $V$.
This works for modules over any ring: a submodule $U$ is a direct summand exactly when it is the image of an idempotent endomorphism.
Mathlib states the bijection between complements of $U$ and projections onto $U$ as `Submodule.isComplEquivProj`.
