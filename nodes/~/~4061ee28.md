## Example

On $\mathbb{R}^2$ let $\ip{x}{y}_w = 2x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$.
We show that it is an inner product, and that it changes which pairs of vectors are [[orthogonal|0TS7VZJBWD]].

1. It is an [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]]: it is linear in $x$, symmetric, and $\ip{x}{x}_w = 2x_1^2 + x_2^2 > 0$ for $x \neq 0$.
2. Let $u = (1, 1)$ and $v = (1, -1)$. The dot product is $u \cdot v = 1 - 1 = 0$, so $u$ and $v$ are orthogonal for the dot product.
3. For the new inner product, $\ip{u}{v}_w = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (-1) = 1 \neq 0$. So $u$ and $v$ are not orthogonal for $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}_w$.
4. Conversely, let $p = (1, -2)$ and $q = (1, 1)$. Then $\ip{p}{q}_w = 2 - 2 = 0$, so $p \perp q$ for $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}_w$.
5. But $p \cdot q = 1 - 2 = -1 \neq 0$, so $p$ and $q$ are not orthogonal for the dot product.

## Remarks

The example shows that "orthogonal" has a meaning only together with a fixed inner product.
The map $(x_1, x_2) \mapsto (\sqrt{2}x_1, x_2)$ carries $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}_w$ to the dot product.
So the new right angles are the old right angles after a stretch of the first axis.
If we change the weight $2$ to a weight $c > 0$, then $(1, 1) \perp (1, -1)$ holds only for $c = 1$, since the value is $c - 1$.
The same vector space with two inner products gives two different inner product spaces.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A right angle is not a property of two arrows alone.
It depends on the rule that measures angles.
With the usual dot product, $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ meet at a right angle.
If we count the first coordinate twice, the rule gives $2 - 1 = 1$, and the right angle is gone.

### L2 human

To decide orthogonality, compute the inner product that the problem uses, not the dot product.

1. Fix the inner product, here $2x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$.
2. For $u = (1, 1)$, solve $\ip{u}{x}_w = 2x_1 + x_2 = 0$. The solutions are the multiples of $(1, -2)$.
3. So in this space, the line orthogonal to $(1, 1)$ is spanned by $(1, -2)$, not by $(1, -1)$.

The typical use is in statistics and physics, where weights or a mass matrix define the relevant inner product.
A trap: a picture drawn with the usual axes shows the dot product geometry, and it can mislead for a weighted inner product.
