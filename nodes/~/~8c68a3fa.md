## Example

Let $P$ be the property "the linear order has at most $4$ elements", on finite [[linear orders|0FRXBVVR03]].
The orders $L_4$ and $L_5$ differ in $P$, and they satisfy the same sentences of rank $2$, by the [[games on linear orders|~992acee7]].
This one pair does not show that $P$ is undefinable, and indeed $P$ is defined by a first-order sentence.

1. The sentence $\neg\exists x_1 \cdots \exists x_5 \bigwedge_{i < j} \neg(x_i = x_j)$ holds in a structure exactly when it has at most $4$ elements, so it defines $P$.
2. Its rank is $5$, and on orders rank $3$ suffices. With the rank $2$ formula $\beta(x)$ for "at least $3$ elements lie below $x$" from [[the counting sentences|~b3bcbe39]], the sentence $\exists x \, (\beta(x) \wedge \exists y \, (x < y))$ has rank $3$ and says "at least $5$ elements", so its negation defines $P$ on orders.
3. The pair $L_4, L_5$ shows only that no sentence of rank at most $2$ defines $P$.
4. The [[method of games|~51a6b3d5]] needs, for every $n$, a pair that differs in $P$ and agrees up to rank $n$. For $P$ such pairs exist for $n \le 2$ only.

## Remarks

The point of the method is the quantifier: for every sentence there is a pair that it cannot separate.
A pair for one $n$ shows that a property needs rank above $n$, which is a lower bound on complexity, not undefinability.
Such lower bounds are useful in their own right; for instance, by the [[Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé theorem|~9f527b73]], any sentence that says "at least $2^n$ elements" on linear orders has rank above $n$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Showing that two structures look alike to short sentences only proves that short sentences cannot express the property.
A longer sentence might still express it.
To prove that no sentence at all works, one needs a pair of look-alike structures for every possible length.

### L2 human

We find the least $n$ at which $L_4$ and $L_5$ differ.

1. For $n = 2$: both have at least $2^2 - 1 = 3$ elements, so they agree at rank $2$.
2. For $n = 3$: $4 < 2^3 - 1 = 7$, so Spoiler wins $G_3$, and some sentence of rank $3$ separates them.
3. So rank $3$ is the first level that sees the difference, and a property true in $L_4$ and false in $L_5$ may well be definable at that level.

A trap is to state "Duplicator wins $G_2$, hence $P$ is not first-order"; the definition of the method asks for all $n$.
