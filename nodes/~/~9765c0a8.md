## Example

Let $\sigma = \set{+, \cdot, -, 0, 1}$ be the signature of [[rings|0B1261B5ED]].
Let $\varphi$ be the string $x \cdot (y + 1) = x \cdot y + x$.
We check that $\varphi$ is an [[atomic formula|~de213704]] and find where it is true.

1. The strings $x \cdot (y + 1)$ and $x \cdot y + x$ are [[terms|6D3WA7N2W4]] of $\sigma$, built from $x$, $y$ and $1$ by $+$ and $\cdot$.
2. The string $\varphi$ is $t_1 = t_2$ for these two terms, so it is atomic, by form 1 of the [[definition|~7e884063]].
3. The variables of $\varphi$ are $x$ and $y$, and both are free. So $\varphi$ is a formula with two free variables, not a sentence.
4. In the ring $\mathbb{Z}$, the distributive law gives $x \cdot (y + 1) = x \cdot y + x \cdot 1 = x \cdot y + x$ for all integers $x$ and $y$.
5. So $\varphi$ holds in $\mathbb{Z}$ under every assignment of values to $x$ and $y$.
6. The atomic formula $x \cdot y = y \cdot x$ holds in $\mathbb{Z}$ under every assignment, but in the ring of $2 \times 2$ real matrices it fails for some pairs.

So equations between terms are atomic, and their truth depends on the structure and on the values of the variables.

## Remarks

The universal closure $\forall x \, \forall y \, \varphi$ is a sentence, and it is true in every ring by the distributive law.
Identities of this kind are the subject of equational logic.
A class of structures defined by universal closures of equations is a variety, such as the class of rings or of groups.
A literal $\neg (x \cdot y = y \cdot x)$ is not atomic, but it is quantifier-free.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An equation between two expressions, such as $x \cdot (y + 1) = x \cdot y + x$, is an atomic formula.
It has no "and", "not" or "for all" in it.
In the integers it is true for every choice of $x$ and $y$.
For $x = 2$ and $y = 5$ both sides are $12$.

### L2 human

We check step 6 with two matrices.

1. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Then $A B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. So $x \cdot y = y \cdot x$ fails with $x = A$ and $y = B$.

The typical use of atomic equations is to state algebraic identities, such as the ring axioms.
A trap: an atomic formula with free variables is neither true nor false until the variables have values.
