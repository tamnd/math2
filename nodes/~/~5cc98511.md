## Example

Let $S$ be the statement "every prime is odd".
We [[negate|~f72ba5a2]] $S$, and we use the negation to decide whether $S$ is true.

1. Write $S$ with its quantifier and its [[conditional|~38816aeb]]: for every integer $n$, if $n$ is [[prime|01826KMG2H]], then $n$ is odd.
2. The [[negation of a universal statement|0P3A06QNDP]] is an existential statement: there is an integer $n$ such that not (if $n$ is prime, then $n$ is odd).
3. The negation of "if $P$, then $Q$" is "$P$ and not $Q$". So the negation is: there is an integer $n$ such that $n$ is prime and $n$ is not odd.
4. In plain words, the negation of $S$ is "there is an even prime".
5. The number $2$ is prime, and $2$ is even. So the negation is true, and $S$ is false.

## Remarks

The number $2$ is a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]] to $S$, and step 3 shows what a counterexample must be: a prime that is not odd.
The search for a counterexample is the search for a witness of the negation.

The phrase "if $n$ is prime, then" turns into "$n$ is prime and" in the negation.
This matches the rule for bounded quantifiers.
Read with the domain "primes", $S$ is "for every prime $p$, $p$ is odd", and its negation is "there is a prime $p$ such that $p$ is not odd".
Both readings give the same negation.

The statement "every prime greater than $2$ is odd" is true.
Its negation, "there is a prime greater than $2$ that is even", is false, because an even number greater than $2$ has the divisor $2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"Every prime is odd" is a claim about all primes.
To show that it is false, we need just one prime that is not odd.
The first prime, $2$, is even.
So the claim is false, and the single number $2$ is enough.

### L2 human

We negate "every multiple of $4$ is a multiple of $8$" and decide its truth value.

1. With quantifier and conditional: for every integer $n$, if $4 \mid n$, then $8 \mid n$.
2. Negate the quantifier: there is an integer $n$ such that not (if $4 \mid n$, then $8 \mid n$).
3. Negate the conditional: there is an integer $n$ with $4 \mid n$ and $8 \nmid n$.
4. The number $n = 4$ has $4 \mid 4$ and $8 \nmid 4$. So the negation is true, and the original is false.

The typical use of this negation is to know what a counterexample must satisfy before the search starts.

A trap: a student writes the negation as "every multiple of $4$ is not a multiple of $8$".
This statement is false, since $8$ is a multiple of both, so it is not the negation.
