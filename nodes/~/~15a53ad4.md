## Example

Let $A = \operatorname{diag}(2, 1, 1)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, $u = (1, 1, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $v = (1, 0, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
Then $B = A + uv^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $A$ is [[diagonal|0Q9PGXD9E4]], so $A^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(1/2, 1, 1)$.
2. $A^{-1}u = (1/2, 1, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $v^{\mathsf{T}}A^{-1} = (1/2, 0, 1)$.
3. $1 + v^{\mathsf{T}}A^{-1}u = 1 + (1 \cdot \tfrac12 + 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0) = 3/2$. This is nonzero, so $B$ is invertible, by the [[Sherman-Morrison formula|~e4e78e97]].
4. $A^{-1}u\, v^{\mathsf{T}}A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}(1/2, 0, 1) = \begin{pmatrix} 1/4 & 0 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
5. Divide by $3/2$, that is, multiply by $2/3$: $\begin{pmatrix} 1/6 & 0 & 1/3 \\ 1/3 & 0 & 2/3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
6. Subtract from $A^{-1}$: $B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/3 & 0 & -1/3 \\ -1/3 & 1 & -2/3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
7. Check by multiplying back, row by row of $B$. Row 1 of $B$ times $B^{-1}$ is $(3 \cdot \tfrac13,\ 0,\ -1 + 1) = (1, 0, 0)$. Row 2 gives $(\tfrac13 - \tfrac13,\ 1,\ -\tfrac13 - \tfrac23 + 1) = (0, 1, 0)$. Row 3 gives $(0, 0, 1)$. So $BB^{-1} = I_3$.

Now update a solution.
Let $b = (4, 3, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$.

8. $y = A^{-1}b = (2, 3, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $z = A^{-1}u = (1/2, 1, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
9. $v^{\mathsf{T}}y = 2 + 1 = 3$, and $1 + v^{\mathsf{T}}z = 3/2$.
10. $x = y - \tfrac{3}{3/2}\, z = y - 2z = (1, 1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
11. Check: $Bx$ is the vector of row sums of $B$, which is $(4, 3, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} = b$.

## Remarks

The update used only products with $A^{-1}$ and one division, and no new elimination.
For a large $n$, this costs about $n^2$ operations, while a new inversion costs about $n^3$.
The [[matrix determinant lemma|0BC3Q6K92H]] gives $\det B = \tfrac32 \det A = \tfrac32 \cdot 2 = 3$; expansion along row 3 gives $\det B = 3 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 1 = 3$ as well.

## Explanations

### L1 human

We change a simple diagonal matrix by adding a rank one matrix.
Instead of inverting the new matrix from scratch, we correct the old inverse.
The correction is one outer product divided by the number $3/2$.

### L2 human

The denominator $1 + v^{\mathsf{T}}A^{-1}u$ is the test for invertibility.
If $v$ were $(-2, 0, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$, the denominator would be $1 - 2 \cdot \tfrac12 = 0$.
Then $B = A + uv^{\mathsf{T}}$ has first column $(2 - 2, 1 - 2, 0)^{\mathsf{T}} = (0, -1, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$, which is a multiple of the second column $(0, 1, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$, so $B$ is singular.
