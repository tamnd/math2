## Example

Let $f(x, y) = x^3 - 3x + y^2$ on $\R^2$.

1. The gradient is $\nabla f = (3x^2 - 3, 2y)$. It is $0$ at the [[critical points|0YTCMFB4HC]] $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0)$.
2. The [[Hessian matrix|0693Y2METT]] is $H(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} 6x & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. At $(1, 0)$: $H = \operatorname{diag}(6, 2)$. The Hessian form $6u^2 + 2v^2$ is positive definite.
4. By [[the second derivative test|0GYDV9FE98]], $(1, 0)$ is a strict local minimum, with $f(1, 0) = 1 - 3 = -2$.
5. At $(-1, 0)$: $H = \operatorname{diag}(-6, 2)$. The Hessian form $-6u^2 + 2v^2$ is indefinite.
6. So $(-1, 0)$ is a saddle point, with $f(-1, 0) = -1 + 3 = 2$.
7. Check of step 6: $f(-1 + t, 0) = 2 - 3t^2 + t^3 < 2$ for small $t \ne 0$, and $f(-1, s) = 2 + s^2 > 2$ for $s \ne 0$.

## Remarks

Near a critical point $c$, Taylor's formula gives $f(c + h) = f(c) + \frac12 h^T H(c) h + o(\norm{h}^2)$.
So the [[type of the quadratic form|01RBSSGMY5]] $h \mapsto h^T H(c) h$ decides the local behaviour when it is definite or indefinite.
When it is only semidefinite, the test gives no answer; the full theory is in the analysis of several variables.
$f$ has no global minimum: $f(x, 0) = x^3 - 3x$ tends to $-\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

At a critical point the function is flat to first order.
The quadratic form of second derivatives tells how the function curves.
It can curve up in every direction, down in every direction, or up in some and down in others.
Here it curves up in every direction at $(1, 0)$ and has a saddle at $(-1, 0)$.

### L2 human

The typical use is in optimization in several variables.
A trap: a local minimum is not a global minimum; this $f$ goes to $-\infty$ along the $x$-axis.
