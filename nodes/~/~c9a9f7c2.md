## Statement

Fix a first-order language with equality.
Read $\neg B$ as $B \to \bot$, and let $A \mapsto A^N$ be the Gödel-Gentzen [[negative translation|QEZR7PZ2DD]].
For a set of formulas $\Gamma$, write $\Gamma^N = \set{B^N : B \in \Gamma}$.
Write $\Gamma \vdash_c A$ when classical [[natural deduction|PAQ4ZX2TX1]] NK derives $A$ with open assumptions in $\Gamma$.
Write $\Gamma \vdash_i A$ for the same with intuitionistic natural deduction NJ, which is NK without the rule RAA.

**Theorem.**

1. For every set of formulas $\Gamma$ and every formula $A$: $\Gamma \vdash_c A$ if and only if $\Gamma^N \vdash_i A^N$.
2. For every formula $A$ of arithmetic: [[Peano arithmetic|0QFT7Z76N5]] proves $A$ if and only if [[Heyting arithmetic|RXHJPVMPJH]] proves $A^N$.
3. PA is [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]] if and only if HA is consistent.

Here PA and HA have the same language and the same axioms; PA uses classical logic and HA uses [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The assumptions must be translated too. Let $\Gamma = \set{\forall x\, (P(x) \vee \neg P(x)) \to Q}$ with a unary predicate $P$ and a sentence $Q$. Then $\Gamma \vdash_c Q$, but $\Gamma \vdash_i Q^N$ fails, as the Kripke model of the [[non-example of a prefixed double negation|~15207926]] with $Q$ false everywhere shows.
- In part 2, HA must prove the translation of every axiom of PA. This holds because the translation of an instance of the induction scheme is again an instance of the scheme. For a classical theory whose axioms have translations that the intuitionistic theory does not prove, part 2 can fail.
- In part 1 the translation must act on every subformula of $A$. Prefixing $\neg \neg$ to the whole formula does not suffice in predicate logic, by the same Kripke model.

### Consequences

- Equiconsistency: $\bot^N = \bot$, so a classical derivation of $\bot$ from $\Gamma$ gives an intuitionistic derivation of $\bot$ from $\Gamma^N$.
- Conservativity for negative formulas: if $A$ has no $\vee$ and no $\exists$, then $\mathsf{HA} \vdash A^N \leftrightarrow A$, so [[PA proves A only if HA proves A|~b2915593]].
- Glivenko's theorem: for a propositional formula $A$, $\vdash_i A^N \leftrightarrow \neg \neg A$, so $\vdash_c A$ if and only if $\vdash_i \neg \neg A$.
- The theorem is the first step of the [[Dialectica interpretation|HZ17ES9PDY]] of PA and of [[Friedman's A-translation|~10a427f4]].

### Converse

Part 1 is an equivalence.
The direction from right to left holds because $A^N$ and $A$ are classically equivalent, and NJ is a part of NK.
A different converse is false: $\Gamma^N \vdash_i A^N$ does not imply $\Gamma \vdash_i A$, since $\vdash_i (P \vee \neg P)^N$ but not $\vdash_i P \vee \neg P$.

### Generalizations

- The Kolmogorov and Kuroda translations satisfy the same theorem, because they are intuitionistically equivalent to $A^N$.
- The theorem holds for arithmetic in all finite types: classical $\mathsf{PA}^\omega$ proves $A$ if and only if intuitionistic $\mathsf{HA}^\omega$ proves $A^N$.
- It holds for a classical theory and its intuitionistic version whenever the intuitionistic version proves the translation of each axiom. Universal axioms qualify when every atomic formula $P$ satisfies $P \vee \neg P$.

## History

Kolmogorov gave a translation of this kind in 1925, and Glivenko proved the propositional case in 1929.
Gödel and Gentzen proved the theorem for arithmetic independently in 1933.
It showed that classical arithmetic is no less safe than intuitionistic arithmetic.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The theorem says that a classical proof can always be rewritten as an intuitionistic proof.
The price is that the conclusion becomes weaker: "there is a number with property $P$" becomes "it is impossible that no number has property $P$".
For example, classical logic proves "every number is even or not even" in one step.
Intuitionistic logic proves the rewritten claim "it is impossible that some number is neither even nor not even".
So any contradiction found by classical reasoning would also appear in intuitionistic reasoning.

### L2 human

We apply part 2 to the sentence $A = \forall x\, \forall y\, (x = y \vee \neg x = y)$.

1. PA proves $A$ by the law of excluded middle.
2. Part 2 gives $\mathsf{HA} \vdash A^N$, where $A^N = \forall x\, \forall y\, \neg (\neg \neg \neg x = y \wedge \neg \neg \neg \neg x = y)$.
3. In this case HA proves $A$ itself as well, by induction on $x$ and $y$.
4. For $B = \forall x\, (\exists y\, T(x, x, y) \vee \neg \exists y\, T(x, x, y))$, with Kleene's predicate $T$, part 2 again gives $\mathsf{HA} \vdash B^N$.
5. HA does not prove $B$ itself, as the [[definition of HA|RXHJPVMPJH]] explains.

The typical use is to prove the consistency of a classical theory from that of an intuitionistic theory.
A trap is to read $\mathsf{HA} \vdash A^N$ as a constructive proof of $A$.

### L3 human

The theorem says that classical first-order logic is the "$\neg \neg$-part" of intuitionistic logic.
Algebraically, the regular elements $a = \neg \neg a$ of a Heyting algebra form a Boolean algebra, and the translation maps formulas to such elements.
In categorical logic it corresponds to the inclusion of the double-negation sheaves into a topos.
In proof theory it is the first step of Gödel's [[Dialectica interpretation|HZ17ES9PDY]] of PA and of Spector's interpretation of analysis by [[bar recursion|~a7df5e71]].
The [[no-counterexample interpretation|~42908449]] of PA can be obtained in the same way, from the Dialectica interpretation of the translation of a prenex formula.
