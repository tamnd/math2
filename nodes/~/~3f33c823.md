## Example

Let $n$ and $k$ be integers with $1 \le k \le n$.
We prove $k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ by algebra, and we check why this is not a [[combinatorial proof|~a16dfc7e]].

1. We use the factorial formula $\binom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$ for the [[binomial coefficient|0GBZM0MT0F]].
2. Then $k\binom{n}{k} = \dfrac{k \cdot n!}{k!(n-k)!} = \dfrac{n!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}$, since $k! = k \cdot (k-1)!$.
3. Also $n\binom{n-1}{k-1} = \dfrac{n \cdot (n-1)!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!} = \dfrac{n!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}$, since $(n-1) - (k-1) = n - k$ and $n \cdot (n-1)! = n!$.
4. The results of steps 2 and 3 are equal, so the identity holds. The proof is correct.
5. The proof never names a set. No step says what $k\binom{n}{k}$ or $n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ counts.
6. So the proof is a valid algebraic proof, and it is not a combinatorial proof: the condition that both sides count one set, or two sets in bijection, is not met.

## Remarks

The proof and the [[combinatorial proof with a chair|~9956f43c]] prove the same identity.
The algebraic proof is shorter to check, line by line.
The combinatorial proof explains the identity: the left side chooses a committee and then its chair, and the right side chooses the chair first.

The algebraic proof also depends on the factorial formula, which itself needs a proof.
The combinatorial proof uses only the definition of $\binom{n}{k}$ as a number of subsets.
Neither kind of proof is better in general; they answer different questions.

## Explanations

### L1 human

We can prove the identity by writing each binomial coefficient as a fraction of factorials and cancelling.
Both sides turn into the same fraction, so they are equal.
This is a correct proof, but it does not say what the numbers count.
A combinatorial proof would name a set of objects and count it in two ways.
So this proof is a proof by algebra, not a combinatorial proof.

### L2 human

We sort four proofs of identities about binomial coefficients into combinatorial and algebraic.

1. Counting the pairs (committee, chair) in two orders: combinatorial, since it names a set and counts it twice.
2. Cancelling factorials, as in this example: algebraic, since no set appears.
3. Proving $\sum_k \binom{n}{k} = 2^n$ by putting $x = y = 1$ in the binomial theorem: algebraic, since it substitutes numbers in a formula.
4. Proving $\sum_k \binom{n}{k} = 2^n$ by counting the subsets of $[n]$ by size and by "in or out" for each element: combinatorial.

The typical use of the distinction is the reading of a problem that asks for a combinatorial proof: an algebraic proof, even a correct one, does not answer it.
A trap is to call a proof combinatorial because it is about binomial coefficients; what matters is whether it counts a set.
