## Statement

Let $G$ be a [[group|G5S1M9]] with identity element $e$.
Assume that $g^2 = e$ for each $g \in G$.
Then $G$ is [[abelian|082GHNPJ53]]: $ab = ba$ for all $a, b \in G$.

## Remarks

The hypothesis says that the [[exponent|0ZDW0H0VX4]] of $G$ is $1$ or $2$.
The exponent is $1$ only for the trivial group, which is abelian, so the statement is the same as: a group of exponent $2$ is abelian.
The [[proof|~f712ca53]] uses only the rule for the inverse of a product.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The condition $g^2 = e$ must hold for each element, not only for some elements.
In the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$, each [[transposition|0FWVRHBRWM]] $t$ has $t^2 = e$, but [[$S_3$ is not abelian|QSMVQ4EJ3D]].
The cycle $(1\,2\,3)$ of $S_3$ has $(1\,2\,3)^2 = (1\,3\,2) \neq e$, so the hypothesis fails for $S_3$.
The exponent $2$ cannot be replaced by $3$.
Let $H$ be the group of $3 \times 3$ upper unitriangular matrices with entries in $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, a version of the [[Heisenberg group|ZZYCV5EX9J]].
It has $3^3 = 27$ elements.
Each element is $I + N$ with $N^3 = 0$, so $(I + N)^3 = I + 3N + 3N^2 + N^3 = I$.
Thus $g^3 = e$ for each $g \in H$.
With $A = I + E_{12}$ and $B = I + E_{23}$, where $E_{ij}$ has the entry $1$ at $(i, j)$ and $0$ elsewhere, we get $AB = I + E_{12} + E_{23} + E_{13}$ and $BA = I + E_{12} + E_{23}$.
So $AB \neq BA$, and $H$ is not abelian.

### Consequences

Write $G$ additively, with identity $0$.
Then $2g = 0$ for each $g$, and $G$ is a vector space over the field $\mathbb{F}_2 = \{0, 1\}$, with $0 \cdot g = 0$ and $1 \cdot g = g$.
So a finite group with $g^2 = e$ for each $g$ has $2^n$ elements for some $n \in \mathbb{N}$, and it is [[isomorphic|08MG7H8JW5]] to a product of $n$ copies of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
Such a group is an [[elementary abelian group|DZSZ8ZS554]].
The [[Klein four-group|DX6X1D21FE]] is the case $n = 2$.
The statement also gives the case of order $4$ in the result that [[each group of order at most 5 is abelian|~86185f14]].

### Converse

The converse is false.
The group $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian, but its element $1$ satisfies $1 + 1 = 2 \neq 0$.

### Generalizations

If $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$ for all $a, b \in G$, then $G$ is abelian.
Indeed $abab = aabb$, and [[cancellation|0VSNX4P2Q0]] of $a$ on the left and of $b$ on the right gives $ba = ab$.
When $g^2 = e$ for each $g$, both sides of $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$ are $e$, so the statement is a special case.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose that in a group, doing any step two times always brings us back to the start.
The statement says that then the order of two steps never matters.
For example, flip a card over from left to right, or from top to bottom.
Each flip done two times does nothing.
Doing the two flips in either order gives the same result: the card is turned by a half turn.

### L2 human

We check the statement on the [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]] $V = \{e, a, b, c\}$, where each element is its own inverse and $ab = c$.

1. From $a^2 = e$, the inverse of $a$ is $a$. In the same way, $b^{-1} = b$ and $c^{-1} = c$.
2. The inverse of $ab = c$ is $c$.
3. The inverse of a product is $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1} a^{-1} = ba$.
4. By steps 2 and 3, $ba = c = ab$.

The typical use is to show that a group is abelian when its multiplication table has only $e$ on the diagonal.
A trap is to apply the statement when only some elements have order $2$, as for $S_3$.

### L3 human

The groups with $g^2 = e$ for each $g$ are exactly the vector spaces over $\mathbb{F}_2$, so their theory is linear algebra over $\mathbb{F}_2$.
For example, the power set of a set with the symmetric difference is such a group.
For an odd prime $p$, the condition $g^p = e$ does not force commutativity, as the Heisenberg group over $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ shows.
The [[Burnside problem|XWEMJ7T2XV]] asks whether a finitely generated group of exponent $n$ must be finite.
For $n = 2$ the answer is yes by this statement, because such a group is a finite dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{F}_2$.
