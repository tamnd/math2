## Statement

Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ complex matrix of rank $r$, and let $A = U \Sigma V^*$ be a [[singular value decomposition|000NPTMX6J]] with nonzero singular values $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r$.
Let $\Sigma^+$ be the $n \times m$ matrix with $(\Sigma^+)_{ii} = 1/\sigma_i$ for $i \le r$ and all other entries $0$.

1. Existence. The matrix $X = V \Sigma^+ U^*$ satisfies the four Penrose conditions of the [[Moore-Penrose inverse|0PSRCMCM5X]].
2. Uniqueness. At most one matrix satisfies the four conditions. So $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^*$, and this matrix does not depend on the chosen SVD.
3. Projections. $A A^+ = \sum_{i=1}^r u_i u_i^*$ is the [[orthogonal projection|0Q8HZTJC7G]] onto the [[column space|03AJKXH1AV]] of $A$. $A^+ A = \sum_{i=1}^r v_i v_i^*$ is the orthogonal projection onto the column space of $A^*$.
4. Least squares. Let $b \in \C^m$. The vector $x^+ = A^+ b$ minimizes $\norm{Ax - b}$ over $x \in \C^n$. Among all minimizers, it is the only one of smallest norm.
5. If $A$ is real, then $A^+$ is real.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- There is no hypothesis on $A$. Every matrix, also a rectangular or singular one, has exactly one pseudoinverse.
- All four conditions are needed in part 2. [[For $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, 0)$|~157686fd]], the matrix $I$ satisfies conditions 1, 3 and 4, and $I \ne A^+ = A$.
- The norms in part 4 are the standard norms. With a weighted norm $\norm{x}_W^2 = x^* W x$ the least-norm solution changes, and it is given by a weighted pseudoinverse.

### Consequences

- If the columns of $A$ are independent, then $A^+ = (A^* A)^{-1} A^*$. This is [[the example with a $3 \times 2$ matrix|~ea697397]].
- If the rows of $A$ are independent, then $A^+ = A^* (A A^*)^{-1}$, and $A^+ b$ is the [[minimum-norm solution|QM2Y84VDX8]] of $Ax = b$.
- For real $A$, part 4 contains the [[least-squares solution from a reduced SVD|SBP2PCHH9M]].
- [[The rank-one example|~ea4778b5]] computes $A^+$ for a matrix with dependent columns, where the normal equations have many solutions.

### Converse

The least-squares property determines $A^+$.
If an $n \times m$ matrix $X$ has $X b = x^+$ for every $b \in \C^m$, then $X = A^+$.
The reason is that the columns $X e_j = A^+ e_j$ agree for the standard basis vectors $e_j$.
So part 4 can serve as a second definition of $A^+$.

### Generalizations

For a bounded operator between Hilbert spaces, the four conditions have a bounded solution exactly when the range is closed.
This belongs to functional analysis.
Weighted inner products on $\C^m$ and $\C^n$ give weighted pseudoinverses with the same proof.

## History

The Wikipedia article dates the description of the inverse by Moore to 1920, by Bjerhammar to 1951, and by Penrose to 1955.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every matrix has exactly one pseudoinverse.
To find it, write the SVD, invert the nonzero singular values, and swap $U$ and $V$.
The pseudoinverse gives the best solution of $Ax = b$: closest to $b$, and then shortest.

### L2 human

The recipe is $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^*$, and in the reduced form $A^+ = V_r \Sigma_r^{-1} U_r^*$.
In the coordinates $y = V^* x$ and $c = U^* b$, the problem $\min \norm{Ax - b}$ splits into $r$ scalar problems $\sigma_i y_i \approx c_i$.
The remaining coordinates $y_{r+1}, \dots, y_n$ do not change $Ax$, so the shortest choice sets them to zero.
A trap: inverting all diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ is wrong when $r < \min(m, n)$, because $1/0$ is not defined.

### L3 human

Part 3 says that $A^+$ is the inverse of $A$ as a map from the column space of $A^*$ to the column space of $A$, extended by zero.
The uniqueness proof in part 2 uses only the four equations and the rule $(XY)^* = Y^* X^*$.
So it works in any ring with an involution, and the four conditions define the Moore-Penrose inverse in such rings.
The map $A \mapsto A^+$ is continuous on the matrices of a fixed rank, but not across a change of rank.
