## Example

Problem: prove that the two diagonals of each parallelogram $ABCD$ meet at the midpoint of each of them.
We solve it by [[drawing a figure and choosing coordinates|~d39dfb22]].

1. Figure. We draw a parallelogram $ABCD$ in general position: no right angles and no equal sides. The vertices go around in the order $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, so $AB$ is parallel to $DC$ and $AD$ is parallel to $BC$.
2. Choice of coordinates. Translations and rotations keep lengths, midpoints and parallel lines. So we may put $A$ at the origin and $B$ on the positive $x$-axis: $A = (0, 0)$ and $B = (a, 0)$ with $a > 0$.
3. Names for the free data. The vertex $D$ can be anywhere off the $x$-axis, so we write $D = (b, c)$ with $c \neq 0$.
4. The fourth vertex. In a parallelogram $\overrightarrow{DC} = \overrightarrow{AB} = (a, 0)$, so $C = D + (a, 0) = (a + b, c)$.
5. Midpoint of $AC$. The [[midpoint|0S706JBZBG]] of $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is $\left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$. For $A = (0, 0)$ and $C = (a + b, c)$ it is $\left(\frac{a + b}{2}, \frac{c}{2}\right)$.
6. Midpoint of $BD$. For $B = (a, 0)$ and $D = (b, c)$ it is $\left(\frac{a + b}{2}, \frac{c}{2}\right)$.
7. Conclusion. The two midpoints are the same point $M$. The point $M$ lies on both diagonals, and two distinct lines meet in at most one point, so the diagonals meet at $M$, the midpoint of each.

The diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ are distinct lines, because $A$, $B$, $D$ are not on one line ($c \neq 0$).

## Remarks

The example shows the main gain of coordinates: the general parallelogram has only the three parameters $a$, $b$ and $c$.
With the arbitrary placement $A = (p, q)$, $B = (r, s)$, $D = (t, u)$, the calculation is the same, but with six letters instead of three.
The choice in step 2 is allowed because the statement does not change under translations and rotations.
For a rectangle, $b = 0$; for a rhombus, $b^2 + c^2 = a^2$.
The same coordinates show that the diagonals have equal length exactly when $(a + b)^2 + c^2 = (b - a)^2 + c^2$, that is, when $ab = 0$, so exactly for rectangles.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Put a parallelogram on squared paper with one corner at the origin and one side along the bottom line.
Then three numbers describe it: the length $a$ of the bottom side, and the position $(b, c)$ of the corner above the origin.
The middle of each diagonal is found by averaging the coordinates of its two ends.
Both diagonals give the same middle point, so they cross there.
For $a = 4$, $b = 1$, $c = 2$, both middles are at $(2.5, 1)$.

### L2 human

We use the same coordinates to compute the lengths of the diagonals.

1. $|AC|^2 = (a + b)^2 + c^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 + c^2$.
2. $|BD|^2 = (b - a)^2 + c^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2 + c^2$.
3. Adding: $|AC|^2 + |BD|^2 = 2a^2 + 2(b^2 + c^2) = 2|AB|^2 + 2|AD|^2$. This is the [[parallelogram law|026HPGYGS4]].
4. Subtracting: $|AC|^2 - |BD|^2 = 4ab$, which is $0$ exactly when $b = 0$, that is, when the angle at $A$ is a right angle.

The typical use: one choice of coordinates answers several questions about the same figure.

A trap: the placement $D = (0, c)$ looks simpler, but it makes every parallelogram a rectangle, so the results would hold only for rectangles.
