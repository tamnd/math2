## Statement

Consider the propositional [[relevance logics|~366e4c45]] $\mathbf{R}$, $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{T}$, with the connectives $\rightarrow$, $\wedge$, $\vee$ and $\neg$.

1. (Urquhart) For each of $\mathbf{R}$, $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{T}$, the set of theorems is not decidable.
2. The set of theorems of $\mathbf{R}$ is [[computably enumerable|MWSD85BB4X]], and the set of formulas that are not theorems of $\mathbf{R}$ is not computably enumerable.
3. $\mathbf{R}$ does not have the [[finite model property|3NT8RCA6HE]] for [[Routley-Meyer frames|~212d1096]]: some formula that is not a theorem of $\mathbf{R}$ holds on every finite Routley-Meyer frame on which all theorems of $\mathbf{R}$ hold.

Part 1 is cited from Urquhart (1984).
Parts 2 and 3 are proved in the [[proof node|~43654817]] from part 1.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The full language matters: the abstract of Urquhart's paper lists decidable fragments.

1. The first-degree fragment, with no nested $\rightarrow$, is decidable; the abstract attributes this to Anderson and Belnap.
2. The pure implication fragments of $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{E}$ are decidable; the abstract attributes this to Kripke, and states that his methods extend to implication with conjunction.
3. The implication-negation fragments are decidable; the abstract attributes this to Belnap and Wallace.
4. The SEP entry on substructural logics states that the fragment of $\mathbf{R}$ without disjunction is decidable by proof search in a Gentzen system.

Changing the logic also matters: the abstract states that R-mingle is decidable (Meyer), and that positive $\mathbf{R}$ without contraction is decidable (Giambrone).

### Consequences

The SEP entry on substructural logics states that undecidability comes partly from the proof theory of $\mathbf{R}$, which must handle distribution, $p \wedge (q \vee r) \vdash (p \wedge q) \vee (p \wedge r)$.
It states that the natural proof of distribution uses weakening and contraction.
Part 3 shows that no search for finite countermodels can replace proof search for $\mathbf{R}$.

### The method of proof

The abstract states that the proof uses von Neumann's coordinatization theorem for [[modular lattices|QX7WZ92QAK]].
This node does not reproduce the coding, and it does not state its details.

### Converse

A converse of part 3 holds: a logic with a finite Hilbert system and the finite model property for a decidable class of finite frames is decidable.
This is the argument of [[Harrop's theorem|VSYQ1AZ9G7]] for modal logics, and the proof of part 3 uses it.

### Generalizations

The SEP entry on relevance logic states that many logics weaker than $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{E}$ are decidable, so undecidability is not a property of all relevance logics.

## History

The undecidability theorem is in Urquhart's paper of 1984, cited in this node.
The decidable fragments above are those that the abstract of that paper lists, with the attributions it gives.

## Explanations

### L1 human

There is no computer program that takes a formula and always answers correctly whether $\mathbf{R}$ proves it.
A program can list all theorems one by one, but it cannot list all non-theorems.
So small countermodels cannot always show that a formula is not a theorem.

### L2 human

We see how a program lists the theorems of $\mathbf{R}$, which is part 2.

1. List all finite sequences of formulas, one after another, by a fixed computable enumeration.
2. Check that each line of the sequence is an axiom instance, or follows from earlier lines by modus ponens or adjunction.
3. Each check is a finite comparison of formulas, so a program can do it.
4. When a sequence passes the check, output its last line.
5. Every theorem has a derivation, so it is output at some stage.

The typical use of the theorem is to know that a complete decision procedure for $\mathbf{R}$ cannot exist, so tools must use proof search or semi-decision.
A trap: undecidability of $\mathbf{R}$ does not mean that $\mathbf{R}$ has no complete axiomatization; its theorems are computably enumerable.
