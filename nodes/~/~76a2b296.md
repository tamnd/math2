## Example

Problem: find the [[greatest common divisor|02WF878BH1]] of $1071$ and $462$.
The [[Euclidean algorithm|0TRNT4X7J0]] solves it by repeated division with remainder.

1. $1071 = 2 \cdot 462 + 147$, so $\gcd(1071, 462) = \gcd(462, 147)$.
2. $462 = 3 \cdot 147 + 21$, so $\gcd(462, 147) = \gcd(147, 21)$.
3. $147 = 7 \cdot 21 + 0$, so $\gcd(147, 21) = \gcd(21, 0) = 21$.

So $\gcd(1071, 462) = 21$.

We check the conditions that separate an algorithm from a [[heuristic|~fd7a5df5]].

1. Each step is fixed by a rule. The solver makes no choice: the next pair is $(b, a \bmod b)$.
2. The procedure stops. The second number decreases strictly, $462 > 147 > 21 > 0$, and a strictly decreasing sequence of natural numbers is finite.
3. The answer is correct. Each step keeps the set of common divisors, since a common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides $a - qb$, and a common divisor of $b$ and $a - qb$ divides $a$.
4. The guarantee holds for every input pair of natural numbers, not only for this one.

A heuristic fails all three tests: it leaves choices to the solver, it may not end, and it may not give an answer.
So the Euclidean algorithm is not a heuristic.

## Remarks

The non-example marks the border of the concept.
A heuristic such as "look at small cases" may or may not lead to an answer, and two solvers may use it in different ways.
The Euclidean algorithm gives the same answer in the same number of steps to every user.

The border can move.
When a class of problems gets an algorithm, the heuristics for it become unnecessary for that class.
For example, the problem "is $n$ prime?" has the algorithm "try all divisors up to $\sqrt{n}$", and heuristics are no longer needed to decide it, only to decide it fast.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A recipe that always works is not a heuristic.
To find the largest number that divides both $1071$ and $462$, we divide, keep the remainder, and repeat.
After three divisions, the remainder is $0$, and the last divisor, $21$, is the answer.
The method works every time, for every pair of numbers.
A heuristic is different: it is a hint that often helps, but it can fail.

### L2 human

We compare the algorithm with a heuristic for the same problem.

1. Heuristic: "guess a common factor from the last digits". The numbers $1071$ and $462$ are not both even, and neither ends in $0$ or $5$.
2. Try $3$: the digit sums are $9$ and $12$, so $3$ divides both. Try $7$: $1071 = 7 \cdot 153$ and $462 = 7 \cdot 66$.
3. The guesses find the factor $21$, but they do not show that no larger common factor exists.
4. The Euclidean algorithm settles this in three divisions, with a proof of correctness for all inputs.

The typical use of the distinction: when an algorithm exists, use it; heuristics are for problems without one.

A trap: a computer program is not always an algorithm in this sense, because many programs for hard problems, such as large searches, are heuristics that can stop without an answer.
