## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix over $F$, and let $r = \rank A$.
The four fundamental subspaces of $A$ are the following:

- the [[column space|03AJKXH1AV]] $\operatorname{Col}(A) \subseteq F^m$;
- the [[null space|0PHDYNR24D]] $\operatorname{Null}(A) \subseteq F^n$;
- the [[row space|069MCGX635]] $\operatorname{Row}(A) \subseteq F^n$;
- the left null space $\operatorname{Null}(A^\top) \subseteq F^m$.

Then:

1. Their dimensions are $r$, $n - r$, $r$ and $m - r$, in this order.
2. For $F = \R$ with the dot product, $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \operatorname{Row}(A)^\perp$ and $\operatorname{Null}(A^\top) = \operatorname{Col}(A)^\perp$.
3. For $F = \R$, $\R^n = \operatorname{Row}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A)$ and $\R^m = \operatorname{Col}(A) \oplus \operatorname{Null}(A^\top)$.
4. For $F = \R$, the map $x \mapsto Ax$ restricts to a linear isomorphism from $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Parts 2 to 4 need the real dot product, or another positive definite form.
  Over $\C$ with the bilinear product $x^\top y$, the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \end{pmatrix}$ has row $(1, i)$ in its own null space, since $1 + i^2 = 0$.
- Over the field $\mathbb{F}_2$, the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has row $(1, 1)$ in its null space, since $1 + 1 = 0$.
  So $\operatorname{Row}(A) \cap \operatorname{Null}(A) \ne \{0\}$, and part 3 fails.
- Over $\C$, parts 2 to 4 hold with the conjugate transpose $A^*$ in place of $A^\top$, and with the Hermitian inner product.

### Consequences

- The [[rank-nullity theorem for matrices|TR48YQGCCA]]: $r + (n - r) = n$.
- [[Row rank equals column rank|0TZAG60ZYT]] appears in part 1 as the equal dimensions of $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Col}(A)$.
- The [[Fredholm alternative|4HH8C9V8JQ]]: $Ax = b$ is solvable if and only if $b$ is orthogonal to $\operatorname{Null}(A^\top)$.
- For a real matrix, the [[orthogonal complement of the column space|J4SZVFBHS5]] is the left null space.

### Converse

The four dimensions determine $r$, $m$ and $n$, but they do not determine $A$ up to more than [[equivalence|0CMHSGTWXB]].
Any two $m \times n$ matrices of rank $r$ have four subspaces of the same dimensions.

### Generalizations

For a linear map $T \colon V \to W$, the four spaces become $\im T$, $\ker T$, and the image and kernel of the dual map $T^*$.
The orthogonality in part 2 then becomes the statement that $\ker T^*$ is the annihilator of $\im T$.
That form belongs to the [[dual map|0AZ5ARP287]] and holds over every field.

## History

Strang named the four subspaces and drew the picture of part 4 in his textbooks.
He called the whole result the fundamental theorem of linear algebra.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every matrix has four subspaces attached to it.
Two live in the input space $F^n$: the row space and the null space.
Two live in the output space $F^m$: the column space and the left null space.
Their dimensions are fixed by the rank $r$ and the size of the matrix.

### L2 human

In the input space $\R^n$, every vector splits into a row space part and a null space part.
The matrix kills the null space part and sends the row space part to the column space.
This map from the row space to the column space is one-to-one and onto.
In the output space $\R^m$, the column space and the left null space are orthogonal and fill the whole space.

### L3 human

The four subspaces are the images and kernels of $A$ and of $A^\top$.
Over any field, $\operatorname{Null}(A^\top)$ is the annihilator of $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ under the standard pairing of $F^m$ with itself.
Over $\R$ this pairing is positive definite, so the annihilator is a complement.
That is the only place where the real numbers enter.
