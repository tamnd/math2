## Example

Let $G$ be the set of the eight real $2 \times 2$ matrices
$$\begin{pmatrix} \pm 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \pm 1 \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \pm 1 \\ \pm 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
with all choices of signs.
We check that $G$ is a [[subgroup|0N0PXCTYA7]] of the [[orthogonal group|~dc22fe40]] $O(2)$, and that it is the symmetry group of a square.

1. Each matrix in $G$ has columns $\pm e_1$ and $\pm e_2$ in some order. These columns are orthonormal, so each matrix is [[orthogonal|0QRBVFJ50X]].
2. Each matrix in $G$ is a [[permutation matrix|0Q5SM60HTH]] times a diagonal matrix with entries $\pm 1$. A product of two such matrices again sends each $\pm e_j$ to some $\pm e_k$, so it lies in $G$.
3. $I_2 \in G$, and the inverse $Q^{-1} = Q^T$ of each $Q \in G$ is in $G$, because the transpose has the same form.
4. So $G$ is a subgroup of $O(2)$ of order $8$.
5. The four matrices with determinant $1$ are $I$, $-I$, $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. These are the rotations by $0$, $\pi$, $\pi/2$ and $-\pi/2$.
6. The four matrices with determinant $-1$ are $\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$, $\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1)$, $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. These are the reflections in the lines $y = 0$, $x = 0$, $y = x$ and $y = -x$.
7. Each matrix of $G$ permutes the four points $(\pm 1, \pm 1)$, the vertices of a square. The square has exactly $8$ symmetries that fix its centre, so $G$ is its whole symmetry group, the [[dihedral group|09GK3DE0WV]] of order $8$.

## Remarks

- The example shows a finite subgroup of $O(2)$ with both rotations and reflections.
- Its rotations form the cyclic subgroup of order $4$, which is $G \cap SO(2)$, of index $2$ in $G$.
- For size $n$, the signed permutation matrices form a subgroup of $O(n)$ of order $2^n n!$. For $n = 3$, this is the symmetry group of the cube, of order $48$.
- With $\pm 1$ replaced by any other numbers, the matrices are no longer orthogonal, and the set is no longer a group of isometries.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Put a square with its centre at the origin and its sides parallel to the axes.
There are eight ways to move the square onto itself.
Four ways are turns: by $0$, by a quarter turn, by a half turn and by three quarter turns.
Four ways are mirror images: in the two axes and in the two diagonals.
Each way is one of the eight matrices.

### L2 human

Worked check of one product:

1. Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the quarter turn, and $B = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$, the reflection in the $x$-axis.
2. $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the reflection in the line $y = x$.
3. $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the reflection in the line $y = -x$.
4. Both products are in $G$, but $AB \neq BA$.

The typical use: finite symmetry groups of figures are subgroups of $O(n)$.
A trap: the group is not commutative, so the order of the moves matters.
