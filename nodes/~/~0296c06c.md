## Statement

All [[ordinals|0YSCSX5YZC]] and operations below are those of [[ordinal arithmetic|KAEDCQ8RM6]].
Define the tower $\tau_0 = 1$ and $\tau_{n+1} = \omega^{\tau_n}$ for $n \in \N$, so that $\tau_1 = \omega$, $\tau_2 = \omega^\omega$ and $\tau_3 = \omega^{\omega^\omega}$.
Let $\varepsilon_0 = \sup_{n \in \N} \tau_n$.

**Theorem.**

1. $\omega^{\varepsilon_0} = \varepsilon_0$.
2. If $\beta$ is an ordinal with $\omega^\beta = \beta$, then $\varepsilon_0 \le \beta$.
3. For every ordinal $\alpha < \varepsilon_0$, $\alpha < \omega^\alpha$.
4. If $\alpha < \varepsilon_0$ and $\beta < \varepsilon_0$, then $\alpha + \beta < \varepsilon_0$ and $\omega^\alpha < \varepsilon_0$.
5. $\varepsilon_0$ is a countable limit ordinal.

So $\varepsilon_0$ is the least fixed point of the map $\alpha \mapsto \omega^\alpha$, that is, the least [[epsilon number|PQSHBKCE7V]].
A full proof is in [[the proof by the tower of powers|~19397ad9]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The base $\omega$: for the base $2$, the map $\alpha \mapsto 2^\alpha$ has the fixed point $\omega$, since $2^\omega = \sup_n 2^n = \omega$. So the least fixed point depends on the base, and the theorem is about the base $\omega$.
- The continuity of $\alpha \mapsto \omega^\alpha$ at limits: the map $\alpha \mapsto \omega^\alpha + 1$ is strictly increasing but not continuous, and it has no fixed point, because $\omega^\alpha + 1 > \omega^\alpha \ge \alpha$ for all $\alpha$.
- The supremum over all $n$: each $\tau_n$ is strictly below $\omega^{\tau_n} = \tau_{n+1}$, so no finite stage of the tower is a fixed point.

### Consequences

- Every ordinal $\alpha$ with $0 < \alpha < \varepsilon_0$ has a [[Cantor normal form|6GD30FGYDZ]] $\omega^{\beta_1} c_1 + \cdots + \omega^{\beta_k} c_k$ whose exponents satisfy $\beta_i \le \beta_1 < \alpha$, by part 3. Writing the exponents in normal form again ends after finitely many steps, and this gives the notation system of [[epsilon nought|KT50SMH3EC]].
- The set of ordinals below $\varepsilon_0$ is the least set that contains $0$ and is closed under $+$ and $\alpha \mapsto \omega^\alpha$. The set is closed under both maps by part 4, and every ordinal below $\varepsilon_0$ lies in it by the Cantor normal form.

### Converse

Part 2 has a partial converse: every ordinal $\beta \ge \varepsilon_0$ with $\omega^\beta = \beta$ is an epsilon number by definition, and the epsilon numbers form a proper class.
The converse of part 3 fails: $\varepsilon_0 < \omega^{\varepsilon_0}$ is false, since the two are equal.

### Generalizations

- Every [[normal function|ERTAM0SFVB]] $f$ has a least fixed point above any given $\gamma$, namely $\sup_n f^n(\gamma + 1)$, by the same argument. With $f(\alpha) = \omega^\alpha$ and $\gamma = \varepsilon_0$ this gives $\varepsilon_1$.
- The [[Veblen function|JQFAE9S2F4]] lists these fixed points: $\varphi_1(\alpha) = \varepsilon_\alpha$.

## History

Georg Cantor introduced the epsilon numbers, with the letter $\varepsilon$, in his work on ordinal arithmetic in the 1890s.
The role of $\varepsilon_0$ in proof theory comes from Gerhard Gentzen's consistency proof for arithmetic of 1936.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Start with $1$ and keep putting $\omega$ under what you have as an exponent: $1$, $\omega$, $\omega^\omega$, $\omega^{\omega^\omega}$, and so on.
Each step gives a much larger ordinal, but the list never stops.
The ordinal $\varepsilon_0$ is the first ordinal after all the stages of this list.
The theorem says that $\varepsilon_0$ does not change when we raise $\omega$ to it.
Every smaller ordinal does grow: for example, $\omega^2$ is bigger than $2$, and $\omega^\omega$ is bigger than $\omega$.

### L2 human

We find the least fixed point of $f(\alpha) = \omega^\alpha$ above $\varepsilon_0$, that is, $\varepsilon_1$.

1. Start at $\varepsilon_0 + 1$, which is not a fixed point, since $\omega^{\varepsilon_0 + 1} = \varepsilon_0 \cdot \omega > \varepsilon_0 + 1$.
2. Iterate: $\omega^{\varepsilon_0 + 1}$, then $\omega^{\omega^{\varepsilon_0 + 1}}$, and so on.
3. The supremum $\varepsilon_1$ of these stages is a fixed point, by the continuity argument of the proof.
4. Any fixed point $\beta > \varepsilon_0$ is at least $\varepsilon_0 + 1$, and the induction of the proof gives that it lies above every stage, so $\beta \ge \varepsilon_1$.

The typical use of the theorem is to show that a notation system by Cantor normal forms reaches exactly the ordinals below $\varepsilon_0$.
The trap: $\varepsilon_0$ is not the least fixed point of every exponential map; the map $\alpha \mapsto 2^\alpha$ already fixes $\omega$.

### L3 human

The theorem is the first case of the fixed-point lemma for normal functions.
A strictly increasing function that is continuous at limits has a closed unbounded class of fixed points, and the least one is the supremum of the iterates.
Iterating this step, and taking common fixed points at limit stages, is the construction of the [[Veblen function|JQFAE9S2F4]], which names the ordinals up to the [[Feferman-Schütte ordinal|~5f65c1fc]].
In proof theory, $\varepsilon_0$ is the [[proof-theoretic ordinal|C72RBH0CDR]] of Peano arithmetic, and the tower $\tau_n$ is the sequence of ordinals along which Peano arithmetic proves [[transfinite induction|~e065bc19]].
