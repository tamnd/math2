## Summary

Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over a field $K$, and let $\sigma$ be an [[automorphism|DSQ17TNW1C]] of $K$.
A map $T \colon V \to W$ is $\sigma$-semilinear if $T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v)$ and $T(\lambda v) = \sigma(\lambda)\, T(v)$ for all $u, v \in V$ and $\lambda \in K$.
For $\sigma = \id_K$, these are the [[linear maps|0NRYHJPQG2]].
For $K = \C$ and $\sigma$ the [[complex conjugation|0FTH2E9TAS]], they are the antilinear maps, with $T(\lambda v) = \bar\lambda\, T(v)$.

## Motivation

Some natural maps respect sums but twist scalars.
Complex conjugation $z \mapsto \bar z$ on $\C^n$ is the basic case: it sends $iz$ to $-i\bar z$.
In an inner product space over $\C$, the map $v \mapsto \langle \cdot, v \rangle$ to the dual space is antilinear, so antilinear maps appear in Hilbert space theory and quantum mechanics.
In projective geometry, semilinear maps describe all collineations of a projective space of dimension at least $2$.

## Notation

A $\sigma$-semilinear map is also called a semilinear map with companion automorphism $\sigma$.
An antilinear map is also called conjugate-linear.
The invertible semilinear maps $V \to V$, for all automorphisms $\sigma$, form the general semilinear group $\Gamma L(V)$.
More generally, $\sigma$ may be a [[field homomorphism|EZVN87C88J]] $K \to L$ between two fields.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[Complex conjugation on $\C^n$|~f988d0c4]], $(z_1, \dots, z_n) \mapsto (\bar z_1, \dots, \bar z_n)$, is antilinear.
- [[Squaring on the field with four elements|~5706804e]] is semilinear for the Frobenius automorphism $a \mapsto a^2$, and it is not linear.
- Each linear map is semilinear with $\sigma = \id_K$.

### Non-examples

- [[The real part $\C \to \C$|~9ea8a9ef]] is additive, but it is semilinear for no automorphism of $\C$.
- The map $z \mapsto z^2$ on $\C$ is not additive, so it is not semilinear.

### Basic properties

- If $T \ne 0$ is $\sigma$-semilinear, then $\sigma$ is unique: pick $v$ with $T(v) \ne 0$ and compare $T(\lambda v) = \sigma(\lambda) T(v)$.
- The composite of a $\sigma$-semilinear and a $\tau$-semilinear map is $(\sigma\tau)$-semilinear. So the composite of two antilinear maps is linear.
- The kernel and the image of a semilinear map are subspaces. A semilinear bijection sends bases to bases.
- Fix a basis $B$ of $V$. Each semilinear bijection of $V$ is a linear bijection composed with the map that applies $\sigma$ to the coordinates in $B$. So $\Gamma L(V) = GL(V) \rtimes \Aut(K)$.
- The field $\R$ has no automorphism other than the identity, so every semilinear map of real spaces is linear.

### History

Semilinear maps arose in projective geometry.
The [[fundamental theorem of projective geometry|GKV4RVXBRE]] states that the collineations of a projective space of dimension at least $2$ come from semilinear bijections.
The projective semilinear group $P\Gamma L(3, 4)$ is a maximal subgroup of the Mathieu group $M_{24}$.

### Common errors

- Calling an antilinear map linear over $\C$. It is linear over $\R$, but $T(iv) = -i\,T(v)$.
- Forgetting additivity. Semilinearity requires both $T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v)$ and the twisted homogeneity.
- Mixing conventions for [[sesquilinear forms|054YEQ0Y8M]] and inner products. Mathematicians often make $\langle u, v \rangle$ antilinear in the second slot; physicists make it antilinear in the first.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A semilinear map respects sums, and it respects scalars up to a fixed twist of the field.
Over the complex numbers, the twist is usually complex conjugation, and the map is called antilinear.

### L2 human

To test whether $T$ is antilinear, check additivity and $T(\lambda v) = \bar\lambda\, T(v)$.
For $T(z, w) = (\bar w, \bar z)$ on $\C^2$, $T(i(z, w)) = (-i\bar w, -i\bar z) = -i\,T(z, w)$, so $T$ is antilinear.
The trap is to check only real scalars; every additive continuous map passes that test.

### L3 human

A $\sigma$-semilinear map $V \to W$ is the same as a linear map $V \to \sigma^* W$, where $\sigma^* W$ is $W$ with scalars acting through $\sigma$.
Through this, semilinear maps reduce to linear maps, and the theory transfers.
By the fundamental theorem of projective geometry, $P\Gamma L(V)$ is the full collineation group of $\mathbb P(V)$ when $\dim V \ge 3$.
The [[Riesz map|04GYPXDXNH]] $V \to V^*$ of a complex inner product space and the [[adjoint|0R9NMSM1ZP]] $T \mapsto T^*$ are the standard antilinear maps of functional analysis.
