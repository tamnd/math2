## Counterexample

Let $F$ be a field and let $V = F^{(\N)}$ be the [[space of sequences|0GZB2SN0PH]] $x = (x_0, x_1, \dots)$ in $F$ with only finitely many nonzero terms.
Let $e_k$ be the sequence with $1$ at place $k$ and $0$ elsewhere, so $(e_k)_{k \in \N}$ is a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] of $V$.
Let $e_k^* \in V^*$ be the coordinate form $e_k^*(x) = x_k$, so $e_k^*(e_j) = \delta_{kj}$.
We show that the family $(e_k^*)$ is [[linearly independent|0CRMGPAH83]] but does not span the [[dual space|0M856Q1AMZ]] $V^*$.

1. Independence: let $S \subseteq \N$ be finite and $\sum_{k \in S} a_k e_k^* = 0$. Evaluating at $e_j$ with $j \in S$ gives $a_j = 0$.
2. Define $s \colon V \to F$ by $s(x) = \sum_k x_k$. The sum has only finitely many nonzero terms, so $s$ is well defined, and $s$ is linear.
3. We have $s(e_m) = 1$ for every $m \in \N$.
4. Suppose that $s$ lies in the [[span|0DTY8B9HMS]] of the $e_k^*$. Then $s = \sum_{k \in S} a_k e_k^*$ for a finite set $S$ and scalars $a_k$.
5. The set $\N$ is infinite and $S$ is finite, so there is $m \in \N$ with $m \notin S$.
6. Then $\sum_{k \in S} a_k e_k^*(e_m) = 0$, because $e_k^*(e_m) = 0$ for $k \ne m$.
7. By step 3, $s(e_m) = 1 \ne 0$. This contradicts step 4, so $s$ is not in the span.
8. Hence $(e_k^*)$ is not a basis of $V^*$, and the finite-dimensional result on the dual basis fails here.

## Remarks

- The span of the $e_k^*$ is exactly the set of forms $f$ with $f(e_k) = 0$ for all but finitely many $k$. Under the isomorphism $V^* \cong F^{\N}$ of the [[theorem on maps determined by a basis|0R8X9XA83A]], this span is $F^{(\N)}$ inside $F^{\N}$; see [[the dual of F^(N)|~95d934a4]].
- The same argument works for every infinite-dimensional $V$ with a basis $B$: the form that is $1$ on every element of $B$ is not a finite combination of coordinate forms.
- The hypothesis "finite-dimensional" in the theorem on the [[dimension of the dual space|00889YD15K]] is needed exactly because of this example.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Each coordinate form looks at only one place of a sequence.
A finite combination of them looks at only finitely many places.
The form "add up all the terms" looks at every place, so it is not such a combination.

### L2 human

To show that a form is not in a span, find one test vector on which every element of the span vanishes but the form does not.

1. Assume $s = \sum_{k \in S} a_k e_k^*$ with $S$ finite.
2. Take a place $m$ outside $S$, and test on $e_m$.
3. The right side gives $0$ and the left side gives $1$.

The typical use is to explain why the dual of an infinite-dimensional space is "larger" than the space.
A trap: the infinite sum $\sum_k e_k^*$ does make sense pointwise and equals $s$, but a span only allows finite sums.
