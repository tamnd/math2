## Summary

The distance between two sets $S$ and $T$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the least [[Euclidean distance|0NPPGHQNXQ]] $\|s - t\|$ for $s \in S$ and $t \in T$.
For points, [[lines|0399QFDFS8]] and [[planes|07SDJ5DNE4]] this least value exists, and three formulas give it.

- Point $q$ and plane $n \cdot r = d$: $\dfrac{|n \cdot q - d|}{\|n\|}$.
- Point $q$ and line $a + t d$: $\dfrac{\|(q - a) \times d\|}{\|d\|}$.
- Lines $a + t d$ and $b + s e$ with $d \times e \neq 0$: $\dfrac{|(b - a) \cdot (d \times e)|}{\|d \times e\|}$.

The [[point-plane theorem|0K1E2S6Z69]] proves the first formula, and the [[distance theorem|~c9d9dc6b]] proves the other two.
Each formula is a length of a [[projection|0JW13B48YP]] or a [[cross product|0N5N15HWZ7]], and the closest points are where the connecting vector is perpendicular to the given sets.

## Motivation

The distance from a point to a plane is the length of the shortest path to it, and the shortest path goes straight along the normal.
So the distance is the part of $q - a$ along the normal $n$, for any point $a$ of the plane.
For a line, the shortest path is perpendicular to the line, and its length is the height of a parallelogram with the base $d$.
For two skew lines, the shortest segment is perpendicular to both, so it is along $d \times e$.
These three pictures turn into three formulas with dot and cross products.

## Notation

- $\operatorname{dist}(q, P)$, $\operatorname{dist}(q, L)$ and $\operatorname{dist}(L_1, L_2)$ for the three distances.
- Hesse normal form: for a unit normal $\hat n$, $\operatorname{dist}(q, P) = |\hat n \cdot q - p|$ for the plane $\hat n \cdot r = p$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- Point $(1, 2, 3)$ and the plane $6x + 3y + 2z = 6$: $\dfrac{|6 + 6 + 6 - 6|}{7} = \dfrac{12}{7}$. The [[point example|~e3b37a1a]] gives the closest point.
- Point $(1, 1, 1)$ and the line $t(1, 2, 2)$: $(1, 1, 1) \times (1, 2, 2) = (0, -1, 1)$, so the distance is $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{3}$.
- Lines $(1, 0, 0) + t(1, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 2) + s(0, 1, 1)$: $d \times e = (1, -1, 1)$ and $(b - a) \cdot (d \times e) = 1$, so the distance is $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$. The [[skew lines example|~3cb725af]] finds the closest points.
- The origin and the plane $x + y + z = 3$: $\dfrac{3}{\sqrt{3}} = \sqrt{3}$, reached at $(1, 1, 1)$.

### Non-examples

- The distance between two lines is not the distance between their base points. The [[non-example|~480f8a04]] gets $\sqrt{5}$ instead of $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$.
- The number $|n \cdot q - d|$ without the division by $\|n\|$ is not a distance; it changes when the equation is multiplied by $2$.
- The skew lines formula does not apply to parallel lines, where $d \times e = 0$.

### Basic properties

- The distance is $0$ exactly when the sets meet.
- The closest point of the plane to $q$ is $q - \dfrac{n \cdot q - d}{n \cdot n} \, n$, the foot of the perpendicular.
- The closest point of the line to $q$ is $a + \dfrac{(q - a) \cdot d}{d \cdot d} \, d$, by the [[projection theorem|~16119a5a]].
- Parallel lines $a + t d$ and $b + s d$ have the distance $\operatorname{dist}(b, L_1) = \dfrac{\|(b - a) \times d\|}{\|d\|}$.
- Parallel planes $n \cdot r = d_1$ and $n \cdot r = d_2$ have the distance $\dfrac{|d_1 - d_2|}{\|n\|}$.
- The sign of $n \cdot q - d$ tells on which side of the plane $q$ lies.

### History

The distance from a point to a line in the plane, $\dfrac{|ax_0 + by_0 + c|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}$, is a classical result of analytic geometry.
The vector forms with the cross product came with the vector analysis of Gibbs and Heaviside in the 1880s.

### Common errors

- Students forget to divide by $\|n\|$, $\|d\|$ or $\|d \times e\|$.
- Students compute the distance between two points of two lines and take it as the distance between the lines.
- Students use the skew lines formula for parallel lines and get $\tfrac{0}{0}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The distance from a point to a wall is the length of the shortest string from the point to the wall.
The shortest string meets the wall at a right angle.
The formulas find the length of this string without drawing it.

### L2 human

Worked example: the distance from $q = (2, 0, 1)$ to the plane $x - 2y + 2z = 1$.

1. Normal $n = (1, -2, 2)$, with $\|n\| = 3$.
2. $n \cdot q - d = 2 - 0 + 2 - 1 = 3$.
3. The distance is $\tfrac{3}{3} = 1$.
4. The foot is $q - \tfrac{3}{9} n = (2, 0, 1) - \tfrac{1}{3}(1, -2, 2) = (\tfrac{5}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})$.
5. Check: $\tfrac{5}{3} - \tfrac{4}{3} + \tfrac{2}{3} = 1$, so the foot is on the plane.

The typical uses are collision tests, the fit of points to planes, and the nearest points in design.
A trap: the formula for a point and a plane needs the equation in the form $n \cdot r = d$; for $n \cdot r + c = 0$ the numerator is $|n \cdot q + c|$.

### L3 human

All three formulas are cases of one fact: the distance from a point to an affine subspace is the length of the component perpendicular to the subspace.
The sub-area on [[orthogonality and least squares|8GQVME4SZZ]] proves this for subspaces of any dimension, by the [[orthogonal projection|0B8KZRT28M]].
The skew lines case is the projection of $b - a$ onto the orthogonal complement of $\operatorname{span}\{d, e\}$.
