## Example

In the calculus [[LJ|PZX9J3VEMT]], the sequent $\Rightarrow p \to (q \to p)$ has a derivation that uses [[weakening|~20ee74f2]] once.

1. $p \Rightarrow p$ is an axiom.
2. Left weakening WL adds $q$ at the left end: $q, p \Rightarrow p$.
3. The rule ${\to}\mathrm{R}$ of LK derives $\Gamma \Rightarrow A \to B$ from $A, \Gamma \Rightarrow B$.
   With $A = q$ and $\Gamma = p$, it gives $p \Rightarrow q \to p$.
4. The rule ${\to}\mathrm{R}$ again, with $A = p$ and empty $\Gamma$, gives $\Rightarrow p \to (q \to p)$.

Every rule other than WL in this derivation is a logical rule.

## Remarks

The formula $p \to (q \to p)$ is one of the [[paradoxes of material implication|3T7ZJFC9QF]]: the premise $q$ is never used.
Without step 2 the derivation fails, and indeed $p \to (q \to p)$ is not a theorem of [[relevance logic|~366e4c45]] or of linear logic.
The same derivation with $q$ replaced by any formula $B$ derives the axiom scheme $A \to (B \to A)$ of the Hilbert system.
If WL could add $q$ only at the right end, we would need the exchange rule before step 3.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Start with "if $p$, then $p$".
Weakening lets us add an unused assumption $q$.
Then we get "if $p$, then (if $q$, then $p$)", although $q$ plays no role.

### L2 human

We repeat the derivation in the shortest form and record which formulas each step uses.

1. Axiom: $p \Rightarrow p$, which uses $p$.
2. WL: $q, p \Rightarrow p$, which adds $q$ and does not use it.
3. ${\to}\mathrm{R}$ on the leftmost formula $q$: $p \Rightarrow q \to p$.
4. ${\to}\mathrm{R}$ on $p$: $\Rightarrow p \to (q \to p)$.

The typical use of this example is to locate the step that a relevance logic forbids, namely step 2.
A trap: the paradox $q \to (p \to p)$ also needs one weakening, but there it comes after the first ${\to}\mathrm{R}$, on the sequent $\Rightarrow p \to p$.
