## Summary

A *nonmonotonic logic* is a logic whose consequence relation $\mathrel{|\!\sim}$ is not monotonic.
So a conclusion of a set of premises may fail to follow from a larger set.
Classical [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]] is monotonic: if $\Sigma \vDash \varphi$ and $\Sigma \subseteq \Sigma'$, then $\Sigma' \vDash \varphi$.
Nonmonotonic logics model defeasible reasoning, where new information can withdraw a conclusion.
The three classical formalisms are default logic, circumscription and autoepistemic logic.

## Motivation

From "Tweety is a bird" and "birds usually fly", we conclude "Tweety flies".
When we learn that Tweety is a penguin, we withdraw this conclusion.
A monotonic [[consequence relation|J78Y1M4GND]] cannot do this, because it never loses a conclusion when premises are added.
Similar patterns occur in databases that assume that missing facts are false, in abduction, and in reasoning from inconsistent information.
Nonmonotonic logics give precise rules for such reasoning, and they replace Monotony with weaker principles.

## Notation

We write $\Sigma \mathrel{|\!\sim} \varphi$ for "$\varphi$ follows defeasibly from $\Sigma$", and $\vDash$ for classical consequence.
The weaker principles most often used are these:

- *Cautious Monotony*: if $\Sigma \mathrel{|\!\sim} \psi$ and $\Sigma \mathrel{|\!\sim} \varphi$, then $\Sigma \cup \set{\varphi} \mathrel{|\!\sim} \psi$;
- *Cut*: if $\Sigma \mathrel{|\!\sim} \varphi$ and $\Sigma \cup \set{\varphi} \mathrel{|\!\sim} \psi$, then $\Sigma \mathrel{|\!\sim} \psi$;
- *Rational Monotony*: if $\Sigma \mathrel{|\!\sim} \psi$ and not $\Sigma \mathrel{|\!\sim} \neg\varphi$, then $\Sigma \cup \set{\varphi} \mathrel{|\!\sim} \psi$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[Tweety in default logic|~e3291b56]]: a default theory concludes that Tweety flies, and does not after we add that Tweety is a penguin.
- [[Circumscription of abnormality|~7848a134]]: minimizing the abnormal birds gives "Tweety flies" as a consequence.
- [[The closed-world assumption is not monotonic|W4QZGRCDPA]]: a database infers $\neg p$ from no premises, but not from $\set{p}$.

### Non-examples

- [[Classical consequence is monotonic|~9e373fcc]]: every conclusion of $\Sigma$ is a conclusion of each larger set.
- Intuitionistic and modal consequence relations are monotonic as well; non-classical does not mean nonmonotonic.

### Basic properties

The three classical formalisms, compared:

| Formalism | Origin | Idea | Main object |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Default logic | Reiter 1980 | rules "if $\gamma$, and $\theta$ is consistent, infer $\tau$" | extensions, as fixed points |
| Circumscription | McCarthy 1980 | minimize the extension of chosen predicates | a [[second-order|G6X7SZSNC5]] axiom |
| Autoepistemic logic | Moore 1985 | an agent reasons about its own beliefs with an operator $\mathsf{B}$ | stable expansions, as fixed points |

- Default logic and autoepistemic logic define their conclusions by fixed points; a theory can have no fixed point, one, or several.
- Circumscription defines a consequence by truth in minimal models; it is a single formula, but a second-order one.
- Konolige translated default logic into a variant of autoepistemic logic: a default $(\gamma : \theta)/\tau$ becomes $(\mathsf{B}\gamma \land \neg\mathsf{B}\neg\theta) \supset \tau$.
- Gelfond and Lifschitz expressed the stable models of logic programs with negation as failure in autoepistemic logic; logic programming is treated in the [[theory of computation|52TNMMSXD3]].
- [[Preferential consequence relations|~5ba6fe6b]] give a common semantic frame: $\Sigma \mathrel{|\!\sim} \varphi$ if $\varphi$ holds in the most normal models of $\Sigma$.

### History

The SEP entry names McCarthy, McDermott and Doyle, and Reiter as pioneers in the late 1970s.
In 1980 the journal Artificial Intelligence published an issue (volume 13) on these formalisms.
Moore introduced autoepistemic logic in 1985.
Kraus, Lehmann and Magidor studied the general properties of nonmonotonic consequence relations in 1990.

### Common errors

- To think that a nonmonotonic relation has no structural rules. Most systems keep Reflexivity, Cut and Cautious Monotony.
- To confuse nonmonotonic reasoning with belief revision. A nonmonotonic relation draws conclusions from fixed premises; belief revision changes a belief set.
- To think that nonmonotonic logics reject classical logic. Most of them extend classical consequence: if $\Sigma \vDash \varphi$, then $\Sigma \mathrel{|\!\sim} \varphi$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In everyday life we often jump to a conclusion that is usually right.
If we hear that Tweety is a bird, we think that Tweety can fly.
If we then hear that Tweety is a penguin, we change our mind.
Nonmonotonic logics describe this kind of reasoning with exact rules.

### L2 human

A logic is monotonic when more premises never give fewer conclusions.
Nonmonotonic logics drop this rule, but keep weaker ones such as Cautious Monotony: adding a conclusion as a premise loses nothing.
Default logic does this with rules that have consistency conditions.
Circumscription does it with minimal models, and autoepistemic logic with a belief operator.
A trap is to think that "nonmonotonic" means "not classical"; the defeasible conclusions extend the classical ones.

### L3 human

Default logic and autoepistemic logic are fixed-point formalisms: the set of conclusions occurs in its own definition, through the consistency or belief tests.
Circumscription and preferential semantics are minimal-model formalisms, and they lead to the KLM properties.
The [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]] reading of autoepistemic logic uses the logic K45 for the belief operator.
In the propositional case, the main reasoning tasks of default logic are complete for the second level of the polynomial hierarchy, by results of Gottlob (1992).
