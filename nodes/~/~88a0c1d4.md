## Proof

Each part has the same pattern.
We name a linear candidate map and check the defining identity.
Then the uniqueness part of [[the existence theorem|~f77002ca]] shows that the candidate is the adjoint.
We use that the [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] is linear in the first slot, conjugate linear in the second slot, and conjugate symmetric.

1. Part 1. For $v \in V$ and $w \in W$, linearity in the first slot gives $\ip{(S + T)v}{w} = \ip{Sv}{w} + \ip{Tv}{w}$.
2. By the [[definition of the adjoint|0ST8QF9T3T]], this is $\ip{v}{S^* w} + \ip{v}{T^* w}$. Additivity in the second slot makes it $\ip{v}{(S^* + T^*) w}$.
3. The map $S^* + T^*$ is linear and satisfies the defining identity of $(S + T)^*$. By uniqueness, $(S + T)^* = S^* + T^*$.
4. Part 2. For $a \in \mathbb{K}$, we have $\ip{(aT)v}{w} = a \ip{Tv}{w} = a \ip{v}{T^* w}$, by linearity in the first slot and the definition.
5. Conjugate linearity in the second slot gives $a \ip{v}{T^* w} = \ip{v}{\overline{a}\, T^* w}$. By uniqueness, $(aT)^* = \overline{a}\, T^*$.
6. Part 3. The map $T^*$ goes from $W$ to $V$, so its adjoint $(T^*)^*$ goes from $V$ to $W$. It is the unique map $R$ with $\ip{T^* w}{v} = \ip{w}{Rv}$ for all $w \in W$ and $v \in V$.
7. By conjugate symmetry and the definition of $T^*$, $\ip{T^* w}{v} = \overline{\ip{v}{T^* w}} = \overline{\ip{Tv}{w}} = \ip{w}{Tv}$. So $R = T$ satisfies the identity, and uniqueness gives $(T^*)^* = T$.
8. Part 4. Let $T \colon V \to W$ and $S \colon W \to U$. For $v \in V$ and $x \in U$, the definition of $S^*$ applied to the vector $Tv$ gives $\ip{S(Tv)}{x} = \ip{Tv}{S^* x}$.
9. The definition of $T^*$ applied to the vector $S^* x$ gives $\ip{Tv}{S^* x} = \ip{v}{T^*(S^* x)}$. So $T^* S^*$ satisfies the defining identity of $(ST)^*$, and uniqueness gives $(ST)^* = T^* S^*$.
10. Part 5. For $v, w \in V$, $\ip{Iv}{w} = \ip{v}{w} = \ip{v}{Iw}$. By uniqueness, $I^* = I$.
11. Part 6. Let $T \colon V \to W$ be invertible, with inverse $T^{-1} \colon W \to V$. Then $T T^{-1} = I_W$ and $T^{-1} T = I_V$.
12. Apply part 4 and part 5 to both equations: $(T^{-1})^* T^* = (T T^{-1})^* = I_W^* = I_W$ and $T^* (T^{-1})^* = (T^{-1} T)^* = I_V$.
13. So $(T^{-1})^*$ is a two-sided inverse of $T^*$. Hence $T^*$ is invertible, and $(T^*)^{-1} = (T^{-1})^*$.
14. The final claims. By part 3, the map $T \mapsto T^*$ has the inverse map $S \mapsto S^*$ from the linear maps $W \to V$ back to the linear maps $V \to W$. So it is a bijection.
15. Parts 1 and 2 say that $T \mapsto T^*$ is additive and that $(aT)^* = \overline{a}\, T^*$. Over $\mathbb{R}$, $\overline{a} = a$, so the map is linear. Over $\mathbb{C}$ it is [[antilinear|BNPDDM95KF]] by definition.

## Idea

The adjoint is unique, so a rule $X^* = Y$ follows as soon as $Y$ satisfies the defining identity of $X^*$.
Each rule then comes from one or two applications of the definition.
The order reverses in a product because each factor must move across the inner product separately, the outer factor first.

## Variants

In orthonormal bases, all six rules follow from the matching rules for the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]] of matrices, by [[the matrix of the adjoint|0DBTE6HVBD]].
This route needs the choice of bases, and the proof above does not.
