## Example

We prove the [[sum of the first $n$ odd positive integers|9PESCNHE7Q]] by [[mathematical induction|0ASJVEFA9J]]:
$$1 + 3 + 5 + \dots + (2n - 1) = n^2 \quad \text{for every integer } n \ge 1.$$

1. Let $P(n)$ be the statement $\sum_{i=1}^{n} (2i - 1) = n^2$. The range is $n \ge 1$, so the base case is $n_0 = 1$.
2. Base case: for $n = 1$, the sum has one term, $2 \cdot 1 - 1 = 1$, and $1^2 = 1$. So $P(1)$ holds.
3. Induction step: let $n \ge 1$ be an arbitrary integer, and assume $P(n)$, that is, $\sum_{i=1}^{n} (2i - 1) = n^2$.
4. The sum for $n + 1$ is the sum for $n$ plus the term with $i = n + 1$, which is $2(n + 1) - 1 = 2n + 1$.
5. By the induction hypothesis of step 3, $\sum_{i=1}^{n+1} (2i - 1) = n^2 + 2n + 1$.
6. We expand $(n + 1)^2 = n^2 + 2n + 1$. So the sum for $n + 1$ is $(n + 1)^2$, and $P(n + 1)$ holds.
7. Steps 3 to 6 show that $P(n)$ implies $P(n + 1)$ for each $n \ge 1$.
8. By steps 2 and 7 and the principle of induction, $P(n)$ holds for every $n \ge 1$.

The first values agree with the formula:

| $n$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $1 + 3 + \dots + (2n - 1)$ | 1 | 4 | 9 | 16 | 25 | 36 |

## Remarks

1. The example shows the typical shape of an induction for a sum: the sum for $n + 1$ is the sum for $n$ plus one new term.
2. The step used the hypothesis in step 5. Without it, step 5 has no value for the old sum.
3. If the sum starts at $0$ with the empty sum, the base case is $n = 0$: the empty sum is $0 = 0^2$. The step does not change.
4. The same pattern proves $\sum_{i=1}^{n} i^3 = \left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2$, with the new term $(n + 1)^3$ and more algebra in step 6.
5. Francesco Maurolico proved this identity by an induction argument in 1575, one of the first recorded uses of the method.
6. A proof without induction also exists: pair the terms as $(2i - 1) + (2(n + 1 - i) - 1) = 2n$, so twice the sum is $n \cdot 2n$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Put dots in a square: one dot, then a $2$ by $2$ square, then a $3$ by $3$ square.
To grow a square of side $n$ into a square of side $n + 1$, we add one row and one column that share a corner.
That adds $n + n + 1 = 2n + 1$ dots, which is the next odd number.
So the odd numbers $1, 3, 5, \ldots$ add up to the squares $1, 4, 9, \ldots$, and the growth rule never stops working.

### L2 human

We use the same method for the sum of the first $n$ even numbers, $2 + 4 + \dots + 2n = n(n + 1)$.

1. Base case: for $n = 1$, the left side is $2$ and the right side is $1 \cdot 2 = 2$.
2. Induction step: assume $2 + 4 + \dots + 2n = n(n + 1)$ for some $n \ge 1$.
3. Add the next term $2(n + 1)$: the sum becomes $n(n + 1) + 2(n + 1) = (n + 1)(n + 2)$.
4. This is the formula for $n + 1$, so by induction it holds for all $n \ge 1$.

The typical use is a closed formula for a finite sum that a pattern of small cases suggests.
A trap: the term added in the step is the term with index $n + 1$, here $2(n + 1) - 1 = 2n + 1$, not $2n - 1$.
A wrong new term gives an algebra error that looks like a failure of the formula.
