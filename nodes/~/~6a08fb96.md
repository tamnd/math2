## Example

Claim: let $F$ be a [[field|0QA8CHH8MG]], and let $f \in F[x]$ be a nonzero polynomial of [[degree|0DPF6WRZW6]] $d$.
Then $f$ has at most $d$ [[roots|00EHX6FWC0]] in $F$.
We prove the claim by [[induction on size|~ad849a99]], with the degree as the size.
Let $P(d)$ be the statement "every nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ over $F$ has at most $d$ roots in $F$".

1. Base case $d = 0$: a nonzero polynomial of degree $0$ is a constant $f = a$ with $a \neq 0$. Then $f(r) = a \neq 0$ for each $r \in F$, so $f$ has $0$ roots.
2. Induction step: let $d \ge 0$, assume $P(d)$, and let $f$ be an arbitrary nonzero polynomial of degree $d + 1$.
3. If $f$ has no root in $F$, then it has $0 \le d + 1$ roots, and we are done.
4. Otherwise let $c \in F$ be a root of $f$. By the [[factor theorem|0K3MWA7DMC]], $f = (x - c) q$ for some $q \in F[x]$.
5. The polynomial $x - c$ has leading coefficient $1$. So the leading coefficient of $(x - c) q$ equals that of $q$, and $\deg f = 1 + \deg q$. Thus $q \neq 0$ and $\deg q = d$.
6. Let $r \in F$ be a root of $f$. Then $(r - c) \, q(r) = f(r) = 0$.
7. A field has no zero divisors: if $ab = 0$ and $a \neq 0$, then $b = a^{-1} a b = 0$. So $r - c = 0$ or $q(r) = 0$.
8. So each root of $f$ is $c$ or a root of $q$. By $P(d)$, $q$ has at most $d$ roots.
9. So $f$ has at most $1 + d$ roots, and $P(d + 1)$ holds.
10. By induction, $P(d)$ holds for every $d \ge 0$.

For instance, $f = x^3 - x = x(x - 1)(x + 1)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ has degree $3$ and the $3$ roots $0$, $1$ and $-1$.
So the bound $d$ can be reached.

## Remarks

1. The reduction is "factor out one root": it lowers the degree by exactly $1$ and keeps the hypothesis "nonzero polynomial over $F$". This is why the degree is the right size here.
2. The hypothesis "field" is used only in step 7. Over the ring $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$, the polynomial $x^2 - 1$ of degree $2$ has the $4$ roots $1$, $3$, $5$ and $7$, because $1$, $9$, $25$ and $49$ are all $1$ modulo $8$. At the root $3$, the product $(3 - 1)(3 + 1) = 8$ is $0$, but neither factor is $0$.
3. The hypothesis "nonzero" is needed: every element of $F$ is a root of the zero polynomial.
4. The same proof works over every integral domain, because step 7 uses only the absence of zero divisors.
5. A consequence: two polynomials of degree at most $d$ over a field that agree at $d + 1$ distinct points are equal. Otherwise their difference is a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $d$ with $d + 1$ roots.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A polynomial of degree $2$, such as $x^2 - 5x + 6$, can be zero at most at $2$ numbers.
Here these numbers are $2$ and $3$.
The reason is that each zero lets us split off a factor, here $x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3)$.
Each factor uses up one degree.
After all the degree is used up, nothing is left that could be zero, so there are no more zeros.

### L2 human

We follow the proof for $f = x^3 - 2x^2 - x + 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

1. We test $c = 1$: $f(1) = 1 - 2 - 1 + 2 = 0$. So $1$ is a root.
2. Division gives $f = (x - 1)(x^2 - x - 2)$. Expanding, $(x - 1)(x^2 - x - 2) = x^3 - x^2 - 2x - x^2 + x + 2 = x^3 - 2x^2 - x + 2$.
3. The quotient $q = x^2 - x - 2$ has degree $2$. By the induction hypothesis, it has at most $2$ roots.
4. Indeed $q = (x - 2)(x + 1)$, with roots $2$ and $-1$.
5. So the roots of $f$ are among $1$, $2$ and $-1$, and all three are roots. The bound $3$ is reached.

The typical use of the result is the uniqueness of polynomial interpolation, and the bound of $n$ on the number of $n$th roots of unity in a field.
A trap is to apply it over a ring with zero divisors, as Remark 2 shows.
