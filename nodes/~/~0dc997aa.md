## Proof

Let $K$ be a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]], and let $G$ be a finite subgroup of the [[group of units|07C6ECNJET]] $K^\times$, of order $n$.
For each positive divisor $d$ of $n$, let $\psi(d)$ be the number of elements of $G$ of [[order|0WE2NHZJ7B]] $d$.
Let $\varphi$ be [[Euler's totient function|0DAMHCT17R]]: $\varphi(d)$ is the number of integers $k$ with $1 \leq k \leq d$ and $\gcd(k, d) = 1$.

1. By [[the divisibility of the group order by element orders|08V7JHS3QZ]], the order of each element of $G$ is a positive divisor of $n$. Each element has exactly one order, so we count every element once when we sum over the divisors: $\sum_{d \mid n} \psi(d) = n$.
2. Fix a positive divisor $d$ of $n$ with $\psi(d) \geq 1$, and choose $a \in G$ of order $d$.
3. By [[the order of a cyclic subgroup|031M8HX7DA]], the subgroup $\langle a \rangle$ has exactly $d$ elements, namely $1, a, \ldots, a^{d-1}$.
4. Each element $a^i$ of $\langle a \rangle$ satisfies $(a^i)^d = (a^d)^i = 1$. So the $d$ elements of $\langle a \rangle$ are roots of the [[polynomial|N2EY1EVPRB]] $t^d - 1 \in K[t]$.
5. This polynomial is nonzero of degree $d$. By [[the bound on the number of roots|0KJVQV3E0F]], it has at most $d$ roots in $K$. With step 4, the roots of $t^d - 1$ in $K$ are exactly the elements of $\langle a \rangle$.
6. Let $b \in G$ have order $d$. Then $b^d = 1$, so $b$ is a root of $t^d - 1$, and $b \in \langle a \rangle$ by step 5.
7. So the elements of order $d$ in $G$ are the elements of order $d$ in the cyclic group $\langle a \rangle$ of order $d$. By [[the count of elements of each order in a cyclic group|0XCYGCCX8V]], there are exactly $\varphi(d)$ of them. So $\psi(d) = \varphi(d)$.
8. By steps 2 to 7, for each positive divisor $d$ of $n$, either $\psi(d) = 0$ or $\psi(d) = \varphi(d)$. In both cases $\psi(d) \leq \varphi(d)$, because $\varphi(d) \geq 0$.
9. By [[the sum of the totient over the divisors|09JXE6XMCC]], $\sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d) = n$. With step 1, $\sum_{d \mid n} (\varphi(d) - \psi(d)) = n - n = 0$.
10. By step 8, each term $\varphi(d) - \psi(d)$ of this sum is at least $0$. A sum of nonnegative integers is $0$ only when each term is $0$. So $\psi(d) = \varphi(d)$ for every positive divisor $d$ of $n$.
11. In particular, $\psi(n) = \varphi(n) \geq 1$, because $k = 1$ satisfies $\gcd(1, n) = 1$. So $G$ has an element $g$ of order $n$.
12. By the order of a cyclic subgroup, $\langle g \rangle$ has $n$ elements. It is a subset of $G$, and $G$ also has $n$ elements, so $\langle g \rangle = G$. Thus $G$ is cyclic.
13. For the last claim, apply step 5 with $d = n$ and $a = g$: the roots of $t^n - 1$ in $K$ are exactly the elements of $\langle g \rangle = G$. $\blacksquare$

## Idea

Count the elements of $G$ by their orders.
If $G$ has one element of order $d$, then it generates $d$ roots of $t^d - 1$, and the field allows no more.
So all elements of order $d$ lie in one cyclic group of order $d$, which has exactly $\varphi(d)$ of them.
Thus each divisor $d$ of $n$ contributes either $0$ or $\varphi(d)$ elements.
The totals agree, because $\sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d) = n$, so no divisor contributes $0$, and in particular $G$ has an element of order $n$.

## Variants

1. The proof uses the field only in step 5, through the fact that at most $d$ elements of $G$ satisfy $x^d = 1$. So it proves a more general result: a finite group $G$ of order $n$ in which, for each $d \mid n$, at most $d$ elements satisfy $x^d = e$, is cyclic. The proof does not need $G$ to be abelian.
2. The same count shows that a finite cyclic group of order $n$ has exactly $\varphi(n)$ generators. For $K = \mathbb{F}_q$, the field has exactly $\varphi(q - 1)$ [[primitive elements|00QSK85Y2X]]. For example, $\mathbb{F}_{11}$ has $\varphi(10) = 4$ of them: $2, 6, 7, 8$.
3. The [[exponent proof|A2VZWSBS4B]] is shorter, but it uses the theorem that a finite abelian group with exponent equal to its order is cyclic.
