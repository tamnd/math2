## Example

Let $P$ be a unary predicate symbol and $y$ a variable.
We derive $\forall x\, P(x) \Rightarrow \exists x\, P(x)$ in LK without cut.

1. $P(y) \Rightarrow P(y)$ is an axiom.
2. ${\forall}$L with the term $t = y$ gives $\forall x\, P(x) \Rightarrow P(y)$, since $P(x)[y/x] = P(y)$.
3. ${\exists}$R with the term $t = y$ gives $\forall x\, P(x) \Rightarrow \exists x\, P(x)$.

We check the subformula property.

4. The formulas of the derivation are $P(y)$, $\forall x\, P(x)$ and $\exists x\, P(x)$.
5. $P(y)$ is the [[substitution|NCDZ969V7X]] instance $P(x)[y/x]$, so it is a subformula of $\forall x\, P(x)$ in the extended sense.
6. The other two formulas occur in the end sequent.

## Remarks

The example shows why first-order subformulas include instances $B[t/x]$: without them, no cut-free derivation of this sequent would have the property.
The derivation needs a term, here the variable $y$, so it uses that the domain of a structure is nonempty.
Neither ${\forall}$L nor ${\exists}$R has an eigenvariable condition, so any term would do.
The converse sequent $\exists x\, P(x) \Rightarrow \forall x\, P(x)$ is not derivable: in a structure where $P$ holds of one element but not of another, it is false.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The claim is: if everything has property $P$, then something has property $P$.
The proof picks one object $y$ and notes that $y$ has $P$.
The statement "$y$ has $P$" is a piece of "everything has $P$", made by naming one object.

### L2 human

First apply ${\forall}$L to put $\forall x\, P(x)$ on the left, with the instance $P(y)$.
Then apply ${\exists}$R to put $\exists x\, P(x)$ on the right, with the same instance.
A trap is to use ${\forall}$R or ${\exists}$L here: those rules have eigenvariable conditions, and they do not fit this sequent.
