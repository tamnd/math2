## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, a $2 \times 3$ matrix over $\mathbb{R}$, and let $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, a $3 \times 2$ matrix.

1. $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \\ 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$. So $B$ is a [[right inverse|0WWRD82HD2]] of $A$.
2. $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 - 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is not $I_3$.
3. So $B$ is not a left inverse of $A$.
4. In fact $A$ has no left inverse. Let $x = (1, -1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$. Then $Ax = (1 - 1 + 0, \; 0 - 1 + 1)^{\mathsf{T}} = (0, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
5. If $CA = I_3$ for a $3 \times 2$ matrix $C$, then $x = (CA)x = C(Ax) = 0$, by [[associativity|073E4XC016]]. This contradicts $x \ne 0$.
6. The right inverse is not unique: $B' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ also gives $AB' = I_2$, because each column of the added matrix is a multiple of $x$.
7. Check step 6: $AB' = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 + 0 & 0 + 0 + 0 \\ 0 - 1 + 1 & 0 + 0 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$.

## Remarks

A $2 \times 3$ matrix never has a left inverse, by the same argument: its null space is not $\set{0}$, by [[rank-nullity for matrices|TR48YQGCCA]].
In the other direction, the $3 \times 2$ matrix $B$ has the left inverse $A$ and no right inverse.
So the theorem that [[$AB = I$ implies $BA = I$|0753Y2X5K6]] needs square matrices.
The product $P = BA$ of step 2 satisfies $P^2 = B(AB)A = BA = P$, so it is a projection of rank $2$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix $A$ takes points of space to points of the plane.
The matrix $B$ takes each point of the plane to a point of space that $A$ sends back to it.
But $A$ sends a whole line of points to each point of the plane, so no matrix can recover the starting point of space.

### L2 human

The trap: for square matrices one product equal to $I$ is enough, but for non-square matrices it is not.
The test is the null space: here $Ax = 0$ for $x = (1, -1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$, so a left inverse cannot exist.
A typical use of right inverses is to produce a solution of $Ax = b$ for each $b$, namely $x = Bb$; this solution is one of many.

### L3 human

A matrix over a field has a right inverse exactly when it has full row rank.
It has a left inverse exactly when it has full column rank.
The [[Moore-Penrose inverse|0YY8VX28JR]] picks one particular right inverse here, namely $A^{\mathsf{T}}(AA^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}$; this belongs to matrix theory.
