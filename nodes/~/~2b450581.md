## Statement

Let $U$, $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional inner product spaces over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.
Write $T^*$ for the [[adjoint|0ST8QF9T3T]] of a linear map $T$.

1. $(S + T)^* = S^* + T^*$ for all linear maps $S, T \colon V \to W$.
2. $(aT)^* = \overline{a}\, T^*$ for each linear map $T \colon V \to W$ and each $a \in \mathbb{K}$.
3. $(T^*)^* = T$ for each linear map $T \colon V \to W$.
4. $(ST)^* = T^* S^*$ for all linear maps $T \colon V \to W$ and $S \colon W \to U$.
5. $I^* = I$, where $I$ is the identity map of $V$.
6. If $T \colon V \to W$ is invertible, then $T^*$ is invertible, and $(T^*)^{-1} = (T^{-1})^*$.

So the map $T \mapsto T^*$ from the linear maps $V \to W$ to the linear maps $W \to V$ is a bijection.
It is linear over $\mathbb{R}$ and [[antilinear|BNPDDM95KF]] over $\mathbb{C}$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Part 2 needs the conjugate over $\mathbb{C}$. For $T = I$ on $\mathbb{C}$ with $\ip{x}{y} = x \overline{y}$, the map $iI$ has $\ip{i x}{y} = i x \overline{y} = \ip{x}{-iy}$. So $(iI)^* = -iI$, and not $iI$.
- Part 4 needs the reversed order. For $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, we get $ST = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is its own adjoint. But $S^* T^* = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which differs from $(ST)^*$.
- Part 6 needs $T$ invertible. Otherwise $T^{-1}$ does not exist; and by [[the kernel and image of the adjoint|~09be9c75]], $T^*$ is then not invertible either.

### Consequences

- The adjoint of a polynomial in $T$ is the polynomial with conjugate coefficients in $T^*$: if $p(T) = \sum_k a_k T^k$, then $p(T)^* = \sum_k \overline{a_k} (T^*)^k$, by parts 1, 2, 4 and 5.
- The maps $T^* T$ and $T T^*$ are self-adjoint, by parts 3 and 4: $(T^* T)^* = T^* T^{**} = T^* T$.
- Each operator $T$ on a complex space is $T = A + iB$ with $A = \tfrac{1}{2}(T + T^*)$ and $B = \tfrac{1}{2i}(T - T^*)$, and $A$ and $B$ are self-adjoint by parts 1 to 3.

### Converse

The six rules do not characterize the adjoint without the defining identity.
On $2 \times 2$ complex matrices, let $U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\phi(T) = U T^* U$.
Since $U^2 = I$, the map $\phi$ satisfies parts 1 to 6, with $\phi$ in place of the adjoint.
But $\phi \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, while the adjoint is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

### Generalizations

The same rules hold for bounded operators on a Hilbert space, with the same proofs, and they are the axioms of an involution in a $C^*$-algebra.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The adjoint behaves like the transpose of a matrix, with one extra rule over the complex numbers.
The adjoint of a sum is the sum of the adjoints.
The adjoint of a product is the product of the adjoints in the reverse order.
A complex number in front of a map becomes its conjugate: the adjoint of $i T$ is $-i T^*$.
Taking the adjoint twice gives the original map back.

### L2 human

Worked example: the product rule for two complex matrices.

1. Let $S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$, with the standard inner product.
2. Then $ST = \begin{pmatrix} 2 + i & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so $(ST)^* = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - i & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. Also $T^* = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $S^* = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. Then $T^* S^* = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - i & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which equals $(ST)^*$.

The typical use is to simplify expressions such as $\ip{STv}{w} = \ip{v}{T^* S^* w}$.
A trap: $(ST)^* = S^* T^*$ is false in general, as the $2 \times 2$ example in the remarks shows.

### L3 human

Parts 1 to 4 say that the space of operators on $V$ is a $*$-algebra: an algebra with an antilinear involution that reverses products.
Finite-dimensional inner product spaces with the adjoint form a dagger category, and the unitary maps are the maps with $T^* = T^{-1}$.
The [[normal operators|0C8BR8XRTR]] and the [[self-adjoint operators|0NEGN5CRSC]] are defined by these relations, and the [[spectral theorems|04CW1P44VD]] describe them.
