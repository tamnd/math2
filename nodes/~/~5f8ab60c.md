## Example

In $\R^3$ with the [[dot product|05HQYM0R4G]], let $U = \R(1, 1, 0)$ and $W = \R(0, 1, 1)$.
We check the sum rule $(U + W)^\perp = U^\perp \cap W^\perp$ of the [[basic properties|~2c397fab]].

1. $U^\perp$ is the plane $x_1 + x_2 = 0$, because $x \cdot (1, 1, 0) = x_1 + x_2$.
2. $W^\perp$ is the plane $x_2 + x_3 = 0$, because $x \cdot (0, 1, 1) = x_2 + x_3$.
3. $U^\perp \cap W^\perp$ is the set of solutions of both equations. With $x_3 = t$ we get $x_2 = -t$ and $x_1 = t$. So $U^\perp \cap W^\perp = \R(1, -1, 1)$.
4. The [[sum|0RMKABS2S5]] $U + W$ is the plane spanned by $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$.
5. By [[the plane example|~f9f46f63]], the complement of this plane is $\R(1, -1, 1)$.
6. Steps 3 and 5 give the same line, so $(U + W)^\perp = U^\perp \cap W^\perp$ here.

## Remarks

Each of $U^\perp$ and $W^\perp$ is a plane, and two different planes through $0$ in $\R^3$ meet in a line.
If $W = U$, then $U + W = U$, and both sides are the plane $x_1 + x_2 = 0$.
The union $U \cup W$ is not a subspace, but $(U \cup W)^\perp$ is the same line $\R(1, -1, 1)$, as the general rule for subsets says.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A vector is at right angles to two lines exactly when it is at right angles to the plane that the two lines span.
Here that vector lies on the line through $(1, -1, 1)$.

### L2 human

The rule turns one hard question into two easy ones.
To find the complement of a sum, find the complement of each part and intersect them.
In matrix form, the intersection is the null space of the matrix with rows $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$, which is the same computation as for the plane.
The trap is to think that $(U \cap W)^\perp = U^\perp \cap W^\perp$; here $U \cap W = \set{0}$, so the left side is all of $\R^3$, while the right side is a line.
