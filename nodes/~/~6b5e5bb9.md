## Example

On $\mathbb{R}^2$, take the inner product $\ip{x}{y} = 2x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$.
Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $T(x) = Ax = (x_2, 0)$.
We show that the map $y \mapsto A^T y$ is not the [[adjoint|0ST8QF9T3T]] of $T$ for this inner product, and we find the adjoint.

1. The form is an [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]]. It is bilinear and symmetric, and $\ip{x}{x} = 2x_1^2 + x_2^2 > 0$ for $x \neq 0$.
2. Test the identity. Take $x = (0, 1)$ and $y = (1, 0)$. Then $Tx = (1, 0)$, and $\ip{Tx}{y} = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 + 0 = 2$.
3. On the other side, $A^T y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = (0, 1)$, and $\ip{x}{A^T y} = 0 + 1 \cdot 1 = 1$.
4. Since $2 \neq 1$, the defining identity fails. So $y \mapsto A^T y$ is not the adjoint of $T$.
5. The correct adjoint. For all $x$ and $y$, $\ip{Tx}{y} = 2 x_2 y_1$. We need $S(y) = (s_1, s_2)$ with $2 x_1 s_1 + x_2 s_2 = 2 x_2 y_1$ for all $x$.
6. Comparing coefficients gives $s_1 = 0$ and $s_2 = 2y_1$. So $T^*(y_1, y_2) = (0, 2y_1)$, with the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
7. By [[uniqueness of the adjoint|~f77002ca]], this is the adjoint, and it is not $A^T$.

## Remarks

- The matrix of the adjoint is $W^{-1} A^T W$ with $W = \operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$, the matrix of the inner product. This is $\begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
- With the weight $1$ in place of $2$, the inner product becomes the dot product, and the adjoint becomes $A^T$ again.
- A symmetric matrix need not give a self-adjoint map for a weighted inner product. For example $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has the adjoint $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1/2 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ here.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The rule "adjoint equals transpose" is true for the usual dot product.
Here we measure the first coordinate twice as strongly as the second.
With this new rule, the transpose no longer gives the same numbers on both sides.
For the vectors $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$, one side gives $2$ and the other gives $1$.

### L2 human

To find the adjoint for any inner product on $\mathbb{R}^n$:

1. Write the inner product as $\ip{x}{y} = y^T W x$ with a symmetric positive definite matrix $W$.
2. Then $\ip{Ax}{y} = y^T W A x$ and $\ip{x}{Sy} = y^T S^T W x$.
3. These agree for all $x$ and $y$ exactly when $S^T W = W A$, that is, $S = W^{-1} A^T W$.

The typical use is in statistics and in mechanics, where weighted inner products are common.
A trap: "symmetric" and "self-adjoint" mean the same only for the dot product.
