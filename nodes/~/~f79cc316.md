## Statement

Let $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and give $\mathbb{K}^n$ the [[standard inner product|~a4d9ba49]] $\ip{x}{y} = y^* x$.
Let $Q$ be an $n \times k$ matrix over $\mathbb{K}$ with columns $q_1, \dots, q_k$, and let $Q^*$ be its [[conjugate transpose|0DXKE10PHF]].

1. For all $i, j$, the entry $(Q^* Q)_{ij}$ equals $\ip{q_j}{q_i}$.
2. The columns $q_1, \dots, q_k$ form an [[orthonormal family|09VYHEF2VT]] if and only if $Q^* Q = I_k$, the [[identity matrix|06DW9BAWB1]].
3. Let $k = n$. Then the following are equivalent: the columns form an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of $\mathbb{K}^n$; $Q^* Q = I_n$; $Q Q^* = I_n$; the rows of $Q$, as vectors of $\mathbb{K}^n$, are orthonormal.
4. If these hold, then $Q$ is invertible and $Q^{-1} = Q^*$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Part 3 needs a square matrix. For the $2 \times 1$ matrix $Q = (1, 0)^\top$, $Q^\top Q = (1) = I_1$, but $Q Q^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \neq I_2$.
- Over $\mathbb{C}$ the conjugate transpose is needed. For $Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ i & -i \end{pmatrix}$, $Q^* Q = I_2$, but $Q^\top Q = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
- The inner product must be the standard one. For $2x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, the identity matrix has the columns $e_1$ and $e_2$, which are not orthonormal, although $I^\top I = I$.

### Consequences

- A square real matrix is [[orthogonal|0W02QESPJQ]] exactly when its columns form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. A square complex matrix is [[unitary|0AR68JTMDT]] exactly when its columns form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^n$.
- If $Q^* Q = I_k$, then $\ip{Qx}{Qy} = \ip{x}{y}$ for all $x, y \in \mathbb{K}^k$, so $Q$ preserves lengths and angles.
- If $Q^* Q = I_k$, then the coordinates of a vector $v$ of the column space are $Q^* v$. Then $Q Q^*$ is the matrix of the [[orthogonal projection|0B8KZRT28M]] onto the column space.

### Converse

Each part is an equivalence, so the converse is part of the statement.
Part 4 has no converse: an invertible matrix need not satisfy $Q^{-1} = Q^*$, for example $Q = 2I_n$.

### Generalizations

- With any inner product on $\mathbb{K}^n$ of the form $\ip{x}{y} = y^* A x$, the condition becomes $Q^* A Q = I_k$.
- For vectors $v_1, \dots, v_k$ in any inner product space, the [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]] plays the role of $Q^* Q$, and orthonormality means that it is $I_k$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Put the vectors as the columns of a matrix $Q$.
The product $Q^* Q$ is the table of all inner products of the columns.
The columns are orthonormal when this table has $1$ on the diagonal and $0$ elsewhere.

### L2 human

Worked example: a $3 \times 2$ matrix.

1. Let $Q = \frac13\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Then $Q^\top Q = \frac19\begin{pmatrix} 1 + 4 + 4 & 2 + 2 - 4 \\ 2 + 2 - 4 & 4 + 1 + 4 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$. So the two columns are orthonormal.
3. But $Q Q^\top$ is a $3 \times 3$ matrix of rank at most $2$, so it is not $I_3$. Its first entry is $\frac{1 + 4}{9} = \frac59$.

The typical use is a quick test of orthonormality in a computer: compute $Q^* Q - I$ and look at its size.
A trap: for a non-square $Q$, $Q^* Q = I$ does not give $Q Q^* = I$.

### L3 human

The statement identifies orthonormal families in $\mathbb{K}^n$ with isometric embeddings $\mathbb{K}^k \to \mathbb{K}^n$.
For square matrices, these form the [[orthogonal group|0TKYAEJ8N7]] and the [[unitary group|0R9R0Y1HDK]].
The [[QR decomposition|0ERWG4NXQ9]] writes a matrix with independent columns as $QR$ with $Q^* Q = I$, which is the matrix form of the Gram-Schmidt process.
