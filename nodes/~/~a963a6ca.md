## Example

For a vector $v = (v_1, v_2)$ of the plane, let $\|v\|_1 = |v_1| + |v_2|$, the taxicab length.
We show that $\|v\|_1$ is not the [[Euclidean length|0XKBKGTCPC]] $\|v\|$.

1. For $v = (3, 4)$: $\|v\|_1 = 3 + 4 = 7$.
2. For the same $v$: $\|v\| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.
3. The values differ, so $\|\cdot\|_1$ is not the Euclidean length.
4. The taxicab length is not invariant under rotations. The rotation by $90^\circ$ sends $(1, 0)$ to $(0, 1)$, both of taxicab length $1$. The rotation by $45^\circ$ sends $(1, 0)$ to $(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}})$, of taxicab length $\sqrt{2}$.
5. The Euclidean length of $(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}})$ is $1$, the same as for $(1, 0)$.

## Remarks

The taxicab length has the three properties of a norm: positivity, homogeneity and the triangle inequality.
So it is a norm, but a different one.
It measures the distance that a car drives in a city with a square grid of streets.
For vectors on an axis, such as $(5, 0)$, the two lengths agree.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A taxi in a city with square blocks cannot drive along a diagonal.
To go 3 blocks east and 4 blocks north it drives $7$ blocks.
A bird flies straight and covers only $5$ blocks.
The length of a vector is the bird's distance.

### L2 human

Each norm on the plane that does not change under rotations is a constant multiple of the Euclidean length.
Step 4 shows that the taxicab length changes under a rotation by $45^\circ$.
A trap: two norms can agree on many vectors, here on the axes, and still be different norms.
