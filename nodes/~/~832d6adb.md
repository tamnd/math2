## Example

Let $R$ be a binary relation symbol, and let $\varphi = \exists x \, \forall y \, R(x, y)$.
We translate $\varphi$ into the [[epsilon calculus|~41c81e04]], with $\exists x \, A \mapsto A(\varepsilon x \, A)$ and $\forall y \, B \mapsto B(\varepsilon y \, \neg B)$.

1. The inner part $\forall y \, R(x, y)$ becomes $R(x, \varepsilon y \, \neg R(x, y))$. Call this formula $B(x)$. It has the free variable $x$ and no quantifier.
2. The term $\varepsilon y \, \neg R(x, y)$ contains $x$ free. So it denotes a "counterexample candidate" for each $x$.
3. The sentence $\varphi$ is $\exists x \, B(x)$, and it becomes $B(t)$ with $t = \varepsilon x \, B(x)$.
4. Written out, $t = \varepsilon x \, R(x, \varepsilon y \, \neg R(x, y))$, and the translation of $\varphi$ is $R(t, \varepsilon y \, \neg R(t, y))$.
5. The substitution of $t$ for $x$ in $B(x)$ is allowed. Reason: $t$ is closed, so no variable of $t$ can be captured, and [[substitution|NCDZ969V7X]] applies as for quantifiers.
6. The result has no quantifiers. Its only binding operators are the two occurrences of $\varepsilon$ inside the terms.

So $\varphi$ is equivalent in the epsilon calculus to a quantifier-free formula about the term $t$.

## Remarks

Check the meaning in the structure $(\mathbb{N}, \le)$, with $R$ read as $\le$ and $\varepsilon$ read by the choice "least element, and $0$ for the empty set".
For each $x$, the set of $y$ with $\neg (x \le y)$ is $\set{0, \dots, x - 1}$, so $\varepsilon y \, \neg R(x, y)$ is $0$ for $x \ge 1$, and $0$ by default for $x = 0$.
So $B(x)$ says $x \le 0$, and $t$ is the least such $x$, which is $0$.
The translation then says $0 \le 0$, which is true, and $\varphi$ is true: $0$ is below every number.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"There is an $x$ below every $y$" becomes: "the chosen candidate $t$ is below the chosen counterexample for $t$".
If even the chosen counterexample fails to refute $t$, then nothing refutes $t$, so $t$ is below every $y$.

### L2 human

We see why $\forall y \, B$ becomes $B(\varepsilon y \, \neg B)$.

1. The term $s = \varepsilon y \, \neg B$ names a $y$ where $B$ fails, if there is one.
2. If $B(s)$ holds, then no $y$ makes $B$ fail, by the critical formula $\neg B(y) \to \neg B(s)$.
3. So $B(s)$ implies $\forall y \, B$, and the converse is an instance of $\forall y \, B$.

The typical use is in the epsilon substitution method, which tries values for $t$ and $s$ in turn.
A trap: nested $\varepsilon$-terms grow fast, so translations of long formulas are large.
