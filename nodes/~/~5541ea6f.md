## Proof

Let $F$ be a [[finite field|0ZXZ82FTX2]] with $q$ elements, $q$ odd.
Then $2 \ne 0$ in $F$, so $1 \ne -1$.
For a quadratic form, $B$ denotes its associated symmetric bilinear form, as in [[the correspondence theorem|~00a7a300]].

**Part 1.**

1. The map $s(x) = x^2$ from $F^\times$ to $F^\times$ is a group homomorphism, since $(xy)^2 = x^2 y^2$.
2. Its kernel is the set of roots of $x^2 - 1$. It contains $1$ and $-1$, which are distinct.
3. A polynomial of degree $2$ over a field has [[at most $2$ roots|0KJVQV3E0F]]. So the kernel is $\set{1, -1}$.
4. Each fiber of $s$ over a square is a coset of the kernel. By step 3, it has $2$ elements. So the image $F^{\times 2}$ has $(q - 1)/2$ elements, and $F^\times / F^{\times 2}$ has $2$ elements.

**Part 2.**

5. Let $a, b \in F^\times$ and $c \in F$. Let $S = \set{a x^2 : x \in F}$ and $T = \set{c - b y^2 : y \in F}$.
6. By part 1, there are $(q - 1)/2 + 1 = (q + 1)/2$ squares in $F$, counting $0$.
7. The maps $t \mapsto at$ and $t \mapsto c - bt$ are injective, since $a, b \ne 0$. So $S$ and $T$ each have $(q + 1)/2$ elements.
8. The two sets lie in $F$, and $\abs S + \abs T = q + 1 > q$. By the [[pigeonhole principle|0HGVCWQGY3]], $S \cap T$ is not empty.
9. So $a x^2 = c - b y^2$ for some $x, y \in F$, that is, $a x^2 + b y^2 = c$.

**Part 3.**

10. Let $Q$ be a quadratic form on $F^n$, $n \ge 3$. By [[the diagonalization theorem|0Z3FEWZ8HA]], there is a basis $(e_i)$ with $Q(\sum_i x_i e_i) = \sum_i a_i x_i^2$.
11. If some $a_i = 0$, then $Q(e_i) = 0$ and $e_i \ne 0$. So $Q$ is isotropic.
12. Otherwise $a_1, a_2, a_3 \ne 0$. By part 2 with $c = -a_3$, there are $x, y$ with $a_1 x^2 + a_2 y^2 = -a_3$.
13. Then $v = x e_1 + y e_2 + e_3$ has $Q(v) = a_1 x^2 + a_2 y^2 + a_3 = 0$, and $v \ne 0$ since its $e_3$ coordinate is $1$.

**Part 4.**

14. Let $a, b \in F^\times$, and let $Q = a x^2 + b y^2$ on $F^2$. By part 2, there are $x, y$ with $a x^2 + b y^2 = 1$.
15. Let $v_1 = (x, y)$ and $v_2 = (-by, ax)$. Then $Q(v_1) = 1$ and $Q(v_2) = a b^2 y^2 + b a^2 x^2 = ab (a x^2 + b y^2) = ab$.
16. $B(v_1, v_2) = a x (-by) + b y (ax) = 0$. The matrix with columns $v_1, v_2$ has determinant $a x^2 + b y^2 = 1 \ne 0$.
17. So $(v_1, v_2)$ is a basis, orthogonal for $B$, and $Q \cong X^2 + ab Y^2$. We write this as $\langle a, b \rangle \cong \langle 1, ab \rangle$.
18. Let $Q$ be nondegenerate on $F^n$. By step 10, $Q \cong \langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$. All $a_i \ne 0$, since the diagonal matrix is invertible by [[nondegeneracy|01NHFZF2SK]].
19. By step 17 applied to the first two coefficients, $\langle a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots, a_n \rangle \cong \langle 1, a_1 a_2, a_3, \dots, a_n \rangle$.
20. Repeat step 19 on the coefficients from position $2$ on. Each step keeps the product of the coefficients, so after $n - 1$ steps $Q \cong \langle 1, \dots, 1, a_1 a_2 \cdots a_n \rangle$.
21. Let $A$ be the matrix of $Q$ and $P$ the invertible matrix of the change of basis, so that $P^T A P = \operatorname{diag}(a_1, \dots, a_n)$.
22. By [[multiplicativity|04CPJ6AF00]] and [[the transpose rule|0ZZQ2E1MTE]], $a_1 \cdots a_n = \det(P)^2 \det A = \det(P)^2 d$.
23. So $Q \cong \langle 1, \dots, 1, \det(P)^2 d \rangle$. Scaling the last basis vector by $\det(P)^{-1}$ gives $Q \cong \langle 1, \dots, 1, d \rangle$.

**Part 5.**

24. Let $Q$ and $Q'$ be nondegenerate on $F^n$, with determinants $d$ and $d'$. Suppose $d' = c^2 d$ with $c \in F^\times$.
25. By part 4, $Q \cong \langle 1, \dots, 1, d \rangle$ and $Q' \cong \langle 1, \dots, 1, c^2 d \rangle$. Scaling the last basis vector by $c$ shows that these are equivalent.
26. Conversely, suppose $Q' = Q \circ T$ with $T$ invertible, of matrix $S$. Then the matrix of $Q'$ is $S^T A S$.
27. By the rules of step 22, $d' = \det(S)^2 d$, and $\det S \ne 0$. So $d$ and $d'$ lie in the same square class.
28. By part 1, there are two square classes. The forms $\langle 1, \dots, 1, 1 \rangle$ and $\langle 1, \dots, 1, g \rangle$, with $g$ a nonsquare, give both classes. So there are exactly two classes for each $n \ge 1$.

## Idea

Half of the nonzero elements are squares, so two sets of size $(q + 1)/2$ must meet.
This makes every binary form $a x^2 + b y^2$ take every value, which gives isotropy in dimension $3$.
The value $1$ gives the new basis $v_1, v_2$ that moves all coefficients but the last to $1$.
The last coefficient is the determinant, up to squares.

## Variants

- Part 1 also follows from the fact that $F^\times$ is cyclic of even order $q - 1$; the squares are the even powers of a generator.
- Part 3 follows from the [[Chevalley-Warning theorem|08895Q5W96]], which also covers characteristic $2$. The proof here is more elementary.
- Part 2 can be counted exactly: the number of solutions of $a x^2 + b y^2 = c$ with $c \ne 0$ is $q - \chi(-ab)$, where $\chi$ is the quadratic character.
