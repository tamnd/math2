## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $M$ and $N$ be $K$-modules, and let $n \ge 2$.
Let $f \colon M^n \to N$ be multilinear, and let $v_1, \dots, v_n \in M$.

1. If $f$ is [[alternating|0MSWYWHYF5]], then exchanging two arguments changes the sign: for $i < j$,
$$f(\dots, v_j, \dots, v_i, \dots) = -f(\dots, v_i, \dots, v_j, \dots).$$
2. If $f$ is alternating, then $f(v_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, v_{\sigma(n)}) = \sgn(\sigma)\, f(v_1, \dots, v_n)$ for each permutation $\sigma$ in the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_n$, where $\sgn$ is the [[sign|0SK90238BH]].
3. If $f$ is alternating and some $v_i$ is a [[linear combination|0JB3DP1BMF]] of the other $v_k$, then $f(v_1, \dots, v_n) = 0$. When $K$ is a field, $f$ vanishes on each [[linearly dependent|0EVHJFTG9Z]] family.
4. Conversely, let $f$ satisfy the sign rule of part 1, and let $2$ be a non-zero-divisor on $N$, that is, $2y = 0$ implies $y = 0$ for $y \in N$. Then $f$ is alternating.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

In part 4 the condition on $2$ is needed.
Over the field $\mathbb{F}_2$ the form $f(x, y) = xy$ satisfies the sign rule, since $-xy = xy$, but $f(1, 1) = 1 \neq 0$; see the [[non-example|~a63aeed2]].
Multilinearity is needed in part 1.
The map $g(x, y) = (x - y)^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is zero on equal pairs, but $g(1, 0) = g(0, 1) = 1$, so it does not change sign.

### Consequences

- A swap of two rows of a matrix changes the sign of the determinant; see the [[effect of row operations|09YB3WQG38]].
- On a vector space of dimension $n$, each alternating $k$-linear map with $k > n$ is zero, since $k$ vectors are then dependent.
- The values of an alternating $n$-linear form on $K^n$ are fixed by its value on the standard basis; this is the [[existence and uniqueness theorem|~1e90e396]].

### Converse

Part 4 is the converse of part 1 under the condition on $2$.
Without that condition the converse is false, as the example over $\mathbb{F}_2$ shows.
The converse of part 3 is false: the form $\omega(u, v) = u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ is zero on the independent pair $e_1, e_3$.

### Generalizations

The same proof shows that an alternating map is zero on each family with a repeated vector after any permutation.
For modules that are not free, part 3 uses only the linear combination, not a notion of dimension.

## History

Vandermonde first recognized determinants as independent functions in 1771.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose a rule gives zero whenever two inputs are equal, and grows in proportion with each input.
Then swapping two inputs changes the sign of the answer.
For the signed area of two plane vectors, the area of $(u, v)$ is minus the area of $(v, u)$.
For example $1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0 = 1$ for $u = (1, 0)$, $v = (0, 1)$, and $0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1 = -1$ after the swap.

### L2 human

We check the sign rule for $\omega(u, v) = u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1$ with $u = (1, 2)$ and $v = (3, 4)$.

1. $\omega(u, v) = 1 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 3 = -2$.
2. $\omega(v, u) = 3 \cdot 2 - 4 \cdot 1 = 2$.
3. So $\omega(v, u) = -\omega(u, v)$, as part 1 says.
4. For $w = 2u - v = (-1, 0)$, the family $(u, v, w)$ is dependent, and an alternating $3$-linear form on $\mathbb{R}^3$ would vanish on any such family by part 3.

The typical use is to compute the effect of a permutation of rows on a determinant.
A trap: in characteristic $2$ "antisymmetric" means "symmetric", so the sign rule gives no information there.

### L3 human

Part 4 shows that, away from characteristic $2$, alternating and antisymmetric multilinear maps are the same, and the exterior power is the quotient of the tensor power by the antisymmetric relations.
In characteristic $2$ the correct relations are $v \otimes v = 0$, which is why the exterior algebra is defined with them.
The same distinction appears for quadratic forms, where symmetric bilinear forms and quadratic forms differ in characteristic $2$.
