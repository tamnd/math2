## Summary

AGM belief revision is the theory of how a rational agent changes a belief set when new information arrives.
A belief set is a set $K$ of formulas of [[propositional logic|09K9MWD2QQ]] closed under consequence, $K = \mathrm{Cn}(K)$, as in [[deductive closure|CT4FZ84V45]].
There are three operations: expansion $K + \varphi := \mathrm{Cn}(K \cup \set{\varphi})$, contraction $K \div \varphi$ (give up $\varphi$), and revision $K * \varphi$ (accept $\varphi$ and stay consistent).
An AGM revision operator satisfies the eight postulates:

1. (closure) $K * \varphi = \mathrm{Cn}(K * \varphi)$;
2. (success) $\varphi \in K * \varphi$;
3. (inclusion) $K * \varphi \subseteq K + \varphi$;
4. (vacuity) if $\neg\varphi \notin K$, then $K + \varphi \subseteq K * \varphi$;
5. (consistency) if $\varphi$ is consistent, then $K * \varphi$ is consistent;
6. (extensionality) if $\vdash \varphi \leftrightarrow \psi$, then $K * \varphi = K * \psi$;
7. (superexpansion) $K * (\varphi \wedge \psi) \subseteq (K * \varphi) + \psi$;
8. (subexpansion) if $\neg\psi \notin K * \varphi$, then $(K * \varphi) + \psi \subseteq K * (\varphi \wedge \psi)$.

Revision and contraction determine each other by the Levi identity $K * \varphi = (K \div \neg\varphi) + \varphi$ and the Harper identity $K \div \varphi = K \cap (K * \neg\varphi)$.
The theory matters because it gives exact axioms for minimal change of beliefs, used in philosophy, databases and artificial intelligence.

## Motivation

New information can contradict what we believe.
Adding it naively, by expansion, then gives an inconsistent belief set from which everything follows.
So we must first give up some old beliefs, and we should give up as little as possible.
There are many ways to do this, and logic alone does not choose among them.
The AGM postulates do not fix one method; they list the properties that every rational method should have.
The representation theorems then show that the methods with these properties are exactly the methods that minimize change with respect to a plausibility ordering of possible worlds.

## Notation

1. $K$ is a belief set, $K_\bot$ the inconsistent belief set (all formulas), and $\mathrm{Mod}(K)$ the set of valuations that satisfy $K$.
2. $+$, $\div$ (also $-$ or $\dot{-}$) and $*$ are expansion, contraction and revision.
3. $K * 1$ to $K * 8$ and $K \div 1$ to $K \div 8$ are the revision and contraction postulates; the first six of each are the basic postulates.
4. $\le_K$ is a plausibility preorder on valuations or worlds, where $x \le_K y$ means "$x$ is at least as plausible as $y$".

## Remarks

### Examples

1. With two atoms and the ranking $11 < 10 \approx 01 < 00$ of valuations of $(p, q)$, revising $\mathrm{Cn}(p \wedge q)$ by $\neg p$ gives $\mathrm{Cn}(\neg p \wedge q)$, see [[a revision on two atoms|~cc94d121]].
2. On the same ranking, the Harper identity gives $\mathrm{Cn}(p \wedge q) \div p = \mathrm{Cn}(q)$, and the Levi identity recovers the revision, see [[the Levi and Harper identities|~be6e2cd9]].
3. Full meet revision: $K * \varphi = K + \varphi$ if $\neg\varphi \notin K$, and $K * \varphi = \mathrm{Cn}(\varphi)$ otherwise. It satisfies all eight postulates; its ranking puts all models of $K$ first and all other valuations in one tie.
4. Revising by a formula consistent with $K$ is expansion, by postulates 3 and 4.

### Non-examples

1. Expansion is not an AGM revision: $\mathrm{Cn}(p) + \neg p$ is inconsistent, against postulate 5, see [[expansion is not revision|~e076927f]].
2. The operator $K * \varphi := \mathrm{Cn}(\varphi)$ for all $\varphi$ is not an AGM revision. It forgets $q$ when $\mathrm{Cn}(p \wedge q)$ is revised by $p$, against postulate 4.
3. Let $K = \mathrm{Cn}(p \wedge q)$ and let $r$ be a third atom. An operator with $K * \neg p = \mathrm{Cn}(\neg p)$ and $K * (\neg p \wedge q) = \mathrm{Cn}(\neg p \wedge q \wedge r)$ breaks postulate 7, since $r \notin \mathrm{Cn}(\neg p \wedge q)$.

### Basic properties

1. Representation for a finite language: an operator satisfies the eight postulates for a fixed $K$ if and only if $\mathrm{Mod}(K * \varphi)$ is the set of minimal models of $\varphi$ in a total [[preorder|0VABYWJWH9]] whose minimal elements are the models of $K$, see [[the representation theorem|~d81739e6]].
2. Grove's theorem: for any language, the AGM revision operators are those given by systems of spheres of maximal consistent sets centered on the models of $K$.
3. If $\div$ satisfies the basic contraction postulates, the Levi identity gives a revision with the basic revision postulates, and conversely by the Harper identity.
4. The basic contraction postulates are closure, inclusion ($K \div \varphi \subseteq K$), vacuity, success (if $\nvdash \varphi$ then $\varphi \notin K \div \varphi$), recovery ($K \subseteq (K \div \varphi) + \varphi$) and extensionality.
5. Alchourrón, Gärdenfors and Makinson showed that the contractions with the basic postulates are exactly the partial meet contractions, built from selections among maximal subsets of $K$ that do not imply $\varphi$.

### History

Alchourrón, Gärdenfors and Makinson published the postulates and the partial meet construction in 1985.
Grove gave the sphere semantics in 1988, and Katsuno and Mendelzon gave the version with total preorders on valuations in 1991.
Gärdenfors's book Knowledge in Flux (1988) set out the theory, and his [[triviality theorem|WMZ1QP7KQY]] showed its limits with the Ramsey test for conditionals.

### Common errors

1. Reading the postulates as fixing one revision operator. They allow many; the choice is the choice of a plausibility ordering.
2. Assuming that the postulates say how to revise twice. They speak about one belief set at a time; iterated revision needs extra postulates.
3. Confusing revision with update. Update models a change in the world, not new information about a fixed world, and it obeys different postulates.

## Explanations

### L1 human

You believe that Tweety is a bird and that Tweety flies.
Then you learn that Tweety does not fly.
You should drop the belief that Tweety flies, and keep as much as you can of the rest, such as "Tweety is a bird".
AGM theory lists rules that every sensible way of doing this follows.
One rule: the new information is always accepted.
Another rule: if the news does not contradict you, just add it.

### L2 human

We revise $K = \mathrm{Cn}(p \wedge q)$ by $\neg p$ with the ranking $11 < 10 \approx 01 < 00$ of valuations $(p, q)$.

1. The models of $\neg p$ are $01$ and $00$.
2. The most plausible of them is $01$, since $01 < 00$.
3. So $\mathrm{Mod}(K * \neg p) = \set{01}$ and $K * \neg p = \mathrm{Cn}(\neg p \wedge q)$.
4. Postulate 2 holds: $\neg p \in K * \neg p$.
5. Postulate 5 holds: $\neg p \wedge q$ is consistent.

The typical use is to design a revision operator for a knowledge base by choosing the ranking.
A trap: belief sets are infinite; in a finite language one works with a single formula $\kappa$ with $K = \mathrm{Cn}(\kappa)$.

### L3 human

A total preorder of worlds is the same structure as a system of spheres in [[conditional logic|ZGYG7WN7EC]], and the Ramsey test reads $\varphi > \psi \in K$ as $\psi \in K * \varphi$.
The postulates 7 and 8 correspond to the laws of preferential and rational consequence in nonmonotonic reasoning, so AGM revision and nonmonotonic logic are two views of one structure.
Dynamic epistemic logic adds belief change to the object language with plausibility models, where revision is an action that reorders worlds.
Iterated revision (Darwiche and Pearl) and ranking functions (Spohn) extend the theory to sequences of changes.
