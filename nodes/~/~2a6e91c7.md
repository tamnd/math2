## Example

Let $A = 1$ and $\varepsilon = \tfrac18$, so $4 \varepsilon A = \tfrac12 < 1$.
Define $u \colon [0, 2] \to [0, \infty)$ by
$$u(t) = 0 \text{ for } 0 \le t < 1, \qquad u(t) = 7 \text{ for } 1 \le t \le 2.$$
We check that $u$ satisfies every hypothesis of [[the continuity argument|~8a214a45]] except continuity, and that its conclusion fails.

1. $u(0) = 0 \le 1 = A$.
2. The inequality $u \le 1 + \tfrac18 u^2$ is equivalent to $u^2 - 8u + 8 \ge 0$, after multiplication by $8$.
3. The roots of $r^2 - 8r + 8$ are $r = 4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 8} = 4 \pm 2\sqrt2$. So the inequality holds exactly when $u \le 4 - 2\sqrt2 \approx 1.172$ or $u \ge 4 + 2\sqrt2 \approx 6.828$.
4. For $t < 1$: $u(t) = 0$, and $0^2 - 8 \cdot 0 + 8 = 8 \ge 0$. The inequality holds.
5. For $t \ge 1$: $u(t) = 7$, and $7^2 - 8 \cdot 7 + 8 = 49 - 56 + 8 = 1 \ge 0$. The inequality holds.
6. The conclusion of the argument would be $u(t) \le 2A = 2$. It fails for $t \in [1, 2]$, where $u(t) = 7$.
7. The failure is in the closed step. The set $S = \{ t : u(t) \le 2 \} = [0, 1)$ is non-empty and open in $[0, 2]$, but it is not closed: the limit $1$ of the points $1 - \tfrac1n \in S$ is not in $S$.
8. $u$ is not [[continuous|0X3WGX95FE]] at $t = 1$, since $u(1 - \tfrac1n) = 0$ for each $n$ while $u(1) = 7$. This is the hypothesis that the example drops.

## Remarks

1. The example shows that the inequality $u \le A + \varepsilon u^2$ alone does not bound $u$. It allows a lower range $[0, 4 - 2\sqrt2]$ and an upper range $[4 + 2\sqrt2, \infty)$.
2. [[Bootstrapping|~57b93b38]] works because a continuous $u$ cannot pass from the lower range to the upper range without taking a value in the forbidden zone $(4 - 2\sqrt2, 4 + 2\sqrt2)$.
3. A jump avoids the forbidden zone. Any value $c \ge 4 + 2\sqrt2$ in place of $7$ gives the same failure.
4. In applications, continuity in time is part of the local existence theorem. A proof by bootstrapping must cite it, because it is the only reason that the argument closes.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A rule says that a quantity is either small, at most about $1.17$, or large, at least about $6.83$.
A quantity that changes smoothly and starts small stays small, because it cannot cross the gap between the two ranges.
But a quantity that may jump can leap from $0$ to $7$ in one moment, and the rule is still true at each time.
So the rule alone does not keep the quantity small.

### L2 human

We compare the jump with a continuous function that tries to reach $7$.

1. Let $v(t) = 7t$ on $[0, 1]$. It is continuous, with $v(0) = 0$ and $v(1) = 7$.
2. At $t = \tfrac14$ we get $v = 1.75$, and $1.75^2 - 8 \cdot 1.75 + 8 = 3.0625 - 14 + 8 = -2.9375 < 0$.
3. So $v$ violates the hypothesis $v \le 1 + \tfrac18 v^2$ at $t = \tfrac14$.
4. By the [[intermediate value theorem|00KAFBR32E]], every continuous function from $0$ to $7$ takes the value $2$, and at the value $2$ the hypothesis fails: $4 - 16 + 8 = -4 < 0$.

The typical use of this non-example is to locate the role of continuity in a bootstrap proof.
A trap is to think that the inequality bounds $u$ by itself, because it has the form "$u$ is at most something".
