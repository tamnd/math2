## Example

We follow Archimedes' [[physical argument|~eff91bdf]] for the volume of a [[sphere|P9CM4GQ2T0]] of radius $r$, in modern notation.

1. The solids. Put the axis along $x \in [0, 2r]$. The sphere has centre $(r, 0, 0)$. The cone has its apex at $x = 0$ and radius $x$ at height $x$. The cylinder has radius $2r$.
2. The slices at $x$. The sphere's slice is a disc of radius$^2$ equal to $r^2 - (x - r)^2 = 2rx - x^2$, so its area is $\pi(2rx - x^2)$. The cone's slice has area $\pi x^2$. The cylinder's slice has area $4\pi r^2$.
3. The key identity. The sphere slice and the cone slice together have area $\pi(2rx - x^2) + \pi x^2 = 2\pi r x$.
4. The lever. Take the $x$-axis as a lever with its fulcrum at $x = 0$, and extend it to the other side. Hang the sphere slice and the cone slice at distance $2r$ on the other side. Leave the cylinder slice in place, at distance $x$.
5. The balance of slices. The moment of the hung slices is $2\pi r x \cdot 2r = 4\pi r^2 x$. The moment of the cylinder slice is $4\pi r^2 \cdot x$. So each pair of slices balances.
6. The balance of solids. Archimedes concluded that the whole sphere and cone, hung at distance $2r$, balance the whole cylinder. The cylinder acts at its [[centre of mass|0HNK6KT05K]], at distance $r$.
7. The computation. The cylinder has volume $\pi (2r)^2 \cdot 2r = 8\pi r^3$, and the cone has volume $\frac13 \pi (2r)^2 \cdot 2r = \frac83 \pi r^3$. The balance gives $2r(V + \frac83 \pi r^3) = r \cdot 8\pi r^3$.
8. The result. $V + \frac83 \pi r^3 = 4\pi r^3$, so $V = \frac43 \pi r^3$.

Steps 4 to 6 use the law of the lever for geometric slices, and step 6 treats a solid as the sum of its plane slices.
Both are physical assumptions, so the argument finds the volume without proving it.

## Remarks

Archimedes described this argument in "The Method of Mechanical Theorems", a letter to Eratosthenes.
He wrote there that the mechanical method gives an indication of the result but not a demonstration.
He had proved the volume of the sphere by the method of exhaustion in "On the Sphere and Cylinder".
In modern terms, step 5 for all $x$ gives the identity of integrals $\int_0^{2r} 2r \cdot 2\pi r x \, dx = \int_0^{2r} x \cdot 4\pi r^2 \, dx$, and this makes the argument a proof.
A sphere has $\frac23$ of the volume of the cylinder of radius $r$ and height $2r$ around it, the result that Archimedes wished to have on his tomb.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Archimedes imagined cutting a ball, a cone and a cylinder into thin slices.
He hung the slices of the ball and the cone at one end of a balance and kept the slices of the cylinder at the other side.
Every pair of slices balanced, so he concluded that the whole solids balance too.
The balance told him the volume of the ball: $\frac43 \pi r^3$.
He did not count this as a proof, and he proved the result separately by geometry.

### L2 human

We check step 5 at the middle slice, $x = r$.

1. The sphere slice is the largest disc, of radius $r$ and area $\pi r^2$.
2. The cone slice has radius $r$ and area $\pi r^2$.
3. Together they have area $2\pi r^2$, and at distance $2r$ their moment is $4\pi r^3$.
4. The cylinder slice has area $4\pi r^2$, and at distance $r$ its moment is $4\pi r^3$.
5. The moments agree, as step 5 says.

The typical use: the lever turns an unknown volume into a comparison with volumes already known, here the cone and the cylinder.
A trap: step 6 is not a consequence of step 5 without a theory of limits or integrals, which Archimedes did not count as proof.
