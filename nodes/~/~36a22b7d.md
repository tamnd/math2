## Example

Let $E = (e_1, e_2, e_3)$ be the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$, with the standard orientation.
Compare $B_1 = (e_2, e_3, e_1)$ and $B_2 = (e_2, e_1, e_3)$ with $E$.

1. The change of basis matrix from $E$ to $B_1$ has columns $e_2, e_3, e_1$: $P_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Expansion along row 1 gives $\det P_1 = 1 \cdot (1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0) = 1 > 0$.
3. So by the [[definition|0Q8BZXVT1Z]], $B_1$ has the orientation of $E$: it is right-handed.
4. The matrix $P_2$ with columns $e_2, e_1, e_3$ is the identity with rows 1 and 2 exchanged, so $\det P_2 = -1 < 0$.
5. So $B_2$ has the opposite orientation: it is left-handed.
6. Check with the [[cross product|0N5N15HWZ7]]: for a basis $(u, v, w)$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$, $\det(u, v, w) = (u \times v) \cdot w$.
7. For $B_1$: $e_2 \times e_3 = e_1$, and $e_1 \cdot e_1 = 1 > 0$.
8. For $B_2$: $e_2 \times e_1 = -e_3$, and $(-e_3) \cdot e_3 = -1 < 0$. Both signs agree with steps 2 and 4.

## Remarks

A cyclic shift of three vectors is the product of two exchanges, so it keeps the orientation.
One exchange reverses it.
The right-hand rule: if the thumb points along $u$ and the index finger along $v$, the middle finger points to the side of $w$ for a right-handed basis $(u, v, w)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Hold the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger of the right hand at right angles.
The standard axes $x$, $y$, $z$ follow these fingers.
The order $y$, $z$, $x$ also fits the right hand, but the order $y$, $x$, $z$ needs the left hand.

### L2 human

The determinant of the change of basis matrix gives the orientation in one computation.
The cross product gives a second check in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
A trap: the same three vectors in another order can have the other orientation.
