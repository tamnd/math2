## Summary

An existence and uniqueness statement says that there is exactly one object with a given property $P$.
In symbols, it is $\exists! x \, P(x)$, read "there is a unique $x$ such that $P(x)$".
It is the conjunction of two statements.
Existence says that there is at least one $x$ with $P(x)$.
Uniqueness says that there is at most one such $x$: for all $x$ and $y$, if $P(x)$ and $P(y)$, then $x = y$.
The two parts are independent, so a proof proves them separately, and a statement of "at most one" alone is also common.
Uniqueness justifies the word "the" in phrases such as "the identity element" or "the limit of a sequence".

## Motivation

Many objects of mathematics are named with "the": the identity of a group, the quotient and the remainder of a division, the limit of a convergent sequence, the solution of a linear system with an invertible matrix.
Each such name rests on an existence and uniqueness theorem.
Without existence, the name refers to nothing.
Without uniqueness, the name does not say which object is meant, and two proofs could use two different objects.
So a definition by "the $x$ such that $P(x)$" is legitimate only after a proof of $\exists! x \, P(x)$.

## Notation

The [[uniqueness quantifier|0QAJHFGZZJ]] is $\exists!$, and some books write $\exists_1$.
The two equivalent expansions in terms of $\exists$ and $\forall$ are
$$\exists! x \, P(x) \iff \exists x \, \big( P(x) \land \forall y \, ( P(y) \Rightarrow y = x ) \big),$$
$$\exists! x \, P(x) \iff \exists x \, P(x) \land \forall x \, \forall y \, \big( P(x) \land P(y) \Rightarrow x = y \big).$$
In words, "exactly one" means "at least one and at most one".
The statement "at most one" has no standard symbol, and authors write it out as in the second conjunct.
In the Lean library Mathlib, $\exists! x, \, P(x)$ is notation for `ExistsUnique`, defined by the first expansion.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[A group has exactly one identity element|~94180677]]: the axiom gives existence, and $e = e e' = e'$ gives uniqueness.
2. [[Division of $17$ by $5$ has a unique quotient and remainder|~5f6881b4]]: $17 = 5 \cdot 3 + 2$ with $0 \leq 2 < 5$, and no other pair works.
3. For a real number $a > 0$, there is exactly one real $x > 0$ with $x^2 = a$. This is the [[square root|0F7FQS6XY6]] $\sqrt{a}$.
4. A [[strictly monotone|011EJQWWJS]] function $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ has at most one zero: if $x < y$, then $f(x) \neq f(y)$, so two different zeros are impossible. The [[exponential function|0J0FCWV32D]] is strictly increasing and has no zero, so "at most one" can hold with none.
5. A convergent sequence has exactly one limit. Existence is the hypothesis, and uniqueness is the theorem on the [[uniqueness of limits|0ZXJ2455NJ]].

### Non-examples

1. [[The equation $x^2 = 4$ has a solution but not exactly one|~7ee15055]]: both $2$ and $-2$ are solutions.
2. The equation $x^2 = -1$ has at most one real solution, because it has none. So "there is a unique real $x$ with $x^2 = -1$" is false, and existence is the part that fails.
3. "A polynomial of degree $2$ has at most two real roots" is not a uniqueness statement. It bounds the number of roots by two, and $x^2 - 1$ has the two roots $1$ and $-1$.

### Basic properties

1. $\exists! x \, P(x)$ implies $\exists x \, P(x)$. The converse fails, as non-example 1 shows.
2. Uniqueness alone, "at most one", holds whenever no object has the property.
3. $\exists! x \, P(x)$ holds exactly when the set $\{x : P(x)\}$ has exactly one element.
4. The negation of $\exists! x \, P(x)$ is "no $x$ has $P(x)$, or there are $x \neq y$ with $P(x)$ and $P(y)$".
5. The formula $\exists! x \, \exists! y \, P(x, y)$ does not mean "exactly one pair". On $\{0, 1\}$, let $P(x, y)$ be "$x = 1$ or $y = 0$". Only $x = 0$ has exactly one such $y$, so the formula is true, but three pairs satisfy $P$.

### History

Bertrand Russell's theory of [[definite descriptions|PD2R4XT79Q]] of 1905 analysed "the $x$ such that $P(x)$" through existence and uniqueness.
Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica (1910 to 1913) wrote such a description with an inverted Greek letter iota.

### Common errors

1. A student proves existence and forgets uniqueness, and then writes "the" for the object.
2. A student proves uniqueness by showing one object with the property. Uniqueness needs two arbitrary objects $x$ and $y$ with the property and a proof that $x = y$.
3. A student proves uniqueness by contradiction from "suppose $x \neq y$", but never uses $P(x)$ or $P(y)$. Such an argument cannot be right, because uniqueness depends on the property.
4. A student reads "$\exists! x \, \exists! y$" as "exactly one pair $(x, y)$". Property 5 gives a counterexample.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"There is exactly one even prime" makes two claims.
First, some prime is even: $2$ is one.
Second, no other prime is even: every even number greater than $2$ has the divisor $2$, so it is not prime.
The first claim is "there is one".
The second claim is "there is no other one".
Together they say "exactly one".

### L2 human

We prove that the equation $3x + 5 = 11$ has exactly one real solution.

1. Existence: take $x = 2$. Then $3 \cdot 2 + 5 = 11$, so a solution exists.
2. Uniqueness: let $x$ and $y$ be real numbers with $3x + 5 = 11$ and $3y + 5 = 11$.
3. Subtract: $3x - 3y = 0$, so $3(x - y) = 0$.
4. Since $3 \neq 0$, we get $x - y = 0$, so $x = y$.
5. Step 1 proves existence, and steps 2 to 4 prove uniqueness. So $x = 2$ is the unique solution.

The typical use is the two-part proof: one object for existence, and two arbitrary objects for uniqueness.

A trap: in step 2, the student must not take $y = 2$.
Uniqueness compares two arbitrary solutions, and neither may be fixed in advance.

### L3 human

In first-order logic with equality, $\exists!$ is a defined quantifier, and the uniqueness part needs the [[equality|E918704SKV]] symbol.
The quantifiers "there are exactly $n$" for each fixed $n$ are definable in the same way, but "there are infinitely many" is not first-order definable.
A proof of $\exists! x \, P(x)$ justifies a definite description, and Lean, Isabelle and other proof assistants turn a proof of `ExistsUnique` into a term with a choice operator or a description operator.
In constructive mathematics, the axiom of unique choice says that $\forall x \, \exists! y \, R(x, y)$ gives a function $f$ with $R(x, f(x))$, and it holds in many settings where full choice fails.
Universal properties in category theory are existence and uniqueness statements about morphisms: the morphism is unique, and the object is unique up to a unique isomorphism.
