## Example

We show that $(r \land s) \to (\neg r \to (r \land s))$ is a [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]], first by substitution and then by a [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]].

1. The formula $p \to (q \to p)$ is a tautology. It is false only if $p$ is $\mathsf{T}$ and $q \to p$ is $\mathsf{F}$, but $q \to p$ is $\mathsf{T}$ when $p$ is $\mathsf{T}$.
2. Replace $p$ by $r \land s$ at both occurrences, and replace $q$ by $\neg r$.
3. The result is $(r \land s) \to (\neg r \to (r \land s))$.
4. A substitution instance of a tautology is a tautology, so this formula is a tautology.

We check the result directly with the rows $\mathsf{T}\mathsf{T}$, $\mathsf{T}\mathsf{F}$, $\mathsf{F}\mathsf{T}$, $\mathsf{F}\mathsf{F}$ for $r$ and $s$.

| $r$ | $s$ | $r \land s$ | $\neg r$ | $\neg r \to (r \land s)$ | whole formula |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | F | T | T |
| T | F | F | F | T | T |
| F | T | F | T | F | T |
| F | F | F | T | F | T |

5. The last column is $\mathsf{T}$ in every row, as step 4 predicts.

## Remarks

The substitution needs no table: any values of $r$ and $s$ give values to $r \land s$ and $\neg r$, and these form a row of the table of $p \to (q \to p)$.
Every row of that table is $\mathsf{T}$.
Two different variables may be replaced by the same formula: replacing both $p$ and $q$ by $p$ gives the tautology $p \to (p \to p)$.
The replacement must be uniform, with the same formula at every occurrence of a variable.
Replacing the first $p$ by $r$, the second $p$ by $s$, and $q$ by $r$ gives $r \to (r \to s)$.
At $r = \mathsf{T}$, $s = \mathsf{F}$ its value is $\mathsf{T} \to (\mathsf{T} \to \mathsf{F}) = \mathsf{T} \to \mathsf{F} = \mathsf{F}$, so it is not a tautology.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The formula $p \to (q \to p)$ says: if $p$ is true, then anything implies $p$.
It is true for every choice of $p$ and $q$.
So it stays true when $p$ and $q$ stand for longer statements, such as "$r$ and $s$" and "not $r$".
This saves a new table: the shape alone shows that the long formula is always true.

### L2 human

We use substitution to show that $\neg\neg(a \lor b) \to (a \lor b)$ is a tautology.

1. The formula $\neg\neg p \to p$ is a tautology: if $p$ is $\mathsf{T}$ the consequent is $\mathsf{T}$, and if $p$ is $\mathsf{F}$ the antecedent is $\mathsf{F}$.
2. Replace $p$ by $a \lor b$ at both occurrences.
3. The result is $\neg\neg(a \lor b) \to (a \lor b)$, so it is a tautology.

The typical use of substitution is to apply a known law, such as a De Morgan law, to compound formulas without new tables.
A trap: substitution goes from a tautology to its instances, not back; $p \to p$ is a tautology, but $p \to q$ is not.
