## Proof

Let $n = \dim V \ge 1$.

Part 1.

1. Choose $v \in V$ with $v \ne 0$. The $n + 1$ vectors $v, Tv, \dots, T^n v$ are linearly dependent, by the [[Steinitz exchange lemma|08ZDRN6H41]].
2. So there is a nonzero $p \in \R[x]$ of degree at most $n$ with $p(T)v = 0$, by the definition of the [[value of a polynomial at an endomorphism|0BNEXSKNRV]].
3. The polynomial $p$ is not constant: a constant $a_0 \ne 0$ with $a_0 v = 0$ would give $v = 0$.
4. Claim: every nonconstant $f \in \R[x]$ has a monic factor $q \in \R[x]$ of degree $1$ or $2$. Here is the proof of the claim.
5. By the [[fundamental theorem of algebra|0JP0W07Y9A]], $f$ has a root $z \in \C$.
6. If $z \in \R$, then $q = x - z$ divides $f$ in $\R[x]$, by the [[factor theorem|0K3MWA7DMC]].
7. If $z \notin \R$, then [[complex conjugation|0FTH2E9TAS]] preserves sums and products and fixes real numbers. So $f(\bar z) = \overline{f(z)} = 0$.
8. Let $q = (x - z)(x - \bar z) = x^2 - (z + \bar z)x + z \bar z$. Its coefficients $2 \operatorname{Re} z$ and $|z|^2$ are real.
9. By the [[division algorithm|0MES3KS0ZC]] in $\R[x]$, $f = qs + r$ with $r = \alpha x + \beta$ for some $\alpha, \beta \in \R$.
10. At $x = z$, $0 = f(z) = q(z)s(z) + \alpha z + \beta = \alpha z + \beta$. If $\alpha \ne 0$, then $z = -\beta/\alpha$ is real, which is false. So $\alpha = 0$, then $\beta = 0$, and $q$ divides $f$.
11. By induction on the degree, using step 4 on each quotient, $p = c\, q_1 q_2 \cdots q_k$ with $c \in \R$, $c \ne 0$, and each $q_j$ monic of degree $1$ or $2$.
12. The map $f \mapsto f(T)$ preserves products, by the remarks on the [[value of a polynomial at an endomorphism|0BNEXSKNRV]]. So $p(T) = c\, q_1(T) q_2(T) \cdots q_k(T)$.
13. If every $q_j(T)$ were injective, then $p(T)$ would be injective, as a composite of injective maps with $c \ne 0$. This contradicts $p(T)v = 0$ with $v \ne 0$.
14. So there are $j$ and $w \ne 0$ with $q_j(T)w = 0$.
15. Case $\deg q_j = 1$. Then $q_j = x - r$ and $Tw = rw$. The line $U = \R w$ is [[invariant|0ACX5QT8TE]], because $T(aw) = (ar)w \in U$. So $\dim U = 1$.
16. Case $\deg q_j = 2$. Then $q_j = x^2 + bx + d$ and $T^2 w = -bTw - dw$.
17. Let $U = \operatorname{span}\{w, Tw\}$. Then $Tw \in U$, and $T(Tw) = -bTw - dw \in U$. By linearity, $T(U) \subseteq U$.
18. Since $w \ne 0$, $\dim U \ge 1$, and $U$ is spanned by two vectors, so $\dim U \le 2$. This proves part 1.

Part 2.

19. Let $n$ be odd, and let $p_T(t) = t^n + a_{n-1}t^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0$ be the [[characteristic polynomial|0BZ81AJNWF]] of $T$, with real coefficients.
20. Let $M = 1 + |a_0| + \cdots + |a_{n-1}|$. For $|t| \ge M$, we have $|a_{n-1}t^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0| \le (M - 1)|t|^{n-1} < |t|^n$, because $|t|^k \le |t|^{n-1}$ for $k \le n - 1$.
21. So for $|t| \ge M$, $p_T(t)$ has the sign of $t^n$, which is the sign of $t$ because $n$ is odd. Thus $p_T(-M) < 0 < p_T(M)$.
22. A polynomial function is continuous. By the [[intermediate value theorem|00KAFBR32E]], there is $\lambda \in (-M, M)$ with $p_T(\lambda) = 0$.
23. By the theorem that [[eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial|053QVHF6N7]], $\lambda$ is a real [[eigenvalue|0ZND7NQ970]] of $T$. An eigenvector spans an invariant line.

## Idea

A nonzero polynomial kills a fixed vector, as in [[Axler's proof|~7e0a20f5]].
Over $\R$ the polynomial splits into factors of degree $1$ and $2$, and one factor kills a nonzero vector $w$.
A linear factor gives an eigenvector.
A quadratic factor makes the plane spanned by $w$ and $Tw$ invariant.
For odd dimension, the characteristic polynomial has odd degree, so it changes sign and has a real root.

## Variants

- Complexify: an eigenvector $u + iw$ of the complex extension of $T$ for $\lambda = a + ib$ with $b \ne 0$ gives $Tu = au - bw$ and $Tw = bu + aw$. So $\operatorname{span}\{u, w\}$ is invariant.
- Part 2 also follows from part 1 by induction on the dimension.
  If there is no invariant line, take an invariant plane $W$.
  The induced map on $V/W$ acts on a space of odd dimension, so it has a real eigenvalue $\lambda$.
  Its characteristic polynomial divides $p_T$, by the [[theorem on block triangular matrices|~756cfc1f]].
  So $\lambda$ is a root of $p_T$ and an eigenvalue of $T$.
