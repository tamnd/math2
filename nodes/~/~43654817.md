## Proof

This node proves parts 2 and 3 of the [[statement|~35a13d34]] from part 1.
It does not prove part 1; part 1 is cited from Urquhart (1984).
We use the Hilbert system for $\mathbf{R}$ of the SEP supplement: eleven axiom schemes, with modus ponens and adjunction as rules.
Each scheme has at most three schematic letters.

**Part 2: the theorems of $\mathbf{R}$ are computably enumerable, and the non-theorems are not.**

1. Formulas and finite sequences of formulas have computable codes in $\mathbb{N}$, by a fixed coding.
2. Whether a formula is an instance of a given scheme is decidable: match the scheme against the formula symbol by symbol.
3. Whether $C$ follows from $A$ and $B$ by modus ponens or adjunction is decidable: compare $B$ with $A \rightarrow C$, or $C$ with $A \wedge B$.
4. So whether a finite sequence is a derivation is decidable, by steps 2 and 3 applied to each line.
5. A formula is a theorem if and only if it is the last line of some derivation.
6. So the set of theorems is the projection of a decidable relation, and it is [[computably enumerable|MWSD85BB4X]].
7. Assume that the set of non-theorems is also computably enumerable.
8. Then the set of theorems is decidable, by the [[characterization of decidable sets|EZ6Z9V91C2]].
9. This contradicts part 1, so the set of non-theorems is not computably enumerable.

**Part 3: no finite model property for Routley-Meyer frames.**

Call a finite [[Routley-Meyer frame|~212d1096]] $F = (W, R, {}^*, 0)$ an *$\mathbf{R}$-frame* if every theorem of $\mathbf{R}$ holds on $F$.
For a model on $F$, write $\|A\| = \set{a \in W \mid a \Vdash A}$.

10. $\|A\|$ is hereditary for each formula $A$, by the hereditariness remark of the definition.
11. $\|A \wedge B\|$, $\|A \vee B\|$, $\|\neg A\|$ and $\|A \rightarrow B\|$ are computed from $\|A\|$ and $\|B\|$ alone, by the truth clauses.
12. So for each scheme $\sigma$ and hereditary sets $X, Y, Z$, a set $\sigma(X, Y, Z) \subseteq W$ is defined, and $\|\sigma(A, B, C)\| = \sigma(\|A\|, \|B\|, \|C\|)$.
13. Claim: $F$ is an $\mathbf{R}$-frame if and only if $0 \in \sigma(X, Y, Z)$ for every scheme $\sigma$ and all hereditary $X, Y, Z$.
14. Proof of "if", by induction on derivations: an axiom instance $\sigma(A, B, C)$ holds at $0$ by step 12 and the hypothesis.
15. Modus ponens keeps truth at $0$: condition 1 of the definition gives $R000$, so $0 \Vdash A$ and $0 \Vdash A \rightarrow B$ give $0 \Vdash B$.
16. Adjunction keeps truth at $0$, by the clause for $\wedge$.
17. Proof of "only if": give the variables $p, q, r$ the truth sets $X, Y, Z$.
18. The instance $\sigma(p, q, r)$ is an axiom, so it holds at $0$, and $0 \in \sigma(X, Y, Z)$ by step 12.
19. $W$ is finite, so it has finitely many hereditary subsets, and the test of step 13 is a finite computation.
20. The five frame conditions are also finite checks, so whether a finite structure is an $\mathbf{R}$-frame is decidable.
21. Assume, for a contradiction, that every non-theorem of $\mathbf{R}$ fails at $0$ in some model on some finite $\mathbf{R}$-frame.
22. Enumerate all triples of a formula $A$, a finite structure on $\set{0, \ldots, n-1}$ with $n \geq 1$, and a valuation of the variables of $A$.
23. For each triple, decide whether the structure is an $\mathbf{R}$-frame, by step 20, and compute $\|A\|$.
24. Output $A$ when the structure is an $\mathbf{R}$-frame and $0 \notin \|A\|$.
25. By step 21, this procedure outputs every non-theorem.
26. It outputs no theorem, since every theorem holds on every $\mathbf{R}$-frame.
27. So the set of non-theorems is computably enumerable, which contradicts part 2.
28. So some non-theorem of $\mathbf{R}$ holds on every finite $\mathbf{R}$-frame, which is part 3.

## Idea

Part 2 is the standard fact that a logic with a finite Hilbert system has computably enumerable theorems; with part 1, the complement cannot be computably enumerable.
Part 3 is the argument of [[Harrop's theorem|VSYQ1AZ9G7]] read backwards.
The key step is that "every theorem of $\mathbf{R}$ holds on $F$" reduces to finitely many checks on the hereditary subsets of $F$.

## Variants

Part 2 holds for $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{T}$ by the same argument, with the finite Hilbert systems that the Wikipedia article gives.
This node does not prove part 3 for $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{T}$, since their Hilbert systems have rules other than modus ponens and adjunction.
In the SEP supplement, disjunction is defined by $A \vee B = \neg(\neg A \wedge \neg B)$; on a frame with $a^{**} = a$, this agrees with the clause for $\vee$ in the definition.
