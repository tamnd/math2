## Summary

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$, let $T \colon V \to V$ be an [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]], and let $v \in V$.
The *$T$-cyclic subspace generated by $v$* is $Z(v; T) = \operatorname{span}\set{v, Tv, T^2 v, \dots}$, the set of all $f(T)v$ with $f \in F[x]$.
It is the smallest [[$T$-invariant subspace|0ACX5QT8TE]] that contains $v$.
The *$T$-annihilator* of $v$ is the monic polynomial $m_v$ of least degree with $m_v(T)v = 0$.
The vector $v$ is a *cyclic vector* for $T$ when $Z(v; T) = V$, and $T$ is a *cyclic endomorphism* when it has a cyclic vector.
A square matrix is cyclic when the map $x \mapsto Ax$ is cyclic.
Cyclic subspaces are the building blocks of every endomorphism: each $V$ is a direct sum of them, and this gives the rational and Jordan canonical forms.

## Motivation

To understand an endomorphism $T$, we look for subspaces that $T$ maps into themselves.
The smallest such subspace that contains a given vector $v$ must contain $v$, $Tv$, $T^2 v$ and so on.
So it is the span of the orbit of $v$ under $T$.
On this subspace $T$ acts in the simplest possible way: it moves each vector of the list to the next one.
The last vector of the list is a combination of the earlier ones, and the coefficients of this combination are the coefficients of a polynomial.
So the study of $T$ on one cyclic subspace is the study of one polynomial.

## Notation

Hoffman and Kunze write $Z(v; T)$ for the cyclic subspace and $p_v$ for the $T$-annihilator.
Other books write $\langle v \rangle_T$, $F[T]v$ or $\mathcal{C}_v$.
In the language of modules, $Z(v; T)$ is the cyclic submodule $F[x] \cdot v$ of the [[$F[x]$-module of $T$|~772e5994]].
A cyclic endomorphism is also called *nonderogatory*, and a matrix with a cyclic vector is also called *regular* in some texts.

## Remarks

### Examples

- For $T = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ on $F^2$, the vector $(1, 1)$ is a [[cyclic vector|~88ca456f]], since $(1, 1)$ and $T(1, 1) = (1, 2)$ form a basis.
- The derivative $D$ on the polynomials of degree at most $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is [[cyclic with cyclic vector $x^2$|~1e9d4e7e]], since $x^2, 2x, 2$ form a basis.
- The [[companion matrix|0ZCWPNZTQ3]] $C(p)$ of a monic polynomial $p$ of degree $n$ is cyclic with cyclic vector $e_1$, since $C(p) e_i = e_{i+1}$ for $i < n$.
- For an [[eigenvector|0N6960W0BV]] $v$ of $T$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, $Z(v; T) = Fv$ and $m_v = x - \lambda$.
- The nilpotent shift $e_1 \mapsto 0$, $e_i \mapsto e_{i-1}$ on $F^n$ has cyclic vector $e_n$.

### Non-examples

- The [[identity matrix $I_2$ is not cyclic|~d92dcecd]]: for every $v$, $Z(v; I_2) = Fv$ has dimension at most $1$.
- The zero map on $F^n$ with $n \ge 2$ is not cyclic, for the same reason.
- For $T = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$, the vector $e_1$ is not a cyclic vector, since $Z(e_1; T) = Fe_1$, although $T$ itself is cyclic.

### Basic properties

- If $k = \deg m_v$, then $v, Tv, \dots, T^{k-1} v$ is a [[basis of $Z(v; T)$|~9c6edc2a]], so $\dim Z(v; T) = \deg m_v$.
- In this basis the restriction of $T$ to $Z(v; T)$ has the [[companion matrix|0ZCWPNZTQ3]] $C(m_v)$ as its matrix.
- The $T$-annihilator $m_v$ [[divides|0PG2H7WKGG]] every polynomial $f$ with $f(T)v = 0$, in particular the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] $m_T$.
- [[There is always a vector $v$ with $m_v = m_T$|~a478c4ba]].
- $T$ is cyclic [[if and only if $m_T$ equals the characteristic polynomial $\chi_T$|~55322f5b]].
- Each $V$ is a direct sum of $T$-cyclic subspaces, by the [[cyclic decomposition theorem|~168ae65b]].

### History

The cyclic subspaces are the cyclic submodules of $V$ viewed as a module over $F[x]$.
The decomposition of $V$ into cyclic subspaces underlies the rational canonical form, which carries the name of Frobenius.
Hoffman and Kunze give a full treatment in their chapter on the rational and Jordan forms.

### Common errors

- A cyclic endomorphism has a cyclic vector, but not every nonzero vector is a cyclic vector, as $e_1$ for $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ shows.
- The list $v, Tv, T^2 v, \dots$ is not a basis of $Z(v; T)$: it stops being independent after $\deg m_v$ terms.
- Distinct eigenvalues are sufficient for a cyclic map, but not necessary: the [[Jordan block|07PH4RX0X5]] $J_n(\lambda)$ is cyclic with only one eigenvalue.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take a vector and apply a map to it again and again.
You get a list of vectors: the vector, its image, the image of the image, and so on.
All the vectors that you can build from this list with sums and multiples form the cyclic subspace of the vector.
For the map that doubles the second coordinate of a point in the plane, start at the point one, one.
The next point is one, two, and these two points already give every point in the plane.
So one vector "generates" the whole plane under this map, and the map is cyclic.
For the map that does nothing, every vector generates only its own line, and the map is not cyclic.

### L2 human

We compute a cyclic subspace and an annihilator.
Let $T$ be the map of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ on $\mathbb{Q}^3$, so $Te_1 = e_2$, $Te_2 = e_3$ and $Te_3 = e_1$.

1. Take $v = e_1 + e_2 + e_3$. Then $Tv = v$, so $Z(v; T) = \mathbb{Q} v$ and $m_v = x - 1$.
2. Take $w = e_1$. Then $w = e_1$, $Tw = e_2$, $T^2 w = e_3$ form a basis, so $w$ is a cyclic vector.
3. $T^3 w = e_1 = w$, so $(x^3 - 1)(T) w = 0$. The degree of $m_w$ is $\dim Z(w; T) = 3$, so $m_w = x^3 - 1$.
4. Take $u = e_1 - e_2$. Then $Tu = e_2 - e_3$ and $T^2 u = e_3 - e_1 = -u - Tu$.
5. So $(x^2 + x + 1)(T) u = 0$, and $u, Tu$ are independent. Hence $m_u = x^2 + x + 1$ and $\dim Z(u; T) = 2$.

The typical use is a basis $v, Tv, \dots, T^{k-1}v$ in which $T$ has a companion matrix.
The trap is to assume that every nonzero vector of a cyclic space is cyclic: here $v$ and $u$ are not.
Here $m_v$ and $m_u$ both divide $m_T = x^3 - 1 = (x - 1)(x^2 + x + 1)$.

### L3 human

Let $V_T$ be $V$ seen as a module over $F[x]$, with $x$ acting as $T$.
Then $Z(v; T)$ is the cyclic submodule $F[x] v$, and $m_v$ is the monic generator of the annihilator ideal of $v$.
So $Z(v; T) \cong F[x]/(m_v)$, and a cyclic endomorphism is exactly a module of the form $F[x]/(f)$.
The [[cyclic decomposition theorem|~168ae65b]] is the structure theorem for modules over the principal ideal domain $F[x]$.
Cyclic vectors appear in control theory as controllability of a pair $(A, b)$, and in numerical linear algebra as the [[Krylov subspaces|QB45PCGQPV]] $\operatorname{span}\set{b, Ab, \dots, A^{k-1} b}$.
Among all matrices, the cyclic ones form a dense open set over $\mathbb{C}$, and their [[centralizer|~0a19e561]] is as small as possible.
