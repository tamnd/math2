## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix and let $B$ be an $n \times m$ matrix over $K$.

1. The [[characteristic polynomials|039HF58KQ5]] of the square matrices $AB$ and $BA$ satisfy
$$x^n \, p_{AB}(x) = x^m \, p_{BA}(x) \quad \text{in } K[x].$$
2. If $m = n$, then $p_{AB} = p_{BA}$.

Now let $K = F$ be a field.

3. $AB$ and $BA$ have the same nonzero [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]], with the same [[algebraic multiplicities|0FY42N02DV]].
4. For each $\lambda \ne 0$, the eigenspaces $E_\lambda(AB)$ and $E_\lambda(BA)$ have the same dimension, and $v \mapsto Bv$ is an isomorphism from the first onto the second.

## Remarks

The proof is in [[the proof by two block products|~ea1bca54]].
Part 2 implies $\tr(AB) = \tr(BA)$ and $\det(AB) = \det(BA)$ for square $A$ and $B$, because these are coefficients of $p_{AB} = p_{BA}$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Part 3 fails for the eigenvalue $0$. For $A = (1 \ \ 2)$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$, $AB = (11)$ and $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 4 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalues $0$ and $11$.
- Part 4 fails for $\lambda = 0$, even when $m = n$. For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $AB = 0$ but $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. So $\dim E_0(AB) = 2$ and $\dim E_0(BA) = 1$.

### Consequences

- $AB$ and $BA$ need not be [[similar|0E89HQATM8]], by the second example above, although their characteristic polynomials agree.
- If $A$ is invertible, then $BA = A^{-1}(AB)A$ is similar to $AB$. Part 2 extends this to all square $A$.
- [[Sylvester's determinant theorem|0G24VQ1088]], $\det(I_m + AB) = \det(I_n + BA)$, is the value of part 1 at $x = -1$, up to the sign $(-1)^{m+n}$ on both sides.

### Generalizations

For bounded operators $S$ and $T$ on a Banach space, $\sigma(ST) \cup \{0\} = \sigma(TS) \cup \{0\}$.
This belongs to [[spectral theory|XXXSST2DR5]].

## History

The identity is a classical fact of matrix theory.
Its value at one point is Sylvester's determinant theorem, named after Sylvester.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Multiplying two matrices in the two possible orders can give different matrices, even of different sizes.
But the two products have the same nonzero eigenvalues.
Only the eigenvalue $0$ can appear more often in the larger product.

### L2 human

We check part 1 for $A = (1 \ \ 2)$, with $m = 1$, and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$, with $n = 2$.

1. $AB = (1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 4) = (11)$, so $p_{AB}(x) = x - 11$.
2. $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 4 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$, with trace $11$ and determinant $24 - 24 = 0$. So $p_{BA}(x) = x^2 - 11x$.
3. $x^2 (x - 11) = x^3 - 11x^2 = x \cdot (x^2 - 11x)$, as part 1 says.
4. The eigenvector of $BA$ for $11$ is $B$ itself: $BA \cdot B = B(AB) = 11B$. Check: $(3 \cdot 3 + 6 \cdot 4, 4 \cdot 3 + 8 \cdot 4) = (33, 44) = 11 \cdot (3, 4)$.

The typical use is to compute the spectrum of a large product $BA$ from a small product $AB$.
A trap: the theorem does not say that $AB$ and $BA$ are similar.

### L3 human

The identity holds over $\mathbb{Z}[a_{ij}, b_{jk}]$ with independent variables, so it is a polynomial identity.
For $m = n$, one proof uses density: for invertible $A$ the two products are similar, and the invertible matrices are Zariski dense.
