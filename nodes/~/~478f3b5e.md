## Summary

Generalization, as a heuristic, is the replacement of a given problem by a more general problem that contains it as a special case, in the hope that the general problem is easier to solve.
The usual moves are to replace a number by a parameter, a fixed object by a variable one, or a special figure by an arbitrary one.
The general problem can be easier because it has more structure: a parameter can be varied, differentiated or used in an induction.
After the general problem is solved, the solver specializes back to the given problem.
Generalization matters because many single problems become routine once they are seen as one case of a family, and the page [[abstraction and generalization|976RZZCT1H]] treats the general idea behind it.

## Motivation

A problem with a fixed number such as $\frac{1}{2}$ or $100$ gives the solver nothing to vary.
If the number is replaced by a parameter $x$ or $n$, the problem becomes a function of the parameter, and the tools for functions become available.
The given problem is then one value of that function.
The general problem is a stronger statement, so it may seem harder; often it is easier, because the parameter shows the structure.
This effect is the [[inventor's paradox|~aaa3fffe]], and generalization is the heuristic that looks for it.

## Remarks

### The steps

1. Find a quantity in the problem that can be varied: a number, a dimension, a point, a function.
2. Replace it by a parameter, and state the general problem.
3. Solve the general problem, using the new tool that the parameter gives: differentiation, induction, symmetry in the parameter.
4. Specialize the general result to the given value.
5. Check the given case directly when possible.

### When it applies

Generalization applies when the problem contains a special number or object that plays no role in the method, or when a tool such as differentiation or induction needs a parameter that the problem lacks.
It also applies when two analogous problems have a common general form.

### Examples

1. [[The integral of x times ln x squared by a parameter|~fd21a870]]: the integral $\int_0^1 x (\ln x)^2 \, dx$ becomes the second derivative of $\int_0^1 x^t \, dx = \frac{1}{t + 1}$ at $t = 1$.
2. [[The sum of k over 2 to the k by a power series|~d084d0c9]]: the sum $\sum \frac{k}{2^k}$ becomes the value at $x = \frac{1}{2}$ of $\sum k x^k = \frac{x}{(1 - x)^2}$.
3. A number replaced by $n$. To show that $2^{100} - 1$ is divisible by $3$, prove that $4^n - 1$ is divisible by $3$ for every $n$, since $4^n - 1 = (4 - 1)(4^{n-1} + \cdots + 1)$, and take $n = 50$.
4. A figure replaced by a family. The [[Pythagorean theorem|0VP3A1290K]] is the right-angle case of the [[law of cosines|060ZVZBV3E]], and the law of cosines has a proof that uses no special angle.
5. An inequality with a stronger form. The [[strengthening of the induction hypothesis|F94KJC2RXP]] replaces $\sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{k^2} < 2$ by the general bound $2 - \frac{1}{n}$.

### Non-examples

1. [[The quintic has no formula like the quadratic formula|~05f1e5d6]]: the formulas for degrees $2$, $3$ and $4$ suggest a formula for every degree, and there is none for degree $5$.
2. A generalization that loses the property used. The claim "every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence" generalizes from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}^n$, but it fails in every infinite-dimensional normed space, where the unit sphere contains a sequence with no convergent subsequence.
3. A generalization that is too wide. To prove that $1 + 2 + \cdots + 100 = 5050$, the problem "find $\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(k)$ for every function $f$" has no closed answer; the useful generalization is $\sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.

### Basic properties

1. A general statement implies each of its special cases. So a proof of the general problem solves the given problem.
2. A general statement can be false while the given case is true. Then the generalization was a wrong choice, not a proof that the given case fails.
3. The right generalization keeps the features that the method uses and drops the others. Too narrow a generalization gives no new tool, and too wide a generalization gives a false or hopeless problem.
4. Generalization and [[specialization|~701d4f5c]] are used in turn: special cases suggest the general statement, and the general statement is specialized back.

### Related methods

The [[inventor's paradox|~aaa3fffe]] names the observation that the more general problem can be easier.
[[Abstraction and generalization|976RZZCT1H]] describes the same move for definitions and theories, where it gives notions such as a group.
[[Analogy|~02ce22ff]] often comes first: two analogous problems are seen as two cases of one general problem.
[[Variation of the problem|~9b655a34]] includes generalization as one of its moves.

### History

Pólya's dictionary of heuristic in *How to Solve It* (1945) has entries on generalization and on the inventor's paradox.
The trick of example 1 is old: Leibniz used differentiation with respect to a parameter, and Richard Feynman made it popular among physicists, who sometimes call it Feynman's trick.
In the history of algebra, the search for a general formula for equations of every degree ended with the theorem of Ruffini (1799) and Abel (1824) that no such formula exists for degree $5$.

### Common errors

1. To generalize so far that the general claim is false, as in non-example 2.
2. To introduce a parameter and then forget to check that the new tool applies, for example to differentiate under the integral sign without the conditions of the theorem.
3. To expect a pattern in a parameter to continue without a proof, as in non-example 1.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Sometimes a question about one number is easier as a question about all numbers.
For example: is $1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + 99$ a square number?
Ask the general question: what is the sum of the first $n$ odd numbers?
$1 = 1$, $1 + 3 = 4$, $1 + 3 + 5 = 9$, $1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16$: the answer seems to be $n^2$.
A picture of squares growing by an L-shape of $2n - 1$ dots shows why.
There are $50$ odd numbers from $1$ to $99$, so the sum is $50^2 = 2500$.

### L2 human

We generalize to find $\sum_{k=0}^{n} k \binom{n}{k}$.

1. Generalize: put a variable $x$ into the binomial theorem, $(1 + x)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^k$.
2. Use the new tool: differentiate both sides, $n(1 + x)^{n-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} k \binom{n}{k} x^{k-1}$.
3. Specialize: put $x = 1$, which gives $\sum_{k=0}^{n} k \binom{n}{k} = n 2^{n-1}$.
4. Check with $n = 3$: $0 + 3 + 2 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 1 = 12$, and $3 \cdot 2^2 = 12$.

The typical use: the given sum has no parameter, and the variable $x$ makes differentiation available.

A trap: putting $x = 1$ before differentiating gives the number $2^n$, which has no derivative that helps; the parameter must stay until the tool has been used.

### L3 human

In research, generalization is a main way to find the right setting for a theorem: a result about the real line becomes a result about metric spaces, and the proof shows which hypotheses matter.
Grothendieck's general theory of schemes and étale cohomology gave the setting in which Deligne proved the Weil conjectures in 1974.
The danger is the opposite case, where a general statement is false or out of reach while the special case is true, as in Fermat's Last Theorem for $n = 4$ against the general theorem.
Pólya's view was that the solver should try generalization and specialization in turn, and judge the generalization by whether it gives a new tool.
