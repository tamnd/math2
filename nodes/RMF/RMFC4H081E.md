## Example

Photograph the Sun from one place at the same mean time of day on many days of one year.
For example, take noon of local mean time.
The images of the Sun trace a closed curve in the shape of a figure eight, the *analemma*.
Its vertical extent is given by the [[declination|5HD86E1TEH]] of the Sun, which ranges between about $-23.4^\circ$ and $+23.4^\circ$.
Its horizontal extent is given by the [[equation of time|F8JG5255EE]], which ranges between about $-14$ and $+16$ minutes.
Check: at a fixed mean time, the hour angle of the mean Sun is fixed.
The hour angle of the true Sun differs from it by exactly the equation of time.
So the east–west offset of the Sun measures the equation of time, and the north–south position measures the declination.
The declination goes up once and down once in a year, while the equation of time has two maxima and two minima.
So the curve has two loops, and it crosses itself where two dates share both coordinates.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If you mark the Sun's position in the sky at the same clock time every day for a year, the marks do not repeat in a line.
They draw a lopsided figure eight.
The up-and-down motion is the change of the seasons.
The side-to-side motion is the gap between the sundial and the clock, the equation of time.
The two loops are of unequal size because the Earth's orbit is slightly elliptical.
