## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $X = I_2$.

1. Condition 1: $A X A = A^2 = A$, since $A$ is a diagonal matrix with entries $0$ and $1$.
2. Condition 3: $A X = A$ is symmetric.
3. Condition 4: $X A = A$ is symmetric.
4. Condition 2 fails: $X A X = A \ne I_2 = X$.
5. So $X = I_2$ is not the [[Moore-Penrose inverse|0PSRCMCM5X]] of $A$.
6. The pseudoinverse is $A^+ = A$. Check: $A^3 = A$ gives conditions 1 and 2, and $A^2 = A$ is symmetric, which gives conditions 3 and 4.

## Remarks

- $X = I_2$ is a generalized inverse of $A$, since $A X A = A$. Generalized inverses are not unique.
- Condition 2 says that $X$ is not larger than needed: here $X$ acts as the identity on the null space of $A$, where $A^+$ is zero.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix $A$ keeps the first coordinate and kills the second.
The identity matrix undoes the first part, but it does not kill the second coordinate.
The pseudoinverse also kills the second coordinate.

### L2 human

To test a candidate pseudoinverse, all four conditions must hold.
Here three of them hold and the fourth fails.
A trap: a matrix that "inverts $A$ where possible" is not yet the pseudoinverse; it must also be zero on the null space of $A^*$.
