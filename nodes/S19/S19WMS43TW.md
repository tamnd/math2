## Summary

Mathematical language in education is the study of how learners acquire the [[language of mathematics|661V0DH7V3]]: its symbols, its definitions, its special meanings of everyday words, and its forms of argument.
Linguists call the words, meanings and forms of a field its register, and the mathematics register is the register of mathematics.
The field also studies learners who learn mathematics in a second language.
It matters because a student who misreads a word or a symbol cannot follow a proof, even when the mathematics behind the proof is within reach.

## Motivation

Many words of mathematics also have an everyday meaning, and the two meanings differ.
In everyday speech, "similar" means "alike"; in geometry, similar triangles have equal angles and proportional sides.
In everyday speech, "or" often excludes both; in mathematics, "$P$ or $Q$" is true when both are true.
A [[mathematical definition|8ZCZ3HW91Z]] also works differently from a dictionary entry: it fixes the meaning, and only the stated conditions count.
Symbols add a third layer, because one symbol can have several meanings, as the page on [[mathematical notation|QKEX4PD029]] shows.
A learner must learn all three layers, often without explicit teaching.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. Words with two meanings: "product", "mean", "odd", "volume", "prime", "similar", "differentiate" and "range" each have an everyday meaning and a mathematical meaning.
2. Words with a stricter meaning: "any", "some", "a" and "the" carry quantifiers in mathematics, and their reading can change the statement. The page on [[ambiguity in mathematical language|8BZPKYF912]] gives cases.
3. Use of definitions: a student who can recite a definition may still reason from a familiar example, not from the definition. Barbara Edwards and Michael Ward (2004) reported this among undergraduates.
4. Symbols: the sign $=$ is read by many young students as "write the answer", not as "has the same value as". A student with this reading rejects $8 = 3 + 5$ as backwards.
5. Second language: a student who learns mathematics in a second language may solve a problem correctly in symbols and fail to write the argument in words.

### Non-examples

1. The design of mathematical notation, for example the choice of $\frac{dy}{dx}$ or $f'$, belongs to [[notation, conventions and jargon|Z2WNQ1DNTX]]. It is about the language, not about its learning.
2. The teaching of a foreign language with mathematics as the content is language education. Its aim is the language, not the mathematics.

### Research findings

1. Edwards and Ward (2004) found that undergraduate students often did not use mathematical definitions in the way that mathematicians use them. Students who could state a definition still reasoned from examples or from their own ideas of the concept.
2. Shlomo Vinner (1983) found that many students judged whether a correspondence is a function by their mental picture of a function, not by the definition. The page on [[concept image and concept definition|C5S2X0WDJ8]] treats this research.
3. Judit Moschkovich (2002) argued for a sociocultural view of bilingual learners of mathematics. In this view, both languages, gestures and objects are resources for mathematical talk, and the focus moves from missing vocabulary to participation in mathematical practices.
4. David Pimm, in "Speaking Mathematically" (1987), described the mathematics register in the classroom and the role of spoken language in learning mathematics.

### History

The linguist Michael Halliday introduced the idea of a mathematics register in the 1970s.
Pimm's book of 1987 brought the idea into mathematics education research.
The research on definitions joined the research on concept images, which began with David Tall and Vinner in 1981.

### Common errors

1. A teacher assumes that a correct definition on the board gives the students the concept. Students often keep their own meaning of the word.
2. A teacher treats a second-language learner's weak writing as weak mathematics. The learner may reason well and lack only the words.
3. A student reads "a", "any" or "some" in their everyday sense. In mathematics "any" in "if $x$ is any real number" means "each", and the statement is universal.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Mathematics has its own language.
Some words look like everyday words but mean something else.
For example, in everyday speech an "odd" idea is strange, while in mathematics an odd number is a whole number that leaves remainder $1$ when divided by $2$, such as $7$.
Symbols also need learning: $3 \times 4$ and $3 \cdot 4$ and $(3)(4)$ all mean $12$.
Students learn this language slowly, and teachers help when they point out each new meaning.
Students who learn mathematics in a second language must learn two new languages at once.

### L2 human

We follow how a student reads one definition and its use.
The definition: a real function $f$ is increasing on an interval $I$ when, for all $x, y$ in $I$, if $x < y$, then $f(x) \leq f(y)$.

1. The word "increasing" has an everyday meaning, "growing". The student first pictures a rising graph.
2. The definition says "$f(x) \leq f(y)$", not "$f(x) < f(y)$". So a constant function is increasing by this definition, which surprises the everyday reading.
3. Some books call this property "non-decreasing" and keep "increasing" for the strict inequality. This is a [[conflicting convention|CSFEP56A7D]] between books.
4. The definition has a hidden structure: "for all $x, y$ in $I$" is a universal quantifier, and "if $x < y$, then ..." is a conditional.
5. To prove that $f(x) = x^3$ is increasing on $\mathbb{R}$, the student must start with arbitrary $x < y$. A student who checks $f(1) < f(2)$ and $f(2) < f(3)$ has used the everyday meaning, not the definition.
6. The correct start: for $x < y$, we have $y^3 - x^3 = (y - x)(x^2 + xy + y^2)$. Here $y - x > 0$, and $x^2 + xy + y^2 = (x + y/2)^2 + 3y^2/4 \geq 0$, so $y^3 \geq x^3$.

The typical use of this analysis is to plan the first lesson with a new definition: name the everyday meaning, then show where the mathematical meaning differs.
A trap is to assume that one correct example of use shows that the student has the definition.
The student of step 5 gets correct numbers and still does not use the definition.

### L3 human

Research on mathematical language joins linguistics, the psychology of concepts and the sociology of classrooms.
Systemic functional linguistics, in the tradition of Halliday, studies how mathematical texts build meaning with dense noun phrases, symbols and diagrams.
The research on definitions links to the [[concept image and concept definition|C5S2X0WDJ8]] of Tall and Vinner, and to the page on [[unpacking definitions|41JZ39GE3F]].
Work on bilingual learners links to the [[social, cultural and linguistic dimensions|3H96N14VQK]] of mathematics education.
