## Summary

A Hermitian form is the complex version of a [[symmetric bilinear form|0AXZNE5XNH]]: swapping its two vectors replaces the value by its complex conjugate.
Because of this rule, the value of every vector with itself is a real number, so it makes sense to ask whether the form is positive, as a length must be.
Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{C}$.
A *Hermitian form* on $V$ is a [[sesquilinear form|054YEQ0Y8M]] $h$, linear in the first slot and conjugate-linear in the second, such that
$$h(v, u) = \overline{h(u, v)} \qquad \text{for all } u, v \in V,$$
where the bar is the [[complex conjugate|0FTH2E9TAS]].
The [[formal definition|8NJ7XY6F1S]] states the condition.
In a basis with $M_{ij} = h(e_i, e_j)$, the form is $h(x, y) = x^{\mathsf{T}} M \bar y$, and $M$ is a [[Hermitian matrix|0P17NGQQMQ]], $M^* = M$.
The complex [[inner products|0NB0AWGJBW]] are exactly the Hermitian forms with $h(v, v) > 0$ for all $v \ne 0$.

## Motivation

Over $\mathbb{R}$, a symmetric bilinear form $B$ has real values $B(v, v)$, and we can ask whether they are positive.
Over $\mathbb{C}$, a sesquilinear form can have values $h(v, v)$ that are not real.
For $h(x, y) = x_1 \bar y_2$ and $v = (1, i)$, we get $h(v, v) = 1 \cdot \bar i = -i$, and a length of $-i$ has no meaning.
The Hermitian condition is exactly what rules this out: put $u = v$ and it says $h(v, v)$ equals its own conjugate.

This is why Hermitian forms appear wherever complex numbers must produce real measurements.
In quantum mechanics, observable quantities are Hermitian operators, and their expected values are real values of Hermitian forms.
In linear algebra they lead to the [[spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices|0DGZBQWZWN]], and in geometry to the unitary groups.

## Notation

On $\mathbb{C}^n$ we write $h(x, y) = x^{\mathsf{T}} M \bar y$ in our convention, which conjugates the second slot, as the inner product pages do.
Physics books conjugate the first slot and write $h(x, y) = x^* M y$.
The condition $h(v, u) = \overline{h(u, v)}$ and the condition $M^* = M$ read the same in both conventions.
The function $v \mapsto h(v, v)$ is the *Hermitian quadratic form* of $h$.
A form with $h(v, u) = -\overline{h(u, v)}$ is *skew-Hermitian*, and each skew-Hermitian form is $i$ times a Hermitian form.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take the form on $\mathbb{C}^2$ with the matrix
$$M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 + i \\ 1 - i & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad h(x, y) = 2 x_1 \bar y_1 + (1 + i) x_1 \bar y_2 + (1 - i) x_2 \bar y_1 + 3 x_2 \bar y_2.$$
The conjugate transpose has the entry $\overline{1 - i} = 1 + i$ in position $(1, 2)$, so $M^* = M$ and $h$ is Hermitian.
Now evaluate on $v = (1, i)$, with $\bar v = (1, -i)$, term by term:
$$h(v, v) = 2 + (1 + i)(-i) + (1 - i)(i) + 3 = 2 + (1 - i) + (1 + i) + 3 = 7.$$
The two middle terms are conjugates of each other, so their imaginary parts cancel.
That cancellation happens for every $v$, and it is the reason why the result is real.

### Why the values on the diagonal are real

Put $u = v$ in the Hermitian condition: $h(v, v) = \overline{h(v, v)}$, and a number equal to its conjugate is real.
The converse holds too: a sesquilinear form with $h(v, v)$ real for every $v$ is Hermitian, by the [[characterization of Hermitian forms|AXH4S084MR]].
In coordinates, the test is $M^* = M$, that is, $M_{ji} = \overline{M_{ij}}$.
So the diagonal entries $M_{ii} = h(e_i, e_i)$ are real, the entries across the diagonal are conjugate pairs, and $\det M$ is real, since $\overline{\det M} = \det M^* = \det M$.

A change of basis keeps all this.
The new matrix $P^{\mathsf{T}} M \bar P$ is again Hermitian, because its conjugate transpose is $P^{\mathsf{T}} M^* \bar P = P^{\mathsf{T}} M \bar P$.
Its determinant is $\lvert \det P \rvert^2 \det M$, so the sign of the determinant does not depend on the basis.

### More examples

The standard inner product $h(x, y) = x_1 \bar y_1 + \dots + x_n \bar y_n$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$ is Hermitian, with matrix $I$.
The [[form with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ -i & 2 \end{pmatrix}$|8T68MSTVM7]] is Hermitian and positive definite, so it is a second inner product on $\mathbb{C}^2$.
The [[form $x_1 \bar y_1 - x_2 \bar y_2$|HPFSXJNG8R]] is Hermitian but indefinite: it gives $1$ on $e_1$, $-1$ on $e_2$ and $0$ on $(1, 1)$.
Every real symmetric matrix $S$ gives a Hermitian form $x^{\mathsf{T}} S \bar y$, since $S^* = S^{\mathsf{T}} = S$.
On the continuous functions $[0, 1] \to \mathbb{C}$, the form $h(f, g) = \int_0^1 f(t)\, \overline{g(t)}\, dt$ is Hermitian, because conjugating the integral swaps the roles of $f$ and $g$.

### What it is not

The [[form $x_1 \bar y_1 + 2 x_1 \bar y_2 + 3 x_2 \bar y_2$|HTRVMDMNH0]] is sesquilinear but not Hermitian, since $h(e_1, e_2) = 2$ and $h(e_2, e_1) = 0$.
The form $h(x, y) = i x_1 \bar y_1$ on $\mathbb{C}$ is skew-Hermitian, not Hermitian: its value $h(1, 1) = i$ is not real.
The bilinear form $B(x, y) = x_1 y_1$ on $\mathbb{C}$ is symmetric but not sesquilinear, so it fails before the Hermitian condition is even tested.
It gives $B(i, i) = -1$, while every Hermitian form has $h(i, i) = i \bar i\, h(1, 1) = h(1, 1)$.

### Diagonal form and signs

Completing the square works for Hermitian forms, with $\lvert \cdot \rvert^2$ in place of squares.
For the matrix $M$ of the first computation, collect the terms with $v_1$:
$$h(v, v) = 2 \left\lvert v_1 + \tfrac{1 - i}{2} v_2 \right\rvert^2 + 2 \lvert v_2 \rvert^2.$$
Check: expanding the first term gives $2\lvert v_1 \rvert^2 + (1 + i) v_1 \bar v_2 + (1 - i) v_2 \bar v_1 + \lvert v_2 \rvert^2$, since $2 \cdot \lvert \tfrac{1 - i}{2} \rvert^2 = 1$.
So in the basis $b_1 = (1, 0)$, $b_2 = (-\tfrac{1 - i}{2}, 1)$ the matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(2, 2)$, and $h$ is positive definite.
As for real forms in [[Sylvester's law of inertia|0WFED3K3DQ]], the numbers of positive, negative and zero entries of a diagonal matrix of $h$ do not depend on the basis, and the same proof works.

### Watch out

The condition is $h(v, u) = \overline{h(u, v)}$, not $h(v, u) = h(u, v)$.
A [[complex symmetric matrix that is not Hermitian|DAZBNZXZK7]], such as $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, gives $h(v, v) = 2 + 2i$ on $v = (1, 1)$.

A second trap is to write $y^* M x$ in our convention.
Since $y^* M x = \sum_{i, j} \bar y_i M_{ij} x_j$, it is the form with the matrix $M^{\mathsf{T}} = \bar M$, which differs from $h$ unless $M$ is real.
A third trap is to think that $h(v, v) \ge 0$ always: the form $x_1 \bar y_1 - x_2 \bar y_2$ gives $-1$ on $e_2$.

### History

Hermitian matrices are named after Charles Hermite.
In 1855 he showed that these matrices share a property of real symmetric matrices: their eigenvalues are real.

### Try it

First, find the complex number $c$ that makes $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 - i \\ c & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ the matrix of a Hermitian form.
The entry must be $c = \overline{3 - i} = 3 + i$.

Second, for $h(x, y) = x_1 \bar y_1 + 2 x_2 \bar y_2$, compute $h(v, v)$ for $v = (1 + i, 1)$.
The answer is $\lvert 1 + i \rvert^2 + 2 \lvert 1 \rvert^2 = 2 + 2 = 4$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A Hermitian form takes two complex vectors and gives a complex number.
If you swap the two vectors, the number turns into its conjugate, so $3 + 2i$ becomes $3 - 2i$.
A vector paired with itself is "swapped with itself", so its number must equal its own conjugate.
That forces the number to be real, and real numbers can be compared with zero.
The complex dot product $x_1 \bar y_1 + x_2 \bar y_2$ is the main example, and it gives $\lvert x_1 \rvert^2 + \lvert x_2 \rvert^2$ on the diagonal.

### L2 human

Here is a typical problem: is the form with matrix $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2i \\ -2i & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ an inner product on $\mathbb{C}^2$?
First, $M^* = M$, since $\overline{-2i} = 2i$, so the form is Hermitian.
Next, complete the square on the terms with $v_1$:
$$h(v, v) = \lvert v_1 \rvert^2 + 2i\, v_1 \bar v_2 - 2i\, v_2 \bar v_1 + 3 \lvert v_2 \rvert^2 = \lvert v_1 - 2i\, v_2 \rvert^2 - \lvert v_2 \rvert^2.$$
The coefficient $-1$ says that the form is not positive definite.
To find a bad vector, make the square vanish: $v = (2i, 1)$ gives
$$h(v, v) = 2i \cdot (-2i) + 2i \cdot 2i \cdot 1 - 2i \cdot 1 \cdot (-2i) + 3 = 4 - 4 - 4 + 3 = -1.$$
Check: $\det M = 3 - (2i)(-2i) = 3 - 4 = -1 < 0$, and a positive definite form has a positive determinant.
So positive diagonal entries are not enough, and the test must look at the whole matrix, for example with [[Sylvester's criterion|0WKEF78PMV]].

### L3 human

Once an inner product is fixed on a finite-dimensional complex space, each Hermitian form is $h(x, y) = \langle Tx, y \rangle$ for one [[self-adjoint operator|0NEGN5CRSC]] $T$.
The spectral theorem then gives an orthonormal basis in which $h$ is diagonal with real entries, the eigenvalues of $T$.
The isometry group of a nondegenerate Hermitian form with $p$ positive and $q$ negative entries is the [[unitary group|VW5H9XFNJB]] $U(p, q)$.
Over a field $F$ with an automorphism $\sigma$ of order $2$, a $\sigma$-Hermitian form satisfies $h(v, u) = \sigma(h(u, v))$, and Hermitian forms over quadratic extensions of number fields have an arithmetic theory parallel to that of [[quadratic forms|0RBQS1JJCR]].
