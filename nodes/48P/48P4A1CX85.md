## Summary

Let $V$ be a vector space over $\C$.
A Hermitian form on $V$ is a [[sesquilinear form|054YEQ0Y8M]] $h$, linear in the first slot and conjugate-linear in the second, with $h(v, u) = \overline{h(u, v)}$ for all $u, v \in V$.
Then $h(v, v)$ is real for every $v$, and conversely a sesquilinear form with real values $h(v, v)$ is Hermitian, by the [[characterization of Hermitian forms|~debbafcd]].
In a basis, the matrix $M_{ij} = h(e_i, e_j)$ is a [[Hermitian matrix|0P17NGQQMQ]], $M^* = M$, and $h(x, y) = x^T M \bar y$.
The [[formal definition|8NJ7XY6F1S]] states the condition.
Hermitian forms are the complex analogue of [[symmetric bilinear forms|0AXZNE5XNH]], and the complex [[inner products|0NB0AWGJBW]] are exactly the positive definite Hermitian forms.

## Motivation

Over $\R$, a symmetric bilinear form $B$ has real values $B(v, v)$, and we can ask whether they are positive.
Over $\C$, a sesquilinear form can have values $h(v, v)$ that are not real.
For $h(x, y) = x_1 \bar y_2$ and $v = (1, i)$, we get $h(v, v) = 1 \cdot \bar i = -i$.
For a notion of length or energy, we need real values $h(v, v)$.
The condition $h(v, u) = \overline{h(u, v)}$ gives exactly this, and it is the complex version of symmetry.
Quantum mechanics uses Hermitian forms and Hermitian operators because measured values must be real.

## Notation

- We use the convention of most mathematics books: $h$ is linear in the first slot. The physics convention is linear in the second slot, and the condition $h(v, u) = \overline{h(u, v)}$ reads the same.
- On $\C^n$: $h(x, y) = x^T M \bar y$ in our convention, and $h(x, y) = x^* M y$ in the physics convention, with $M^* = M$ in both.
- A form with $h(v, u) = -\overline{h(u, v)}$ is skew-Hermitian. Each skew-Hermitian form is $i$ times a Hermitian form.
- $h(v, v)$ is often written $q(v)$ or $\abs{v}_h$, the Hermitian quadratic form of $h$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The standard inner product $h(x, y) = \sum_i x_i \bar y_i$ on $\C^n$ is Hermitian, with matrix $I$.
- [[The form on $\C^2$ with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ -i & 2 \end{pmatrix}$|~02eb123b]] is Hermitian and positive definite.
- [[The form $x_1 \bar y_1 - x_2 \bar y_2$ on $\C^2$|~493c0077]] is Hermitian and indefinite.
- For each real symmetric matrix $S$, $h(x, y) = x^T S \bar y$ is Hermitian, since $S^* = S$.
- On continuous functions $[0, 1] \to \C$, $h(f, g) = \int_0^1 f(t) \overline{g(t)}\,dt$ is Hermitian.

### Non-examples

- [[The form $x_1 \bar y_1 + 2 x_1 \bar y_2 + 3 x_2 \bar y_2$|~eab02e8b]] is sesquilinear but not Hermitian: $h(e_1, e_2) = 2$ and $h(e_2, e_1) = 0$.
- The form $i(x_1 \bar y_1)$ on $\C$ is skew-Hermitian, not Hermitian: $h(1, 1) = i$ is not real.
- The bilinear form $x_1 y_1$ on $\C$ is symmetric, but not Hermitian, since it is not sesquilinear: $B(i, i) = -1$, while a Hermitian form gives $h(i, i) = i \bar i\,h(1, 1) = h(1, 1) = 1$.

### Basic properties

- $h(v, v)$ is real for every $v$, and a sesquilinear form with this property is Hermitian, by the [[characterization|~debbafcd]].
- A sesquilinear form on $\C^n$ is Hermitian exactly when its matrix is a Hermitian matrix.
- A change of basis $M \mapsto P^T M \bar P$ keeps the matrix Hermitian.
- The diagonal entries $h(e_i, e_i)$ of the matrix are real, and the determinant of the matrix is real.
- A Hermitian form is reflexive: $h(u, v) = 0$ implies $h(v, u) = 0$. Each reflexive sesquilinear form is a scalar multiple of a Hermitian form, by the [[statement on reflexive forms|~b3103bb5]].

### History

Hermitian matrices are named after Charles Hermite.
In 1855 he showed that these matrices share a property of real symmetric matrices: their eigenvalues are real.

### Common errors

- Forgetting the conjugation: $h(v, u) = h(u, v)$ is the condition for a symmetric bilinear form, not for a Hermitian form.
- Writing the matrix formula as $y^* M x$ in our convention. Since $y^* M x = \sum_{i, j} x_j M_{ij} \bar y_i$, this is the form with the matrix $M^T$, which differs from $h$ unless $M$ is real.
- Thinking that $h(v, v) \geq 0$ for every Hermitian form. A Hermitian form can be indefinite, like $x_1 \bar y_1 - x_2 \bar y_2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A Hermitian form is the complex version of a symmetric bilinear form.
Swapping the two vectors conjugates the value.
So the value of a vector with itself is always a real number.
The complex dot product $\sum_i x_i \bar y_i$ is the main example.

### L2 human

We test $h(x, y) = 2 x_1 \bar y_1 + (1 + i) x_1 \bar y_2 + (1 - i) x_2 \bar y_1 + 3 x_2 \bar y_2$ on $\C^2$.

1. The matrix is $M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 + i \\ 1 - i & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. $M^* = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & \overline{1 - i} \\ \overline{1 + i} & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 + i \\ 1 - i & 3 \end{pmatrix} = M$. So $h$ is Hermitian.
3. For $v = (1, 1)$: $h(v, v) = 2 + (1 + i) + (1 - i) + 3 = 7$, a real number.
4. For $v = (1, i)$: $h(v, v) = 2 + (1 + i)(-i) + (1 - i)(i) + 3 = 2 + (1 - i) + (1 + i) + 3 = 7$.

The typical use is to decide whether a form is an inner product: test $M = M^*$, then positivity.
A trap: the matrix entries need not be real; only the diagonal entries must be real.

### L3 human

Hermitian forms on $\C^n$ correspond to self-adjoint operators once an inner product is fixed, and the spectral theorem diagonalizes both at once.
Over a field $F$ with an automorphism $\sigma$ of order $2$, a $\sigma$-Hermitian form satisfies $h(v, u) = \sigma(h(u, v))$, and its isometry group is a unitary group.
Over number fields and their quadratic extensions, Hermitian forms have an arithmetic theory, with classification results like those for quadratic forms.
