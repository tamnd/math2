## Statement

Let $q$ be a prime power, and let $\F_q$ be the [[finite field|0ZXZ82FTX2]] with $q$ elements.
Let $C \subseteq \F_q^n$ be a [[perfect code|94K1WWWGF6]] with at least two codewords and radius $t \ge 1$.
Then the triple $(q, n, t)$ and the number $\abs{C}$ of codewords are as in one of the following cases.

1. $t = 1$, $n = (q^r - 1)/(q - 1)$ for some integer $r \ge 2$, and $\abs{C} = q^{n - r}$, the parameters of the [[Hamming codes|D68NF1S2NW]].
2. $q = 2$, $n = 23$, $t = 3$ and $\abs{C} = 2^{12}$, the parameters of the [[binary Golay code|SM1TJSAR75]].
3. $q = 3$, $n = 11$, $t = 2$ and $\abs{C} = 3^6$, the parameters of the [[ternary Golay code|ERXD4K3GHG]].
4. $q = 2$, $n = 2t + 1$ and $\abs{C} = 2$, the parameters of the binary [[repetition code|PZV5HW9BT7]] of odd length.

## Remarks

The code need not be linear.
A perfect code gives equality in the [[Hamming bound|RZEGDKR7C7]], so the volume $V = \sum_{i=0}^{t} \binom{n}{i}(q-1)^i$ of a ball of radius $t$ divides $q^n$.
Further arithmetic conditions rule out all other parameters.
The main one is Lloyd's theorem: a certain polynomial of degree $t$ must have $t$ distinct integer roots in $\set{1, \dots, n}$.
The theorem was completed in 1973 by Aimo Tietäväinen, and independently by Zinoviev and Leontiev, after work of van Lint.
It classifies parameters, not codes: there are nonlinear perfect codes with the parameters of Hamming codes that are not equivalent to any linear code.
For the Golay parameters, every perfect code is equivalent to the corresponding Golay code.
For alphabets whose size is not a prime power, the classification is not complete.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Perfect codes pack their error zones with no gap and no overlap.
This theorem says that such perfect packings are extremely rare.
Apart from trivial cases and the family of Hamming codes, which only correct one error, there are just two: the binary and the ternary Golay codes.

### L2 human

The theorem explains why the Golay codes are special objects, linked with the Mathieu groups and with the Steiner systems $S(5, 8, 24)$ and $S(5, 6, 12)$.
In practice it means that, for $t \ge 2$, codes meeting the Hamming bound almost never exist, and code design aims at other bounds.
Its proof uses Lloyd's theorem, which turns the existence of a perfect code into a statement about the integer roots of a Krawtchouk-type polynomial.
