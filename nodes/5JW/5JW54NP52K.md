## Summary

The strict implication of $\varphi$ and $\psi$ is the modal formula $\square(\varphi \to \psi)$, equivalently $\neg\lozenge(\varphi \wedge \neg\psi)$: it is impossible that $\varphi$ holds and $\psi$ fails.
In a [[Kripke model|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]] it is true at $w$ when every successor of $w$ that makes $\varphi$ true also makes $\psi$ true.
Clarence Irving Lewis introduced it as a stronger implication than the material one.
His systems $\mathsf{S1}$ to $\mathsf{S5}$, built around it, are the origin of modern [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]].
It avoids the paradoxes of material implication, but it has its own paradoxes for necessary and impossible propositions.

## Motivation

The material implication $p \to q$ is true whenever $p$ is false or $q$ is true.
So "if the moon is cheese, then $2 + 2 = 4$" is true, and a false sentence implies every sentence.
Lewis wanted an implication that holds only when $q$ follows from $p$.
With such an implication, the truth of $p \to q$ is not an accident of the actual truth values.
He read "$p$ strictly implies $q$" as "it is impossible that $p$ and not $q$", and he took "possible" as a new primitive.
The question of the right laws for "possible" led to his five systems.

## Notation

1. Lewis wrote strict implication with a fishhook symbol; we write $\square(\varphi \to \psi)$.
2. Strict equivalence is $\square(\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi)$.
3. Lewis took $\lozenge$ as primitive and defined strict implication as $\neg\lozenge(\varphi \wedge \neg\psi)$; in every [[normal modal logic|JS8W7PA6JK]] this is equivalent to $\square(\varphi \to \psi)$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The paradoxes of strict implication: $\mathsf{K} \vdash \square\neg p \to \square(p \to q)$ and $\mathsf{K} \vdash \square q \to \square(p \to q)$, see [[the paradoxes|HTR97RBERE]].
2. Strict implication is transitive in $\mathsf{K}$: $\square(p \to q) \wedge \square(q \to r) \to \square(p \to r)$, see [[the transitivity example|ZA0C75W1PH]].
3. Strict modus ponens $\square(p \to q) \wedge p \to q$ holds in $\mathsf{T}$ and fails in $\mathsf{K}$, see [[the transitivity example|ZA0C75W1PH]].
4. Strict implication is reflexive: $\mathsf{K} \vdash \square(p \to p)$ by necessitation.

### Non-examples

1. A material implication $p \to q$ can be true at a world where $\square(p \to q)$ is false, see [[the material implication example|RF9G0JQ73G]].
2. The formula $(p \to q) \to \square(p \to q)$ is not a theorem of $\mathsf{K}$, by the same model.
3. The counterfactual "if $p$ were true, $q$ would be true" is not a strict implication. It is checked at the worlds closest to $w$, not at all successors.

### Basic properties

1. $\square(\varphi \to \psi)$ implies $\varphi \to \psi$ in $\mathsf{T}$, so strict implies material in every logic with $\mathrm{T}$.
2. $\square(\varphi \to \psi)$ implies $\square\varphi \to \square\psi$ in $\mathsf{K}$, by the axiom $\mathrm{K}$.
3. In $\mathsf{S4}$, $\square(\varphi \to \psi) \to \square\square(\varphi \to \psi)$, so strict implications are themselves necessary.
4. In $\mathsf{S5}$, a true strict implication is necessary and a false one is impossible: $\square\chi \to \square\square\chi$ and $\neg\square\chi \to \square\neg\square\chi$, with $\chi = \varphi \to \psi$.

### History

Lewis presented strict implication in A Survey of Symbolic Logic (1918), as a reply to the paradoxes of material implication.
Lewis and Langford's Symbolic Logic (1932) contains the systems $\mathsf{S1}$ to $\mathsf{S5}$, each with strict implication as its central notion.
Of these, $\mathsf{S4}$ and $\mathsf{S5}$ are normal, while $\mathsf{S1}$, $\mathsf{S2}$ and $\mathsf{S3}$ are not closed under necessitation.
Gödel's note of 1933 gave the normal axiomatization of $\mathsf{S4}$ with $\square$ as primitive, which became the standard form.

### Common errors

1. Thinking that strict implication avoids all paradoxes; an impossible proposition strictly implies every proposition.
2. Reading $\square(p \to q)$ as $p \to \square q$; the second says that $q$ is necessary whenever $p$ is true.
3. Using strict modus ponens in $\mathsf{K}$ or $\mathsf{D}$; it needs $\mathrm{T}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"If $p$ then $q$" in ordinary logic is true whenever $p$ is false.
So "if pigs fly, then the sun is hot" counts as true, which sounds wrong.
Strict implication asks more: in every possible situation where $p$ is true, $q$ must be true.
"If a number is divisible by 4, then it is even" is a strict implication, since it holds in every case.
"If it is Monday, then it rains" may be true today by chance, but it is not a strict implication.

### L2 human

We verify the paradox $\square\neg p \to \square(p \to q)$ in $\mathsf{K}$.

1. $\neg p \to (p \to q)$ is a tautology.
2. Rule RM of [[normal modal logic K|JS8W7PA6JK]] gives $\square\neg p \to \square(p \to q)$.
3. Read with strict implication: if $p$ is impossible, then $p$ strictly implies every $q$.

The typical use of strict implication is to state laws that hold in every world, such as definitions and mathematical facts.
A trap: in $\mathsf{K}$ without $\mathrm{T}$, the strict implication $\square(p \to q)$ together with $p$ does not give $q$.

### L3 human

Strict implication is one of several attempts to capture "entailment"; relevance logic and the theory of conditionals (Stalnaker, Lewis) are others.
Counterfactual conditionals are variably strict: the set of worlds over which the implication is checked depends on the antecedent, a topic of [[applied modal logics|CQM9SRN8PM]].
Intuitionistic implication is the strict implication of $\mathsf{S4}$ under Gödel's translation, which boxes every implication, see [[modal logic S4|N60RVXSA5W]].
