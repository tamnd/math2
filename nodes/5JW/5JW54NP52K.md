## Summary

Today the shop is open, so "if it is Monday, then the shop is open" is true as a material implication.
Now picture a possible Monday on which the shop is closed.
Then the implication is not necessary, and $p$ does not strictly imply $q$.
Strict implication asks that $p \to q$ hold in every possible world, not only in the actual one.

Exactly, the strict implication of $\varphi$ and $\psi$ is the modal formula $\square(\varphi \to \psi)$, or equivalently $\neg\lozenge(\varphi \wedge \neg\psi)$.
In a [[Kripke model|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]] it is true at $w$ when every successor of $w$ that makes $\varphi$ true also makes $\psi$ true.
Clarence Irving Lewis introduced it as an implication stronger than the material one.
His systems $\mathsf{S1}$ to $\mathsf{S5}$, built around it, are the origin of modern [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]].
It avoids the paradoxes of material implication, but it has its own paradoxes for necessary and impossible propositions.

## Motivation

The material implication $p \to q$ is true whenever $p$ is false or $q$ is true.
So "if the moon is cheese, then $2 + 2 = 4$" is true, and a false sentence implies every sentence.
Lewis wanted an implication that holds only when $q$ follows from $p$.
With such an implication, the truth of "$p$ implies $q$" is not an accident of the actual truth values.
He read "$p$ strictly implies $q$" as "it is impossible that $p$ and not $q$".
The question of the right laws for "possible" led to his five systems.

## Notation

Lewis wrote strict implication with a fishhook symbol, and we write $\square(\varphi \to \psi)$.
Strict equivalence is $\square(\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi)$.
Lewis and Langford took $\lozenge$ as primitive and defined strict implication as $\neg\lozenge(\varphi \wedge \neg\psi)$.
In every [[normal modal logic|JS8W7PA6JK]] this is equivalent to $\square(\varphi \to \psi)$.

## Remarks

### Laws that hold in K

Strict implication is reflexive, since $\mathsf{K} \vdash \square(p \to p)$ by necessitation.
It is transitive, since $\mathsf{K} \vdash \square(p \to q) \wedge \square(q \to r) \to \square(p \to r)$, by [[the transitivity example|ZA0C75W1PH]].
Axiom $\mathrm{K}$ says that $\square(\varphi \to \psi)$ implies $\square\varphi \to \square\psi$.
The paradoxes $\square\neg p \to \square(p \to q)$ and $\square q \to \square(p \to q)$ are theorems of $\mathsf{K}$, by [[the paradoxes|HTR97RBERE]].
They say that an impossible proposition strictly implies everything, and a necessary one is strictly implied by everything.

### Laws that need more axioms

Strict modus ponens, $\square(p \to q) \wedge p \to q$, holds in $\mathsf{T}$ and fails in $\mathsf{K}$, by [[the transitivity example|ZA0C75W1PH]].
So strict implies material in every logic that contains $\mathrm{T}$.
In $\mathsf{S4}$, axiom $\mathrm{4}$ gives $\square(\varphi \to \psi) \to \square\square(\varphi \to \psi)$, so a strict implication is itself necessary.
In $\mathsf{S5}$, axiom $\mathrm{5}$ adds $\neg\square\chi \to \square\neg\square\chi$ for $\chi = \varphi \to \psi$, so a false strict implication is necessarily false.

### What is not a strict implication

A material implication $p \to q$ can be true at a world where $\square(p \to q)$ is false, by [[the material implication example|RF9G0JQ73G]].
The same model shows that $(p \to q) \to \square(p \to q)$ is not a theorem of $\mathsf{K}$.
The counterfactual "if $p$ were true, $q$ would be true" is not a strict implication either.
It is checked at the worlds closest to $w$, not at all successors.

### Watch out

Strict implication does not avoid all paradoxes, since an impossible proposition strictly implies every proposition.
Do not read $\square(p \to q)$ as $p \to \square q$.
The second says that $q$ is necessary whenever $p$ is true.
Strict modus ponens needs $\mathrm{T}$, so do not use it in $\mathsf{K}$ or in $\mathsf{D}$.

### History

Lewis introduced strict implication in a paper in Mind in 1912, as a reply to the paradoxes of material implication.
He developed it in A Survey of Symbolic Logic in 1918.
Lewis and Langford's Symbolic Logic of 1932 contains the systems $\mathsf{S1}$ to $\mathsf{S5}$, each with strict implication as its central notion.
Of these, $\mathsf{S4}$ and $\mathsf{S5}$ are normal, while $\mathsf{S1}$, $\mathsf{S2}$ and $\mathsf{S3}$ are not closed under necessitation.
Gödel's note of 1933 axiomatized $\mathsf{S4}$ with $\square$ as primitive, in the form over classical logic that is standard today.

### Try it

First, take $W = \{ 1, 2 \}$ with $R = \{ (1, 2) \}$, $V(p) = \{ 1 \}$ and $V(q) = \varnothing$.
Is $\square(p \to q)$ true at $1$, and is $p \to q$ true at $1$?
The only successor $2$ lacks $p$, so $p \to q$ holds at $2$ and $\square(p \to q)$ is true at $1$.
But $p \to q$ is false at $1$, which is possible because $1$ does not see itself.

Second, does $\mathsf{K}$ prove $\square(p \to q) \wedge \lozenge p \to \lozenge q$?
Yes: a successor with $p$ has $q$ by the strict implication, so it is a successor with $q$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"If $p$ then $q$" in ordinary logic is true whenever $p$ is false.
So "if pigs fly, then the sun is hot" counts as true, which sounds wrong.
Strict implication asks more: in every possible situation where $p$ is true, $q$ must be true.
"If a number is divisible by 4, then it is even" is a strict implication, since it holds in every case.
"If it is Monday, then it rains" may be true today by chance, but it is not a strict implication.

### L2 human

We verify the paradox $\square\neg p \to \square(p \to q)$ in $\mathsf{K}$.

1. $\neg p \to (p \to q)$ is a tautology.
2. Rule RM of [[normal modal logic K|JS8W7PA6JK]] gives $\square\neg p \to \square(p \to q)$.
3. Read with strict implication: if $p$ is impossible, then $p$ strictly implies every $q$.

You use strict implication to state laws that hold in every world, such as definitions and mathematical facts.
Watch out: in $\mathsf{K}$ without $\mathrm{T}$, the strict implication $\square(p \to q)$ together with $p$ does not give $q$.

### L3 human

Strict implication is one of several attempts to capture entailment, and relevance logic and the theory of conditionals of Stalnaker and David Lewis are others.
Counterfactual conditionals are variably strict: the set of worlds over which the implication is checked depends on the antecedent; see [[applied modal logics|CQM9SRN8PM]].
Intuitionistic implication becomes the strict implication of $\mathsf{S4}$ under the [[Gödel-McKinsey-Tarski translation|BXVNEN55MR]], which boxes every implication; see [[modal logic S4|N60RVXSA5W]].
[[Impossible worlds|8PAJMAN8DM]] give one way to block the paradoxes, since an impossible world can make $p \wedge \neg p$ true.
