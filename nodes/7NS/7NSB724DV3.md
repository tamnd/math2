## Proof

For part 1 we compare $\ip{Tv}{v}$ with $\ip{v}{Tv}$ at an eigenvector.
For part 2 we maximize $\ip{Tv}{v}$ on the unit sphere and show that $Tu$ has no component orthogonal to a maximum point $u$.
Part 2 does not use the fundamental theorem of algebra.
Throughout, $V$ is a finite-dimensional inner product space over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and $T$ is [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]], so $\ip{Tv}{w} = \ip{v}{Tw}$ for all $v, w \in V$.
The [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] is linear in the first slot and conjugate linear in the second.

**Part 1.**

1. If $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$, every scalar is real, and there is nothing to prove. So let $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$, and let $\lambda$ be an [[eigenvalue|0ZND7NQ970]] with eigenvector $v \ne 0$, so $Tv = \lambda v$.
2. Linearity in the first slot gives $\ip{Tv}{v} = \lambda \ip{v}{v}$, and conjugate linearity in the second slot gives $\ip{v}{Tv} = \overline{\lambda} \ip{v}{v}$.
3. Self-adjointness says that the two numbers of step 2 are equal, so $\lambda \ip{v}{v} = \overline{\lambda} \ip{v}{v}$.
4. Since $v \ne 0$, positivity gives $\ip{v}{v} > 0$, and we may divide by it. So $\lambda = \overline{\lambda}$, and a complex number equal to its [[conjugate|0BJV24C4SY]] is real.

**Part 2.**

5. Let $V \ne 0$ and $q(v) = \ip{Tv}{v}$. Self-adjointness and conjugate symmetry give $q(v) = \ip{v}{Tv} = \overline{q(v)}$, so $q$ has real values.
6. Choose an orthonormal basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ of $V$, by [[the existence of an orthonormal basis|0Y85GYEGSF]]. For $v = \sum_j c_j e_j$, Pythagoras gives $\norm{v}^2 = \sum_j \abs{c_j}^2$.
7. Split each $c_j$ into its real and imaginary parts when $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$. Then $v$ becomes a point $x$ of $\mathbb{R}^m$, with $m = n$ or $m = 2n$, and $\norm{v}$ is the Euclidean length of $x$.
8. So the unit sphere $S$ of $V$ becomes the unit sphere of $\mathbb{R}^m$, which is closed and bounded, and hence compact by the [[Heine-Borel theorem|07PFYV65MB]].
9. In these coordinates $q(v) = \sum_{j,k} c_k \overline{c_j} \ip{T e_k}{e_j}$ is a polynomial in the coordinates of $x$, so $q$ is continuous, because [[polynomial functions are continuous|0C5TP6RYSV]].
10. A [[continuous real function on a compact set attains its maximum|0V4DET59NW]]. So there are $\lambda = \max_S q$ and a point $u \in S$ with $q(u) = \lambda$. Let $u$ be any such point.
11. For a scalar $c$ we have $q(cv) = \abs{c}^2 q(v)$. Applying step 10 to $x / \norm{x}$ gives $q(x) \le \lambda \norm{x}^2$ for every $x \ne 0$, and also for $x = 0$.
12. Let $w \in V$ with $\ip{w}{u} = 0$, and let $t$ be real. Expanding, and using $\ip{Tw}{u} = \ip{w}{Tu} = \overline{\ip{Tu}{w}}$ from self-adjointness, gives
$$q(u + tw) = \lambda + 2t \operatorname{Re} \ip{Tu}{w} + t^2 q(w).$$
13. Pythagoras gives $\norm{u + tw}^2 = 1 + t^2 \norm{w}^2$. Write $a = \operatorname{Re} \ip{Tu}{w}$ and $b = q(w) - \lambda \norm{w}^2$. Step 11 at $x = u + tw$ becomes
$$\lambda + 2ta + t^2 q(w) \le \lambda + \lambda t^2 \norm{w}^2, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad 2ta + t^2 b \le 0 \text{ for all real } t.$$
14. Suppose $a \ne 0$, and take $t = sa$ with $s > 0$. Then $2sa^2 + s^2 a^2 b \le 0$, and division by $sa^2 > 0$ gives $2 + sb \le 0$. This is false for every $s > 0$ with $s \abs{b} < 2$, so $a = 0$.
15. If $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$, the vector $iw$ is also orthogonal to $u$. Step 14 for $iw$ gives $0 = \operatorname{Re} \ip{Tu}{iw} = \operatorname{Re}\big(-i \ip{Tu}{w}\big) = \operatorname{Im} \ip{Tu}{w}$.
16. Steps 14 and 15 give $\ip{Tu}{w} = 0$ for every $w$ orthogonal to $u$, over $\mathbb{R}$ and over $\mathbb{C}$.
17. Let $r = Tu - \ip{Tu}{u} u$. Since $\ip{u}{u} = 1$, we get $\ip{r}{u} = \ip{Tu}{u} - \ip{Tu}{u} = 0$, so step 16 applies to $w = r$ and gives $\ip{Tu}{r} = 0$.
18. Then $\norm{r}^2 = \ip{Tu}{r} - \ip{Tu}{u} \ip{u}{r} = 0$, because $\ip{u}{r} = \overline{\ip{r}{u}} = 0$. So $r = 0$.
19. Hence $Tu = \ip{Tu}{u} u = q(u) u = \lambda u$ with $u \ne 0$. So $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $T$, and every maximum point $u$ is an eigenvector for it.
20. A real symmetric $n \times n$ matrix $A$ with $n \ge 1$ gives the self-adjoint operator $x \mapsto Ax$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the dot product. Part 2 gives a real eigenvalue and a real eigenvector of $A$.

## Idea

For part 1, self-adjointness lets $T$ move from one side of the inner product to the other.
At an eigenvector this puts the factor $\lambda$ on one side and $\overline{\lambda}$ on the other, so $\lambda = \overline{\lambda}$.
For part 2, think of the sphere as a globe and of $q$ as a height.
At the highest point $u$, the height does not change to first order in any direction $w$ along the sphere, and that first-order change is $2 \operatorname{Re} \ip{Tu}{w}$.
So $Tu$ has no component along the sphere, which means that $Tu$ points along $u$.

## Variants

Part 2 also follows by complexification, if we accept the fundamental theorem of algebra.
For a real symmetric $A$, that theorem gives a complex eigenvalue, by [[the existence of eigenvalues over an algebraically closed field|08CJB2NQ92]], and part 1 shows that it is real.
Then $A - \lambda I$ is a real singular matrix, so it has a nonzero real null vector.

Lagrange multipliers give a third route.
A maximum of $x^{\mathsf{T}} A x$ under the constraint $x^{\mathsf{T}} x = 1$ has parallel gradients, $2Ax = 2\lambda x$.
Steps 12 to 19 prove this conclusion directly, without the multiplier theorem.
