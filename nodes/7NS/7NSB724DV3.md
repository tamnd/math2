## Proof

Part 1 compares $\ip{Tv}{v}$ with $\ip{v}{Tv}$ on an eigenvector.
Part 2 maximizes $\ip{Tv}{v}$ on the unit sphere, and it does not use the fundamental theorem of algebra.

1. Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional inner product space over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, with [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}$. Let $T \colon V \to V$ be [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]], so $\ip{Tv}{w} = \ip{v}{Tw}$ for all $v, w \in V$.
2. Part 1. If $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$, every scalar is real, so there is nothing to prove. Let $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$.
3. Let $\lambda$ be an [[eigenvalue|0ZND7NQ970]] of $T$. So there is $v \in V$ with $v \neq 0$ and $Tv = \lambda v$.
4. Linearity in the first slot gives $\ip{Tv}{v} = \ip{\lambda v}{v} = \lambda \ip{v}{v}$.
5. Conjugate symmetry and linearity in the first slot give $\ip{v}{\lambda v} = \overline{\lambda \ip{v}{v}} = \overline{\lambda}\, \ip{v}{v}$, because $\ip{v}{v}$ is real.
6. By step 1, $\ip{Tv}{v} = \ip{v}{Tv} = \ip{v}{\lambda v}$. So steps 4 and 5 give $\lambda \ip{v}{v} = \overline{\lambda} \ip{v}{v}$.
7. Since $v \neq 0$, positivity gives $\ip{v}{v} > 0$. Dividing by it gives $\lambda = \overline{\lambda}$.
8. A complex number equal to its [[complex conjugate|0BJV24C4SY]] has imaginary part $0$. So $\lambda$ is real.
9. Part 2. Let $V \neq 0$, and let $q(v) = \ip{Tv}{v}$. By step 1 and conjugate symmetry, $q(v) = \ip{v}{Tv} = \overline{q(v)}$, so $q$ takes real values.
10. Choose an orthonormal basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ of $V$, by [[the existence of an orthonormal basis|0Y85GYEGSF]]. For $v = \sum c_j e_j$, Pythagoras gives $\norm{v}^2 = \sum \abs{c_j}^2$.
11. Write each $c_j$ by its real and imaginary parts when $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$. Then $v$ corresponds to a point $x$ of $\mathbb{R}^m$, with $m = n$ or $m = 2n$, and $\norm{v}$ is the Euclidean length of $x$.
12. So the unit sphere $S$ of $V$ corresponds to the unit sphere of $\mathbb{R}^m$. It is closed and bounded, so it is compact by the [[Heine-Borel theorem|07PFYV65MB]].
13. In these coordinates, $q(v) = \sum_{j,k} c_k \overline{c_j} \ip{T e_k}{e_j}$ is a real polynomial in the coordinates of $x$. So $q$ is continuous, because [[polynomial functions are continuous|0C5TP6RYSV]].
14. A [[continuous real function on a compact set attains its bounds|0V4DET59NW]]. So there is $u \in S$ with $q(u) = \lambda$, where $\lambda = \max_S q$.
15. Since $q(cv) = c \overline{c}\, q(v) = \abs{c}^2 q(v)$ for scalars $c$, step 14 gives $q(x) \leq \lambda \norm{x}^2$ for every $x \in V$. For $x \neq 0$, apply step 14 to $x / \norm{x}$.
16. Let $w \in V$ with $\ip{w}{u} = 0$, and let $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Expanding gives $q(u + tw) = q(u) + t\big(\ip{Tu}{w} + \ip{Tw}{u}\big) + t^2 q(w)$.
17. Self-adjointness and conjugate symmetry give $\ip{Tw}{u} = \ip{w}{Tu} = \overline{\ip{Tu}{w}}$. So the middle term is $2t \operatorname{Re} \ip{Tu}{w}$.
18. Pythagoras gives $\norm{u + tw}^2 = 1 + t^2 \norm{w}^2$. By step 15, $\lambda + 2t a + t^2 q(w) \leq \lambda + \lambda t^2 \norm{w}^2$, where $a = \operatorname{Re} \ip{Tu}{w}$.
19. So $2ta + t^2 b \leq 0$ for all real $t$, where $b = q(w) - \lambda \norm{w}^2$.
20. Assume $a \neq 0$. Take $t = s a$ with $s > 0$. Then $2 s a^2 + s^2 a^2 b \leq 0$, and division by $s a^2 > 0$ gives $2 + s b \leq 0$.
21. For $s > 0$ small enough that $s \abs{b} < 2$, this is false. This contradiction shows $a = 0$, that is, $\operatorname{Re} \ip{Tu}{w} = 0$.
22. If $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$, the vector $iw$ is also orthogonal to $u$. Step 21 for $iw$ gives $0 = \operatorname{Re} \ip{Tu}{iw} = \operatorname{Re}\big(-i \ip{Tu}{w}\big) = \operatorname{Im} \ip{Tu}{w}$.
23. So $\ip{Tu}{w} = 0$ for every $w$ orthogonal to $u$, over $\mathbb{R}$ by step 21 and over $\mathbb{C}$ by steps 21 and 22.
24. Let $r = Tu - \ip{Tu}{u} u$. Since $\ip{u}{u} = 1$, we get $\ip{r}{u} = \ip{Tu}{u} - \ip{Tu}{u} = 0$. So step 23 applies to $w = r$, and $\ip{Tu}{r} = 0$.
25. Then $\norm{r}^2 = \ip{Tu}{r} - \ip{Tu}{u} \ip{u}{r} = 0 - 0 = 0$, because $\ip{u}{r} = \overline{\ip{r}{u}} = 0$. So $r = 0$.
26. Hence $Tu = \ip{Tu}{u} u = q(u) u = \lambda u$, and $u \neq 0$. So $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ with eigenvector $u$. The same argument applies at every point $u$ of $S$ with $q(u) = \lambda$.
27. For a real symmetric $n \times n$ matrix $A$ with $n \geq 1$, the operator $x \mapsto Ax$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the dot product is self-adjoint. Part 2 gives a real eigenvalue and a real eigenvector.

## Idea

For part 1, self-adjointness lets $T$ move from one side of the inner product to the other.
On an eigenvector, this gives the factor $\lambda$ on one side and $\overline{\lambda}$ on the other, so $\lambda = \overline{\lambda}$.
For part 2, at a maximum point $u$ of the quadratic form on the sphere, the derivative in every direction $w$ orthogonal to $u$ is $0$.
This derivative is $2 \operatorname{Re} \ip{Tu}{w}$, so $Tu$ has no component orthogonal to $u$, and $u$ is an eigenvector.

## Variants

- Part 2 by complexification. Let $A$ be real symmetric. Over $\mathbb{C}$, the fundamental theorem of algebra gives [[an eigenvalue|08CJB2NQ92]] $\lambda$, which is real by part 1. Then $A - \lambda I$ is a real singular matrix, so it has a nonzero real null vector.
- Part 2 by Lagrange multipliers. A maximum of $x^T A x$ subject to $x^T x = 1$ satisfies $2Ax = 2\lambda x$ for a multiplier $\lambda$. Steps 16 to 26 prove this without the multiplier theorem.
