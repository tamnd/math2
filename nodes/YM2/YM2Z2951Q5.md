## Definition

Let $H_0$ be a [[self-adjoint operator|0NEGN5CRSC]] on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$, the unperturbed [[Hamiltonian|VXDEQB46GV]].
Let $E_0$ be an isolated simple eigenvalue of $H_0$ with unit eigenvector $\psi_0$, and let $V$ be a symmetric operator, the perturbation.
The *Rayleigh–Schrödinger perturbation series* for $H(\lambda) = H_0 + \lambda V$ are the formal power series
$$E(\lambda) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \lambda^k E_k, \qquad \psi(\lambda) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \lambda^k \psi_k,$$
whose coefficients are fixed by the three conditions:

1. $E_0$ and $\psi_0$ are the given eigenvalue and eigenvector of $H_0$.
2. $(H_0 + \lambda V)\psi(\lambda) = E(\lambda)\psi(\lambda)$ holds coefficient by coefficient in $\lambda$.
3. $\ip{\psi_0}{\psi_k} = 0$ for every $k \ge 1$.

$E_k$ is the $k$-th order energy correction.

## Remarks

The first two corrections are $E_1 = \ip{\psi_0}{V\psi_0}$ and, when $H_0$ has an orthonormal eigenbasis $(\psi_j)$ with eigenvalues $(E^{(j)})$, $E_2 = \sum_{j \ne 0} \frac{\abs{\ip{\psi_j}{V\psi_0}}^2}{E_0 - E^{(j)}}$.

Example: let $H_0 = \frac12\left(-\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + x^2\right)$ on $L^2(\R)$, with ground energy $E_0 = \frac12$, and $V = x$.
Then $E_1 = 0$ by symmetry, and only the first excited state contributes to $E_2$, with $\abs{\ip{\psi_1}{x\psi_0}}^2 = \frac12$, so $E_2 = -\frac12$.
This agrees with the exact answer: $\frac{x^2}{2} + \lambda x = \frac{(x + \lambda)^2}{2} - \frac{\lambda^2}{2}$, so the ground energy is $\frac12 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2}$.

Non-example of the hypothesis: let $E_0$ be a degenerate eigenvalue.
Then $E_0 - E^{(j)}$ vanishes for some $j \ne 0$, and the formulas break down.
One must first diagonalise $V$ on the eigenspace, which is degenerate perturbation theory.

When $V$ is bounded, the series converge for small $\abs{\lambda}$ to an eigenvalue and an eigenvector of $H(\lambda)$, by the analytic theory of [[eigenvalue perturbation|VR89YHC0TX]].
For unbounded perturbations they may diverge: for $V = x^4$ in the example above, the energy series has radius of convergence $0$, although its first terms are still good approximations.
