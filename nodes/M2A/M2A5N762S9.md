## Proof

1. Let $G$ be a [[cyclic group|0B85Q41RP6]] of finite order $n$ with generator $g$. Let $d$ be a positive [[divisor|096PW7Q014]] of $n$, and put $c = n/d$.
2. By [[the order of a cyclic subgroup|031M8HX7DA]], $g$ has [[order|0JPYSD5E0B]] $n$. Also $G = \set{g^0, g^1, \ldots, g^{n-1}}$, and these $n$ elements are distinct.
3. By [[the order of a power|0VVPQNVM8S]], $\operatorname{ord}(g^k) = n/\gcd(n, k)$ for each $k$. So $\operatorname{ord}(g^k) = d$ if and only if $\gcd(n, k) = n/d = c$.
4. By steps 2 and 3, the number $N$ of elements of order $d$ is the number of $k \in \set{0, 1, \ldots, n-1}$ with $\gcd(n, k) = c$.
5. Each such $k$ is a multiple of $c$, since the [[greatest common divisor|02WF878BH1]] divides $k$. Write $k = cj$. As $n = cd$ and $c > 0$, $0 \leq k \leq n-1$ means $0 \leq j \leq d-1$.
6. Claim: for each integer $j$, $\gcd(n, cj) = c$ if and only if $\gcd(d, j) = 1$.
7. Suppose $\gcd(d, j) = 1$. By [[Bézout's identity|0DQVHMYAGA]], $dx + jy = 1$ for some integers $x, y$. Multiplying by $c$ gives $nx + (cj)y = c$.
8. So every common divisor of $n$ and $cj$ divides $c$, hence is at most $c$. Also $c$ divides $n$ and $cj$. So $\gcd(n, cj) = c$.
9. Suppose $f = \gcd(d, j) \neq 1$. Since $d \neq 0$, $f \geq 1$, so $f \geq 2$. Then $cf$ divides $cd = n$ and $cj$.
10. So $cf > c$ is a common divisor of $n$ and $cj$, and $\gcd(n, cj) \neq c$. This proves the claim.
11. By steps 4 to 10, $N$ is the number of $j \in \set{0, 1, \ldots, d-1}$ with $\gcd(d, j) = 1$.
12. By definition, [[Euler's totient|0VV04M41AN]] $\varphi(d)$ is the number of $j \in \set{1, 2, \ldots, d}$ with $\gcd(j, d) = 1$.
13. The two ranges differ only in $j = 0$ and $j = d$. Since every integer divides $0$, $\gcd(d, 0) = d = \gcd(d, d)$.
14. So $j = 0$ is counted in step 11 exactly when $j = d$ is counted in step 12. Hence $N = \varphi(d)$.

## Idea

The order of $g^k$ is $n/\gcd(n, k)$, so the elements of order $d$ are the $g^k$ with $\gcd(n, k) = n/d$. Writing $k = (n/d)j$, this condition says that $j$ is coprime to $d$. Counting such $j$ in one full range of length $d$ gives $\varphi(d)$.
