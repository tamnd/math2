## Statement

A modal formula is *valid* in a topological space $X$ if its truth set is $X$ under every valuation, with $\square$ read as [[interior|0RZH6W98HK]]; see [[topological semantics|HWJXMBNHQ0]].
$\mathrm{Log}(X)$ is the set of formulas valid in $X$.

**Theorem.**

1. Every topological space validates every theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$.
2. A modal formula is valid in the [[real line|08J2M1JYAG]] $\R$ if and only if it is a theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$. So $\mathrm{Log}(\R) = \mathsf{S4}$.
3. Hence $\mathsf{S4}$ is the logic of the class of all topological spaces.

The proof is in [[the proof of the McKinsey-Tarski theorem|VGWDMGPMB3]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. Points that are not isolated are needed. A discrete space validates $p \to \square p$, which is not a theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$.
2. A dense-in-itself space need not have logic $\mathsf{S4}$. An infinite indiscrete space has no isolated points, and its logic is $\mathsf{S5}$, since $\mathrm{Int}\, A$ is $X$ or $\emptyset$.
3. So the proof uses more than the absence of isolated points: it uses the order and metric structure of $\R$ to build interior maps onto finite frames.

### Consequences

1. $\mathrm{Log}(\R)$ is decidable, since $\mathsf{S4}$ is.
2. The McKinsey formula $\square \lozenge p \to \lozenge \square p$ and Grzegorczyk's formula fail on $\R$, as they are not theorems of $\mathsf{S4}$. With $V(p) = \mathbb{Q}$, both fail: the second at every irrational point.
3. Through the [[Gödel-McKinsey-Tarski translation|BXVNEN55MR]], intuitionistic propositional logic is the logic of the open sets of $\R$, a result that goes back to Tarski's topological semantics for intuitionistic logic of 1938.

### Converse

The converse question asks which spaces have logic exactly $\mathsf{S4}$.
Every space whose logic is $\mathsf{S4}$ must refute $p \to \square p$, so it has a point that is not isolated.
By the generalizations below, every dense-in-itself metric space has logic $\mathsf{S4}$, while finite spaces, discrete spaces and indiscrete spaces do not.

### Generalizations

1. McKinsey and Tarski proved $\mathrm{Log}(X) = \mathsf{S4}$ for every dense-in-itself separable metric space $X$, such as $\mathbb{Q}$, $\R^n$ and the Cantor space. The separability assumption was later removed.
2. Grigori Mints gave a direct proof for the Cantor space.
3. Philip Kremer proved strong completeness: every $\mathsf{S4}$-consistent set of formulas, even infinite, is satisfiable in every dense-in-itself metric space.
4. With the derived-set operator in place of interior, the logics of $\R$ and of $\R^n$ for $n \geq 2$ are different extensions of $\mathsf{K4}$.

## History

McKinsey and Tarski proved the theorem in 1944, in "The algebra of topology", published in the Annals of Mathematics.
They worked with closure algebras and proved that every finite well-connected closure algebra embeds into the closure algebra of a dense-in-itself separable metric space.
Later proofs replaced the algebraic embedding by interior maps from $\R$ onto finite frames.
Kremer's strong completeness theorem appeared in 2013.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Read "necessarily" as "true in a small interval around the point".
The theorem says that the laws of $\mathsf{S4}$ are exactly the laws that hold on the number line under this reading.
Every law of $\mathsf{S4}$ holds there, and every formula that $\mathsf{S4}$ does not prove can be refuted by a clever choice of regions of the line.

### L2 human

1. To refute $p \to \square p$ on $\R$, let $p$ be true only on $[0, 1]$. At $0$, $p$ holds but $\square p$ fails, since $0$ is not interior to $[0, 1]$.
2. To refute the $\mathsf{S5}$ axiom $\lozenge p \to \square \lozenge p$, let $p$ be true on $(0, 1)$.
3. Then $\lozenge p = [0, 1]$ and $\square \lozenge p = (0, 1)$, so the formula fails at $0$.

The typical use of the theorem is to prove that a formula is not a theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$ by drawing regions on $\R$.
A trap: the theorem is about the logic of $\R$, not about one valuation; a single valuation can make non-theorems true.

### L3 human

The theorem is the first completeness result for a natural mathematical structure instead of a class of frames.
Algebraically, it says that the interior algebra $(\mathcal{P}(\R), \mathrm{Int})$ generates the variety of all interior algebras.
It opened the field of spatial logics, where modal languages describe regions, connectedness and dimension, and where the choice between interior and derivative semantics matters.
