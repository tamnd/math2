## Statement

The theorem says that the laws of $\mathsf{S4}$ are exactly the laws of the real line, when $\square$ is read as interior.

A modal formula is valid in a topological space $X$ when its truth set is $X$ under every valuation, with $\square$ read as [[interior|0RZH6W98HK]], as in [[topological semantics|HWJXMBNHQ0]].
Let $\mathrm{Log}(X)$ be the set of formulas valid in $X$.

**Theorem.**

1. Every topological space validates every theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$.
2. A modal formula is valid in the [[real line|08J2M1JYAG]] $\mathbb{R}$ if and only if it is a theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$, so $\mathrm{Log}(\mathbb{R}) = \mathsf{S4}$.
3. Hence $\mathsf{S4}$ is the logic of the class of all topological spaces.

More generally, $\mathrm{Log}(X) = \mathsf{S4}$ for every dense-in-itself separable [[metric space|0VKD1TP7F3]] $X$, which is the form in which McKinsey and Tarski proved it.
The proof for $\mathbb{R}$ is in [[the proof of the McKinsey-Tarski theorem|VGWDMGPMB3]].

## Remarks

### A first use

Is $p \to \square p$ a theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$?
Let $p$ be true on $[0, 1]$ in $\mathbb{R}$.
At the point $0$, $p$ holds, but $0$ is not an interior point of $[0, 1]$, so $\square p$ fails.
By part 1 of the theorem, a theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$ could not fail anywhere, so $p \to \square p$ is not a theorem.
The same method refutes the $\mathsf{S5}$ axiom $\lozenge p \to \square \lozenge p$: with $V(p) = (0, 1)$, the closure is $[0, 1]$, its interior is $(0, 1)$, and the formula fails at $0$.

### Why the hypotheses on the space are needed

The space needs points that are not isolated.
A discrete space validates $p \to \square p$, which is not a theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$, so its logic is larger.

Having no isolated points is not enough on its own.
An infinite indiscrete space has no isolated points, yet its logic is $\mathsf{S5}$, because $\mathrm{Int}\, A$ is always $X$ or $\emptyset$.
The metric does real work: the proof uses it to build continuous open maps from the space onto every finite rooted preorder.

### What it gives you

The logic of $\mathbb{R}$ is decidable, since $\mathsf{S4}$ is.
Formulas that are not theorems of $\mathsf{S4}$ fail on $\mathbb{R}$, and often a simple valuation shows it.
With $V(p) = \mathbb{Q}$, the McKinsey formula $\square \lozenge p \to \lozenge \square p$ fails at every point.
Grzegorczyk's formula $\square(\square(p \to \square p) \to p) \to p$ fails at every irrational point, since there $\square(p \to \square p)$ is false everywhere and the antecedent holds.
Through the [[Gödel-McKinsey-Tarski translation|BXVNEN55MR]], intuitionistic propositional logic is the logic of the open sets of $\mathbb{R}$.

### The converse

Which spaces have logic exactly $\mathsf{S4}$?
Such a space must refute $p \to \square p$, so it has a point that is not isolated.
Later work dropped separability, so every dense-in-itself metric space has logic $\mathsf{S4}$.
Finite spaces, discrete spaces and indiscrete spaces do not have logic $\mathsf{S4}$.

### Generalizations

Grigori Mints gave a direct proof of completeness for the [[Cantor space|W9VYJRBF9N]].
Philip Kremer proved strong completeness: every $\mathsf{S4}$-consistent set of formulas, even an infinite one, is satisfiable in every dense-in-itself metric space.
With the derived-set operator in place of the interior, the logics of $\mathbb{R}$ and of $\mathbb{R}^2$ differ, so the derivative reading sees dimension.

### Watch out

The theorem is about validity, not about one valuation.
A single valuation can make a non-theorem true everywhere: with $V(p) = [0, 1]$ the McKinsey formula holds at every point.
To show that a formula is not a theorem you need one refuting valuation, and to show that it is a theorem you need every valuation.

## History

McKinsey and Tarski proved the theorem in 1944, in "The algebra of topology", published in the Annals of Mathematics.
They worked with closure algebras and proved the result for every dense-in-itself separable metric space.
Later proofs replaced their algebraic embedding by continuous open maps from $\mathbb{R}$ onto finite frames.
Kremer's strong completeness theorem appeared in 2013.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Read "necessarily" as "true in a small interval around the point".
The theorem says that the laws of $\mathsf{S4}$ are exactly the laws that hold on the number line under this reading.
Every law of $\mathsf{S4}$ holds there.
Every formula that $\mathsf{S4}$ does not prove can be refuted by a clever choice of regions of the line.

### L2 human

We refute the formula $\lozenge \square p \to \square p$ on $\mathbb{R}$, which is not a theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$.
Take $V(p) = [0, 1]$.
The interior is $(0, 1)$, and its closure is $[0, 1]$, so $\lozenge \square p$ holds on $[0, 1]$.
But $\square p$ holds only on $(0, 1)$, so at the point $1$ the antecedent is true and the consequent false.
This is the typical use of the theorem: to show that $\mathsf{S4}$ does not prove a formula, draw regions on the line and find a bad point.
The theorem promises that such regions exist for every non-theorem, though finding them can take thought.

### L3 human

The theorem was the first completeness result for a natural mathematical structure rather than for a class of frames.
Algebraically, it says that the interior algebra $(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}), \mathrm{Int})$ generates the variety of all interior algebras.
It opened the field of spatial logics, where modal languages describe regions, connectedness and dimension, and where the choice between interior and derivative semantics matters.
