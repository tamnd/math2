## Example

Let $V = C[-1, 1]$ be the real continuous functions on $[-1, 1]$, an [[inner product space|0QVNCJXRY3]] with $\ip{f}{g} = \int_{-1}^{1} f(x) g(x) \, dx$.
The [[Gram-Schmidt process|0VT6P0GNQR]] applied to $1, x, x^2, x^3$ gives, up to scaling, the [[Legendre polynomials|QFYGJVA996]]
$$P_0 = 1, \quad P_1 = x, \quad P_2 = \tfrac{1}{2} (3x^2 - 1), \quad P_3 = \tfrac{1}{2} (5x^3 - 3x),$$
which are pairwise orthogonal, with $\norm{P_n}^2 = \frac{2}{2n + 1}$.
Let $U_d$ be the polynomials of degree at most $d$, with orthogonal basis $P_0, \dots, P_d$.
By the [[best approximation theorem|0MCDDSCB9Z]], the polynomial $p \in U_d$ that minimizes $\int_{-1}^{1} (f - p)^2 \, dx$ is the [[orthogonal projection|0B8KZRT28M]] of $f$ onto $U_d$.
By the orthogonal-basis formula of the [[projection formulas|AK3JJ5HHG2]], it is
$$p = \sum_{n=0}^{d} c_n P_n, \qquad c_n = \frac{\ip{f}{P_n}}{\ip{P_n}{P_n}},$$
and the squared error is $\norm{f - p}^2 = \norm{f}^2 - \sum_{n=0}^{d} c_n^2 \norm{P_n}^2$.

1. $f(x) = \abs{x}$ and $d = 2$. Here $\ip{f}{P_0} = 1$, $\ip{f}{P_1} = 0$ because $f$ is even, and $\ip{f}{P_2} = \int_0^1 (3x^3 - x) \, dx = \frac{3}{4} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$.
2. So $c_0 = \frac{1}{2}$, $c_1 = 0$ and $c_2 = \frac{1/4}{2/5} = \frac{5}{8}$, and $p(x) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{5}{8} \cdot \frac{3x^2 - 1}{2} = \frac{3}{16} + \frac{15}{16} x^2$.
3. The squared error is $\norm{f}^2 - c_0^2 \cdot 2 - c_2^2 \cdot \frac{2}{5} = \frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{2} - \frac{5}{32} = \frac{1}{96}$.
4. For comparison, the polynomial $x^2$ has $\int_{-1}^{1} (\abs{x} - x^2)^2 \, dx = 2 \left( \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{5} \right) = \frac{1}{15}$, which is more than six times larger.
5. $f(x) = x^3$ and $d = 2$. Since $x^3 = \frac{2}{5} P_3 + \frac{3}{5} P_1$ and $P_3 \perp U_2$, the projection is $p(x) = \frac{3}{5} x$, and the error is $\frac{2}{5} P_3$, with $\norm{\frac{2}{5} P_3}^2 = \frac{4}{25} \cdot \frac{2}{7} = \frac{8}{175}$.
6. $f(x) = e^x$ and $d = 1$. Here $\ip{f}{P_0} = e - e^{-1}$ and $\ip{f}{P_1} = \int_{-1}^{1} x e^x \, dx = \frac{2}{e}$. So $c_0 = \sinh 1 \approx 1.1752$ and $c_1 = \frac{2/e}{2/3} = \frac{3}{e} \approx 1.1036$.
7. The best line is $p(x) = \sinh 1 + \frac{3}{e} x$, and its squared error is $\frac{e^2 - e^{-2}}{2} - 2 \sinh^2 1 - \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{9}{e^2} = 1 - \frac{7}{e^2} \approx 0.0527$.
8. The Taylor line $1 + x$ has squared error $\int_{-1}^{1} (e^x - 1 - x)^2 \, dx \approx 0.1212$, more than twice as large. Taylor polynomials are best near $0$; projections are best on average over the whole interval.

Fourier coefficients as projections.

9. Let $W = C[-\pi, \pi]$ with $\ip{f}{g} = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f g \, dx$. The functions $1, \cos x, \sin x, \dots, \cos Nx, \sin Nx$ are pairwise orthogonal, with $\norm{1}^2 = 2\pi$ and $\norm{\cos kx}^2 = \norm{\sin kx}^2 = \pi$ for $k \ge 1$.
10. The projection of $f$ onto the [[trigonometric polynomials|J3NJQ5FZJ5]] of degree at most $N$ is, by the same formula, $\frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^{N} (a_k \cos kx + b_k \sin kx)$, where $a_k = \frac{1}{\pi} \ip{f}{\cos kx}$ and $b_k = \frac{1}{\pi} \ip{f}{\sin kx}$. These are the [[Fourier coefficients|5NN2YZZR3V]].
11. For $f(x) = x$: $f$ is odd, so all $a_k = 0$, and integration by parts gives $b_k = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x \sin kx \, dx = \frac{2 (-1)^{k+1}}{k}$. So $b_1 = 2$ and $b_2 = -1$.
12. The squared errors are $\norm{x}^2 - \pi \sum_{k=1}^{N} b_k^2$ with $\norm{x}^2 = \frac{2\pi^3}{3}$. For $N = 1$ this is $\frac{2\pi^3}{3} - 4\pi \approx 8.1045$, and for $N = 2$ it is $\frac{2\pi^3}{3} - 5\pi \approx 4.9629$.

## Remarks

Every coefficient is computed on its own, because the basis is orthogonal.
To pass from degree $d$ to $d + 1$, we add one term and keep the old coefficients; with the basis $1, x, x^2, \dots$ we would have to solve a new linear system.
The squared error decreases as the degree grows, since $U_d \subseteq U_{d+1}$.
The inequality $\sum_k \pi b_k^2 \le \norm{f}^2$ in step 12 is [[Bessel's inequality|0RBT5PB74K]].
For $f(x) = x$, the errors tend to $0$ by [[mean-square convergence of Fourier series|3DGHC142FT]], so equality holds in the limit, which is [[Parseval's identity|047RFCNVSK]]: $4\pi \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{k^2} = \frac{2\pi^3}{3}$, that is, $\sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{k^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$.
The theory of [[Fourier series|G12KXDHPPS]] and of [[orthogonal polynomials|8TXASARQN1]] in general belongs to analysis.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In a space of functions, the integral of a product plays the role of the dot product.
Then the best approximation of a function by a polynomial, in the sense of the average squared error, is an orthogonal projection.
With an orthogonal basis, such as the Legendre polynomials or sines and cosines, each coefficient comes from one integral.

### L2 human

The method is: choose an orthogonal basis of the approximating space, compute $c_n = \ip{f}{P_n} / \ip{P_n}{P_n}$, and add up.
The error formula $\norm{f}^2 - \sum c_n^2 \norm{P_n}^2$ checks the result without one more integral of $(f - p)^2$.
The typical use is to approximate a function on a whole interval, as in signal processing and numerical analysis.
The trap is to forget the factor $\ip{P_n}{P_n}$, since the Legendre polynomials are orthogonal but not orthonormal.
Another trap is to expect the best polynomial to agree with $f$ at $0$: here $p(0) = 3/16$, while $\abs{0} = 0$.
