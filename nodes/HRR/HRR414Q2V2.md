## Definition

Let $\mu > 0$ and let a target move on a circular [[Kepler orbit|BXFSBB8Q46]] of radius $R$, with mean motion $n = \sqrt{\mu / R^3}$.
Use the [[rotating frame|HNXK6CJ5B2]] centred at the target.
In it, $x$ points radially outwards, $y$ points along the direction of motion, and $z$ is normal to the orbital plane.
Let $(x, y, z)$ be the position of a chaser in this frame, and assume it is small compared with $R$.
The *Clohessy–Wiltshire equations* are the [[ordinary differential equations|0NT64JV96A]]
$$x'' - 3 n^2 x - 2 n y' = 0, \qquad y'' + 2 n x' = 0, \qquad z'' + n^2 z = 0.$$
They come from the exact relative equations of motion by keeping only the terms of first order in $(x, y, z)$.

## Remarks

The terms $2 n y'$ and $2 n x'$ come from the [[Coriolis force|6HZRX3WSQD]], and the term $3 n^2 x$ combines the centrifugal force with the tidal part of gravity.
The out-of-plane motion is a harmonic oscillation with the orbital period.
The in-plane motion combines an oscillation of the same period, a constant offset and a secular drift along $y$.

Example: $x(t) = x_0$, $y(t) = y_0 - \frac{3}{2} n x_0 t$, $z(t) = 0$ is a solution.
Check: $x'' = 0$ and $y' = -\frac{3}{2} n x_0$, so $x'' - 3 n^2 x - 2 n y' = -3 n^2 x_0 + 3 n^2 x_0 = 0$; and $y'' + 2 n x' = 0$.
A chaser below the target, $x_0 < 0$, thus drifts ahead of it, as a lower circular orbit is faster.

Non-example: $x(t) = x_0 \ne 0$ with $y$ constant and $z = 0$ is not a solution, since the first equation would give $-3 n^2 x_0 = 0$.

The same equations are also called Hill's equations, after Hill's work on the motion of the Moon.
