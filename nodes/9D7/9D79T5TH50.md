## Example

Consider the claim "a group of order $4$ is unique [[up to|CBC72TX3NQ]] isomorphism".
We show that it is false with two groups.
The first is $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} = \{[0], [1], [2], [3]\}$ with addition modulo $4$, as in [[integers modulo $n$|03VFZ2WETT]].
The second is the [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]] $V = \{(0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1)\}$ with addition modulo $2$ in each coordinate.

1. Both groups have $4$ elements.
2. In $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, the element $[1]$ has [[order|0WE2NHZJ7B]] $4$.
   Its multiples are
   $$1 \cdot [1] = [1], \quad 2 \cdot [1] = [2], \quad 3 \cdot [1] = [3], \quad 4 \cdot [1] = [0],$$
   and the first three are not $[0]$.
3. In $V$, every element $v = (a, b)$ satisfies $v + v = (2a, 2b) = (0, 0)$.
   So every element of $V$ has order $1$ or $2$.
4. A [[group isomorphism|08MG7H8JW5]] $\varphi$ preserves the order of each element.
   It is a homomorphism, so $\varphi(k g) = k \varphi(g)$, and it is injective, so $k g = 0$ exactly when $k \varphi(g) = 0$.
5. So an isomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \to V$ would send $[1]$ to an element of order $4$, and $V$ has none.
6. So the two groups are not isomorphic, and "the group of order $4$" is not a legal phrase, even up to isomorphism.

## Remarks

The proof works by finding an invariant, "has an element of order $4$", that isomorphisms preserve and that the two groups do not share.
This is the standard way to show that two structures are not isomorphic.

The claim fails for $4$, but the right repair is a count of classes.
Every group of order $4$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ or to $V$, so the groups of order $4$ form exactly two isomorphism classes.
For a prime $p$ the situation is different: by Lagrange's theorem every element other than the identity of a group of order $p$ has order $p$, so the group is cyclic and isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.
So "the group of order $p$, up to isomorphism" is a legal phrase.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a rectangular card that is not a square, lying in a frame on a table.
It fits the frame in four ways: as it is, after a half turn, after a flip across the long side, and after a flip across the short side.
Each of these moves, done twice, brings the card back to the start.
Now think of a square card and quarter turns: it takes four quarter turns to come back.
Both have four moves, but the moves combine in different ways.
So the number of moves does not tell us how the moves behave.

### L2 human

We identify the group of units modulo $8$, which is $\{[1], [3], [5], [7]\}$ under multiplication, so it has order $4$.
Square each element:
$$3^2 = 9, \qquad 5^2 = 25, \qquad 7^2 = 49,$$
and each of these is $1$ modulo $8$.
So every element has order $1$ or $2$, and no element has order $4$.
The group is not cyclic, so by the classification above it is isomorphic to $V$.
The typical use is to count the elements of each order, which separates or identifies small groups.
Watch out: the order of a group does not determine the group, so you must find an invariant before you claim an isomorphism.
