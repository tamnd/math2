## Statement

Let $G$ be a [[group|G5S1M9]].

1. Let $(H_i)_{i \in I}$ be a family of [[subgroups|0X1JXT47GA]] of $G$, indexed by a nonempty set $I$. Then the intersection $\bigcap_{i \in I} H_i$ is a subgroup of $G$.
2. Let $H$ and $K$ be subgroups of $G$. Then the union $H \cup K$ is a subgroup of $G$ if and only if $H \subseteq K$ or $K \subseteq H$.
3. $G$ is not the union of two proper subgroups.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. In part 1, the index set $I$ must be nonempty. If $I$ is empty, the condition "$x \in H_i$ for each $i \in I$" holds for every object $x$, so the intersection is not a subset of $G$. By a common convention, the intersection of the empty family of subgroups of $G$ is $G$ itself.
2. In part 3, the number two cannot be replaced by three. A group can be the union of three proper subgroups. The [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]] $V = \{e, a, b, c\}$ is the union of its three subgroups $\{e, a\}$, $\{e, b\}$ and $\{e, c\}$.
3. In part 2, the sets $H$ and $K$ must be subgroups. Without this hypothesis, both directions fail.
   - The sets $\{0, 1\} \subseteq \{0, 1, 2\}$ in $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ are nested, but their union $\{0, 1, 2\}$ is not a subgroup.
   - The even integers $2\mathbb{Z}$ and the odd integers $1 + 2\mathbb{Z}$ are not nested, but their union $\mathbb{Z}$ is a subgroup.

### Consequences

1. For each subset $S$ of $G$, the intersection of all subgroups of $G$ that contain $S$ is a subgroup. It is the [[subgroup generated by S|0RP7VWQ0YN]], the smallest subgroup that contains $S$.
2. Any two subgroups $H$ and $K$ have a largest common subgroup, $H \cap K$. With the subgroup $\langle H \cup K \rangle$ generated by $H \cup K$ as the smallest common upper bound, this makes the subgroups of $G$ a [[lattice|0B8BW4YWTF]].
3. To show that $G$ has an element outside two proper subgroups $H$ and $K$, part 3 is enough: such an element always exists.

### Converse

The converse of part 1 is false.
The intersection of two subsets can be a subgroup when the subsets are not subgroups.
In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the sets $\{0, 1\}$ and $\{0, -1\}$ are not subgroups, but their intersection $\{0\}$ is a subgroup.

### Generalizations

1. The union of a nonempty chain of subgroups is a subgroup. Here a chain is a family in which any two members are nested. This fact is the main step when [[Zorn's lemma|04RXTQA5ZA]] is used to find maximal subgroups.
2. Part 1 holds for many other structures: subspaces of a vector space, subrings of a ring and, in general, [[substructures|676H7EGTKJ]] in [[universal algebra|W82R6N0GM9]].
3. A theorem of G. Scorza (1926) says that a group is the union of three proper subgroups if and only if it has a quotient group isomorphic to the Klein four-group.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a subgroup as a club inside a group that passes three tests: it contains the identity, it is closed under products, and it is closed under inverses.
If we take the members that several such clubs have in common, the common part passes the three tests too.
For example, in the integers under addition, the multiples of $2$ and the multiples of $3$ have the multiples of $6$ in common, and the multiples of $6$ form a subgroup.
If we join two clubs instead, the result can fail.
The number $2$ is a multiple of $2$ and the number $3$ is a multiple of $3$, but their sum $5$ is a multiple of neither.

### L2 human

We work in the [[integers|0C4JS9RF5M]] under addition.

1. Intersection: an integer lies in $2\mathbb{Z} \cap 3\mathbb{Z}$ if and only if it is divisible by $2$ and by $3$, that is, by $6$. So $2\mathbb{Z} \cap 3\mathbb{Z} = 6\mathbb{Z}$, which is a subgroup.
2. Union: $4 \in 4\mathbb{Z}$ and $6 \in 6\mathbb{Z}$, but $4 + 6 = 10$ is in neither set. So $4\mathbb{Z} \cup 6\mathbb{Z}$ is not closed under addition.
3. Nested union: $4\mathbb{Z} \subseteq 2\mathbb{Z}$, so $4\mathbb{Z} \cup 2\mathbb{Z} = 2\mathbb{Z}$, which is a subgroup.

The typical use of part 1 is to define the smallest subgroup with a given property, as the intersection of all subgroups with that property.

A trap: the union of two subgroups is almost never a subgroup.
To get a subgroup that contains both $H$ and $K$, take the subgroup that $H \cup K$ generates.

### L3 human

Part 1 says that the subgroups of $G$ form a closure system on the set $G$.
So the map that sends a subset $S$ to the subgroup it generates is a closure operator, and the subgroups form a [[complete lattice|D7A0ZGZT5S]].
Part 2 shows that the join in this lattice is almost never the set union.
The union of a directed family of subgroups is a subgroup, because the operations of a group take finitely many inputs.
This finitary property is what makes the closure operator algebraic.
