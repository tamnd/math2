## Summary

Quantified modal logic, also called first-order modal logic, is [[first-order logic|086QKPF0A6]] with the operators $\square$ and $\lozenge$ of [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]] added to the formation rules.
Its formulas are built from atomic formulas $P(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ and $x = y$ by $\neg$, $\wedge$, $\to$, $\forall x$, $\exists x$, $\square$ and $\lozenge$.
A Kripke model with domains is a tuple $\mathfrak{M} = (W, R, D, I)$.
Here $(W, R)$ is a Kripke frame, that is, a nonempty set of worlds with a binary accessibility relation.
The map $D$ gives each world $w$ a nonempty domain $D(w)$.
The map $I$ gives each $n$-ary predicate $P$ and each world $w$ a set $I(P, w) \subseteq U^n$, where $U = \bigcup_{w \in W} D(w)$.
Under an assignment $\sigma$ of elements of $U$ to the variables, $\mathfrak{M}, w \models_\sigma \forall x \varphi$ holds when $\mathfrak{M}, w \models_{\sigma[x \mapsto d]} \varphi$ for every $d \in D(w)$, and $\mathfrak{M}, w \models_\sigma \square \varphi$ holds when $\mathfrak{M}, v \models_\sigma \varphi$ for every $v$ with $w R v$.
The model has constant domains when $D(w) = U$ for every $w$, and varying domains otherwise.
Quantified modal logic matters because it can say of an individual that it has a property necessarily, so it is the setting for the questions of existence, identity and essence across possible worlds.

## Motivation

Propositional modal logic treats "every number is necessarily even or odd" and "necessarily every number is even or odd" as one unanalysed statement.
The two statements differ in the order of $\forall x$ and $\square$.
The first is de re: it says of each object that it has a property necessarily.
The second is de dicto: it says that a whole sentence is necessary.
Philosophers and logicians want to know when such statements are equivalent, and the answer depends on what exists at each world.
This leads to Kripke models in which each world has its own domain of objects.
The same tools apply when the worlds are states of a computation and the objects are the data that exist in each state.

## Notation

1. A frame is $\mathfrak{F} = (W, R)$, and a frame with domains is $(W, R, D)$. A model is $\mathfrak{M} = (W, R, D, I)$.
2. Truth at a world under an assignment is $\mathfrak{M}, w \models_\sigma \varphi$. For a sentence the assignment does not matter, and we write $\mathfrak{M}, w \models \varphi$.
3. $\sigma[x \mapsto d]$ is the assignment that agrees with $\sigma$ except that it sends $x$ to $d$.
4. For a propositional modal logic $\mathsf{L}$, such as $\mathsf{K}$, $\mathsf{S4}$ or $\mathsf{S5}$, the logic $\mathsf{QL}$ (also written $\mathsf{Q.L}$ or $\mathsf{L}$ plus first-order logic) has the axioms of $\mathsf{L}$, the axioms of first-order logic with identity, modus ponens, generalization and [[necessitation|G989VP2NGP]].
5. The existence predicate $E x$ abbreviates $\exists y \, (y = x)$, with $y$ different from $x$. It holds at $w$ under $\sigma$ exactly when $\sigma(x) \in D(w)$.
6. The domain conditions on a frame with domains are: increasing (or expanding) when $w R v$ implies $D(w) \subseteq D(v)$, and decreasing when $w R v$ implies $D(v) \subseteq D(w)$.

Some books let $I(P, w)$ be a subset of $D(w)^n$ only, and some books allow empty domains.
Some books evaluate a formula at $w$ only under assignments into $D(w)$, as Hughes and Cresswell do.
These choices change which formulas are valid, so a result must be read with its semantics.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[Necessity of identity in quantified modal logic|CYG8CJ45BM]]: the formula $x = y \to \square (x = y)$ is valid on every frame with domains, because a variable denotes the same object at every world.
2. [[A model where the de dicto formula holds and the de re formula fails|WSHWNR803P]]: in a two-world model, $\square \exists x P x$ is true at a world while $\exists x \square P x$ is false there. So the order of $\square$ and $\exists$ matters.
3. The [[Barcan formula|9PYDNBA5X4]] $\forall x \square \varphi \to \square \forall x \varphi$ is valid on every frame with constant domains. With varying domains it is valid exactly on the frames with decreasing domains, and its converse is valid exactly on the frames with increasing domains.
4. A Kripke model of quantified $\mathsf{S5}$ with constant domain $\N$ in which each world is a possible way to colour the numbers. Then $\exists x \lozenge P x$ and $\lozenge \exists x P x$ have the same truth value at every world, as the Barcan formula and its converse in the $\lozenge$ form predict.

### Non-examples

1. [[A non-rigid name breaks the necessity of identity|CWGY8F92MH]]: if a constant may denote different objects at different worlds, the formula $a = b \to \square (a = b)$ can fail. Such a structure does not meet the condition that terms are rigid, which this page assumes.
2. A propositional Kripke model $(W, R, V)$ is not a model of quantified modal logic: it has no domains, so a formula $\forall x \varphi$ has no meaning in it.
3. A first-order structure alone is not a model either. It has no accessibility relation, so it gives no meaning to $\square \varphi$.

### Basic properties

1. Rigidity of variables. An assignment does not depend on the world, so a variable denotes the same object at every world. Hence $x = y \to \square (x = y)$ and $x \neq y \to \square (x \neq y)$ are valid on every frame with domains. The [[rigid designator|HGR6C3KA34]] is the philosophical name for this behaviour of names.
2. Converse Barcan formula. The converse Barcan formula $\square \forall x \varphi \to \forall x \square \varphi$ is derivable in $\mathsf{QK}$ with the classical quantifier rules. The derivation is on the [[Barcan formula|9PYDNBA5X4]] page. Its validity needs increasing domains. So a semantics for the classical rules with varying domains must have increasing domains, and it must evaluate a formula at $w$ only under assignments into $D(w)$, as Hughes and Cresswell do.
3. Free logic for varying domains. With arbitrary varying domains, classical instantiation $\forall x \varphi \to \varphi(y / x)$ fails at a world $w$ when $\sigma(y) \notin D(w)$. A logic for all varying-domain frames uses the free-logic form $\forall x \varphi \to (E y \to \varphi(y / x))$, with the [[existence predicate|XK9WZW5B68]] $E$.
4. Barcan formula in $\mathsf{S5}$. In $\mathsf{QS5}$ the Barcan formula is derivable, so $\mathsf{QS5}$ forces constant domains on each cluster of worlds. The derivation uses only the axiom B, $p \to \square \lozenge p$.
5. Completeness. $\mathsf{QK}$ plus the Barcan formula is sound and complete for the class of all frames with constant domains. The proof is a canonical model construction with Henkin witnesses, and the Barcan formula is the axiom that lets one domain serve all worlds.
6. Incompleteness. Kripke completeness of a propositional modal logic $\mathsf{L}$ does not transfer to $\mathsf{QL}$. Franco Montagna showed in 1984 that the quantified version $\mathsf{QGL}$ of the provability logic $\mathsf{GL}$ is not complete for its Kripke frames with domains, although $\mathsf{GL}$ is complete for the finite transitive irreflexive frames. So the canonical model method, which works for $\mathsf{QK}$, does not work for every quantified logic.
7. Undecidability. A formula without $\square$ is valid in all Kripke models with domains exactly when it is valid in first-order logic, so validity is undecidable by [[Church's theorem|VA6BNNY2YC]]. Saul Kripke showed in 1962 that even the monadic fragment of quantified modal logic is undecidable, while monadic first-order logic is decidable.

### Possibilism, actualism and counterparts

The choice of domains has a philosophical reading.
Constant domains fit [[modal possibilism|RXK66SDTAR]]: the quantifier ranges over all possible objects at each world.
Varying domains fit [[modal actualism|9ZK5VAWDKN]]: the quantifier at $w$ ranges over the objects that exist at $w$.
[[Counterpart theory|KHGAGR73ZM]], due to David Lewis, drops the assumption that one object lives in many worlds: each object lives in one world, and a counterpart relation links objects of different worlds.
In counterpart semantics the necessity of identity can fail, because one object can have two counterparts in another world.

### History

Ruth Barcan (later Ruth Barcan Marcus) published the first axiomatic systems of quantified modal logic in 1946, as extensions of systems of strict implication of Lewis.
Her system had the Barcan formula as an axiom, and her 1947 paper proved the necessity of identity.
Saul Kripke gave the semantics with varying domains in 1963, and in that semantics both the Barcan formula and its converse can fail.

### Common errors

1. Reading $\exists x \square P x$ and $\square \exists x P x$ as the same statement. The first asks for one object that has $P$ at every accessible world. The second only asks that each accessible world has some object with $P$.
2. Using classical instantiation with varying domains. If the value of $y$ does not exist at $w$, then $\forall x \varphi$ can be true at $w$ while $\varphi(y / x)$ is false there.
3. Substituting non-rigid terms inside $\square$. From $a = b$ and $\square (a = a)$ we may conclude $\square (a = b)$ only when $a$ and $b$ are rigid.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Ordinary modal logic talks about whole statements being necessary or possible.
Quantified modal logic also talks about objects, with "for every $x$" and "there is an $x$".
Picture two possible worlds: in the first one there are two cats, in the second one there are three cats.
"Necessarily there is a cat" is true, because each world has a cat.
"There is a cat that exists necessarily" can be false, if no single cat lives in both worlds.
So the order of "necessarily" and "there is" changes the meaning.

### L2 human

We evaluate a de re and a de dicto formula in one model.

1. Let $W = \{w, v\}$ and $R = \{(w, w), (w, v), (v, v)\}$, with $D(w) = \{a, b\}$ and $D(v) = \{a, b\}$.
2. Let $I(P, w) = \{a, b\}$ and $I(P, v) = \{b\}$.
3. At $w$, the formula $\forall x \square P x$ needs $\square P x$ for $x \mapsto a$ and for $x \mapsto b$.
4. For $x \mapsto a$: $w R v$ and $a \notin I(P, v)$, so $\square P x$ fails at $w$.
5. Hence $\mathfrak{M}, w \not\models \forall x \square P x$.
6. The formula $\square \forall x P x$ needs $\forall x P x$ at $w$ and at $v$. At $v$ it fails, since $a \in D(v)$ and $a \notin I(P, v)$.
7. The formula $\square \exists x P x$ holds at $w$: at $w$ the witness is $a$, and at $v$ the witness is $b$.
8. The formula $\exists x \square P x$ also holds at $w$, with the witness $b$, because $b \in I(P, w)$ and $b \in I(P, v)$.

The typical use of such a model is to show that a formula is not valid, by one world where it fails.
A trap: the witness for $\exists x$ must lie in the domain of the world where the quantifier is evaluated, not only in $U$.

### L3 human

Quantified modal logic combines a Kripke frame with a family of first-order structures, one for each world.
Abstractly, a Kripke model with domains is a functor from the frame, seen as a category, to sets, with inclusions as the maps when domains increase.
This view leads to functor and sheaf semantics, which generalize the Kripke semantics of this page.
The same idea underlies the Kripke semantics of intuitionistic predicate logic, where the frame is a preorder and the domains increase.
The interaction of quantifiers and modalities is measured by the [[Barcan formula|9PYDNBA5X4]], and the multi-dimensional view of the same logics appears in the [[products of modal logics|JA2EBWVW8A]], where $\mathsf{S5} \times \mathsf{S5}$ is the modal form of a fragment of first-order logic with two variables.
