## Summary

Quantified modal logic lets us say of an object that it has a property necessarily, and not only that a whole sentence is necessary.
"Necessarily some cat exists" and "some cat exists necessarily" then come apart, and the difference depends on which objects exist at which worlds.

Quantified modal logic, also called first-order modal logic, is [[first-order logic|086QKPF0A6]] with the operators $\square$ and $\lozenge$ of [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]] added.
A *Kripke model with domains* is a tuple $\mathfrak{M} = (W, R, D, I)$, where $(W, R)$ is a [[Kripke frame|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]], $D$ gives each world $w$ a nonempty domain $D(w)$, and $I$ gives each $n$-ary predicate $P$ and world $w$ a set $I(P, w) \subseteq U^n$, with $U = \bigcup_{w} D(w)$.
Assignments $\sigma$ send variables into $U$, and the two new clauses are these:
$$w \models_\sigma \forall x \varphi \iff w \models_{\sigma[x \mapsto d]} \varphi \text{ for all } d \in D(w),$$
$$w \models_\sigma \square \varphi \iff v \models_\sigma \varphi \text{ for all } v \text{ with } w R v$$
Identity $x = y$ is true at $w$ under $\sigma$ when $\sigma(x) = \sigma(y)$.
The model has *constant domains* when $D(w) = U$ for every $w$, and *varying domains* otherwise.

## Motivation

Propositional modal logic treats "every number is necessarily even or odd" and "necessarily every number is even or odd" as unanalyzed statements.
The two differ in the order of $\forall x$ and $\square$.
The first is *de re*: it says of each object that it has a property necessarily.
The second is *de dicto*: it says that a whole sentence is necessary.
When the two are equivalent depends on what exists at each world, and this leads to models in which each world has its own domain.

The same tools apply when the worlds are states of a computation and the objects are the data that exist in each state.
The exchange of $\forall x$ and $\square$ is measured by the [[Barcan formula|9PYDNBA5X4]], and two-dimensional versions of these logics return in the [[products of modal logics|JA2EBWVW8A]].

## Notation

We write $\mathfrak{M}, w \models_\sigma \varphi$ for truth at a world under an assignment, and drop $\sigma$ for sentences.
The assignment $\sigma[x \mapsto d]$ agrees with $\sigma$ except that it sends $x$ to $d$.
For a propositional logic $\mathsf{L}$ such as $\mathsf{K}$, $\mathsf{S4}$ or $\mathsf{S5}$, the logic $\mathsf{QL}$ (also written $\mathsf{Q.L}$) adds the axioms of first-order logic with identity, generalization and [[necessitation|G989VP2NGP]].
The existence predicate $E x$ abbreviates $\exists y \, (y = x)$, and it holds at $w$ exactly when $\sigma(x) \in D(w)$.
Domains are *increasing* (or expanding) when $w R v$ implies $D(w) \subseteq D(v)$, and *decreasing* when $w R v$ implies $D(v) \subseteq D(w)$.
Books differ: some take $I(P, w) \subseteq D(w)^n$, some allow empty domains, and some evaluate at $w$ only under assignments into $D(w)$.
These choices change which formulas are valid, so read every result together with its semantics.

## Remarks

### De re and de dicto in one model

Let $W = \{w, v\}$ with $R = W \times W$, constant domain $\{a, b\}$, $I(P, w) = \{a\}$ and $I(P, v) = \{b\}$.
At each world some object has $P$, so $\square \exists x P x$ holds at $w$.
For $x \mapsto a$, the formula $P x$ fails at $v$, and for $x \mapsto b$ it fails at $w$, so $\exists x \square P x$ fails at $w$.
So the de dicto formula does not give the de re one, even in $\mathsf{S5}$ with a constant domain; [[the de dicto and de re example|WSHWNR803P]] works this through step by step.
The other direction, $\exists x \square P x \to \square \exists x P x$, holds whenever domains increase, because the witness still exists at each successor.

### Identity is rigid

Assignments do not depend on worlds, so a variable names the same object everywhere.
Hence $x = y \to \square (x = y)$ is valid on every frame with domains, and it is derivable from $x = x$ by necessitation and substitution of identicals; see [[necessity of identity|CYG8CJ45BM]].
The same reason makes $x \neq y \to \square (x \neq y)$ valid.
If constants may name different objects at different worlds, the first formula fails, as [[a non-rigid name breaks the necessity of identity|CWGY8F92MH]] shows.
A name that keeps its object at every world is a [[rigid designator|HGR6C3KA34]].

### How domains shape the logic

The [[Barcan formula|9PYDNBA5X4]] $\forall x \square \varphi \to \square \forall x \varphi$ is valid on a frame with domains exactly when domains decrease, and its converse exactly when they increase.
The converse is derivable in $\mathsf{QK}$ with the classical quantifier rules.
So a semantics for those rules with varying domains needs increasing domains and assignments into $D(w)$, or else free-logic rules.
In free logic, instantiation becomes $\forall x \varphi \to (E y \to \varphi(y / x))$, with the [[existence predicate|XK9WZW5B68]].
In $\mathsf{QS5}$ the Barcan formula is derivable, using only the axiom B, so $\mathsf{QS5}$ forces constant domains along $R$.

### Completeness, incompleteness and undecidability

For the language without identity, $\mathsf{QK}$ plus the Barcan formula is complete for the frames with constant domains, by a canonical model with Henkin witnesses.
Completeness of $\mathsf{L}$ does not transfer to $\mathsf{QL}$ in general.
Franco Montagna showed in 1984 that $\mathsf{QGL}$, the quantified provability logic, is not complete for its Kripke frames with domains.
A formula without $\square$ is valid in all models exactly when it is valid in first-order logic, so validity is undecidable by [[Church's theorem|VA6BNNY2YC]].
Saul Kripke showed in 1962 that even the monadic fragment is undecidable, although monadic first-order logic is decidable.

### Possibilism, actualism and counterparts

Constant domains fit [[modal possibilism|RXK66SDTAR]]: the quantifier ranges over all possible objects at each world.
Varying domains fit [[modal actualism|9ZK5VAWDKN]]: the quantifier at $w$ ranges over the objects that exist at $w$.
[[Counterpart theory|KHGAGR73ZM]], due to David Lewis, lets each object live in one world only, and links objects of different worlds by a counterpart relation.
There the necessity of identity can fail, because one object can have two counterparts in another world.

### Watch out

Three slips are common.
First, $\exists x \square P x$ and $\square \exists x P x$ are different: the first needs one object with $P$ at every accessible world, the second only some object at each.
Second, classical instantiation fails with varying domains: if $\sigma(y) \notin D(w)$, then $\forall x P x$ can hold at $w$ while $P y$ fails there.
Third, substituting non-rigid terms inside $\square$ is unsound: $a = b$ and $\square(a = a)$ give $\square(a = b)$ only for rigid $a$ and $b$.

### History

Ruth Barcan (later Ruth Barcan Marcus) published the first axiomatic systems of quantified modal logic in 1946, as extensions of Lewis's systems of strict implication.
Her system had the Barcan formula as an axiom, and her 1947 paper proved the necessity of identity.
Saul Kripke gave the semantics with varying domains in 1963, and in it both the Barcan formula and its converse can fail.

### Try it

First, in the two-world model of the first subsection, is $\square \forall x P x$ true at $w$?
No: at $w$ the object $b$ lacks $P$, and $w$ sees itself.

Second, with $D(w) = \{a\}$, $D(v) = \{a, b\}$, $w R v$ only, and $I(P, v) = \{a\}$, does $\forall x \square P x$ hold at $w$?
Yes: the only object at $w$ is $a$, and $a$ has $P$ at the only successor $v$; the new object $b$ is not quantified over at $w$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Ordinary modal logic talks about whole statements being necessary or possible.
Quantified modal logic also talks about objects, with "for every $x$" and "there is an $x$".
Picture two possible worlds, the first with two cats and the second with three other cats.
"Necessarily there is a cat" is true, because each world has a cat.
"There is a cat that necessarily exists" is false, because no cat lives in both worlds.
So the order of "necessarily" and "there is" changes the meaning.

### L2 human

We test a de re and a de dicto formula in one model.
Let $W = \{w, v\}$ with $R = \{(w, w), (w, v), (v, v)\}$, $D(w) = D(v) = \{a, b\}$, $I(P, w) = \{a, b\}$ and $I(P, v) = \{b\}$.
For $\forall x \square P x$ at $w$ we need $\square P x$ under $x \mapsto a$, but $w R v$ and $a \notin I(P, v)$, so it fails.
For $\square \forall x P x$ at $w$ we need $\forall x P x$ at $v$, which fails at $a$ as well.
For $\exists x \square P x$ at $w$ the witness $b$ works, since $b \in I(P, w)$ and $b \in I(P, v)$.
For $\square \exists x P x$ at $w$, the witnesses are $a$ at $w$ and $b$ at $v$.
The typical use of such a model is to refute a formula with one world where it fails.
Watch out: the witness for $\exists x$ must lie in the domain of the world where the quantifier is read, not merely in $U$.

### L3 human

A Kripke model with domains attaches a first-order structure to each world of a Kripke frame.
With increasing domains it is a functor from the frame, seen as a category, to sets, and this view leads to functor and sheaf semantics.
The Kripke semantics of intuitionistic predicate logic is the case where the frame is a preorder and the domains increase.
In the [[products of modal logics|JA2EBWVW8A]], $\mathsf{S5} \times \mathsf{S5}$ is the modal form of a fragment of first-order logic with two variables, where each box acts as a quantifier.
