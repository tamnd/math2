## Statement

Let $G$ be a [[group|G5S1M9]] with identity element $e$, and let $g \in G$ be an element of finite [[order|0WE2NHZJ7B]] $m$.
Powers $g^n$ for integers $n$ are as in the [[laws of exponents|00DRK90Z6K]].

1. For each integer $n$, $g^n = e$ if and only if $m$ [[divides|0PG2H7WKGG]] $n$.
2. For all integers $a$ and $b$, $g^a = g^b$ if and only if $a \equiv b \pmod m$, in the sense of [[congruence modulo $m$|0WAY5WX8NY]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. The number $m$ must be the least positive integer with $g^m = e$. In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, the element $2$ satisfies $6 \cdot 2 = 0$, but $3 \cdot 2 = 0$ too, and $6$ does not divide $3$.
2. The order must be finite. When $g$ has infinite order, there is no $m$, and the correct statement is the one for infinite order below.
3. The structure must be a group, because the proof cancels powers of $g$. In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ with multiplication, $2^1 = 2^3 = 2$, but no positive power of $2$ is $1$.

### Consequences

1. The powers $e, g, g^2, \ldots, g^{m-1}$ are all different, by part 2, and each power of $g$ is one of them. So the [[cyclic subgroup|031M8HX7DA]] $\langle g \rangle$ has exactly $m$ elements.
2. In a finite group $G$, [[$g^{|G|} = e$|0ZEPNS9BXK]], so part 1 gives that $m$ divides $|G|$.
3. For a prime $p$ and an integer $a$ not divisible by $p$, [[Fermat's little theorem|0M25PRN3WQ]] gives $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$. So the order of $a$ modulo $p$ divides $p - 1$.
4. The formula for [[the order of a power|0VVPQNVM8S]] uses part 1 in each step.

### Converse

Each part is an "if and only if", so it contains its converse.
The direction "$m$ divides $n$ gives $g^n = e$" holds for any $m$ with $g^m = e$, also when $m$ is not the least one.
The direction "$g^n = e$ gives that $m$ divides $n$" needs the minimality of $m$, as hypothesis 1 shows.

### Generalizations

1. The case of infinite order. Let $g$ have infinite order. Then $g^a = g^b$ if and only if $a = b$, and $g^n = e$ if and only if $n = 0$.
2. Proof of item 1. Suppose that $g^a = g^b$ with $a \neq b$, and let $a < b$. Then $g^{b - a} = g^b g^{-a} = g^a g^{-a} = e$, and $b - a$ is a positive integer. This contradicts the infinite order of $g$. The case $n = 0$ is the case $a = n$, $b = 0$.
3. Both cases together say that the set $\{n \in \mathbb{Z} : g^n = e\}$ is the subgroup $m \mathbb{Z}$ of $\mathbb{Z}$, with $m = \ord(g)$ for finite order and $m = 0$ for infinite order. This agrees with the [[classification of the subgroups of the integers|0SRATCFF1W]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

An element of order $m$ comes back to the identity exactly after $m$, $2m$, $3m$ steps, and so on.
It is never at the identity after a number of steps that is not a multiple of $m$.
For example, a rotation by $90$ degrees has order $4$.
It is the identity after $8$ steps, because $8 = 2 \cdot 4$, but not after $6$ steps.

### L2 human

We compute $3^{100}$ modulo $7$ with part 2.

1. In the group of nonzero residues modulo $7$ under multiplication, the element $3$ has order $6$, because the powers $3^1, \ldots, 3^6$ are $3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1$.
2. Divide $100$ by $6$: $100 = 16 \cdot 6 + 4$. So $100 \equiv 4 \pmod 6$.
3. By part 2, $3^{100} \equiv 3^4 \pmod 7$.
4. From step 1, $3^4 \equiv 4 \pmod 7$. So $3^{100} \equiv 4 \pmod 7$.

The typical use of part 1 is to find the order: if $g^{12} = e$, the order is one of the divisors $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$ of $12$.
A trap is to apply part 1 with a number $m$ that is not the least one: $g^{12} = e$ does not give that $g^n = e$ only for multiples of $12$.

### L3 human

The map $\mathbb{Z} \to G$, $n \mapsto g^n$, is a [[group homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]] from $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$.
Part 1 says that its kernel is $m \mathbb{Z}$.
Part 2 says that it induces an injective homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \to G$ with image $\langle g \rangle$.
So each cyclic group of order $m$ is [[isomorphic|08MG7H8JW5]] to $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$, and each infinite cyclic group is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$.
This is the first case of the isomorphism theorems, which the later sub-areas treat in general.
In a monoid, the powers of an element can also repeat, but the repeat need not pass through the identity, as the powers $2, 4, 2, 4, \ldots$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}, \cdot)$ show.
