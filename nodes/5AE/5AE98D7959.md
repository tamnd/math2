## Example

Let $\theta$ be a real number that is not a multiple of $\pi$.
Let $R_\theta \colon \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be the rotation about the origin by the angle $\theta$, with the matrix
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.$$
We show that $R_\theta$ has no real eigenvalue, and we find its complex eigenvalues.

1. By the [[definition of the characteristic polynomial|039HF58KQ5]],
$$p_A(t) = \det \begin{pmatrix} t - \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & t - \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} = (t - \cos\theta)^2 + \sin^2\theta = t^2 - 2\cos\theta \, t + 1.$$
2. Check: $\tr A = 2\cos\theta$ and $\det A = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$, which give the same polynomial.
3. The discriminant of $p_A$ is
$$4\cos^2\theta - 4 = -4\sin^2\theta.$$
   Since $\theta$ is not a multiple of $\pi$, we have $\sin\theta \ne 0$, so the discriminant is negative.
4. So $p_A$ has no real root.
   Since the [[eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial|053QVHF6N7]], $R_\theta$ has no real [[eigenvalue|0ZND7NQ970]] and no real [[eigenvector|0B5H4208Z9]].
5. Over $\mathbb{C}$, the quadratic formula gives the roots $\cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta = e^{\pm i\theta}$.
6. Check for $\lambda = e^{i\theta}$ with $v = (1, -i)$:
$$Av = (\cos\theta + i\sin\theta, \ \sin\theta - i\cos\theta), \qquad \lambda v = (e^{i\theta}, \ -i e^{i\theta}) = (\cos\theta + i\sin\theta, \ \sin\theta - i\cos\theta).$$
7. Since $A$ is real, taking complex conjugates in $Av = \lambda v$ shows that $(1, i)$ is an eigenvector for $e^{-i\theta}$.

## Remarks

The geometry says the same as the algebra.
A rotation by an angle that is not a multiple of $\pi$ moves every line through the origin to a different line, so no nonzero real vector keeps its direction.
The [[characteristic polynomial of the linear map|0BZ81AJNWF]] $R_\theta$ is the same in every basis, so no clever choice of a real basis can produce a real eigenvalue.

Now let $\theta$ be a multiple of $\pi$.
For $\theta = \pi$ the matrix is $-I$, and every nonzero vector is an eigenvector for $-1$; for $\theta = 0$ the matrix is $I$, and every nonzero vector is an eigenvector for $1$.
In both cases the discriminant $-4\sin^2\theta$ is $0$, and the two complex roots meet in one real double root.

The complex eigenvalues are an instance of the [[existence of an eigenvalue over an algebraically closed field|08CJB2NQ92]].
They lie on the unit circle, $|e^{\pm i\theta}| = 1$, as fits a map that keeps lengths.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Turn the plane by $60$ degrees about the origin.
Every arrow from the origin ends up pointing in a new direction, so no arrow is only stretched.
That is why a rotation has no real eigenvalue.
A half turn is the exception: it reverses every arrow, so every arrow is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $-1$.

### L2 human

For the quarter turn $\theta = \pi/2$ the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and $p_A(t) = t^2 + 1$, with the roots $\pm i$.
The typical use of the example is to show that eigenvalues depend on the field, and that a real matrix may need complex numbers.
Watch out: the rotation does have a characteristic polynomial; the polynomial only fails to have real roots.
