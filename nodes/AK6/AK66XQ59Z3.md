## Statement

Let $\Sigma$ be a finite alphabet.
A nonempty word $w = a_0 a_1 \cdots a_{n-1} \in \Sigma^+$ is identified with the [[structure|983GZBQYT4]]
$$\underline{w} = (\set{0, 1, \ldots, n-1}, <, (P_a)_{a \in \Sigma}), \qquad P_a = \set{i : a_i = a}.$$
Monadic second-order logic (MSO) over this signature is the fragment of [[second-order logic|G6X7SZSNC5]] with first-order variables $x, y, \ldots$ for positions and set variables $X, Y, \ldots$ for sets of positions.
Its atomic formulas are $x < y$, $x = y$, $P_a(x)$ and $X(x)$, also written $x \in X$.
Formulas are built with $\neg$, $\wedge$, $\vee$, $\to$, and the quantifiers $\exists x$, $\forall x$, $\exists X$, $\forall X$; a set quantifier ranges over all subsets of the domain.
A language $L \subseteq \Sigma^+$ is MSO-definable when there is an MSO sentence $\varphi$ with $L = \set{w \in \Sigma^+ : \underline{w} \models \varphi}$.

**Theorem.**

1. A language $L \subseteq \Sigma^+$ is MSO-definable if and only if $L$ is a [[regular language|18WVBDCS38]].
2. Both directions are effective: there are algorithms that turn a finite automaton into an equivalent MSO sentence and an MSO sentence into an equivalent finite automaton.
3. The set of MSO sentences that are true in some nonempty finite word, and the set of those true in every nonempty finite word, are decidable.
4. Weak monadic second-order logic of one successor, WS1S, is [[decidable|HWC687ES0Q]]. This is the theory of the structure $(\N, S)$ with $S = \set{(i, i+1) : i \in \N}$, in MSO where set variables range over the finite subsets of $\N$.

The [[proof|NT5RAK54G3]] proves all four parts.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Monadic. If one may also quantify over binary relations, non-regular languages become definable. The language $\set{a^m b^m : m \ge 1}$ is defined by "every $a$ precedes every $b$, both letters occur, and there is a binary relation that is a bijection from the $a$-positions onto the $b$-positions". This language is not regular, by the [[pumping lemma|CBR9WAGZGZ]].
- Finite words. On infinite words, finite automata are replaced by [[Büchi automata|92DM43HQZT]]; this is [[Büchi's theorem on S1S|YPDXVTH5JR]].
- Weak quantifiers in part 4. For WS1S the set variables range over finite sets. With quantifiers over all subsets of $\N$ one gets S1S, which is still decidable, but the proof needs automata on infinite words.

### Consequences

1. Regular languages have a logical description that does not mention automata or expressions. Closure under complement, intersection and projection becomes the closure of MSO under $\neg$, $\wedge$ and $\exists$.
2. MSO over the successor relation $S(x, y)$ defines the same languages as MSO over $<$. Indeed $x \le y$ holds exactly when every set that contains $x$ and is closed under successor contains $y$.
3. A. R. Meyer proved that WS1S is not [[elementary recursive|RJ3MCAAZSV]]: no decision procedure runs in time bounded by a fixed tower of exponentials. So the translation of part 2 from formulas to automata cannot be done in elementary time in general; in the proof, each negation can cost one exponential by the powerset construction.
4. The decidability of [[Presburger arithmetic|4W0E9G7PPV]] and of [[Büchi arithmetic|NSTECGT93D]] follows from part 4. In base $k$, a number is coded by $k - 1$ finite sets: the positions where its digit is $1$, $2$, and so on. Addition with carry and $V_k$ are then definable in WS1S.

### Converse

The theorem is an equivalence, so its converse is part of it.
The analogous statement for first-order logic is false: first-order logic over $<$ defines a proper subclass of the regular languages.
For example, the words over $\set{a}$ of even length form a regular language that no first-order sentence defines, while MSO defines it.
The first-order definable languages are exactly the star-free languages (McNaughton and Papert, 1971).

### Generalizations

1. Infinite words: MSO over $(\N, <)$ corresponds to Büchi automata ([[Büchi's theorem on S1S|YPDXVTH5JR]]).
2. Finite trees: MSO over finite labelled trees corresponds to bottom-up [[tree automata|3VSFM7K70Z]] (Thatcher and Wright, Doner); this is the base of [[Courcelle's theorem|DD4JB8XJ5A]].
3. Infinite trees: MSO over the infinite binary tree corresponds to automata on infinite trees ([[Rabin's tree theorem|PXKKP038RG]]).

## History

The theorem was proved independently by Julius Richard Büchi (1960), Calvin Elgot (1961) and Boris Trakhtenbrot, in work on the logic of finite automata.
Büchi used it to show that WS1S is decidable, and two years later he extended the method to infinite words and S1S.
Meyer's lower bound showed in the 1970s that the procedure cannot be made elementary.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A word such as $abba$ has positions $0, 1, 2, 3$, and each position carries a letter.
A sentence of monadic second-order logic may say "there is a set of positions such that ..." and "for every position ...".
For example, "the word has even length" can be said by colouring the positions alternately, starting with a coloured first position, and asking that the last position is not coloured.
The theorem says that the properties of words that such sentences can express are exactly the properties that a machine with a fixed finite memory can check while reading the word once.
So every such sentence can be turned into a machine, and the machine can be tested.

### L2 human

We define the nonempty words of even length over $\set{a}$ in MSO.
Write $\operatorname{first}(x)$ for $\neg \exists z \, (z < x)$, $\operatorname{last}(x)$ for $\neg \exists z \, (x < z)$, and $S(x, y)$ for $x < y \wedge \neg \exists z \, (x < z \wedge z < y)$.
The sentence is
$$\exists X \, \bigl( \forall x \, (\operatorname{first}(x) \to x \in X) \wedge \forall x \, \forall y \, (S(x, y) \to (x \in X \leftrightarrow y \notin X)) \wedge \forall x \, (\operatorname{last}(x) \to x \notin X) \bigr).$$

1. The first two conjuncts force $X$ to be the set of even positions, by induction along the word.
2. For $aaaa$, the even positions are $0$ and $2$, and the last position $3$ is not in $X$. The sentence is true.
3. For $aaa$, the last position $2$ is even, so it lies in $X$. The sentence is false.
4. The matching automaton has two states, "even so far" and "odd so far", and the set variable $X$ records the state before each letter.

The typical use is to prove that a language is regular by writing an MSO sentence for it, which is often easier than building an automaton.
A trap is to forget that the theorem is about monadic quantifiers: a sentence that quantifies over pairs of positions leaves the class of regular languages.

### L3 human

The theorem is the first instance of the correspondence between logic and automata: definability in MSO equals recognizability by finite automata.
The same pattern holds for infinite words, finite and infinite trees, and graphs of bounded treewidth, and it is the source of the decidability results of this part of logic, from [[Büchi arithmetic|NSTECGT93D]] to [[Rabin's tree theorem|PXKKP038RG]].
Algebraically, a language is regular exactly when it is recognized by a finite monoid, and MSO sentences correspond to finite monoids, while first-order sentences correspond to aperiodic ones.
The non-elementary lower bound makes the correspondence effective but not efficient, and practical tools for WS1S work with minimized deterministic automata.
