## Counterexample

Let $L$ be the seven-element set $\set{p, q, r, a, b, c, 0}$ with one binary operation, written as juxtaposition.
The elements $p, q, r$ are called states, the elements $a, b, c$ are called letters, and $0$ is the default element.
The nine products of a letter with a state are
$$ap = p, \quad bp = p, \quad cp = p, \qquad aq = p, \quad bq = q, \quad cq = r, \qquad ar = r, \quad br = r, \quad cr = r,$$
and every other product equals $0$.
So each letter acts on the states like the transition of a small automaton, and all other products fall to $0$.

Claim: $L$ is a finite algebra of finite signature that is not [[finitely based|YGYZAQ12MZ]].
That is, no finite set of [[identities|PKD440G2XW]] true in $L$ is an [[equational basis|9Q008GMZSW]] for all identities of $L$.

Idea of the check: let $V$ be the [[variety|AJNZJD8442]] generated by $L$.
For each $n$, let $V^{(n)}$ be the class of algebras that satisfy every identity of $L$ in at most $n$ variables.
If $L$ had a finite basis, its identities would use at most $n$ variables for some $n$, and then $V^{(n)} = V$.
For each large $n$ one builds a finite algebra $C_n$ in $V^{(n)}$ and an identity that holds in $L$ but fails in $C_n$.
First take a subalgebra of a power of a quotient of $L$, and collapse to $0$ the tuples that have an entry $0$.
The result $B_n$ is a longer automaton of the same shape, and it lies in $V$.
Then $C_n$ is obtained from $B_n$ by changing a single product.
Hence $V^{(n)} \ne V$ for every $n$, and $L$ has no finite basis.

## Remarks

Roger Lyndon published this example in 1954, in the paper "Identities in finite algebras".
It was the first known finite algebra of finite signature that is not finitely based.
Lyndon used a constant for $0$ as a second operation, but the constant is not needed for the argument.
It contrasts with Lyndon's earlier theorem that every two-element algebra of finite signature is finitely based.
It also contrasts with positive results for natural classes: every finite group, every finite ring and every finite lattice is finitely based.
Later, Murskiĭ found a three-element groupoid that is not finitely based.
Lyndon's algebra is not [[inherently nonfinitely based|Z6TKYRZ9WX]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The laws of a finite algebra are the equations that hold for every choice of its elements.
One might hope that a finite algebra always has a finite list of laws from which all the others follow.
Lyndon's seven-element algebra shows that this hope fails.
It is built like a tiny machine: three letters move three states around, and every other product goes to a sink $0$.
For every number $n$, some law of this algebra needs more than $n$ variables and does not follow from the laws with at most $n$ variables.
