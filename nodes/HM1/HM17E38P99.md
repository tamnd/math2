## Statement

Two players divide a pie of size $1$ in the following [[extensive-form game|4Y8AF9H9HA]] of alternating offers.
In periods $t = 0, 2, 4, \dots$ player $1$ proposes a division $(x, 1 - x)$ with $0 \le x \le 1$, and player $2$ accepts or rejects it.
In periods $t = 1, 3, 5, \dots$ player $2$ proposes a division, and player $1$ accepts or rejects it.
The game ends at the first acceptance.
Fix discount factors $\delta_1, \delta_2$ with $0 < \delta_1 < 1$ and $0 < \delta_2 < 1$.
If a division $(y_1, y_2)$ is accepted in period $t$, player $i$ receives $\delta_i^t y_i$.
If no offer is ever accepted, both players receive $0$.
Then the game has a unique [[subgame perfect equilibrium|HE8K9M4EXN]].
Let
$$x^* = \frac{1 - \delta_2}{1 - \delta_1 \delta_2}, \qquad y^* = \delta_1 x^*.$$
In the equilibrium, player $1$ always proposes $(x^*, 1 - x^*)$, and player $2$ always proposes $(y^*, 1 - y^*)$.
Player $2$ accepts an offer exactly when it gives him at least $1 - x^*$.
Player $1$ accepts an offer exactly when it gives her at least $y^*$.
So the players agree at once, on the division $(x^*, 1 - x^*)$.

## Remarks

The two numbers come from indifference: each responder must be offered exactly the discounted value of being the proposer in the next period.
This gives $1 - x^* = \delta_2 (1 - y^*)$ and $y^* = \delta_1 x^*$, whose solution is the formula above.
Uniqueness is proved by bounding the supremum and infimum of player $1$'s equilibrium payoffs in the subgames where she proposes and showing that both equal $x^*$.
Example: with a common discount factor $\delta$, player $1$ gets $1/(1 + \delta)$ and player $2$ gets $\delta/(1 + \delta)$, so moving first is an advantage that vanishes as $\delta \to 1$.
Discounting is necessary: with $\delta_1 = \delta_2 = 1$, every division can be supported by a subgame perfect equilibrium.
Ariel Rubinstein proved the theorem in 1982.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two people haggle over a cake by taking turns making offers, and waiting is costly because the cake shrinks for each of them.
The theorem says that rational players agree immediately, and it gives the exact split.
Being more patient raises a player's share, and so does making the first offer.

### L2 human

The model is the standard noncooperative account of bargaining and is solved by backward reasoning on an infinite game.
When both players become very patient, the split tends to the one predicted by the [[Nash bargaining solution|KJGNE96Q17]] of the corresponding bargaining problem, with the relative patience setting the weights.
With a common discount factor tending to $1$, the split tends to $(1/2, 1/2)$.
