## Summary

The theories $\mathrm{ID}_\nu$ of iterated inductive definitions extend [[Peano arithmetic|0QFT7Z76N5]] by new predicates for inductively defined sets of natural numbers.
In $\mathrm{ID}_1$, each arithmetical operator $A(X, x)$ in which the set variable $X$ occurs only positively gets a predicate $I_A$ with two axioms.
The first says that $I_A$ is closed under $A$.
The second says that $I_A$ is contained in every set that is defined by a formula and closed under $A$.
So $I_A$ is the [[least fixed point|DBD64TH52D]] of the operator, as far as the language can express it.
In $\mathrm{ID}_\nu$ the definitions are iterated along a primitive recursive well-ordering of type $\nu$: the operator at stage $\mu$ may use the sets of all earlier stages in any way, positively or negatively.
The exact axioms are on the page of the [[definition|~cd19a8f1]].
These theories give a scale of strength between Peano arithmetic and strong subsystems of second-order arithmetic, and their ordinals are standard landmarks of [[ordinal analysis|C5E5TG8PGP]].

## Motivation

Many sets in logic are defined "from below" as the least set closed under some rules.
Examples are the [[well-founded part|0DQPNJNPYK]] of a relation, the set of well-founded recursive trees, and [[Kleene's O|WHAP0K600D]].
Such a definition is not arithmetical in general: the well-founded part of a recursive relation can be $\Pi^1_1$-complete.
The theory $\mathrm{ID}_1$ adds such sets as primitive objects, with only the closure and minimality properties.
Iteration is natural as well: the trees that branch over all well-founded trees of the first kind form a set of the second level, and so on.
The constructive view of these sets, as sets generated by rules, made them a candidate for a constructive foundation of parts of analysis.
For this reason the ID theories were used to reduce impredicative subsystems of analysis to theories of inductive definitions.

## Notation

- $\mathrm{ID}_\nu$: $\nu$ times iterated inductive definitions, for a primitive recursive well-ordering of type $\nu$.
- $\mathrm{ID}_{<\nu}$: the union of the theories $\mathrm{ID}_\mu$ for $\mu < \nu$. So $\mathrm{ID}_{<\omega}$ is the union of $\mathrm{ID}_1, \mathrm{ID}_2, \dots$.
- $\mathrm{ID}^i_\nu$: the intuitionistic version, with Heyting arithmetic as the base. The variant $\mathrm{ID}^i_\nu(\mathcal{O})$ allows only the operators of the tree classes $\mathcal{O}_\mu$ (accessibility inductive definitions).
- $\widehat{\mathrm{ID}}_\nu$ in much of the literature: the fixed-point theories, with the closure axiom and the converse, but without the minimality scheme.
- The predicates are written $I_A$, $P_A$ or $J_A$ in different sources.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The accessible part of an order|~dbbd338b]]: the operator $A(X, x) = \forall y \, (y \prec x \to X(y))$ is $X$-positive, and $I_A$ is the well-founded part of $\prec$. For $<$ on $\mathbb{N}$ it is all of $\mathbb{N}$. Transfinite induction along $\prec$ on $I_A$ is provable in $\mathrm{ID}_1$ for every formula.
- [[The tree classes O₁ and O₂|~83c271f8]]: $\mathcal{O}_1$ is defined in $\mathrm{ID}_1$, and $\mathcal{O}_2$, the trees that branch over $\mathcal{O}_1$, needs a second stage in $\mathrm{ID}_2$, because $\mathcal{O}_1$ occurs negatively in its operator.
- $\mathrm{ID}_0$ is Peano arithmetic. Its ordinal is $\varepsilon_0 = \psi_0(\Omega_1)$ in [[Buchholz's notation|E7WEFRMQTB]].
- The [[proof-theoretic ordinal|C72RBH0CDR]] of $\mathrm{ID}_1$ is the [[Bachmann-Howard ordinal|~fcf34cb0]] $\psi_0(\varepsilon_{\Omega + 1}) = \psi_0(\Omega_2)$. In general, $|\mathrm{ID}_\nu| = \psi_0(\varepsilon_{\Omega_\nu + 1})$ and $|\mathrm{ID}_{<\nu}| = \psi_0(\Omega_\nu)$ (Wikipedia, "Ordinal analysis", section "Table of ordinal analyses").
- $|\mathrm{ID}_\omega|$ is the [[Takeuti-Feferman-Buchholz ordinal|~1df2a94e]] $\psi_0(\varepsilon_{\Omega_\omega + 1})$.

### Non-examples

- [[The operator ¬X|~6d94d991]]: the closure and minimality axioms for this operator hold for $I = \mathbb{N}$, but $\mathbb{N}$ is not a fixed point. The condition that fails is positivity: $X$ occurs negatively.
- The fixed-point theory $\widehat{\mathrm{ID}}_1$ is not $\mathrm{ID}_1$: it states $A(I_A) = I_A$ but not the minimality scheme. So the argument of the first example, which uses the minimality scheme, does not work in it.
- [[Π¹₁-comprehension|~9776e851]] is not a theory of inductive definitions: it is a second-order theory. Its fragment $\Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathrm{CA}_0$ has the same ordinal as $\mathrm{ID}_{<\omega}$, namely $\psi_0(\Omega_\omega)$.

### Basic properties

1. Monotonicity. If $A(X, x)$ is $X$-positive and $\forall x \, (F(x) \to G(x))$, then $\forall x \, (A(F, x) \to A(G, x))$. The proof is an induction on the build of $A$, and the cases are the connectives and quantifiers.
2. Fixed point. $\mathrm{ID}_1$ proves $\forall x \, (I_A(x) \leftrightarrow A(I_A, x))$. The direction from right to left is the closure axiom. For the other direction, take $F(x) = A(I_A, x)$: by the closure axiom and monotonicity, $A(F, x) \to F(x)$, so the minimality scheme gives $I_A(x) \to F(x)$.
3. Stages. In the standard model, $I_A$ is the union of the stages $I^\alpha_A = A(\bigcup_{\beta < \alpha} I^\beta_A)$. The stages of an [[accessible part|~a1b984d8]] show that the union can need infinite ordinals $\alpha$.
4. Comparison with analysis. Each $\mathrm{ID}_\nu$ for $\nu < \omega$ is interpreted in $\Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathrm{CA}_0$: $I_A$ becomes the intersection of all sets closed under $A$, a set by $\Pi^1_1$-comprehension. Every formula of $\mathrm{ID}_\nu$ then defines a set by arithmetical comprehension, so induction and minimality hold.

### History

Georg Kreisel proposed formal theories of generalized inductive definitions in the early 1960s.
Solomon Feferman introduced the iterated theories $\mathrm{ID}_\nu$ in 1970, and used them to study subsystems of analysis.
The ordinal analysis of $\mathrm{ID}_\nu$ was completed by Buchholz, with the $\Omega_{\mu+1}$-rule, and by Pohlers, with local predicativity.
The book "Iterated inductive definitions and subsystems of analysis" (Lecture Notes in Mathematics 897, 1981) by Buchholz, Feferman, Pohlers and Sieg collects these results, with the reduction of the classical theories to the intuitionistic theories $\mathrm{ID}^i_\nu(\mathcal{O})$.

### Common errors

- Allowing negative occurrences of $X$. Then the axioms do not describe a fixed point, see the non-example.
- Confusing $\mathrm{ID}_{<\omega}$ with $\mathrm{ID}_\omega$. Their ordinals are $\psi_0(\Omega_\omega)$ and $\psi_0(\varepsilon_{\Omega_\omega + 1})$, and the second is much larger.
- Thinking that minimality applies only to sets. The scheme applies to every formula $F$ of the extended language, and this is where the strength comes from.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some sets are built step by step by rules, like "$0$ is in the set, and if $n$ is in the set then $n + 2$ is in the set".
The result is the smallest set that obeys the rules, here the even numbers.
A theory of inductive definitions adds a name for each such smallest set, with two axioms: the set obeys the rules, and it is inside every other set that obeys them.
For some rules the building needs infinitely many rounds, and these sets are hard to describe in ordinary arithmetic.
Iterating means that the rules of a later set may refer to sets built earlier.

### L2 human

We prove in $\mathrm{ID}_1$ that the accessible part $I_A$ of a relation $\prec$ supports transfinite induction.

1. Let $F(x)$ be any formula, and assume that $F$ is progressive: $\forall x \, (\forall y \, (y \prec x \to F(y)) \to F(x))$.
2. The operator is $A(X, x) = \forall y \, (y \prec x \to X(y))$. So progressiveness says $\forall x \, (A(F, x) \to F(x))$.
3. The minimality scheme gives $\forall x \, (I_A(x) \to F(x))$.
4. So every progressive formula holds on the accessible part.

The typical use is the lower bound of an ordinal analysis: each notation $\alpha$ that lies in the accessible part of the notation order gives transfinite induction up to $\alpha$.
The trap is to think that $\mathrm{ID}_1$ proves $I_A(x)$ for a given $x$: that needs a proof that all predecessors are accessible, and for large $x$ this is exactly what the theory cannot prove.

### L3 human

The theory $\mathrm{ID}_1$ is proof-theoretically equivalent to Kripke-Platek set theory, and the least fixed points correspond to $\Sigma_1$-definable sets over the least admissible set: the closure ordinal of positive arithmetical operators is at most $\omega_1^{\mathrm{CK}}$.
In the iterated theories each stage corresponds to a further admissible ordinal, and this is why Buchholz's notation uses one cardinal $\Omega_{\nu}$ for each level of iteration.
The proof-theoretic methods for these theories, the $\Omega$-rule and [[local predicativity|~af6dfa84]], were developed for $\mathrm{ID}_\nu$ and then transferred to [[Π¹₁-comprehension|~9776e851]] and to set theories with reflection.
In type theory, inductive types such as the W-types and the higher tree classes play the same role as the operators of $\mathrm{ID}_\nu$, and type theories with such types are analysed with the same collapsing functions.
