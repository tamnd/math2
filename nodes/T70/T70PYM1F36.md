## Summary

A fragment of Peano arithmetic keeps the induction schema only for formulas up to a fixed complexity, and so it measures how much induction a proof really uses.
Take the formula $\exists y \, \mathrm{Exp}(x, y)$, which says "$2^x$ exists", with $\mathrm{Exp}$ a bounded formula for $y = 2^x$.
Its induction instance is
$$\exists y \, \mathrm{Exp}(0, y) \land \forall x \, \big( \exists y \, \mathrm{Exp}(x, y) \to \exists y \, \mathrm{Exp}(Sx, y) \big) \to \forall x \, \exists y \, \mathrm{Exp}(x, y).$$
The formula has one unbounded $\exists$ in front of a bounded part, so it is $\Sigma_1$, and the instance is an axiom of $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$.
Both premises are easy: $y = 1$ works for $x = 0$, and a witness $y$ for $x$ gives the witness $y + y$ for $Sx$, as $2^3 = 8$ gives $2^4 = 16$.
So $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ proves that $2^x$ always exists, while $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$, with induction for bounded formulas only, does not.

Exactly, a fragment of [[Peano arithmetic|HACXBCM7SS]] is a theory in the [[language of arithmetic|D8SD54M5PN]] whose theorems are all theorems of $\mathsf{PA}$.
The standard fragments restrict the [[induction schema|1QD0KDDGYJ]] to a class $\Gamma$ of formulas: $\mathsf{I}\Gamma$ is [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] $\mathsf{Q}$ together with induction for every formula in $\Gamma$.
The main cases are $\mathsf{IOpen}$ for quantifier-free formulas, $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ and $\mathsf{I}\Pi_n$, for the [[formula classes|GE43CSZTS9]] $\Delta_0$, $\Sigma_n$ and $\Pi_n$.
A second family, the collection theories $\mathsf{B}\Sigma_n$, sits between them.

## Motivation

$\mathsf{PA}$ allows induction on formulas of every complexity, but most proofs use induction on simple formulas.
The least complexity that a proof needs is a measure of the strength of the theorem.
For example, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ proves total exactly the primitive recursive functions, while $\mathsf{PA}$ proves much faster functions total.

The fragments also explain why [[PA is not finitely axiomatizable|M5WNB181T7]].
Each $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ with $n \ge 1$ is finitely axiomatizable, $\mathsf{PA}$ is their union, and the union never stops growing.
In model theory, the fragments say which properties of $\mathfrak{N}$ survive in a nonstandard model, and at which level of induction they fail.

## Notation

For a class $\Gamma$, the theory $\mathsf{I}\Gamma$ is $\mathsf{Q}$ plus, for each $\varphi \in \Gamma$, the axiom
$$\forall \vec z \, \big( \varphi(0, \vec z) \land \forall x \, (\varphi(x, \vec z) \to \varphi(Sx, \vec z)) \to \forall x \, \varphi(x, \vec z) \big).$$
The parameters $\vec z$ are allowed; without them the theories are written $\mathsf{I}\Gamma^-$, and they are weaker.
For $\varphi \in \Sigma_n$, the collection axiom says that a bounded family of witnesses has a common bound:
$$\forall a \, \forall \vec z \, \big( \forall x < a \, \exists y \, \varphi(x, y, \vec z) \to \exists b \, \forall x < a \, \exists y < b \, \varphi(x, y, \vec z) \big).$$
The theory $\mathsf{B}\Sigma_n$ is $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$ together with these axioms.
The least number principle $\mathsf{L}\Gamma$ says that a formula of $\Gamma$ that holds of some number holds of a least one.
Some books write $\Sigma_n$-$\mathsf{IND}$ or $\mathsf{I}\exists_n$ for $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$.
We write $T \subseteq T'$ when every theorem of $T$ is a theorem of $T'$.

## Remarks

### Three fragments at work

$\mathsf{IOpen}$ proves $x + y = y + x$ by three inductions on equations; [[commutativity in open induction|GJ9KW45F3C]] gives them.
For instance, the lemma $Sy + x = S(y + x)$ is an induction on $x$ for an equation with the parameter $y$.
[[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] alone does not prove the law, so the law separates $\mathsf{Q}$ from $\mathsf{IOpen}$.

$\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ proves $\forall x \, \exists y \, \mathrm{Exp}(x, y)$, as the Summary showed, but $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$ does not, by [[Parikh's theorem|YPQPC1WJMV]].
So the existence of $2^x$ sits strictly between $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$ and $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$, at the level of [[elementary function arithmetic|2JD80TE553]].

For $n \ge 1$, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ proves every axiom of $\mathsf{I}\Pi_n$.
[[Σn induction implies Πn induction|JZGV1EK4E9]] runs the induction downward from a failure, and the converse holds too.

### What is not a fragment

$\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ is not a fragment of $\mathsf{PA}$.
It proves $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, which $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove by the [[second incompleteness theorem|4PZM5GJTR5]]; [[the non-example|MYN7NA64CA]] gives the argument.
By contrast, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n)$ is a fragment for $n \ge 1$, since it lies inside $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_{n+1}$.

[[True arithmetic|DX0KPMH4QZ]] is not a fragment either.
It contains $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ and the [[Goodstein sentence|XQMNJF241J]], which $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove.
Second-order systems such as $\mathsf{ACA}_0$ are not fragments in this sense, because their language is not $L_A$, although their first-order theorems are theorems of $\mathsf{PA}$.

### The hierarchy

The fragments line up in a strict chain whose union is $\mathsf{PA}$:
$$\mathsf{IOpen} \subsetneq \mathsf{I}\Delta_0 \subsetneq \mathsf{I}\Sigma_1 \subsetneq \mathsf{I}\Sigma_2 \subsetneq \cdots.$$
The union is all of $\mathsf{PA}$, because each formula is provably equivalent to a $\Sigma_n$ formula for some $n$, by the [[prenex normal form theorem|ME9J33BFNC]].
So every finite part of $\mathsf{PA}$ is provable in some $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$.

For each $n$, the theories $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$, $\mathsf{I}\Pi_n$, $\mathsf{L}\Sigma_n$ and $\mathsf{L}\Pi_n$ have the same theorems.
Collection fits in between: for $n \ge 0$,
$$\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n \subsetneq \mathsf{B}\Sigma_{n+1} \subsetneq \mathsf{I}\Sigma_{n+1}.$$
This finer chain is the Paris-Kirby hierarchy.

### Why the chain is strict

For $n \ge 1$, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ is finitely axiomatizable.
The proof uses a $\Sigma_n$ truth formula, so that one induction axiom covers all $\Sigma_n$ formulas at once.
Also $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_{n+1}$ proves $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n)$.
If $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ proved all of $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_{n+1}$, it would prove its own consistency, against the second incompleteness theorem.

The proof-theoretic ordinal gives a second measure.
For $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ it is a tower $\omega^{\omega^{\cdot^{\cdot^{\omega}}}}$ of $n + 1$ letters $\omega$, so $\omega^\omega$ for $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ and $\omega^{\omega^\omega}$ for $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_2$.
These ordinals climb toward $\varepsilon_0$, the ordinal of $\mathsf{PA}$.

### Open induction and its limits

Open induction proves the ring laws, but not all simple number theory.
Shepherdson built a model of $\mathsf{IOpen}$ in which $x \cdot x = \overline{2} \cdot y \cdot y$ has a solution with $y \ne 0$.
So $\mathsf{IOpen}$ does not prove the [[irrationality of √2|0DZGSDHD92]], which $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$ proves by taking a least counterexample.

### History

Shepherdson studied the models of open induction in the 1960s and proved the theorem above.
Kirby and Paris introduced the collection theories $\mathsf{B}\Sigma_n$ and proved the strictness of their hierarchy in 1978.
Around 1970, Parsons, Mints and Takeuti showed independently that $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ proves the same $\Pi_2$ sentences as [[primitive recursive arithmetic|WCXH72T8D8]], so its provably total functions are exactly the primitive recursive ones.

### Watch out

Count quantifiers only after the formula is in prenex form, and ignore bounded quantifiers.
The formula $\forall u < x \, \exists y \, \psi$ looks $\Pi_1$ at first sight, but with $\psi$ bounded it is $\Sigma_1$ in $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$, by collection.

Do not forget the parameters $\vec z$.
Without them, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n^-$ is strictly weaker than $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$.
And do not read $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ as induction in $\mathfrak{N}$ only: it is a theory, and its nonstandard models are the point of most results.

### Try it

Does $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ prove the induction instance for $\varphi(x) = \forall y \, (y + x = x + y)$?
Yes: $\varphi$ is $\Pi_1$, so the instance is not one of the axioms, but $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ proves every $\Pi_1$ induction instance.

Which is the least theory in the chain $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1, \mathsf{I}\Sigma_2, \mathsf{I}\Sigma_3, \ldots$ that proves $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{I}\Sigma_2)$?
$\mathsf{I}\Sigma_3$ proves it, and $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_2$ does not, by the second incompleteness theorem.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Induction proves a statement for all numbers from two facts: it holds for $0$, and it passes from each number to the next.
Peano arithmetic allows induction for every statement, however complicated.
A fragment allows induction only for statements of limited complexity, for example statements with at most one "there exists" in front.
Logicians use fragments to measure how much induction a theorem needs.
For example, the law $x + y = y + x$ needs induction only on plain equations.

### L2 human

We place three statements in the chain.
First, $x + y = y + x$ is proved by induction on quantifier-free formulas, so it is a theorem of $\mathsf{IOpen}$.
Second, $\forall x \, \exists y \, \mathrm{Exp}(x, y)$ is not a theorem of $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$, because Parikh's theorem bounds its provably total functions by polynomials and $2^x$ outgrows every polynomial.
Third, the same sentence is a theorem of $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$, by the induction instance in the Summary.
Check the step of that instance at $x = 4$: from $\mathrm{Exp}(4, 16)$ we get $\mathrm{Exp}(5, 32)$, since $16 + 16 = 32$.

So the existence of $2^x$ needs more than bounded induction and no more than $\Sigma_1$ induction.
The typical use of fragments is this reverse question: given a theorem, find the least fragment that proves it.

### L3 human

Two invariants measure a fragment: its provably total functions and its $\Pi_1$ theorems, which include consistency statements.
For $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ the first is a level of the fast-growing hierarchy below $\varepsilon_0$, and the second grows strictly with $n$.
$\mathsf{B}\Sigma_{n+1}$ and $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ prove the same $\Pi_{n+2}$ sentences, so collection adds no new provably total functions.
The model theory of fragments studies cuts and end extensions of nonstandard models, and collection appears in its main results.
In [[reverse mathematics|7SQS69T17R]], $\mathsf{RCA}_0$ is conservative over $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ and $\mathsf{ACA}_0$ over $\mathsf{PA}$, so first-order fragments measure the arithmetic strength of second-order systems.
The [[arithmetical hierarchy|7CVJCQKY41]] of sets is the semantic side of the same classification of formulas.
