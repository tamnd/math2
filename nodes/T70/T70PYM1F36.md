## Summary

A fragment of [[Peano arithmetic|HACXBCM7SS]] is a theory in the [[language of arithmetic|D8SD54M5PN]] whose theorems are all theorems of $\mathsf{PA}$.
The standard fragments restrict the [[induction schema|1QD0KDDGYJ]] to a class $\Gamma$ of formulas: $\mathsf{I}\Gamma$ is [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] $\mathsf{Q}$ together with induction for every formula in $\Gamma$.
The main cases are $\mathsf{IOpen}$ (quantifier-free formulas), $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$ (bounded formulas), $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ and $\mathsf{I}\Pi_n$, for the [[formula classes|GE43CSZTS9]] $\Delta_0$, $\Sigma_n$ and $\Pi_n$.
A second family is the collection theories $\mathsf{B}\Sigma_n$, which add to $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$ a schema saying that a bounded family of witnesses has a common bound.
Kirby and Paris showed that these theories form a strict hierarchy $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n \subsetneq \mathsf{B}\Sigma_{n+1} \subsetneq \mathsf{I}\Sigma_{n+1}$, whose union is $\mathsf{PA}$.

## Motivation

$\mathsf{PA}$ allows induction on formulas of every complexity, but most proofs use induction on simple formulas.
Measuring the least complexity that a proof needs is a way to measure the strength of a theorem.
For example, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ proves exactly the primitive recursive functions total, while $\mathsf{PA}$ proves much faster functions total.
The fragments also explain the proof that $\mathsf{PA}$ is not finitely axiomatizable: each fragment $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ is finitely axiomatizable, and $\mathsf{PA}$ is their union.
In model theory, the fragments describe which properties of $\mathfrak{N}$ survive in a nonstandard model, and at which level of induction they fail.

## Notation

For a class $\Gamma$ of formulas, $\mathsf{I}\Gamma$ is $\mathsf{Q}$ plus the axioms
$$\forall \vec z \, \big( \varphi(0, \vec z) \land \forall x \, (\varphi(x, \vec z) \to \varphi(Sx, \vec z)) \to \forall x \, \varphi(x, \vec z) \big) \qquad (\varphi \in \Gamma).$$
The parameters $\vec z$ are allowed; without them the theories are written $\mathsf{I}\Gamma^-$ and behave differently.
For $\Gamma = \Sigma_n$, the collection schema $\mathsf{B}\Sigma_n$ consists of the axioms
$$\forall a \, \forall \vec z \, \big( \forall x < a \, \exists y \, \varphi(x, y, \vec z) \to \exists b \, \forall x < a \, \exists y < b \, \varphi(x, y, \vec z) \big) \qquad (\varphi \in \Sigma_n),$$
and the theory $\mathsf{B}\Sigma_n$ is $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$ together with this schema.
The least number principle $\mathsf{L}\Gamma$ says that a formula of $\Gamma$ that holds of some number holds of a least one.
Some books write $\Sigma_n$-$\mathsf{IND}$ or $\mathsf{I}\exists_n$ for $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$, and $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0 + \mathsf{exp}$ is [[elementary function arithmetic|2JD80TE553]].
The order of the fragments is by inclusion of theorems: $T \subseteq T'$ means that every theorem of $T$ is a theorem of $T'$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- $\mathsf{IOpen}$ proves $x + y = y + x$, by three inductions on equations; [[this example|GJ9KW45F3C]] gives them.
- For $n \ge 1$, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ proves every axiom of $\mathsf{I}\Pi_n$; [[this example|JZGV1EK4E9]] gives the proof.
- $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n)$ is a fragment for $n \ge 1$, because it lies inside $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_{n+1}$.
- $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0 + \mathsf{exp}$ is a fragment, and so is $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$, whose provably total functions are the primitive recursive functions.

### Non-examples

- $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ is not a fragment of $\mathsf{PA}$, by the second incompleteness theorem; [[this non-example|MYN7NA64CA]] gives the argument.
- [[True arithmetic|DX0KPMH4QZ]] is not a fragment of $\mathsf{PA}$: it contains $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ and the true [[Goodstein sentence|XQMNJF241J]], which $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove.
- Second-order theories such as $\mathsf{ACA}_0$ are not fragments in this sense, because their language is not $L_A$, although their first-order theorems are theorems of $\mathsf{PA}$.

### Basic properties

- Hierarchy. $\mathsf{IOpen} \subsetneq \mathsf{I}\Delta_0 \subsetneq \mathsf{I}\Sigma_1 \subsetneq \mathsf{I}\Sigma_2 \subsetneq \cdots$, and $\mathsf{PA}$ is the union of the theories $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$.
- Union. Each induction axiom of $\mathsf{PA}$ is equivalent to one for a $\Sigma_n$ formula for some $n$, by the [[prenex normal form theorem|ME9J33BFNC]]. So every finite subset of $\mathsf{PA}$ is provable in some $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$.
- Equivalent forms. For each $n$, the theories $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$, $\mathsf{I}\Pi_n$, $\mathsf{L}\Sigma_n$ and $\mathsf{L}\Pi_n$ have the same theorems.
- Collection. For $n \ge 0$, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_{n+1} \vdash \mathsf{B}\Sigma_{n+1}$ and $\mathsf{B}\Sigma_{n+1} \vdash \mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$, and neither converse holds; this is the Kirby-Paris hierarchy.
- Finite axiomatization. For $n \ge 1$, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ is finitely axiomatizable. The proof uses a $\Sigma_n$ truth predicate, so that one induction axiom covers all $\Sigma_n$ formulas.
- Consistency. For $n \ge 1$, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_{n+1} \vdash \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n)$, and $\mathsf{PA} \vdash \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n)$. Together with the [[second incompleteness theorem|4PZM5GJTR5]] this shows that the hierarchy is strict.
- Ordinals. The proof-theoretic ordinal of $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ is a tower $\omega^{\omega^{\cdot^{\cdot^{\omega}}}}$ of $n + 1$ letters $\omega$; for $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ it is $\omega^\omega$.
- Shepherdson's theorem. $\mathsf{IOpen}$ does not prove that $x \cdot x = \overline{2} \cdot y \cdot y$ has no solution with $y \ne 0$. So $\mathsf{IOpen}$ does not prove the [[irrationality of $\sqrt 2$|0DZGSDHD92]], which $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$ proves.

### History

Shepherdson studied the models of open induction and proved the theorem above.
Kirby and Paris introduced the collection theories $\mathsf{B}\Sigma_n$ and proved the strictness of the hierarchy in 1978.
Parsons, Mints and Takeuti showed independently that $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$ proves total exactly the primitive recursive functions.

### Common errors

- To think that $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ and $\mathsf{I}\Pi_n$ differ because their schemas differ. They prove the same theorems.
- To forget the parameters $\vec z$. Without them, $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n^-$ is strictly weaker than $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$.
- To read "$\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$" as induction in the standard model only. It is a theory, and its nonstandard models are the point of most results.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Induction proves a statement for all numbers from two facts: it holds for $0$, and it passes from each number to the next.
Peano arithmetic allows induction for every statement, however complicated.
A fragment allows induction only for statements of limited complexity, for example statements with at most one "there exists" in front.
Fragments are weaker than Peano arithmetic, and logicians use them to measure how much induction a theorem needs.
For example, the law $x + y = y + x$ needs induction only on equations.

### L2 human

We place the commutativity of addition and the existence of $2^x$ in the hierarchy.

1. $x + y = y + x$ is proved by induction on quantifier-free formulas, so it is a theorem of $\mathsf{IOpen}$.
2. $\forall x \, \exists y \, \mathrm{Exp}(x, y)$ is not a theorem of $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$, by [[Parikh's theorem|~5b376b4b]].
3. $\forall x \, \exists y \, \mathrm{Exp}(x, y)$ is a theorem of $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$: induction on the $\Sigma_1$ formula $\exists y \, \mathrm{Exp}(x, y)$ works, with $\mathrm{Exp}(x, y) \to \mathrm{Exp}(Sx, y + y)$ as the step.
4. So the existence of $2^x$ sits strictly between $\mathsf{I}\Delta_0$ and $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$.

The typical use is a reverse question: given a theorem, find the least fragment that proves it.
A trap is to count quantifiers in a formula before putting it in prenex form; $\Sigma_n$ refers to the prenex form, with bounded quantifiers ignored.

### L3 human

Two invariants measure a fragment: its provably total functions and its $\Pi_1$ theorems, which include its consistency statements.
For $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ the first is a level of the fast-growing hierarchy below $\varepsilon_0$, and the second grows strictly with $n$.
$\mathsf{B}\Sigma_{n+1}$ and $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ prove the same $\Pi_{n+2}$ sentences, so collection adds no new provably total functions.
The model theory of fragments studies cuts and end extensions of nonstandard models, and collection principles appear in its main results.
In reverse mathematics, $\mathsf{RCA}_0$ is a conservative extension of $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$, and $\mathsf{ACA}_0$ of $\mathsf{PA}$, so first-order fragments measure the arithmetic strength of second-order systems.
