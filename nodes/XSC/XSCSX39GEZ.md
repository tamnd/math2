## Summary

Let $\mathcal{K}$ be a class of [[structures|983GZBQYT4]] in a [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]] $L$, closed under isomorphism.
The class has the *amalgamation property* (AP) when, for all [[embeddings|SCRB74SWQN]] $f \colon A \to B$ and $g \colon A \to C$ inside $\mathcal{K}$, there are $D \in \mathcal{K}$ and embeddings $f' \colon B \to D$, $g' \colon C \to D$ with $f' \circ f = g' \circ g$.
So two members that share a common part fit into a third member, and the two copies of the common part coincide there.
AP is one of the three conditions in [[Fraïssé's theorem|~8123841a]], together with the hereditary property and the [[joint embedding property|~3b0d5f2d]].
For the [[age|~41dc9926]] of a countable structure, AP is exactly what lets finite partial isomorphisms be extended step by step.

## Motivation

In a countable [[ultrahomogeneous structure|~7468669e]] $M$, take finite substructures $A \subseteq B$ and $A \subseteq C$ of $M$.
An automorphism of $M$ moves a copy of $C$ so that it agrees with $B$ on $A$, and the substructure generated by both copies is an amalgam.
So the age of $M$ must have AP.
Fraïssé turned this around: a class with AP, and the other two conditions, is the age of a unique countable ultrahomogeneous structure, its [[Fraïssé limit|HJVRBDQSGG]].
AP is the local condition that makes the global back-and-forth construction work.

## Notation

The structure $D$ with the maps $f'$ and $g'$ is an *amalgam* of $B$ and $C$ over $A$.
The data $(A, B, C, f, g)$ is often drawn as a "V" shape, and the amalgam closes it to a commuting square.
If $f'(B) \cap g'(C) = f' f(A)$ can always be arranged, the class has the [[strong amalgamation property|Q94Z7TB8B5]].
The formal definition is [[this definition|2KBFZ1MPMW]].

## Remarks

### Examples

- The class of finite [[graphs|00C0BR7D7H]] has AP by free amalgamation; see [[this example|~dade84ce]].
- The class of finite linear orders has AP, but every amalgam of two incomparable new points must order them; see [[this example|~70d2a0f9]].
- The class of finite triangle-free graphs has AP by free amalgamation, since the glued graph adds no edges.
- The class of finite Boolean algebras has AP, and its Fraïssé limit is the countable atomless Boolean algebra; see [[this example|~efa643d7]].
- The class of finite fields of a fixed characteristic $p$ has AP, since all of them sit inside an algebraic closure of $\mathbb{F}_p$; see [[this example|~7dcd06ac]].

### Non-examples

- The class of finite bipartite graphs fails AP, since a path and a longer path between two points can close an odd cycle; see [[this non-example|~85a0ac66]].
- The class of finite graphs with at most $2$ vertices fails AP: one vertex extended by an edge and by a non-edge needs $3$ vertices.

### Basic properties

- If the empty structure is in $\mathcal{K}$ and embeds into every member, AP over it gives JEP.
- AP for $\mathcal{K}$ does not imply AP for the class of substructures of members of $\mathcal{K}$, so AP and HP are independent.
- For the age of a countable structure in a countable signature, AP holds exactly when some countable ultrahomogeneous structure has the same age.

### History

Fraïssé used amalgamation in his work of the early 1950s on countable relational structures.
In group theory, the amalgamated free product of two groups over a common subgroup gives AP for the class of all groups; see [[amalgamated free products|M848AHBRCM]].

### Common errors

- To check only that $B$ and $C$ embed into a common $D$. That is JEP; AP also asks that the two embeddings agree on $A$.
- To think AP needs the images of $B$ and $C$ to meet only in $A$. That is strong amalgamation, which is a stronger condition.
- To amalgamate along a substructure that is not in $\mathcal{K}$. The base $A$ must be a member.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two drawings both contain the same small picture.
Amalgamation means that one can draw a bigger picture that contains both drawings, with the two copies of the small picture lying on top of each other.
For graphs this is easy: glue the two graphs along the shared part and add no new edges.
For some classes the gluing breaks a rule of the class, and then AP fails.

### L2 human

We amalgamate two orders over a point.

1. Let $A = \set{a}$, $B = \set{a < b}$ and $C = \set{a < c}$, with the inclusion maps.
2. A linear order on $\set{a, b, c}$ must compare $b$ and $c$.
3. Choose $b < c$: the order $a < b < c$ contains $B$ and $C$, and both copies of $a$ agree.
4. The other choice $c < b$ works too, so the amalgam is not unique.

The typical use is to check the hypothesis of Fraïssé's theorem for a class of finite structures.
A trap is to amalgamate only over a single point; AP must hold over every common substructure.

### L3 human

AP says that the category of members of $\mathcal{K}$ with embeddings has pushout-like squares, though the amalgam need not be universal.
In model theory, AP for the class of models of a theory over arbitrary substructures is closely tied to quantifier elimination.
For ages of countable structures in a finite relational signature, AP together with HP and JEP gives an [[$\aleph_0$-categorical|A22K1X8GKD]] limit with quantifier elimination.
Stronger forms, such as free amalgamation and the Ramsey property, govern the dynamics of the automorphism group of the limit.
Hrushovski's construction replaces substructures by self-sufficient ones and keeps an AP for a restricted class of embeddings.
