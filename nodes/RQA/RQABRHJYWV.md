## Summary

A quasicrystal is a structure that is ordered but not periodic.
Its diffraction pattern shows sharp peaks, as for a crystal, yet no translation maps it onto itself.
Quasicrystals can have symmetries that no periodic crystal can have, such as five-fold or ten-fold rotations.
Dan Shechtman observed the first one, an aluminium–manganese alloy with icosahedral symmetry, in 1982, and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2011.
Mathematical models include the vertex sets of the [[Penrose tiling|MBE83FTRBG]] and other [[aperiodic tilings|Z3FCJZF6WK]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A crystal is like a tiled floor that repeats: shift it by one tile and it looks the same.
A quasicrystal is like a Penrose tiling: it never repeats, but it is far from random, and every patch appears again and again.
When X-rays are sent through it, the scattered beams still form sharp spots, the sign of long-range order.
These spots can show a five-fold symmetry, which is impossible for any periodic arrangement in the plane or in space.
