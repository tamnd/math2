## Statement

Let $R$ be a commutative ring in which $2$ is invertible, for example a field of characteristic not $2$.
Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $R$.
Then there are a unique [[symmetric matrix|0N28QN4CYA]] $S$ and a unique [[skew-symmetric matrix|03RNVFDEAN]] $K$ with $A = S + K$, namely
$$S = \tfrac12 \left(A + A^{\mathsf{T}}\right), \qquad K = \tfrac12 \left(A - A^{\mathsf{T}}\right).$$

## Remarks

### A check with numbers

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, [[this example|T4EGXYA8H3]] computes
$$S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 5/2 \\ 5/2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad K = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1/2 \\ 1/2 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
and $S + K = A$.
The symmetric part averages each entry with its mirror entry, and the skew part takes half their difference.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The number $2$ must be invertible for existence.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$, write $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = S + K$; with $s = s_{12} = s_{21}$ and $k = k_{12} = -k_{21}$, entry $(1, 2)$ gives $s + k = 1$ and entry $(2, 1)$ gives $s - k = 0$, so $2s = 1$, which has no solution in $\mathbb{Z}$.

Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ both halves of the theorem fail.
There $-1 = 1$, so every skew-symmetric matrix is symmetric, and then $S + K$ is always symmetric; so $N$, which is not symmetric, has no decomposition.
Uniqueness fails too, since $I = I + 0 = 0 + I$ and $I$ is both symmetric and skew-symmetric over $\mathbb{F}_2$.

Uniqueness alone needs less: if $2$ is not a zero divisor in $R$, as in $\mathbb{Z}$, a matrix that is both symmetric and skew-symmetric satisfies $2M = 0$ and so is $0$.

### What it gives you

Over a field $F$ of characteristic not $2$, the theorem says that $M_n(F) = \operatorname{Sym}_n(F) \oplus \operatorname{Skew}_n(F)$ as vector spaces.
Counting dimensions gives $n^2 = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2} + \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}$.
The maps $A \mapsto S$ and $A \mapsto K$ are [[idempotent|0KCE8CG86P]] linear maps on $M_n(F)$, the projections onto the two parts, and they add up to the identity.

A quadratic form only sees the symmetric part.
For a skew-symmetric $K$ and a column vector $x$, the $1 \times 1$ matrix $x^{\mathsf{T}} K x$ equals its transpose $-x^{\mathsf{T}} K x$, so it is $0$ when $2$ is invertible, and therefore $x^{\mathsf{T}} A x = x^{\mathsf{T}} S x$.
This is why quadratic forms are usually written with a symmetric matrix.

### Generalizations

The same proof works for any linear map $T$ on a module with $T^2 = \mathrm{id}$, when $2$ is invertible: every element is $v = \tfrac12 (v + Tv) + \tfrac12 (v - Tv)$, with $T$ acting as $+1$ on the first part and as $-1$ on the second.
For complex matrices with the conjugate transpose, it gives $A = H + iH'$ with $H$ and $H'$ Hermitian, the [[Cartesian decomposition|E2MNC3W2DM]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every square matrix is a symmetric matrix plus a skew-symmetric matrix.
The symmetric part replaces each pair of mirror entries by their average.
The skew part keeps half their difference, with opposite signs above and below the diagonal.

### L2 human

The transpose is a linear map $T$ on $M_n(F)$ with $T^2 = \mathrm{id}$.
The symmetric matrices are the matrices with $TA = A$, and the skew-symmetric ones those with $TA = -A$.
The formulas for $S$ and $K$ are the usual splitting of a vector into the two eigenspaces of such a map, and the [[proof|T2153B5Y7V]] finds them by solving two linear equations.
Watch out: the halves are essential, and the theorem is false over $\mathbb{F}_2$.
