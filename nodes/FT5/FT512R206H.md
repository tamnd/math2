## Summary

*Montague grammar* is an approach to the semantics of natural language that uses the methods of mathematical logic.
Each expression of a fragment of English gets a meaning in a model, and each syntactic rule has a semantic rule.
Montague translated English into an intensional [[higher-order logic|P1ABGYRQXH]] with [[lambda abstraction|BD756KR8FM]].
Its main ideas are [[compositionality|6D959ESMVJ]], model-theoretic entailment, intensions and noun phrases as [[generalized quantifiers|MFV98WTF4G]].

## Motivation

Logicians give exact syntax and semantics for formal languages.
Montague held that there is no important theoretical difference between these languages and natural languages.
So the aim of semantics is to define truth of a sentence in a model, and entailment between sentences, for a precise fragment of English.
Then "John walks and sings" entails "John walks" because of the form of the sentences and the rules that interpret them.

## Notation

The types are $e$ (entities), $t$ (truth values), $\langle a, b \rangle$ (functions from $a$ to $b$) and $\langle s, a \rangle$ (intensions of type $a$).
For a model with a domain $D$ and a set $I$ of indices, $D_e = D$, $D_t = \set{0, 1}$, $D_{\langle a, b \rangle} = D_b^{D_a}$ and $D_{\langle s, a \rangle} = D_a^{I}$.
An index is a pair of a possible world and a moment of time.
In the intensional logic, ${}^\wedge\alpha$ is the intension of $\alpha$, ${}^\vee\alpha$ is the extension of an intension at the current index, and $\lambda x\, \alpha$ is abstraction.
We write $\mathbf{sing}$, $\mathbf{man}$ and $\mathbf{john}$ for the translations of the words "sing", "man" and "John".

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[Every man is singing by lambda conversion|F8E0PCTM2R]]: the determiner "every" translates to $\lambda P \lambda Q\, \forall x\, (P(x) \to Q(x))$, and two beta reductions give the usual first-order formula.
- [[Two readings of every man loves a woman|25VGETDK53]]: two derivations give two translations, and a small model separates them.
- [[Seeking a unicorn without unicorns|2JKGRNRKM0]]: the de dicto reading of "John seeks a unicorn" does not entail that unicorns exist, but "John finds a unicorn" does.

### Non-examples

- A list of sentences with their first-order translations, given one by one, is not a Montague grammar. Montague grammar needs a rule-by-rule map from derivations to meanings.
- Discourse Representation Theory in its first form is not Montague grammar. It uses representations as an essential level, while Montague's translation into logic is in principle dispensable.

### Basic properties

- Rule-to-rule. For each syntactic rule there is a semantic rule that combines the meanings of the inputs. Bach called this the rule-to-rule hypothesis.
- Compositionality as a homomorphism. Syntax is an algebra, semantics is an algebra, and meaning is a homomorphism between them; this slogan is due to Janssen.
- Uniform noun phrases. "John", "every man" and "a woman" all denote sets of properties, of type $\langle \langle e, t \rangle, t \rangle$ in an extensional setting. This is the source of generalized quantifier theory, by Barwise and Cooper (1981).
- Downward entailing contexts. Ladusaw used generalized quantifiers to describe where negative polarity words such as "ever" can occur: in the scope of downward entailing expressions such as "no".
- Intensional semantics. The meaning of a sentence is a function from indices to truth values. This makes all tautologies synonymous, a known problem of the approach.
- Two-sorted alternative. A two-sorted extensional type logic gives the same interpretive effect (Gallin 1975), and many semanticists now prefer it.
- Scope ambiguity comes from derivational history: one string can have two derivations with different meanings, through the quantifying-in rule.

### History

Montague's interest in natural language arose around 1966, when he prepared a seminar in Amsterdam.
He set out his approach in three papers: "Universal Grammar" (1970), "English as a Formal Language" (1970) and "The Proper Treatment of Quantification in Ordinary English" (1973), called PTQ.
PTQ became the most influential of the three.
Partee (1973) made a first step to join Montague grammar with the syntax of the Chomsky tradition.
Grammars with fully explicit fragments were most popular in the 1980s.
The textbook of Heim and Kratzer (1998) made the type-driven approach popular.

### Common errors

- To think that Montague grammar gives unique logical forms. The translation into logic is a tool; equivalent formulas are equally acceptable.
- To read "every man" as a name of an object. It denotes a set of properties, not a generic man.
- To think that the intensional logic is first-order. It is higher-order, and its variables range over properties and intensions.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Montague grammar treats English like a language of logic.
Each word gets a meaning, and each way of combining words has a rule for combining meanings.
So we can compute what a sentence means, and which sentences follow from it.

### L2 human

Words get meanings of a fixed type: names and noun phrases are functions on properties, and verbs are properties.
Lambda terms describe these functions, and beta reduction simplifies the result to a familiar formula.
Intensions, which are functions from worlds and times, handle words such as "seek" and "necessarily".
A trap is to think that the translation into logic is the meaning; the meaning is the object in the model.

### L3 human

The syntax is a many-sorted algebra of categories, and the interpretation is a homomorphism into an algebra of typed meanings.
The intensional logic is a [[modal|C3RZY20ETK]] higher-order type theory, based on Church's simple theory of types and Kripke's possible worlds.
Generalized quantifier theory, dynamic semantics and type-driven interpretation all grew from this framework.
