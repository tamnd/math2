## Example

We prove that the two diagonals of every parallelogram $ABCD$ meet at the midpoint of each of them.
The tools are a [[figure and a good choice of coordinates|6CVYB413ER]].

1. Draw a parallelogram $ABCD$ in general position, with no right angles and no equal sides.
   The vertices go around in the order $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, so $AB$ is parallel to $DC$ and $AD$ is parallel to $BC$.
2. Translations and rotations keep lengths, midpoints and parallel lines, so we may put $A = (0, 0)$ and $B = (a, 0)$ with $a > 0$.
3. The vertex $D$ can be anywhere off the $x$-axis, so we write $D = (b, c)$ with $c \ne 0$.
4. In a parallelogram $\overrightarrow{DC} = \overrightarrow{AB} = (a, 0)$, so $C = D + (a, 0) = (a + b, c)$.
5. The [[midpoint|0S706JBZBG]] of $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is $\left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$, so the midpoint of $AC$ is
   $$\left( \frac{0 + a + b}{2}, \frac{0 + c}{2} \right) = \left( \frac{a + b}{2}, \frac{c}{2} \right).$$
6. The midpoint of $BD$ is
   $$\left( \frac{a + b}{2}, \frac{0 + c}{2} \right) = \left( \frac{a + b}{2}, \frac{c}{2} \right).$$
7. The two midpoints are one point $M$, which lies on both diagonals.
   The diagonals are distinct lines, because $A$, $B$ and $D$ are not on one line when $c \ne 0$, and two distinct lines meet in at most one point.
   So the diagonals meet at $M$, the midpoint of each.

## Remarks

The general parallelogram needs only the three parameters $a$, $b$ and $c$, and that is the gain from the choice of coordinates.
With an arbitrary placement $A = (p, q)$, $B = (r, s)$, $D = (t, u)$ the calculation is the same, but it carries six letters instead of three.
The choice in step 2 is allowed because the statement does not change under translations and rotations.

The same coordinates describe the special cases.
A rectangle has $b = 0$, and a rhombus has $b^2 + c^2 = a^2$, since then $|AD| = |AB|$.
The diagonals have equal length exactly when $(a + b)^2 + c^2 = (b - a)^2 + c^2$, that is, when $4ab = 0$, so exactly for rectangles.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Put a parallelogram on squared paper with one corner at the origin and one side along the bottom line.
Then three numbers describe it: the length $a$ of the bottom side, and the position $(b, c)$ of the corner above the origin.
The middle of each diagonal is found by averaging the coordinates of its two ends, and both diagonals give the same middle point.
For $a = 4$, $b = 1$ and $c = 2$, both middles are at $(2.5, 1)$.

### L2 human

We use the same coordinates to compare the lengths of the diagonals:
$$|AC|^2 = (a + b)^2 + c^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 + c^2, \qquad |BD|^2 = (b - a)^2 + c^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2 + c^2.$$
Adding gives $|AC|^2 + |BD|^2 = 2a^2 + 2(b^2 + c^2) = 2|AB|^2 + 2|AD|^2$, which is the [[parallelogram law|026HPGYGS4]].
Subtracting gives $|AC|^2 - |BD|^2 = 4ab$, which is $0$ exactly when $b = 0$, that is, when the angle at $A$ is a right angle.

One choice of coordinates answers several questions about the same figure.
The placement $D = (0, c)$ looks simpler still, but it makes every parallelogram a rectangle, and the results would then hold only for rectangles.
