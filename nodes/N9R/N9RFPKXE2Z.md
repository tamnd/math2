## Summary

The geothermal gradient is the rate at which temperature increases with depth inside the Earth.
Near the surface of continents, away from volcanic regions, it is typically about 25 to 30 degrees Celsius per kilometre.
Multiplied by the thermal conductivity of the rock, it gives the heat flow out of the Earth.
Measurements of it constrain the thermal state of the lithosphere and were used in early estimates of the age of the Earth.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Deep mines are hot: the deeper you go, the warmer the rock.
The geothermal gradient says how fast it warms up, for example 25 degrees for each kilometre of depth.
At that rate, rock 4 km down is about 100 degrees hotter than at the surface.
The heat comes from the hot interior of the Earth and from radioactive elements in the rock.
The gradient is not the same everywhere, and it does not stay constant deep down, where heat is carried by flow rather than by conduction.
