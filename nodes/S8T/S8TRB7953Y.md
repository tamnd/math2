## Example

Let $U = \R(1, 0)$ be the $x$-axis in $\R^2$.
We find all [[complements|04C3BAHV03]] of $U$, and we show that they are the lines $W_m = \R(m, 1)$ for $m \in \R$.

1. Let $W$ be a complement of $U$.
   Then $\dim W = \dim \R^2 - \dim U = 1$, by the [[dimension|05G2G0P224]] count for a direct sum.
   So $W = \R(a, b)$ for some $(a, b) \ne (0, 0)$.
2. If $b = 0$, then $W = \R(a, 0) = U$, and $U \cap W = U \ne \{0\}$.
   So a complement has $b \ne 0$.
3. Dividing the spanning vector by $b$ does not change the line, so $W = \R(m, 1)$ with $m = a / b$.
4. Conversely, fix $m$ and let $W_m = \R(m, 1)$.
   A vector $t(m, 1)$ has second entry $t$, so it lies in $U$ only when $t = 0$.
   So the [[intersection|0V33SPH7RR]] $U \cap W_m$ is $\{0\}$.
5. Also $\dim U + \dim W_m = 1 + 1 = 2 = \dim \R^2$.
   By the [[dimension criterion|P4JG9DJ2HH]], $\R^2 = U \oplus W_m$.
6. The decomposition is explicit:
$$(x, y) = (x - m y, 0) + y(m, 1).$$
   Check: the right side is $(x - my + my, y) = (x, y)$.
7. Different values of $m$ give different lines, since $(m, 1)$ and $(m', 1)$ are proportional only when $m = m'$.

So the complements of the $x$-axis are the lines $\R(m, 1)$, one for each real number $m$, and there are infinitely many of them.

## Remarks

The vertical line $\R(0, 1)$, for $m = 0$, is the [[orthogonal complement|0XJXTBCB2T]] of $U$ for the standard inner product.
It is one complement among infinitely many, and nothing in the definition of a complement prefers it.

The $U$-part of a vector changes with the complement.
For $(3, 2)$, step 6 gives $(3, 0) + (0, 2)$ when $m = 0$, and $(1, 0) + (2, 2)$ when $m = 1$.
So "the component of $v$ along $U$" makes sense only after a complement is fixed.

Each $W_m$ is isomorphic to $\R^2 / U$ by $y(m, 1) \mapsto y(m, 1) + U$, as the theorem that [[every complement is isomorphic to the quotient|244KTXDJHW]] predicts.
The coset of $y(m, 1)$ is the horizontal line at height $y$, whatever $m$ is.

The number of complements depends on the field.
Over the field $\mathbb{F}_2$ with two elements, the same steps give only the two lines $\mathbb{F}_2(0, 1)$ and $\mathbb{F}_2(1, 1)$, because $m$ can only be $0$ or $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A complement of the horizontal axis is any line through the origin that is not horizontal.
Such a line passes through exactly one point at height $1$, and that point is $(m, 1)$ for some number $m$.
So there is one complement for each number $m$.

### L2 human

The work splits into a necessary condition and a check.
Steps 1 to 3 show that a complement must be a line $\R(m, 1)$, using only the dimension count and the intersection condition.
Steps 4 and 5 check that every such line is a complement.
The trap is to stop at the orthogonal choice $m = 0$ and call it "the" complement: the line $\R(1, 1)$ for $m = 1$ is just as good.
