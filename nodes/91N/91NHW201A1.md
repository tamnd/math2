## Proof

1. Part 1. Suppose $A$ is [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]]. By the [[determinant of the inverse|0783S9D0ZH]], $\det A$ is a unit of $K$.
2. Conversely, suppose $\det A$ is a unit and $n \ge 2$. The [[adjugate formula for the inverse|0ENVM493VK]] shows that $A$ is invertible, with $A^{-1} = (\det A)^{-1} \operatorname{adj} A$.
3. Let $n = 1$ and $A = (a)$. By [[multilinearity|0PPG793642]] in the only row and the [[determinant of the identity|0Z4BWARC38]], $\det A = a \det I_1 = a$.
4. So $a$ is a [[unit|019NFGFPR5]], and $(a)(a^{-1}) = (a^{-1})(a) = I_1$. So $A$ is invertible, and $\operatorname{adj} A = (1)$ gives the same formula.
5. Part 2. By the [[Leibniz formula|016PE4VZ8Q]], $\det A = \sum_{\sigma} \sgn(\sigma) \prod_i a_{i \sigma(i)}$.
6. A ring homomorphism preserves sums, products, $1$ and $-1$. So $\varphi(\det A) = \sum_{\sigma} \sgn(\sigma) \prod_i \varphi(a_{i \sigma(i)})$.
7. By the Leibniz formula over $L$, the right side of step 6 is $\det \varphi(A)$.
8. Part 3. The units of $\mathbb{Z}$ are $1$ and $-1$. So part 1 with $K = \mathbb{Z}$ gives part 3.

## Idea

One direction uses the product rule: an inverse matrix forces $\det A \cdot \det(A^{-1}) = 1$.
For the other direction, the adjugate divided by $\det A$ is an explicit inverse, and this division needs $\det A$ to be a unit.
Naturality holds because the Leibniz formula uses only sums and products.

## Variants

Over an integral domain $R$ with field of fractions $F$, one can also argue in $F$: $A^{-1}$ exists over $F$, and it has entries in $R$ exactly when $(\det A)^{-1} \in R$.
Row reduction is not available over a general ring, since it divides by pivots; the adjugate replaces it.
