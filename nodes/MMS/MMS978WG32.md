## Definition

Let $b_1, \dots, b_n \in \R^m$ be linearly independent and let $B$ be the $m \times n$ matrix with columns $b_i$.
The [[lattice|Z4FSES3CZ0]] generated by $B$ is $\mathcal{L}(B) = \set{Bz : z \in \Z^n}$.
Its minimum $\lambda_1(\mathcal{L})$ is the least Euclidean [[norm|0V2WP9B1MF]] of a nonzero vector of $\mathcal{L}$.
For $\gamma \ge 1$, the *approximate shortest vector problem* $\mathrm{SVP}_\gamma$ asks, given $B$, for a nonzero $v \in \mathcal{L}(B)$ with $\norm{v} \le \gamma \lambda_1(\mathcal{L}(B))$.
*Lattice-based cryptography* is the part of [[cryptography|7YNRCX56CM]] whose schemes are proved secure under the assumption that such lattice problems are hard.
This covers problems such as $\mathrm{SVP}_\gamma$ for $\gamma$ polynomial in $n$, and average-case problems that reduce to them, such as [[learning with errors|TAKWH3D7R6]].

## Remarks

Example: $\Z^n = \mathcal{L}(I_n)$ is a lattice with $\lambda_1 = 1$, attained by the standard basis vectors.
For this basis $\mathrm{SVP}_1$ is trivial, which shows that hardness depends on the basis given, and is only expected for random bases of large dimension.

Non-example: the vectors $1$ and $\sqrt{2}$ in $\R$ are linearly dependent over $\R$, so they do not form a basis as required.
The set $\set{a + b\sqrt{2} : a, b \in \Z}$ is dense in $\R$, it has no shortest nonzero vector, and it is not a lattice.

The LLL algorithm solves $\mathrm{SVP}_\gamma$ in polynomial time for $\gamma = 2^{(n-1)/2}$, so only small approximation factors can be hard.
