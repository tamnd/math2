## Statement

Let $R$ be a commutative ring.
Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix and $B$ an $n \times p$ matrix over $R$.
Then $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$, where ${}^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The sizes make both sides defined: $B^{\mathsf{T}}$ is $p \times n$ and $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ is $n \times m$.
- The product $A^{\mathsf{T}} B^{\mathsf{T}}$ is $n \times m$ times $p \times n$, so it is not defined unless $m = p$.
- Commutativity of $R$ is necessary. Over the quaternions $\mathbb{H}$, take the $1 \times 1$ matrices $A = (i)$ and $B = (j)$.
- Then $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = (k)$, but $B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} = (ji) = (-k)$.

### Consequences

- By induction on $k$, $(A_1 A_2 \cdots A_k)^{\mathsf{T}} = A_k^{\mathsf{T}} \cdots A_2^{\mathsf{T}} A_1^{\mathsf{T}}$.
- For column vectors $x, y \in R^n$, the $1 \times 1$ matrix $x^{\mathsf{T}} y$ is the [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]] $x \cdot y$.
- So $(Ax) \cdot y = (Ax)^{\mathsf{T}} y = x^{\mathsf{T}} (A^{\mathsf{T}} y) = x \cdot (A^{\mathsf{T}} y)$.
- For an [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] $A$, $(A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} = (A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}$; see [[this example|G9XS9NZ7DV]].
- For every matrix $A$, the matrices $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ and $A A^{\mathsf{T}}$ are [[symmetric|0VR671PHE5]].

### Generalizations

- Over any ring $R$, the same computation gives $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$ with the product taken in the opposite ring $R^{\mathrm{op}}$.
- For complex matrices, the [[conjugate transpose|0DXKE10PHF]] satisfies $(AB)^* = B^* A^*$.
- For linear maps, the dual map satisfies $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$, and the [[matrix of the dual map is the transpose|0ZPB3180AY]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The transpose of a product is the product of the transposes in the reverse order.
This is like taking off socks and shoes: the last thing put on comes off first.
The sizes also force this order.

### L2 human

The entry $(k, i)$ of $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}}$ is row $i$ of $A$ times column $k$ of $B$.
The same numbers appear in $B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$, as column $k$ of $B$ read as a row times row $i$ of $A$ read as a column.
The products of entries come in the other order, so we need $ab = ba$ in $R$.

### L3 human

The theorem says that the transpose is an anti-isomorphism of rings from $M_n(R)$ to itself when $R$ is commutative.
For a general ring it is an isomorphism $M_n(R)^{\mathrm{op}} \cong M_n(R^{\mathrm{op}})$.
