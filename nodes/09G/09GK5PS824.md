## Statement

Let $R$ be a commutative ring.
Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix and $B$ an $n \times p$ matrix over $R$.
Then
$$(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}},$$
where ${}^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]].

## Remarks

### A check with numbers

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$.
Then $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 5 \\ 20 & 11 \end{pmatrix}$, so $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 20 \\ 5 & 11 \end{pmatrix}$.
On the other side,
$$B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 5 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 20 \\ 5 & 11 \end{pmatrix},$$
which agrees.
The product in the same order, $A^{\mathsf{T}} B^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 11 \\ 4 & 18 \end{pmatrix}$, does not.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The sizes are part of the statement.
Here $B^{\mathsf{T}}$ is $p \times n$ and $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ is $n \times m$, so $B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$ is always defined, while $A^{\mathsf{T}} B^{\mathsf{T}}$ is $n \times m$ times $p \times n$ and is defined only when $m = p$.

Commutativity of $R$ is also needed, because the proof uses $b_{jk} a_{ij} = a_{ij} b_{jk}$.
Over the quaternions, take the $1 \times 1$ matrices $A = (i)$ and $B = (j)$.
A $1 \times 1$ matrix is its own transpose, so $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = (ij) = (k)$, but $B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} = (ji) = (-k)$.

### What it gives you

By induction on the number of factors, $(A_1 A_2 \cdots A_k)^{\mathsf{T}} = A_k^{\mathsf{T}} \cdots A_2^{\mathsf{T}} A_1^{\mathsf{T}}$.

For column vectors $x, y \in R^n$, the $1 \times 1$ matrix $x^{\mathsf{T}} y$ is the [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]] $x \cdot y$.
The theorem then moves a matrix across a dot product:
$$(Ax) \cdot y = (Ax)^{\mathsf{T}} y = x^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} y = x \cdot (A^{\mathsf{T}} y).$$

For an [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] matrix $A$, transposing $A^{-1} A = I$ and $A A^{-1} = I$ gives $(A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} = (A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}$, as in [[the 2 × 2 example|G9XS9NZ7DV]].

For every matrix $A$, we get $(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{\mathsf{T}} = A^{\mathsf{T}} (A^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}} = A^{\mathsf{T}} A$, so $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ and likewise $A A^{\mathsf{T}}$ are [[symmetric|0VR671PHE5]].

### Generalizations

Over any ring $R$, the same computation gives $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$ when the product on the right is taken with the multiplication of the opposite ring $R^{\mathrm{op}}$.
For complex matrices, the [[conjugate transpose|0DXKE10PHF]] obeys the same rule, $(AB)^* = B^* A^*$.
For linear maps, the dual maps satisfy $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$, and the [[matrix of the dual map is the transpose|0ZPB3180AY]], so the theorem is the coordinate form of this rule.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The transpose of a product is the product of the transposes in the reverse order.
This is like taking off socks and shoes: what you put on last comes off first.
The sizes force this order, since the factors in the same order often cannot even be multiplied.

### L2 human

The entry $(k, i)$ of $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}}$ is entry $(i, k)$ of $AB$, which is row $i$ of $A$ times column $k$ of $B$.
In $B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$, entry $(k, i)$ is row $k$ of $B^{\mathsf{T}}$ times column $i$ of $A^{\mathsf{T}}$, and these are column $k$ of $B$ and row $i$ of $A$.
So both entries are sums of the same products, with the two factors in each product written in the other order, and $ab = ba$ in $R$ makes the sums equal.
The [[proof|ADD4AZEF2Z]] writes this out.

### L3 human

For a commutative ring $R$, the theorem says that the transpose is an anti-automorphism of the ring $M_n(R)$: it is additive, it fixes $I$, and it reverses products.
For a general ring it gives an isomorphism of rings $M_n(R)^{\mathrm{op}} \cong M_n(R^{\mathrm{op}})$.
The proof uses no subtraction, so the theorem holds over commutative semirings too, and Mathlib states it as `Matrix.transpose_mul` for matrices indexed by arbitrary finite types.
