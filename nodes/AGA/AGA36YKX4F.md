## Summary

A canonical transformation is a change of variables $(q, p) \mapsto (Q, P)$ in phase space that preserves the form of [[Hamilton's equations|CFYQ6R38YP]].
Equivalently, it preserves the symplectic form, or the [[Poisson brackets|2S0MXDT4YH]], so it sends [[canonical coordinates|XKDVF07R1Q]] to canonical coordinates.
Such transformations are the main tool to simplify a Hamiltonian, as in [[action-angle coordinates|M3T81YNSKC]] and in [[Hamilton–Jacobi theory|5TRXQKP7DY]].
They are often produced by a [[generating function|NXK6HD49NZ]].
See the [[definition|G026JG1YX5]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Hamilton's equations have the same shape in many coordinate systems, but not in all of them.
A canonical transformation is a change of coordinates that keeps this shape, so one may solve the problem in whatever canonical variables are easiest.
Swapping position and momentum, $Q = p$ and $P = -q$, is canonical.
Stretching the position alone, $Q = 2q$ and $P = p$, is not, since it changes areas in the $(q, p)$ plane.
The time evolution of a Hamiltonian system is itself a canonical transformation at each moment.
