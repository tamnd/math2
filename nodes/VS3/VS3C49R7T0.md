## Statement

Let $M$ be the mass of a central body, $G$ the gravitational constant and $c$ the speed of light.
Consider a bound timelike [[geodesic|RNRTS8WWNM]] of the [[Schwarzschild metric|6Y50T68WKY]] of mass $M$.
Suppose it is close to a [[Kepler orbit|BXFSBB8Q46]] with semi-major axis $a$ and eccentricity $e$, with $GM/(c^2 a) \ll 1$.
Then, to first order in $GM/(c^2 a)$, the perihelion advances in the direction of motion by the angle
$$\Delta \varphi = \frac{6 \pi G M}{c^2 a (1 - e^2)}$$
per revolution.

## Remarks

The orbit equation of the [[Kepler problem in general relativity|PSR2K4RJ7B]] is $u'' + u = GM/h^2 + 3GM u^2/c^2$, with $u = 1/r$.
Treating the last term as a perturbation of the Newtonian ellipse $u_0 = (GM/h^2)(1 + e\cos\varphi)$ gives a solution of the form $u \approx (GM/h^2)(1 + e \cos((1 - \delta)\varphi))$ with $\delta = 3G^2M^2/(c^2h^2)$.
So the perihelion recurs after the angle $2\pi/(1 - \delta) \approx 2\pi(1 + \delta)$, and $2\pi\delta$ equals the formula above because $h^2 \approx GM a(1 - e^2)$.
For Mercury, with the Sun as central body, the formula gives about $43$ arcseconds per century.
This is the part of Mercury's observed perihelion advance that Newtonian perturbations from the other planets did not explain.
Einstein's derivation of it in 1915 was the first success of [[general relativity|7M7M55TKXA]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A planet does not trace exactly the same ellipse again and again.
In general relativity the ellipse itself turns slowly in the plane of the orbit, so the point of closest approach to the Sun moves forward a little each time.
For Mercury this extra turning is tiny, about $43$ arcseconds per century, but astronomers had measured it decades before Einstein, and Newton's theory could not account for it.

### L2 human

The effect is largest for orbits that are small and eccentric, since the formula has $a(1 - e^2)$ in the denominator.
The same computation applies to binary pulsars, where the periastron advance is measured to high precision and is used to determine the masses of the stars.
In the [[parameterized post-Newtonian formalism|2TAW8GAMM1]] the factor $6$ becomes $(2 + 2\gamma - \beta) \cdot 2$, so the perihelion shift measures a combination of the parameters $\gamma$ and $\beta$.
