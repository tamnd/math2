## Statement

Let $G$ be a [[locally compact abelian group|ZR09S9R3AW]].
In this statement, $\cong$ means isomorphism of topological groups, and products carry the product topology.

1. Let $G$ be compactly generated, that is, generated as a group by a [[compact|0TVJZYB6SH]] subset. Then $G \cong \R^n \times \Z^m \times K$ for some integers $n, m \ge 0$ and some compact abelian group $K$.
2. In general, $G$ has an [[open subgroup|QYCFH68RD1]] $U$ with $U \cong \R^n \times K$ for some integer $n \ge 0$ and some compact abelian group $K$.
3. In general, $G \cong \R^n \times H$ for some integer $n \ge 0$ and some locally compact abelian group $H$ that has a compact open subgroup.

## Remarks

Statement 2 follows from 1.
A compact neighbourhood $V$ of the identity generates an open subgroup $U$, which is compactly generated, so $U \cong \R^n \times \Z^m \times K$.
Inside it, $\R^n \times \set{0} \times K$ is open, because $\Z^m$ is discrete, and it is open in $G$ as well.

Examples: the $p$-adic numbers $\Q_p$ have the compact open subgroup $\Z_p$, so $n = 0$ in statement 3.
The [[direct product|0YP7WYXF9V]] $\R \times \Z \times \mathbb{T}$, with $\mathbb{T}$ the circle group, is compactly generated, as in 1.

The hypothesis in 1 is needed.
The group $\Q$ with the discrete topology is not of the form $\R^n \times \Z^m \times K$.
Since $\Q$ is discrete, such a decomposition would have $n = 0$.
Its compact subgroups are finite, hence trivial, since $\Q$ is torsion free, so it would be $\Z^m$, but $\Q$ is not finitely generated.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every locally compact abelian group is built from three kinds of pieces: copies of the real line, copies of the integers, and a compact group.
For groups generated by a compact set this is literally a product.
In general it holds near the identity: a small open piece of the group looks like a space $\R^n$ times a compact group.

### L2 human

The theorem reduces many questions about locally compact abelian groups to Euclidean spaces, discrete groups and compact groups, where Fourier analysis is classical.
Statement 3 splits off the Euclidean part, so the remaining factor has a compact open subgroup, as the $p$-adic numbers and the discrete groups do.
The proof and the theorem are closely tied to Pontryagin duality: the dual of $\R^n \times \Z^m \times K$ is $\R^n \times \mathbb{T}^m \times \widehat{K}$, with $\widehat{K}$ discrete.
