## Statement

Let $\mu > 0$ and let $V \subseteq \R^3$ be a bounded open set with smooth boundary $S$ and outward unit normal $n$.
For $k = 1, 2$ let $(u_k, p_k)$ be a [[Stokes flow|13CB7V2YM4]] in $V$ without body force, smooth up to the boundary: $\mu \Delta u_k = \nabla p_k$ and $\nabla \cdot u_k = 0$ in $V$.
Let $\sigma_k = -p_k I + \mu (\nabla u_k + \nabla u_k^T)$ be the stress tensor of the flow $k$.
Then
$$\int_S u_1 \cdot (\sigma_2 n) \, dS = \int_S u_2 \cdot (\sigma_1 n) \, dS.$$

## Remarks

The momentum equation says $\nabla \cdot \sigma_k = 0$.
Hence $\nabla \cdot (\sigma_2 u_1) = \sigma_2 : \nabla u_1 = 2 \mu \, e_2 : e_1$, where $e_k$ is the [[strain rate tensor|P1KWMQG4NZ]] of $u_k$ and the pressure term drops because $\nabla \cdot u_1 = 0$.
The right side is symmetric in the two flows, so $\nabla \cdot (\sigma_2 u_1 - \sigma_1 u_2) = 0$, and the [[divergence theorem|0JX86PSSA9]] gives the identity.
The theorem also holds in the exterior of bounded bodies if $u_k = O(1/r)$ and $\sigma_k = O(1/r^2)$ at infinity, because then the integral over a large sphere tends to $0$.
Example: it shows that the resistance matrix of a rigid particle, which maps its translational and angular velocity to the force and torque, is symmetric.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Slow viscous flows obey a kind of exchange rule.
If you know how one flow pushes on the surface of a region, you can learn about a second flow without solving for it.
The work done by the first flow's forces on the second flow's motion equals the work of the second's forces on the first's motion.

### L2 human

The theorem is the Stokes-flow analogue of Green's second identity for the Laplacian, and it reflects the self-adjointness of the Stokes operator.
It lets one compute the force on a particle in a complicated flow from an easy auxiliary flow.
A typical auxiliary flow is the flow past a translating sphere from [[Stokes' law|WW2DTGE881]].
In this way one derives Faxén's laws and the swimming speed of squirmers without solving the full problem.
It requires the linear Stokes equations: the nonlinear inertial term breaks the symmetry.
