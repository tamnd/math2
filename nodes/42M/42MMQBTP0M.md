## Summary

Start with one vector, apply a linear map to it again and again, and collect everything you can build from the results.
That collection is a cyclic subspace, and it is the smallest piece of the space on which the map acts by itself.
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$, let $T \colon V \to V$ be an [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]], and let $v \in V$.
The *$T$-cyclic subspace generated by $v$* is
$$Z(v; T) = \operatorname{span}\{ v, Tv, T^2 v, \dots \} = \{ f(T) v : f \in F[x] \}.$$
The *$T$-annihilator* of $v$ is the monic polynomial $m_v$ of least degree with $m_v(T) v = 0$.
The vector $v$ is a *cyclic vector* for $T$ when $Z(v; T) = V$, and $T$ is *cyclic* when it has a cyclic vector.
A square matrix $A$ is cyclic when the map $x \mapsto Ax$ is cyclic.

## Motivation

To understand a linear map, we look for subspaces that the map sends into themselves, the [[invariant subspaces|0ACX5QT8TE]].
An invariant subspace that contains $v$ must also contain $Tv$, then $T^2 v$, and so on.
So the smallest one is the span of this orbit, and that is $Z(v; T)$.

On a cyclic subspace the map is as simple as it can be.
It moves each vector of the list $v, Tv, T^2 v, \dots$ to the next one, until some vector of the list is a combination of the earlier ones.
The coefficients of that combination form a polynomial, so the whole action of $T$ on $Z(v; T)$ is recorded by one polynomial.
The [[cyclic decomposition theorem|R3Z20J19BR]] cuts every space into cyclic pieces, and this is the road to the rational canonical form and the Jordan form.
The same subspaces appear in numerical linear algebra as [[Krylov subspaces|QB45PCGQPV]], where iterative methods search for solutions of $Ax = b$ inside the span of $b, Ab, A^2 b, \dots$.

## Notation

Hoffman and Kunze write $Z(v; T)$ and $p_v$, and other books write $\langle v \rangle_T$, $F[T] v$ or $\mathcal{C}_v$.
In the language of modules, $Z(v; T)$ is the cyclic submodule $F[x] \cdot v$ of the [[$F[x]$-module of $T$|KGVTFBZX59]].
A cyclic map is also called *nonderogatory*, and some books call a cyclic matrix *regular*.
The word "cyclic" refers to the single generator, as in a cyclic group, and a cyclic map need not satisfy $T^k = I$ for any $k$.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ on $F^2$ and $v = (1, 1)$.
Apply $A$ once and twice:
$$Av = (1, 2), \qquad A^2 v = (1, 4).$$
The vectors $v$ and $Av$ are independent, since $\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 1$, so already $Z(v; A) = F^2$ and $v$ is a cyclic vector.
The third vector depends on the first two:
$$A^2 v = 3 Av - 2 v, \quad\text{so}\quad (A^2 - 3A + 2I) v = 0.$$
No polynomial of degree $1$ kills $v$, so $m_v = x^2 - 3x + 2 = (x - 1)(x - 2)$.
The vector $e_1$ behaves differently: $A e_1 = e_1$, so $Z(e_1; A) = F e_1$ and $m_{e_1} = x - 1$.
The [[worked example for diag(1, 2)|N7V8N48ESF]] finds all cyclic vectors of this map.

### A basis from one vector

The computation shows the general pattern.
If $k = \deg m_v$, then $v, Tv, \dots, T^{k-1} v$ is a [[basis of $Z(v; T)$|5VYBJYPYMW]], so $\dim Z(v; T) = \deg m_v$.
In this basis $T$ sends each basis vector to the next, and it sends the last one to $-c_0 v - c_1 Tv - \cdots - c_{k-1} T^{k-1} v$, where $m_v = x^k + c_{k-1} x^{k-1} + \cdots + c_0$.
That matrix is the [[companion matrix|0ZCWPNZTQ3]] $C(m_v)$.

The derivative $D$ on the polynomials of degree at most $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ gives a second example.
Starting from $x^2$ we get $2x$, then $2$, then $0$, so $x^2$ is a cyclic vector with $m_{x^2} = x^3$, as the [[example of the derivative|XFZ12FWK4F]] checks in detail.
A third example is the shift $e_1 \mapsto 0$, $e_i \mapsto e_{i-1}$ on $F^n$, with cyclic vector $e_n$, since $e_n, e_{n-1}, \dots, e_1$ is the whole standard basis.

### When no vector is enough

The [[identity matrix $I_2$|M135QJH5NQ]] is not cyclic.
For every $v$ the list $v, I_2 v, I_2^2 v, \dots$ is $v, v, v, \dots$, so $Z(v; I_2) = Fv$ has dimension at most $1$.
The zero map on $F^n$ with $n \ge 2$ fails for the same reason.
An [[eigenvector|0N6960W0BV]] $v$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ always gives the smallest possible cyclic subspace, $Z(v; T) = Fv$ with $m_v = x - \lambda$.

### The rules you can use

The annihilator divides every polynomial that kills $v$, so in particular $m_v$ divides the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] $m_T$.
The reason is division with remainder, and the [[lemma on cyclic subspaces|5VYBJYPYMW]] proves it with the basis above.
In the other direction, there is always a [[vector whose annihilator is the whole minimal polynomial|QPE5Y86Y8V]].

These two facts give the test for cyclic maps.
A cyclic vector $v$ needs $\deg m_v = \dim V$, and $\deg m_v \le \deg m_T \le \deg \chi_T = \dim V$.
So $T$ is cyclic [[if and only if $m_T$ equals the characteristic polynomial $\chi_T$|RK1QVMYW7H]].
For $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ both polynomials are $(x - 1)(x - 2)$, and for $I_2$ they are $x - 1$ and $(x - 1)^2$.

### Watch out

A cyclic map has a cyclic vector, but most cyclic maps also have vectors that are not cyclic.
For $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ the vector $e_1$ is not cyclic, so one failed test vector proves nothing about $T$.

The list $v, Tv, T^2 v, \dots$ is not a basis of $Z(v; T)$ either.
It stops being independent after $\deg m_v$ terms, and in the first computation $A^2 v$ is already a combination of $v$ and $Av$.

Distinct eigenvalues are enough for a cyclic map, but they are not needed.
The [[Jordan block|07PH4RX0X5]] $J_2(1) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the single eigenvalue $1$, and still $e_2$ is a cyclic vector, since $J_2(1) e_2 = (1, 1)$ and $e_2, (1, 1)$ is a basis.

### History

The decomposition of a space into cyclic subspaces is the basis of the rational canonical form, which is also called the Frobenius normal form after Georg Frobenius.
The Krylov subspaces of numerical analysis are named after Aleksey Krylov.

### Try it

First, is $v = (1, 0, 1)$ a cyclic vector for $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2, 3)$?
No: $Av = (1, 0, 3)$ and $A^2 v = (1, 0, 9)$ all have second entry $0$, so $Z(v; A)$ is the plane of vectors with second entry $0$, and $m_v = (x - 1)(x - 3)$.

Second, let $N$ be the shift with $N e_1 = 0$, $N e_2 = e_1$, $N e_3 = e_2$ on $F^3$; find $m_{e_1}$ and $m_{e_3}$.
Since $N e_1 = 0$ we get $m_{e_1} = x$, and since $e_3, e_2, e_1$ are independent and $N^3 e_3 = 0$ we get $m_{e_3} = x^3$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take a vector and apply a map to it again and again.
You get a list: the vector, its image, the image of the image, and so on.
Everything you can build from this list with sums and multiples is the cyclic subspace of the vector.
For the map that doubles the second coordinate of a point in the plane, start at the point $(1, 1)$.
The next point is $(1, 2)$, and these two points already reach every point of the plane.
So one vector generates the whole plane under this map, and the map is cyclic.
For the map that does nothing, every vector generates only its own line, and that map is not cyclic.

### L2 human

Here is a typical computation of annihilators.
Let $T$ be the map of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ on $\mathbb{Q}^3$, so that $Te_1 = e_2$, $Te_2 = e_3$ and $Te_3 = e_1$.
The vector $v = e_1 + e_2 + e_3$ is fixed, $Tv = v$, so $Z(v; T) = \mathbb{Q} v$ and $m_v = x - 1$.
The vector $e_1$ runs through the whole basis, $e_1, e_2, e_3$, so it is cyclic, and $T^3 e_1 = e_1$ gives $m_{e_1} = x^3 - 1$.

Now take $u = e_1 - e_2$.
Then $Tu = e_2 - e_3$, and the next vector depends on these two:
$$T^2 u = e_3 - e_1 = -u - Tu.$$
So $(T^2 + T + I) u = 0$, while $u$ and $Tu$ are independent, and $m_u = x^2 + x + 1$ with $\dim Z(u; T) = 2$.
Check: both $m_v = x - 1$ and $m_u = x^2 + x + 1$ divide $m_T = x^3 - 1 = (x - 1)(x^2 + x + 1)$.
The computation shows how a cyclic map can hide smaller cyclic subspaces, one for each factor of its minimal polynomial.

### L3 human

Let $V_T$ be $V$ seen as a module over $F[x]$, with $x$ acting as $T$.
Then $Z(v; T)$ is the cyclic submodule $F[x] v$, the ideal of polynomials that kill $v$ is $(m_v)$, and $Z(v; T) \cong F[x]/(m_v)$.
So a cyclic map is the same thing as a module $F[x]/(f)$, and the [[cyclic decomposition theorem|R3Z20J19BR]] is the structure theorem for finitely generated torsion modules over the principal ideal domain $F[x]$.
In control theory a pair $(A, b)$ is controllable exactly when $b$ is a cyclic vector of $A$, which is the rank condition on $b, Ab, \dots, A^{n-1} b$.
Over $\mathbb{C}$ the cyclic matrices form a dense open set, and their [[centralizers|GXZGWZSJ2J]] have the least possible dimension $n$.
