## Summary

Proof by intimidation, handwaving and the related styles are informal names, from mathematical [[jargon|4M1FGQ0HNZ]], for arguments that look like a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] but replace a needed step by something that is not a reason.
In proof by assertion the step is only stated, perhaps many times, and in proof by authority it is accepted because of who stated it.
In proof by intimidation, a word that calls the step obvious, or the status of the speaker, discourages the question.
Handwaving replaces the step by a vague description or a picture, and an argument that proves too much would, if valid, also prove a false statement.
The names matter because each one marks a gap that a reader must find and fill, even when the claim is true.

## Motivation

A proof must convince a reader who checks each step.
Real proofs leave out routine steps, and the reader fills them in.
The line between a routine omission and a gap is not sharp, and authors sometimes cross it, often without intention.
Mathematicians made up humorous names for the ways of crossing it, and the names help a reader to say exactly what is wrong with an argument.

## Notation

The names are informal, and books use them in slightly different ways.

1. Proof by assertion, or proof by repeated assertion: the claim is stated, maybe several times, without an argument. In logic the fallacy is called *argumentum ad nauseam*.
2. Proof by authority: the claim is true "because an expert says so". In logic this is the argument from authority.
3. Proof by intimidation: words such as "evident" or "it is well known" are used to stop the reader from asking for a step. The page on [[trivial, obvious and clearly|Y0HXCJRB9B]] treats these words.
4. Handwaving: a step is replaced by a vague picture, an analogy or "and so on". The adjective is "handwavy".
5. Proving too much: an argument is invalid because the same argument, with small changes, gives a false conclusion.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The claim "every continuous function has a tangent line at each point, since its graph looks straight when we zoom in" is [[handwaving that fails|VG1JH5V84P]] at the corner of $|x|$.
2. The claim that the diagonal argument shows that the rationals are uncountable is [[an argument that proves too much|C1JT3NBC8V]]. The rationals are countable, so the argument must fail somewhere.
3. A lecturer writes "this is trivial" for a step that needs a page, and moves on. The step is not proved, whether or not it is true.
4. A student writes "by a theorem of Gauss" with no statement or reference. The reader cannot check that a theorem of Gauss gives the step.
5. A "proof" that a series converges because "the terms get small" is handwaving. The [[harmonic series|0CXTMAY5PX]] $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ has terms that tend to $0$, and it [[diverges|0E9M24KS9J]].

### Non-examples

1. A step that [[cites a theorem with an exact statement and reference|HXKWN94QSQ]] is not proof by authority. The reader can check the cited proof and the hypotheses.
2. A step called "routine" for which the author gives the method, such as "expand both sides", is not proof by intimidation. The reader can do the step from the hint.
3. A figure that suggests a proof, followed by an argument that proves each fact read from the figure, is not handwaving. Each step has a reason.

### Basic properties

1. These styles are about the reasons for a step, not about its truth. A handwaving argument can have a true conclusion, as a [[howler|AAS5V2CKBG]] can.
2. A gap that is marked as a gap, for example "we omit the proof of the lemma", is honest. The fault in proof by intimidation is that the gap is hidden.
3. To show that an argument proves too much, it is enough to give one false statement that the same argument proves. This shows that the argument is invalid, but it does not show which step fails.
4. To answer a handwaving step, write the step as a precise statement, and then prove it or find a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]].

### History

Gian-Carlo Rota wrote in *Indiscrete Thoughts* (1997) that Mark Kac coined the phrase "proof by intimidation" after lectures of William Feller.
Nathaniel Bowditch, who translated Laplace's *Mécanique céleste* into English, wrote that each "Thus it plainly appears" of Laplace cost him hours, perhaps days, of hard study; the account is in the memoir of Bowditch published in 1839.
Humorous lists of invalid "proof techniques" circulate among mathematicians.
The Wikipedia glossary of mathematical jargon lists "by intimidation", "by example" and "handwaving" among its proof techniques, next to serious methods such as diagram chasing.

### Common errors

1. A student accepts a step because the lecturer or the book called it obvious. The step still needs a reason, and the student should supply it.
2. A student draws a figure and reads off the conclusion without a proof. The [[geometric fallacies|5CJ4KD4D96]] show how a figure can mislead.
3. A student believes that "proving too much" shows where the error is. It shows only that an error exists.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Sometimes a teacher says "this is obvious" and goes on, and nobody dares to ask why.
That is called proof by intimidation, as a joke.
Handwaving is a similar trick: instead of a reason, the speaker waves the hands and says "it goes like this, and so on".
For example, "the numbers $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$ double, so the next one is $32$" is handwaving.
For the [[regions of a circle cut by chords|E4RAKSC2ZC]] the numbers start in this way, and the next one is $31$.
A real proof gives a reason for every step, so that anyone can check it.

### L2 human

We test a handwaving argument by the method of proving too much.
The argument claims: "if $f(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$, then $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx$ converges, because the function becomes small".

1. Apply the same argument to $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$. Then $f(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$.
2. Compute $\int_1^t \frac{dx}{x} = \ln t$, which tends to $\infty$ as $t \to \infty$.
3. So the integral diverges, and the argument proves a false statement. It proves too much.
4. The precise question is how fast $f$ tends to $0$. For example, $\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x^2}$ converges, because $\int_1^t \frac{dx}{x^2} = 1 - \frac{1}{t} \to 1$.
5. A correct statement is the comparison test: if $0 \le f(x) \le g(x)$ and $\int_1^\infty g$ converges, then $\int_1^\infty f$ converges.

The typical use of the method is in the review of a proof: change the hypotheses a little and see whether the argument still "works".
A trap is to stop after the first step: the method finds that an argument is invalid, and the reader must still find the failing step.

### L3 human

The informal names correspond to classical fallacies of informal logic: proof by assertion to *argumentum ad nauseam*, proof by authority to *argumentum ad verecundiam*, and proving too much to a form of *reductio* applied to the argument itself.
Proving too much is a semantic method: an argument scheme is invalid exactly when some instance of the scheme has true premises and a false conclusion, and the method exhibits such an instance.
In research, the border between an acceptable sketch and handwaving depends on the community, and the topic on [[rigour and the acceptance of proofs|1BTHSHST4M]] discusses how a proof becomes accepted.
Formal proof assistants remove the possibility of all these styles, because each step must be justified by a rule or a cited result, and no phrase that calls a step obvious is accepted.
