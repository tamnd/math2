## Summary

Here is a confident proof; does it convince you?

> Claim: if $a_n \to 0$, then the series $\sum a_n$ converges.
> Proof: the terms become negligible, so after a while adding them changes nothing, and the sums settle down; this is well known.

The claim is false.
The [[harmonic series|0CXTMAY5PX]] $1 + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \cdots$ has terms that tend to $0$, and it [[diverges|0E9M24KS9J]].
The proof gives no reason at all: "negligible" is a picture, and "well known" asks the reader not to look.
The true statement is the converse, and it has a two-line proof.
If the partial sums $S_n$ tend to $S$, then $a_n = S_n - S_{n-1} \to S - S = 0$.

Mathematical [[jargon|4M1FGQ0HNZ]] has playful names for such arguments, which look like a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] but replace a needed step by something that is not a reason.
Handwaving replaces the step by a vague picture, proof by intimidation hides it behind a word like "trivial" or the speaker's status, proof by assertion only states it, and proof by authority accepts it because of who said it.
A related test, proving too much, shows that an argument is invalid because the same argument would also prove a false statement.

## Motivation

Real proofs leave out routine steps, and the reader fills them in.
The line between a routine omission and a gap is not sharp, and authors cross it, usually without meaning to.
The names give a reader a precise way to say what is wrong: not "I don't like it", but "this step is handwaving, and here is what it would need".
They also remind us that a gap is a gap even when the claim is true.

## Notation

The names are informal, and books use them in slightly different ways.
Proof by assertion states the claim, perhaps repeatedly, without an argument; informal logic calls this *argumentum ad nauseam*.
Proof by authority accepts a claim because an expert said it; logic calls this the argument from authority, *argumentum ad verecundiam*.
Proof by intimidation uses phrases such as "evident" or "it is well known" to stop the question, and [[words like trivial and obvious|Y0HXCJRB9B]] are its usual tools.
Handwaving replaces a step by a vague picture, an analogy or "and so on", and such an argument is called handwavy.
Proving too much refutes an argument by showing that, with small changes, it gives a false conclusion.

## Remarks

### Examples to sort

Name the fault in each argument, or decide that there is none.

1. "Every continuous function has a tangent line at each point, since its graph looks straight when we zoom in." This is [[handwaving that fails|VG1JH5V84P]] at the corner of $|x|$.
2. "The diagonal argument shows that the rationals are uncountable." The rationals are countable, so the argument [[proves too much|C1JT3NBC8V]].
3. A lecturer writes "this is trivial" for a step that needs a page. The step is not proved, whether or not it is true.
4. "By a theorem of Gauss, the step follows", with no statement or reference. The reader cannot check that any theorem of Gauss gives the step.
5. "By the intermediate value theorem, stated here, with each hypothesis checked, a root exists." This is [[a citation, not proof by authority|HXKWN94QSQ]].

### Honest gaps and hidden gaps

These styles are about the reasons for a step, not its truth, and a handwaving argument can reach a true conclusion just as a [[howler|AAS5V2CKBG]] can.
A gap marked as a gap, such as "we omit the proof of the lemma", is honest, and the fault in proof by intimidation is that the gap is hidden.
A step called "routine" together with its method, such as "expand both sides", is no intimidation, since the reader can carry it out.
A figure followed by a proof of every fact read from it is no handwaving either.
To answer a handwaving step, write it as a precise statement, then prove it or find a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]].

### Proving too much as a test

To show that an argument proves too much, give one false statement that the same argument proves.
The opening "proof" fails this test at once, since the harmonic series has $a_n \to 0$.
The test shows that an error exists, but it does not show which step fails; that is still the reader's job.

### Watch out

A step is not proved because a lecturer or a book called it obvious, and a student should be able to supply the reason.
A figure can mislead, as the [[geometric fallacies|5CJ4KD4D96]] show, so every fact read from a figure needs a proof.

### History

Gian-Carlo Rota wrote in *Indiscrete Thoughts* (1997) that Mark Kac coined the phrase "proof by intimidation" after lectures by William Feller.
Nathaniel Bowditch translated Laplace's *Mécanique céleste* into English, and the 1839 memoir of his life reports that each "Thus it plainly appears" in Laplace's text cost him hours, perhaps days, of hard study.
Humorous lists of invalid "proof techniques" have long circulated among mathematicians.

### Try it

Is "the sequence $\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$ tends to $1$, since $1 + \frac{1}{n} \to 1$ and $1$ to any power is $1$" a proof?
No: it is handwaving, since the exponent grows too, and in fact the limit is $e \approx 2.718$.

An argument uses only that $f$ is bounded on $[0, 1]$ and concludes that $f$ has a maximum there.
Does it prove too much?
Yes: $f(x) = x$ for $x < 1$ and $f(1) = 0$ is bounded and has no maximum.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Sometimes a teacher says "this is obvious" and moves on, and nobody dares to ask why.
As a joke, that is called proof by intimidation.
Handwaving is similar: instead of a reason, the speaker waves the hands and says "it goes like this, and so on".
For example, "the numbers $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$ double, so the next one is $32$" is handwaving.
For the [[regions of a circle cut by chords|E4RAKSC2ZC]] the numbers start this way, and the next one is $31$.
A real proof gives a reason for every step, so that anyone can check it.

### L2 human

We test a handwaving argument by proving too much.
It claims that if $f(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$, then $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx$ converges, "because the function becomes small".
Apply it to $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$, which tends to $0$.
But $\int_1^t \frac{dx}{x} = \ln t \to \infty$, so the integral diverges, and the argument proves a false statement.
The precise question is how fast $f$ tends to $0$: $\int_1^t \frac{dx}{x^2} = 1 - \frac{1}{t} \to 1$, so that integral converges.
A correct tool is the comparison test: if $0 \le f \le g$ and $\int_1^\infty g$ converges, then $\int_1^\infty f$ converges.

The typical use of the test is in reviewing a proof: change the hypotheses a little and see whether the argument still "works".
Watch out for stopping after the test, since it finds that an argument fails, not where.

### L3 human

The informal names match classical fallacies: proof by assertion is *argumentum ad nauseam*, proof by authority is *argumentum ad verecundiam*, and proving too much is a *reductio* applied to the argument itself.
Proving too much is a semantic method: an argument scheme is invalid exactly when some instance has true premises and a false conclusion, and the method exhibits such an instance.
In research, the border between an acceptable sketch and handwaving depends on the community, and [[rigor and the acceptance of proofs|1BTHSHST4M]] discusses how a proof comes to be accepted.
Proof assistants rule out all these styles, since each step must follow from a rule or a cited result, and no phrase calling a step obvious is accepted.
