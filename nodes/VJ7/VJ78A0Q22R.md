## Summary

The formula $[a^*] p \leftrightarrow p \wedge [a][a^*] p$ says: "$p$ holds after every repetition of $a$" means "$p$ holds now, and after one $a$-step the same is true again".
It is a law about a program, the loop $a^*$, written as a modal formula.
Propositional dynamic logic ($\mathsf{PDL}$) is the [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]] with one box $[\alpha]$ for each program $\alpha$, and laws like this one are its axioms.

Programs and formulas are defined together from atomic programs $a$ and propositional variables $p$:
$$\alpha ::= a \mid \alpha ; \alpha \mid \alpha \cup \alpha \mid \alpha^* \mid \varphi ? \qquad \varphi ::= p \mid \bot \mid \neg \varphi \mid \varphi \wedge \varphi \mid [\alpha] \varphi .$$
A model is a Kripke model $(W, (R_a)_a, V)$, and each program gets a relation.
The relation $R_{\alpha ; \beta}$ is the composite "an $\alpha$-step, then a $\beta$-step", $R_{\alpha \cup \beta} = R_\alpha \cup R_\beta$, and $R_{\alpha^*}$ is the reflexive [[transitive closure|05J9NWX07X]] of $R_\alpha$.
The test $R_{\varphi ?}$ is the set of pairs $(w, w)$ with $w \models \varphi$.
Then $w \models [\alpha] \varphi$ when $\varphi$ holds at every $v$ with $w R_\alpha v$, and $\langle \alpha \rangle \varphi$ is $\neg [\alpha] \neg \varphi$.

## Motivation

A Hoare triple $\{\varphi\} \, \alpha \, \{\psi\}$ says that if $\varphi$ holds before $\alpha$ runs, then $\psi$ holds after every run of $\alpha$ that ends.
Vaughan Pratt saw that this is a modal statement, $\varphi \to [\alpha] \psi$, where $[\alpha]$ looks at the end states of the runs of $\alpha$.
Programs are built from basic steps by sequence, choice and iteration, the operations of [[regular expressions|4DMYF2PR4Q]].
Tests $\varphi ?$ let a program check a condition, so conditionals and while loops become regular programs too.
So one modal logic, with a box for each regular program, can state and prove properties of all of them.

$\mathsf{PDL}$ became the pattern for many later modal logics of action.
Common knowledge in epistemic logic is a star modality, and the [[modal mu-calculus|MZ0M2KR9S0]] extends the idea of the star to arbitrary fixed points.

## Notation

Read $[\alpha] \varphi$ as "after every run of $\alpha$, $\varphi$", and $\langle \alpha \rangle \varphi$ as "some run of $\alpha$ ends where $\varphi$ holds".
Some books write $\alpha \beta$ for $\alpha ; \beta$ and $\alpha + \beta$ for $\alpha \cup \beta$.
The usual programs of a language are abbreviations.
The program $\mathbf{skip}$ is $\top ?$, and $\mathbf{if}\ \varphi\ \mathbf{then}\ \alpha\ \mathbf{else}\ \beta$ is $(\varphi ? ; \alpha) \cup (\neg \varphi ? ; \beta)$.
The loop $\mathbf{while}\ \varphi\ \mathbf{do}\ \alpha$ is $(\varphi ? ; \alpha)^* ; \neg \varphi ?$.
We write $\mathrm{FL}(\varphi)$ for the Fischer-Ladner closure of $\varphi$.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take $W = \{0, 1, 2\}$ with $R_a = \{(0, 1), (1, 2)\}$ and $V(p) = \{0, 1\}$.
The $a^*$-successors of $0$ are $0$, $1$ and $2$, since the star allows zero, one or two steps.
World $2$ is not a $p$-world, so $[a^*] p$ is false at $0$.
Now compute the right side of the unfolding law at $0$.
The part $p$ is true at $0$, and $[a][a^*] p$ asks for $[a^*] p$ at the only $a$-successor, $1$:
$$[a^*] p \text{ at } 1 \iff p \text{ at } 1 \text{ and } p \text{ at } 2 \iff \text{false}.$$
So both sides are false at $0$, as the law demands.
[[Evaluating a PDL formula with star and composition|58ZZBPW3WF]] works a larger case with two atomic programs.

### Programs as relations

Each program operation is an operation on relations.
Composition joins steps end to end, union collects the runs of either program, and the star adds the identity and all finite chains.
A test does not move: it keeps the worlds where the condition holds and drops the rest.
With these four operations [[a while program|DT8R2PB0P7]] becomes the relation that sends each start to the place where the loop stops.
A start where the loop never stops gets no successor at all, so termination is the formula $\langle \alpha \rangle \top$.
Hoare triples become formulas, and the rules of [[Hoare logic|H1M6RSBJ5H]] for propositional programs become theorems.

### The axioms

Segerberg's axiom system has the tautologies, the axiom $\mathrm{K}$ for each $[\alpha]$, modus ponens and [[necessitation|G989VP2NGP]] for each $[\alpha]$, and these axioms.
$$[\alpha ; \beta] \varphi \leftrightarrow [\alpha][\beta] \varphi, \qquad [\alpha \cup \beta] \varphi \leftrightarrow [\alpha] \varphi \wedge [\beta] \varphi, \qquad [\psi ?] \varphi \leftrightarrow (\psi \to \varphi),$$
$$[\alpha^*] \varphi \leftrightarrow \varphi \wedge [\alpha][\alpha^*] \varphi, \qquad [\alpha^*](\varphi \to [\alpha] \varphi) \to (\varphi \to [\alpha^*] \varphi).$$
The fourth axiom unfolds the star once, and the fifth is the induction axiom.
The unfolding axiom alone only says that $R_{\alpha^*}$ contains the closure.
The induction axiom says that it contains nothing more, as [[a star interpreted too large|13HTANHBNW]] shows by a structure where it fails.
Kozen and Parikh proved in 1981 that the system is complete: every formula valid in all models is a theorem.

### Closure, small models and complexity

The completeness proof and the decision method both use the Fischer-Ladner closure.
It is the smallest set that contains $\varphi$ and its subformulas and unfolds programs one level.
So $[\alpha ; \beta] \psi$ brings in $[\alpha][\beta] \psi$, and $[\alpha \cup \beta] \psi$ brings in $[\alpha] \psi$ and $[\beta] \psi$.
A test $[\chi ?] \psi$ brings in $\chi$, and $[\alpha^*] \psi$ brings in $[\alpha][\alpha^*] \psi$.
Its size is linear in the length of $\varphi$.

A satisfiable $\varphi$ has a model with at most $2^{|\mathrm{FL}(\varphi)|}$ worlds, by [[filtration|6ZZSHAKG5F]] through the closure, so validity is decidable.
Fischer and Ladner proved in 1979 that satisfiability is hard for [[EXPTIME|T9G5S33CA0]], and Pratt gave an exponential-time algorithm.
So $\mathsf{PDL}$ satisfiability is EXPTIME-complete.

### What it is not

$\mathsf{PDL}$ is not compact.
The set $\{\langle a^* \rangle p\} \cup \{[a]^n \neg p : n \geq 0\}$ is unsatisfiable, since every $a^*$-successor lies at some distance $n$, yet each finite part has a model on a long enough chain.
So completeness holds for single formulas, not for infinite sets of premises.
Intersection $\alpha \cap \beta$ is not a program of $\mathsf{PDL}$, and the logic with intersection is a different system.

### Watch out

The star includes zero runs, so $R_{\alpha^*}$ is reflexive and $[\alpha^*] \varphi \to \varphi$ is valid.
A common slip is to read $[a] p \to [a^*] p$ as valid, by thinking of the star as "one or more".
Take $w R_a v$ with $p$ true at $v$ and false at $w$.
Then $[a] p$ holds at $w$, but $[a^*] p$ fails there, because $w R_{a^*} w$ and $p$ is false at $w$.
A second slip is to read $[\alpha] \varphi$ as total correctness: it is true when $\alpha$ has no run that ends.

### History

Vaughan Pratt introduced dynamic logic in 1976, as a modal reading of Hoare logic.
Michael Fischer and Richard Ladner defined the propositional version and proved its decidability with the closure that bears their names; the journal paper appeared in 1979.
Krister Segerberg proposed the axiom system in 1977, and Kozen and Parikh published a short completeness proof in 1981.

### Try it

First, is $\langle a^* \rangle p \to p \vee \langle a \rangle \langle a^* \rangle p$ valid?
Yes: a chain of $a$-steps to a $p$-world either has length zero, so $p$ holds now, or its first step leads to a world that starts a shorter chain.

Second, on $W = \{0, 1\}$ with $R_a = \{(0, 1), (1, 0)\}$ and $V(p) = \{0\}$, where does $[a^*] p$ hold?
Nowhere, since both worlds reach $1$, where $p$ fails.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Propositional dynamic logic talks about programs and what is true after they run.
The formula $[\alpha] \varphi$ says "however the program $\alpha$ runs, if it stops, then $\varphi$ is true afterwards".
Programs are built from basic steps: do one thing then another, choose one of two things, repeat something any number of times, or check a condition.
If a counter starts at $0$ and a program adds $1$ any number of times, then afterwards the counter is at least $0$.

### L2 human

We show that the induction axiom $[\alpha^*](\varphi \to [\alpha] \varphi) \to (\varphi \to [\alpha^*] \varphi)$ is valid.
Fix a world $w$ where $[\alpha^*](\varphi \to [\alpha] \varphi)$ and $\varphi$ both hold, and let $w R_{\alpha^*} v$.
By the definition of the reflexive transitive closure there is a chain $w = v_0, v_1, \dots, v_n = v$ with $v_i R_\alpha v_{i+1}$.
We show that $\varphi$ holds at each $v_i$, by induction on $i$.
At $v_0 = w$ it holds by assumption.
If it holds at $v_i$, then $w R_{\alpha^*} v_i$ gives $\varphi \to [\alpha] \varphi$ at $v_i$, so $[\alpha] \varphi$ holds there and $\varphi$ holds at $v_{i+1}$.
So $\varphi$ holds at $v$, and $[\alpha^*] \varphi$ holds at $w$.

This is how one proves a loop invariant: a property that holds at the start and is kept by each step.
Watch out: the hypothesis $\varphi \to [\alpha] \varphi$ must hold at every world reachable by $\alpha^*$, not only at $w$, or the induction step has nothing to use.

### L3 human

$\mathsf{PDL}$ is the modal logic of binary relations under composition, union, reflexive transitive closure and tests, and its program part is close to Kleene algebra with tests.
Its completeness proof became a pattern: a canonical model, a filtration through a finite closure, and a check that the star behaves correctly in the filtered model.
Extensions add converse programs, intersection or first-order assignments.
The modal $\mu$-calculus contains $\mathsf{PDL}$ as a fragment without alternation of fixed points, since $\langle \alpha^* \rangle \varphi$ is $\mu X . (\varphi \vee \langle \alpha \rangle X)$.
