## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear.
The following conditions are equivalent.

1. $T$ is triangularizable: there is a basis of $V$ in which the [[matrix|0YQSGNYE3K]] of $T$ is [[upper triangular|0N4XY7Z3NN]].
2. There is a flag of $T$-[[invariant subspaces|0ACX5QT8TE]] $0 = V_0 \subset V_1 \subset \cdots \subset V_n = V$ with $\dim V_i = i$ for each $i$.
3. The [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] $p_T$ splits over $F$, that is, $p_T = (t - \lambda_1) \cdots (t - \lambda_n)$ with $\lambda_i \in F$.

In a triangular form, the diagonal entries are the $\lambda_i$, each repeated as often as its [[algebraic multiplicity|0FY42N02DV]].
In matrix form: an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over $F$ is [[similar|0E89HQATM8]] to an upper triangular matrix if and only if $p_A$ splits over $F$.
Over an [[algebraically closed field|0PF6TM8TWT]], such as $\C$, every endomorphism and every square matrix is triangularizable.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The splitting condition is needed. Over $\R$, the [[rotation|XS5H42KEP5]] $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has $p = t^2 + 1$ and no invariant line, so it has no flag and no triangular form.
- Finite dimension is needed for the flag. The shift $(x_1, x_2, \dots) \mapsto (0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ on sequences has no eigenvector, so it has no invariant line.

### Consequences

- $\det T = \lambda_1 \cdots \lambda_n$ and $\operatorname{tr} T = \lambda_1 + \cdots + \lambda_n$, from the diagonal of the triangular form.
- For each polynomial $f$, the matrix of $f(T)$ is triangular with diagonal entries $f(\lambda_i)$. So the eigenvalues of $f(T)$ are the $f(\lambda_i)$ when $p_T$ splits.
- A triangular form gives a short proof of the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0QEM68Y0DE]]: $(T - \lambda_1) \cdots (T - \lambda_i)$ kills $V_i$, by induction on $i$.
- A [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]] map is triangularizable with zeros on the diagonal, since $p_T = t^n$.

### Converse

The three conditions are equivalent, so every converse holds.
A triangular form is not unique: the order of the diagonal entries can be changed, and the entries above the diagonal depend on the basis.

### Generalizations

Over $\C$ with an inner product, the basis can be chosen orthonormal: this is the [[Schur decomposition|V1JG4C7R7B]].
A commuting family over $\C$ has a common invariant flag, by [[simultaneous triangularization|42PG67Q5YN]].
For a solvable Lie algebra of matrices over $\C$, a common flag exists by [[Lie's theorem|11K6SVNXMH]].
The triangular form is the first step towards the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].

## History

The unitary version over $\C$ is named after Schur.
The triangular form over an algebraically closed field is a standard textbook theorem, for example in Axler, Chapter 5.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Not every matrix can be made diagonal.
Over the complex numbers, every matrix can at least be made triangular by a change of basis.
A triangular matrix is almost as useful, because its stretch factors are on the diagonal.
Over the real numbers, a rotation cannot be made triangular, because it moves every line.

### L2 human

Triangularize $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\R$.

1. $p_A = (t - 1)(t - 3) + 1 = t^2 - 4t + 4 = (t - 2)^2$, which splits.
2. $A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has kernel spanned by $(1, 1)$. So $V_1 = \operatorname{span}(1, 1)$ is an invariant line.
3. Extend to the basis $(1, 1), (0, 1)$, so $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $Q^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. $A Q = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, so $Q^{-1} A Q = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
5. The diagonal shows the eigenvalue $2$ twice, as $p_A$ says.

The typical use is to reduce a statement about all matrices over $\C$ to triangular matrices, where it can be checked by hand.
The trap is to think that a triangular form shows diagonalizability: here $A$ is triangularizable but not diagonalizable, since its eigenspace is a line.

### L3 human

A full flag of $V$ is a point of the flag variety, and a triangularizable $T$ fixes such a point.
The upper triangular matrices form a Borel subgroup of $GL_n$, and triangularization says that each element of $GL_n(\C)$ lies in a conjugate of it.
Lie's theorem and the Borel fixed point theorem extend this from one element to a connected solvable group.
