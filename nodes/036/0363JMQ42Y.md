## Statement

A linear map can be put in upper triangular form exactly when its characteristic polynomial splits into linear factors, and over $\C$ this always happens.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear.
Then the following conditions are equivalent.

1. $T$ is triangularizable: there is a basis of $V$ in which the [[matrix|0YQSGNYE3K]] of $T$ is [[upper triangular|0N4XY7Z3NN]].
2. There is a flag of $T$-[[invariant subspaces|0ACX5QT8TE]] $0 = V_0 \subset V_1 \subset \dots \subset V_n = V$ with $\dim V_i = i$ for each $i$.
3. The [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] of $T$ splits over $F$, that is, $p_T(t) = (t - \lambda_1) \cdots (t - \lambda_n)$ with all $\lambda_i \in F$.

In every triangular form, the diagonal entries are the $\lambda_i$, and each eigenvalue appears as often as its [[algebraic multiplicity|0FY42N02DV]].
In matrix form: an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over $F$ is [[similar|0E89HQATM8]] to an upper triangular matrix if and only if $p_A$ splits over $F$.
Over an [[algebraically closed field|0PF6TM8TWT]], such as $\C$, every endomorphism of $V$ and every square matrix is triangularizable.

## Remarks

### A first example

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\R$.
Its characteristic polynomial is
$$p_A(t) = (t - 1)(t - 3) + 1 = t^2 - 4t + 4 = (t - 2)^2,$$
which splits, so the theorem promises a triangular form with $2, 2$ on the diagonal.
To find it, take the eigenvector $(1, 1)$, which spans the kernel of $A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and extend it to the basis $(1, 1), (0, 1)$.
With $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $Q^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$,
$$A Q = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q^{-1} A Q = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The flag of condition 2 is $0 \subset \operatorname{span}(1, 1) \subset \R^2$.
The matrix $A$ is not diagonalizable, since its only eigenspace is a line, so triangular is the best that we can reach.

### Why the splitting condition is needed

Over $\R$, the [[rotation by a right angle|XS5H42KEP5]] $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has $p(t) = t^2 + 1$, which has no real root.
A triangular form would put an eigenvalue in the top left corner, and the first basis vector would be a real eigenvector.
The rotation turns every line through $0$, so it has no invariant line, no flag and no triangular form over $\R$.
Over $\C$ the same matrix has the eigenvalues $i$ and $-i$, and it is even diagonalizable there.

The finite dimension matters as well.
On the space of real sequences, the shift $(x_1, x_2, \dots) \mapsto (0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ has no eigenvector at all, so it has no invariant line to start a flag.

### What it gives you

The diagonal of a triangular form holds all the eigenvalues, so
$$\det T = \lambda_1 \cdots \lambda_n, \qquad \operatorname{tr} T = \lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_n.$$
For each polynomial $f$, the matrix of $f(T)$ in the same basis is triangular with diagonal entries $f(\lambda_i)$, so the eigenvalues of $f(T)$ are the numbers $f(\lambda_i)$.
A triangular form also gives a short proof of the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0QEM68Y0DE]]: the map $T - \lambda_i$ sends $V_i$ into $V_{i-1}$, so the product $(T - \lambda_1) \cdots (T - \lambda_n)$ sends $V$ to $0$.
A [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]] map has $p_T(t) = t^n$, so it is triangularizable with zeros on the diagonal.

### The converse and uniqueness

The three conditions are equivalent, so every converse holds.
A triangular form is far from unique.
The eigenvalues can appear on the diagonal in any order, and the entries above the diagonal depend on the basis.
Triangularizable does not mean diagonalizable, as the first example shows.

### Generalizations

Over $\C$ with an inner product, the basis can be chosen orthonormal, which is the [[Schur decomposition|V1JG4C7R7B]].
A commuting family over $\C$ has a common invariant flag, by [[simultaneous triangularization|42PG67Q5YN]].
For a solvable Lie algebra of matrices over $\C$, a common flag exists by [[Lie's theorem|11K6SVNXMH]].
The triangular form is the first step towards the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]] of the sub-area on canonical forms, which chooses the entries above the diagonal as simply as possible.

## History

The unitary form over $\C$ carries the name of Issai Schur.
The triangular form over an algebraically closed field appears in every standard text, for example in Chapter 5 of Axler.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Not every matrix can be made diagonal by a change of basis.
Over the complex numbers, every matrix can at least be made triangular, with zeros below the diagonal.
A triangular matrix is almost as useful as a diagonal one, because its stretch factors stand on the diagonal.
For example, the triangular matrix with rows $(2, 1)$ and $(0, 2)$ stretches by $2$ in both diagonal places.
Over the real numbers, a rotation by a right angle cannot be made triangular, because it moves every line.

### L2 human

Let us triangularize a $3 \times 3$ matrix, $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\R$, in a basis that starts with an eigenvector.
It is lower triangular, so $p_A(t) = (t - 2)^2 (t - 3)$, which splits.
The eigenvector for $3$ is $e_3$, since $A e_3 = 3 e_3$.
Next, $A e_2 = 2 e_2 + e_3$ lies in $\operatorname{span}(e_2, e_3)$, and $A e_1 = 2 e_1 + e_2$ lies in $\R^3$.
So the flag $\operatorname{span}(e_3) \subset \operatorname{span}(e_3, e_2) \subset \R^3$ is invariant, and in the basis $e_3, e_2, e_1$ the matrix is
$$\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: the first column is the coordinate vector of $A e_3 = 3 e_3$, the second that of $A e_2 = e_3 + 2 e_2$, and the third that of $A e_1 = e_2 + 2 e_1$.
The typical use is to reduce a statement about all complex matrices to triangular ones, where you can check it by hand.
The trap is to read diagonalizability from a triangular form: here the eigenvalue $2$ has only a line of eigenvectors, so this $A$ is not diagonalizable.

### L3 human

A full flag of $V$ is a point of the [[flag variety|NHRWCQQKTH]], and a triangularizable $T$ fixes such a point.
The invertible upper triangular matrices form a [[Borel subgroup|R7FTZ813DA]] of $GL_n$, and triangularization says that each element of $GL_n(\C)$ lies in a conjugate of it.
The Lie-Kolchin theorem and the Borel fixed point theorem extend this from one element to a connected solvable group.
