## Example

Label the corners of a square $1, 2, 3, 4$ in counterclockwise order.
A symmetry of the square permutes the corners, so we write each symmetry as a permutation of $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$.
The symmetries form the [[dihedral group|0JZ27DMJ5D]] $D_4$ of order $8$, and the product $xy$ means "first $y$, then $x$".
Let $r = (1\,2\,3\,4)$ be the [[rotation|0HFREYRSZP]] by $90$ degrees counterclockwise.
Let $s = (2\,4)$ be the [[reflection|06BRDF3F0E]] in the diagonal through the corners $1$ and $3$.
We show that $\{r, s\}$ is a [[generating set|0MBV2VHMXA]] of $D_4$.

1. The powers of $r$ are $e$, $r = (1\,2\,3\,4)$, $r^2 = (1\,3)(2\,4)$ and $r^3 = (1\,4\,3\,2)$, and $r^4 = e$. These are four different rotations.
2. The products $s r^k$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, 3$ are $s = (2\,4)$, $sr = (1\,4)(2\,3)$, $sr^2 = (1\,3)$ and $sr^3 = (1\,2)(3\,4)$. These are four different permutations.
3. No $sr^k$ is a power of $r$. If $sr^k = r^j$, then $s = r^{j-k}$ is a rotation. But $s \neq e$ fixes the corner $1$, and the only rotation that fixes a corner is $e$.
4. By steps 1 to 3, the eight elements $e, r, r^2, r^3, s, sr, sr^2, sr^3$ are different, and all of them lie in $\langle r, s \rangle$.
5. $D_4$ has exactly $8$ elements, so $\langle r, s \rangle = D_4$.

## Remarks

The example shows that two elements can describe a group of eight symmetries.
Each symmetry has the form $r^k$ (a rotation) or $s r^k$ (a reflection), with $0 \leq k \leq 3$.

The set $\{r, s\}$ is a minimal generating set.
The powers of $r$ give only the four rotations, and the powers of $s$ give only $\{e, s\}$.
No single element generates $D_4$, because each element has order $1$, $2$ or $4$, and not $8$.
So $d(D_4) = 2$.

Other pairs also generate $D_4$.
Two reflections are enough when their axes meet at an angle of $45$ degrees: $s \cdot sr = s^2 r = r$, so $\langle s, sr \rangle$ contains $r$ and $s$, and it is $D_4$.
Not every pair works.
The set $\{r^2, s\}$ generates only $\{e, r^2, s, sr^2\}$, a subgroup of order $4$.

The same proof works for the regular polygon with $n \geq 3$ sides.
The rotation $r$ by $360/n$ degrees and one reflection $s$ generate the dihedral group $D_n$ of order $2n$.
The generators satisfy $r^n = e$, $s^2 = e$ and $srs = r^{-1}$, and these relations give a [[presentation|0JGEQXQ4AC]] of $D_n$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take a square card with its corners numbered $1$ to $4$.
A quarter turn moves each corner to the next one.
A flip over the line through corners $1$ and $3$ swaps corners $2$ and $4$.
With quarter turns we get $4$ positions of the card.
With a flip and then quarter turns we get $4$ more positions, with the card face down.
These $8$ positions are all the symmetries of the square, so a quarter turn and one flip are enough.

### L2 human

We compute $sr$ as a permutation, to see how the products in step 2 arise.
The product $sr$ means "first $r$, then $s$".

1. Corner $1$: $r$ sends $1$ to $2$, and $s$ sends $2$ to $4$. So $sr$ sends $1$ to $4$.
2. Corner $4$: $r$ sends $4$ to $1$, and $s$ fixes $1$. So $sr$ sends $4$ to $1$.
3. Corner $2$: $r$ sends $2$ to $3$, and $s$ fixes $3$. So $sr$ sends $2$ to $3$.
4. Corner $3$: $r$ sends $3$ to $4$, and $s$ sends $4$ to $2$. So $sr$ sends $3$ to $2$.
5. So $sr = (1\,4)(2\,3)$. This is the reflection in the line through the midpoints of the sides $14$ and $23$.

The typical use of the generators $r$ and $s$ is a short check on a whole group.
For example, a map from $D_4$ to another group is a homomorphism only if it respects the relations $r^4 = e$, $s^2 = e$ and $srs = r^{-1}$.

A trap: the order of the product matters.
The product $rs$ is $(1\,2)(3\,4)$, which is $sr^3$ and not $sr$.
