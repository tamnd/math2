## Summary

A proof of impossibility shows that something cannot be done, not by trying every way, but by finding a property that every attempt has and the goal lacks.
It is how mathematics settles a problem for good when the answer is "no".

Here is the classic case.
Remove two opposite corners from an $8 \times 8$ chessboard, which leaves $62$ squares.
Can $31$ dominoes, each covering two adjacent squares, cover the board exactly?
Two adjacent squares always have different colors, so every domino covers one black and one white square, and $31$ dominoes cover $31$ squares of each color.
The two removed corners have the same color, so the board has $32$ squares of one color and $30$ of the other.
So no covering exists, and we never had to try one.
The [[coloring proof for the mutilated chessboard|TXNHA1AHYV]] gives the details.

In general, a *proof of impossibility* proves a statement of the form "there is no $x$ in $X$ with $P(x)$", or "no method of a given kind solves a given problem".
Its logical form is $\neg \exists x \, P(x)$, which is equivalent to $\forall x \, \neg P(x)$ by the rule for the [[negation of an existential statement|05SZQKH75K]].
The usual tool is an *obstruction*: a property, such as a parity, a remainder, a count or the degree of a field extension, that every candidate has and that the required object would not have.

## Motivation

A problem that resists every attempt may be hard, or it may have no solution, and failed attempts cannot tell the two apart.
Only a proof can show that every attempt must fail.
For that, the class of allowed attempts must be described exactly: ruler and compass steps, formulas built with radicals, or tilings by a fixed set of pieces.
Once the class is exact, a property shared by everything in the class can rule out the target.

This change of view, from searching for a solution to describing all possible solutions, is what settled the classical construction problems of Greek geometry.
The same idea marks the limits of methods in algebra, logic and computation, and its everyday tools, [[invariants|8NQ190278Y]], [[parity arguments|H9JT1XNDKQ]] and [[coloring proofs|90Q4PK1CST]], are the subject of the topic [[counting, invariant and extremal arguments|GB14W4SYPK]].

## Notation

Impossibility results are stated as "there is no $x$ such that $P(x)$", "$P(x)$ has no solution", or "it is impossible to ... by ...".
The last form must name the allowed methods, since "impossible" without them has no precise meaning.
An *invariant* is a quantity that no allowed move changes, and a *monovariant* is one that changes in one direction only.
In Lean, a goal `¬ ∃ x, P x` is usually started with `rintro ⟨x, hx⟩`, which introduces a candidate `x` with `hx : P x` and leaves the goal `False`.

## Remarks

### Why the method works

To prove $\neg \exists x \, P(x)$, we assume a candidate $x$ with $P(x)$ and derive a contradiction.
That is a refutation: it proves a negation by assuming the positive statement, so it needs no law of excluded middle.
The obstruction is what produces the contradiction.
It is a map $f$ from the candidates to some simple set, such as $\{\text{even}, \text{odd}\}$ or the remainders modulo $3$, for which $f(x)$ always lies in a set $A$ while $P(x)$ would force $f(x)$ outside $A$.
In the chessboard, $f$ counts black squares minus white squares, every union of dominoes gives $0$, and the board gives $2$ or $-2$.

### Remainders

Many equations in integers die on a remainder.
Take [[the equation x^2 - 3y^2 = 2|1QT670FE82]].
A solution would give $x^2 = 3y^2 + 2$, so $x^2$ would leave remainder $2$ on division by $3$.
But a square leaves remainder $0$ or $1$, as [[the case split on n modulo 3|N70XWKTVMC]] shows:
$$(3k)^2 = 3(3k^2), \qquad (3k \pm 1)^2 = 3(3k^2 \pm 2k) + 1.$$
So the equation has no integer solution, although it has real ones such as $x = 2$, $y = \sqrt{2/3}$.
The irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ fits the same pattern: in $a^2 = 2b^2$, the exponent of $2$ is even on the left and odd on the right; see [[irrationality of the square root of 2|0DZGSDHD92]].

### Invariants of a process

When the problem asks whether a process can reach a state, look for a quantity that the moves keep fixed.
In [[the last number on the board|7E6HPE5KJA]], the numbers $1$ to $10$ are replaced two at a time by their difference.
The sum starts at $55$, and replacing $a$ and $b$ by $|a - b|$ lowers it by $2 \min(a, b)$, an even number.
So the sum stays odd, and the last number left cannot be $0$.
The same argument covers all of the very many orders of moves at once.

### Algebraic obstructions

The deepest impossibility proofs place every reachable object inside a structure that the target is not in.
With ruler and compass, every constructible length has degree a power of $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.
The number $\cos 20°$ is a root of the irreducible cubic $8t^3 - 6t - 1$, so it has degree $3$, and the angle of $60°$ cannot be trisected; see [[impossibility of trisecting the angle|0EWXPZCSZ0]].
In the same spirit, the [[Abel-Ruffini theorem|163A2WQQJX]] shows that the general equation of degree $5$ has no formula in radicals, and [[Cantor's theorem|0WTEMGYV2C]] shows that no function from a set onto its power set exists.

### What is not a proof of impossibility

A failed search is not a proof.
In [[a failed search for a solution of x^2 - 61y^2 = 1|NAQDKVNJCR]], a program checks every $y$ up to $10^6$ and finds nothing, yet a solution exists with $y = 226153980$.
Tradition is not a proof either.
"Nobody trisected an angle with ruler and compass for two thousand years" does not prove that nobody can, even though the conclusion happens to be true.

### Watch out

Every impossibility theorem is relative to a class of methods, and the result is false outside the class.
With a straightedge that carries two marks, a construction attributed to Archimedes trisects every angle, so the theorem on trisection says nothing about marked rulers.
The theorem also says that some angles cannot be trisected, not all of them: the angle $90°$ can be, because an angle of $30°$ is constructible.
A second trap is a quantity that is not invariant under every move, so check each kind of move separately.

### History

Paolo Ruffini published an incomplete proof of the unsolvability of the quintic in 1799, and Niels Henrik Abel gave a complete proof in 1824.
Pierre Wantzel proved in 1837 that the general angle cannot be trisected and that the cube cannot be doubled with ruler and compass.
Ferdinand von Lindemann proved in 1882 that $\pi$ is transcendental, which showed that the circle cannot be squared; see [[impossibility of the classical construction problems|W8J42M1G28]].
In 1936 Alan Turing proved the [[undecidability of the halting problem|Z7TN2B35B0]], and in 1951 Kenneth Arrow published his [[impossibility theorem|VD8RCR44GQ]] on voting rules.

### Try it

First, show that $6x + 9y = 10$ has no solution in integers.
The left side is $3(2x + 3y)$, a multiple of $3$, and $10$ is not, so no integers work.

Second, can a $5 \times 5$ board be covered by dominoes?
No, because each domino covers $2$ squares, so any number of dominoes covers an even number of squares, and $25$ is odd.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some puzzles have no solution, and the way to know is to find something that never changes.
Take the chessboard with two opposite corners cut off.
Every domino lies on one black and one white square, so dominoes always cover equal numbers of black and white squares.
The cut board has two more squares of one color than of the other.
So the dominoes can never fit, and you know it without trying a single arrangement.

### L2 human

Show that $x^2 + y^2 = 4z + 3$ has no solution in integers.
First find the possible remainders of a square on division by $4$:
$$(2k)^2 = 4k^2, \qquad (2k + 1)^2 = 4(k^2 + k) + 1.$$
So each square leaves remainder $0$ or $1$, and a sum of two squares leaves $0$, $1$ or $2$.
The right side leaves remainder $3$, which is not on the list, so there is no solution.
Check: $3$, $7$ and $11$ are not sums of two squares, since the squares up to $11$ are $0$, $1$, $4$ and $9$.
The problem shows the typical use: reduce both sides modulo a small number and compare the possible remainders.
Watch out: the modulus must be chosen with care, since a modulus where the remainders match proves nothing; modulo $2$, for example, both sides can be odd.

### L3 human

Galois theory turns trisection and the quintic into questions about field extensions and groups, and impossibility becomes the statement that some group is not solvable or some degree is not a power of $2$.
Computability theory gives impossibility results for algorithms, starting with the halting problem, and Gödel's incompleteness theorems are impossibility results for complete and consistent axiom systems of arithmetic.
In computer science, lower bounds play the same role: every comparison sort of $n$ items needs at least $\log_2 n!$ comparisons in the worst case, since a decision tree with fewer than $n!$ leaves cannot separate all orders.
The [[Cantor diagonal argument|0XCH8QVDCQ]] is the model of many impossibility proofs in logic.
