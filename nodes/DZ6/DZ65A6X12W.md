## Statement

Let $\kappa$ be an infinite cardinal, and let $\mathcal{M}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ be $L$-structures of size $\kappa$ that are [[elementarily equivalent|P3B1V182N4]] and [[saturated|RMTZ5J64FX]].

1. Every [[partial elementary map|XTNYY0WRQ5]] $f$ from $\mathcal{M}$ to $\mathcal{N}$ with $\abs{\operatorname{dom} f} < \kappa$ extends to an isomorphism $\mathcal{M} \cong \mathcal{N}$.
2. In particular, $\mathcal{M} \cong \mathcal{N}$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Elementary equivalence: the saturated fields of transcendence degree $\aleph_0$ in characteristics $0$ and $2$ are not isomorphic.
- The same size: under the continuum hypothesis there is a saturated dense linear order of size $\aleph_1$, and it is not isomorphic to $(\Q, <)$.
- Saturation of both structures: $\overline{\Q}$ and the countable algebraically closed field of transcendence degree $\aleph_0$ are countable models of the same complete theory, and they are not isomorphic.
- The size bound on $\operatorname{dom} f$: the order $\Q \setminus \set{0}$ is countable, dense and without endpoints, so Cantor's theorem gives an order isomorphism $g : \Q \setminus \set{0} \to \Q$. By quantifier elimination $g$ is partial elementary, and it has no extension to an injective map on $\Q$, since its image is already all of $\Q$.

### Consequences

- The case $\kappa = \aleph_0$ includes Cantor's theorem that countable dense linear orders without endpoints are isomorphic; see [[Cantor's isomorphism theorem|ABN5PTXEHY]].
- With $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{N}$, part 1 says that a saturated model is strongly homogeneous; see [[homogeneous model|4NJS7W57VK]].
- A complete theory has at most one saturated model in each infinite cardinal, up to isomorphism.

### Converse

The converse is false: isomorphic structures need not be saturated, as $(\Z, <) \cong (\Z, <)$ shows.
A structure isomorphic to a saturated structure is saturated, since isomorphisms preserve types.

### Generalizations

The forth half of the proof alone shows that a $\kappa$-saturated $\mathcal{N}$ contains an elementary copy of every $\mathcal{M} \equiv \mathcal{N}$ of size at most $\kappa$; see [[universal model|F30ZVR4AD5]].
Special models of the same size and theory are isomorphic as well; see [[special model|CBNPB0QZGQ]].
For countable structures, homogeneity and the same realized types already give an isomorphism.

## History

Morley and Vaught proved the theorem in 1962, in their paper on homogeneous universal models.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two saturated structures with the same first-order truths and the same number of elements are copies of each other.
The proof matches elements one at a time, in both directions.
At each step, saturation guarantees that a partner with the same description exists on the other side.

### L2 human

We see the theorem at work for $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{N} = (\Q, <)$ and the map $f = \set{(0, 0), (1, 5)}$.

1. The map $f$ is partial elementary, since $0 < 1$ and $0 < 5$ and dense linear orders have quantifier elimination.
2. To add $1/2$, find an element of $\mathcal{N}$ between $f(0) = 0$ and $f(1) = 5$, such as $2$.
3. Continuing back and forth through enumerations of both copies of $\Q$ gives an automorphism that extends $f$, for example the map that is piecewise linear on $(-\infty, 0]$, $[0, 1]$ and $[1, \infty)$.

The typical use is to prove that two models are isomorphic by showing that both are saturated.
The trap is to forget elementary equivalence or the equal size.

### L3 human

The theorem is the uncountable form of the [[back-and-forth method|6F2DB4HTJR]], with saturation playing the role of the extension property.
It shows that saturated models are determined by their theory and their size, as universal domains in algebra are determined by their characteristic and transcendence degree.
Without a saturated model, special models give the same uniqueness, and a monster model is used as a fixed universe for this reason.
