## Summary

The logical form of a statement tells you how its proof must begin and how it must end.
If you keep a list of what you know (the givens) and of what you must show (the goal), each form of the goal and each form of a given comes with a standard move and a standard sentence.

Here is the method at work on a limit: we prove that $2x + 1 \to 7$ as $x \to 3$.
The goal is $\forall \varepsilon > 0 \; \exists \delta > 0 \; \forall x \; (0 < |x - 3| < \delta \Rightarrow |(2x + 1) - 7| < \varepsilon)$, and we peel it one symbol at a time.

| Stage | Givens | Goal | Sentence of the proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | none | $\forall \varepsilon > 0 \; \exists \delta > 0 \; \forall x \, (\ldots)$ | none yet |
| 2 | $\varepsilon > 0$ | $\exists \delta > 0 \; \forall x \, (\ldots)$ | "Let $\varepsilon > 0$." |
| 3 | $\varepsilon > 0$, $\delta = \varepsilon / 2$ | $\delta > 0$ and $\forall x \, (\ldots)$ | "Let $\delta = \varepsilon / 2$, which is positive." |
| 4 | as before, and $0 < \lvert x - 3 \rvert < \delta$ | $\lvert (2x + 1) - 7 \rvert < \varepsilon$ | "Let $x$ satisfy $0 < \lvert x - 3 \rvert < \delta$." |
| 5 | as before | none left | "Then $\lvert (2x + 1) - 7 \rvert = 2 \lvert x - 3 \rvert < 2 \delta = \varepsilon$." |

Stages 2 and 4 are forced by the forms "for every" and "if ... then".
Only stage 3 needs a thought, the choice $\delta = \varepsilon / 2$, and that thought comes from the [[scratch work|E97XXFW9MV]] $|2x - 6| = 2|x - 3|$.

In general, the outermost form of the goal ($\forall$, $\exists$, $\Rightarrow$, $\iff$, "and", "or", "not") fixes the next sentence and the new goal, and the form of each given fixes how you may use it.
Applied again and again, the moves reduce a statement to equations and facts that you can check directly.

## Motivation

A student who meets "for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that ..." often does not know how to begin.
Yet the first sentence of the [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] is fixed: a goal "for every $\varepsilon > 0$" starts with "Let $\varepsilon > 0$".
So a table of forms and moves gives the skeleton of a proof before any idea is needed, and it shows at once which step does need an idea.

This is the "givens and goals" scheme of Velleman's "How to Prove It".
It returns in every later course, because the definitions of analysis, algebra and topology are all built from the same few logical words.
Proof assistants such as Lean show the givens and the goal on the screen, and each tactic is one move of the scheme.

## Notation

The quantifiers $\forall$ ("for all") and $\exists$ ("there exists") are on the page [[quantifiers in mathematical writing|EGTZPA6QH3]].
The connectives $\Rightarrow$, $\iff$, $\wedge$, $\vee$ and $\neg$ are on the page [[conditional statements|FA6YZGR2SS]].
A scratch sheet for the method has a column "Givens" and a column "Goal", and each move changes one entry and adds one sentence to the proof.
In Lean the goal is printed after the symbol `⊢`, and the givens are the named hypotheses above it.

## Remarks

### Moves for the goal

| Form of the goal | Move | What to write | Lean tactic |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\forall x \, P(x)$ | take an arbitrary $x$; prove $P(x)$ | "Let $x$ be arbitrary." | `intro x` |
| $\exists x \, P(x)$ | name a witness $a$; prove $P(a)$ | "Take $x = a$." | `use a` |
| $P \Rightarrow Q$ | add $P$ to the givens; prove $Q$ | "Suppose that $P$." | `intro h` |
| $P \iff Q$ | prove $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow P$ | "($\Rightarrow$) ... ($\Leftarrow$) ..." | `constructor` |
| $P \wedge Q$ | prove $P$, then $Q$ | "First we prove $P$." | `constructor` |
| $P \vee Q$ | prove one part; or add $\neg P$ and prove $Q$ | "Suppose that $P$ fails." | `left`, `right`, `by_cases` |
| $\neg P$ | add $P$; derive a contradiction | "Suppose, for a contradiction, that $P$." | `intro h` |
| any goal | add its negation; derive a contradiction | "Suppose not." | `by_contra h` |

### Moves for the givens

| Form of the given | Move | What to write | Lean tactic |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\forall x \, P(x)$ | apply it to an object $a$ at hand | "Applied to $a$, the hypothesis gives $P(a)$." | `specialize h a` |
| $\exists x \, P(x)$ | name the witness with a new letter | "Choose $x_0$ with $P(x_0)$." | `obtain ⟨x₀, hx⟩ := h` |
| $P \Rightarrow Q$ | from $P$ conclude $Q$ | "Since $P$, the hypothesis gives $Q$." | `exact h hp`, `apply h` |
| $P \iff Q$ | use either direction | "By the hypothesis, $P$ is the same as $Q$." | `h.mp`, `h.mpr` |
| $P \wedge Q$ | use $P$ and $Q$ as two givens | "So $P$, and $Q$." | `obtain ⟨hp, hq⟩ := h` |
| $P \vee Q$ | split into cases; prove the goal in each | "Case 1: $P$. Case 2: $Q$." | `rcases h with hp \| hq` |
| $\neg P$ | if the goal is a contradiction, prove $P$ | "This contradicts the hypothesis." | `exact h hp` |

### How the moves fit together

Start by writing the givens and the goal in logical form, after [[unpacking the definitions|41JZ39GE3F]] that hide the form.
Apply the move of the outermost symbol of the goal, and write its sentence.
Then look for a given whose form fits the new goal.
Repeat until the goal is an equation, an inequality or a known fact, and then collect the sentences in order.

[[Givens and goals for the composition of surjections|DP58D5CBPF]] shows a whole proof that comes out of the tables with no other idea.
[[An equivalence with a disjunction|RCNCZQXA1F]] shows the two faces of "or": as a goal it becomes "suppose one part fails", and as a given it becomes two cases.
A [[set equality|MKHVRNW68H]] unpacks to $\forall x \, (x \in A \iff x \in B)$, which gives "let $x \in A$" in each direction.
A goal "there is exactly one $x$" splits into existence and uniqueness, as in [[existence and uniqueness statements|ACSC75R7R5]].

### Why the moves are sound

Each goal move is sound: if the new goal follows from the new givens, then the old goal follows from the old givens.
These moves are the introduction rules of [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]], and the given moves are its elimination rules, such as [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] for $\Rightarrow$.
The "suppose not" move is the start of a [[proof by contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]], and proving $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ for a goal $P \Rightarrow Q$ is a [[proof by contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]].
A goal $\neg \forall x \, P(x)$ becomes $\exists x \, \neg P(x)$ when the [[negation|029JBBF693]] is pushed inward, as in [[negating mathematical statements|K930V33BSC]], and then the move is to name a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]].

### What the tables cannot do

Most moves are mechanical, but the move for an $\exists$ goal needs a witness, and the tables do not find it.
They also do not find the right case split or the lemma that closes the last goal.
For "there are infinitely many primes" the frame is short, and all the content is [[Euclid's idea|0292EPPPT0]] of the number $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1$.
When several givens fit, the tables do not say which one to use.

### Watch out

The most common error is to apply a move to an inner part of the goal.
In $\forall x \, (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))$ the first sentence is "let $x$ be arbitrary", and only then "suppose $P(x)$".
A second error is to [[reuse a letter for two witnesses|FDT6332G8D]]: writing $m = 2k$ and $n = 2k$ for two even numbers adds the false assumption $m = n$.
A third error is to treat an existential given as universal, as in "there is $k$ with $n = 2k$, so take $k = 5$".
The witness is given to you; you cannot choose it.

### History

Gerhard Gentzen introduced natural deduction in 1934 and 1935, with one introduction rule and one elimination rule for each connective and each quantifier, and the moves in the tables are the informal form of these rules.
Velleman's "How to Prove It", first published in 1994, built the teaching of proof around the givens and the goal at each stage.

### Try it

What is the first sentence of a proof of "for every integer $n$, if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd"?
"Let $n$ be an integer, and suppose that $n$ is odd": the move for $\forall$ comes first, then the move for $\Rightarrow$.

What may you write when a given says "there is a real $c$ with $f(c) = 0$"?
"Choose $c_0$ with $f(c_0) = 0$", with a new letter; you may not decide what $c_0$ is.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every claim in mathematics is built from a few small words: "for every", "there is", "if ... then", "and", "or", "not".
Each small word tells you how the proof must start.
To show "the square of every even number is even", start with "take any even number".
To show "some number has square $9$", name one: $3$, since $3 \times 3 = 9$.
To show "if it rains, the street is wet", start with "suppose it rains" and show that the street is wet.
Learning these starts is like learning the opening moves of a game.

### L2 human

We plan a proof that for all sets $A$, $B$ and $C$, if $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq C$, then $A \subseteq C$.
The goal starts with "for all", so we write "Let $A$, $B$ and $C$ be sets".
Next comes an implication, so we write "Suppose that $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq C$", and the "and" gives two givens.

The goal is now $A \subseteq C$, and by the definition of a [[subset|0BJR9QR08E]] it means $\forall x \, (x \in A \Rightarrow x \in C)$.
So we write "Let $x \in A$", and the goal becomes $x \in C$.
The given $A \subseteq B$, applied to $x$, gives $x \in B$.
The given $B \subseteq C$, applied to $x$, gives $x \in C$, which is the goal.

Every sentence came from a table, and the proof needed no idea.
Watch out: the moves start only after the definition of "$\subseteq$" has shown its form.

### L3 human

The two tables are the introduction and elimination rules of intuitionistic natural deduction, with a rule of contradiction added for classical logic.
Under the Curry-Howard correspondence each move builds a piece of a program.
An introduction of $\forall$ or $\Rightarrow$ is a function, a witness of $\exists$ is the first part of a pair, and a case split on $\vee$ is a branch.
This is why the tactics of Lean correspond almost one to one with the rows of the tables, as [[a proof checked by the Lean proof assistant|QF0Q7B5Q73]] shows.

The [[structured proofs|H2W8D7BMSW]] of Lamport write the tree of moves with numbered levels.
In a research paper the moves are invisible, because the reader applies them silently, and their main use is in planning and in [[checking a proof|4M98ZZNK0E]].
