## Summary

Proof strategies by logical form are the standard moves that a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] makes.
The outermost logical form of the current goal or of a given chooses the move.
At each stage the writer keeps a list of givens and one goal.
The givens are the hypotheses and the facts proved so far.
The form of the goal ($\forall$, $\exists$, $\Rightarrow$, $\iff$, "and", "or", "not") fixes the next sentence and the new goal.
The form of each given fixes how the given can be used.
Applied again and again, the strategies reduce a statement to equations and facts that the writer can check directly.
The method matters because it turns the first and the last sentences of most proofs into routine.
The writer can then spend the thought on the real step.

## Motivation

A student who looks at a statement such as "for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that ..." often does not know how to begin.
Yet the first sentence of the proof is fixed by the form: a goal "for every $\varepsilon > 0$" starts with "Let $\varepsilon > 0$".
The next goal "there is $\delta > 0$" asks for a choice of $\delta$, which the [[scratch work|E97XXFW9MV]] finds.
The same is true of every logical form.
So a table of forms and moves gives the skeleton of a proof before any idea is needed.
This is the "givens and goals" scheme of Velleman's "How to Prove It".

## Notation

1. The quantifiers are $\forall$ ("for all") and $\exists$ ("there exists"), as on the page [[quantifiers in mathematical writing|EGTZPA6QH3]].
2. The connectives are $\Rightarrow$ ("implies"), $\iff$ ("if and only if"), $\wedge$ ("and"), $\vee$ ("or") and $\neg$ ("not"), as on the page [[conditional statements|FA6YZGR2SS]].
3. A scratch sheet in this method has two columns, "Givens" and "Goal". Each strategy changes one entry and adds a sentence to the proof.
4. In the Lean proof assistant the goal appears after the symbol `⊢`, and the givens are the named hypotheses above it.

## Remarks

### The strategies for goals

| Form of the goal | Strategy | What to write | Lean tactic |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\forall x \, P(x)$ | take an arbitrary $x$; prove $P(x)$ | "Let $x$ be arbitrary." | `intro x` |
| $\exists x \, P(x)$ | name a witness $a$; prove $P(a)$ | "Let $x = a$." or "Take $a$." | `use a` |
| $P \Rightarrow Q$ | add $P$ to the givens; prove $Q$ | "Suppose that $P$." | `intro h` |
| $P \iff Q$ | prove $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow P$ | "($\Rightarrow$) ... ($\Leftarrow$) ..." | `constructor` |
| $P \wedge Q$ | prove $P$, then prove $Q$ | "First we prove $P$." | `constructor` |
| $P \vee Q$ | prove one part; or add $\neg P$ to the givens and prove $Q$ | "Suppose that $P$ fails." | `left`, `right`, `by_cases` |
| $\neg P$ | add $P$ to the givens; derive a contradiction; or rewrite $\neg P$ in positive form | "Suppose, for a contradiction, that $P$." | `intro h`, `push_neg` |
| any goal | add the negation of the goal; derive a contradiction | "Suppose not." | `by_contra h` |

### The strategies for givens

| Form of the given | Strategy | What to write | Lean tactic |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\forall x \, P(x)$ | apply it to any object $a$ at hand | "Applying the hypothesis to $a$, we get $P(a)$." | `specialize h a` |
| $\exists x \, P(x)$ | name the witness with a new letter $x_0$ | "Choose $x_0$ with $P(x_0)$." | `obtain ⟨x₀, hx⟩ := h` |
| $P \Rightarrow Q$ | if $P$ is known, conclude $Q$; if $\neg Q$ is known, conclude $\neg P$ | "Since $P$, the hypothesis gives $Q$." | `exact h hp`, `apply h` |
| $P \iff Q$ | use either direction, or replace $P$ by $Q$ | "By the hypothesis, $P$ is the same as $Q$." | `h.mp`, `h.mpr`, `rw [h]` |
| $P \wedge Q$ | use $P$ and $Q$ as two givens | "So $P$, and $Q$." | `obtain ⟨hp, hq⟩ := h` |
| $P \vee Q$ | split into the case $P$ and the case $Q$; prove the goal in each | "Case 1: $P$. ... Case 2: $Q$. ..." | `rcases h with hp \| hq` |
| $\neg P$ | rewrite in positive form; or, if the goal is a contradiction, prove $P$ | "The hypothesis says that ..." | `push_neg at h`, `exact h hp` |

### The steps

1. Write the givens and the goal in logical form. Unpack the defined terms first, as on the page [[unpacking definitions|41JZ39GE3F]].
2. Look at the outermost form of the goal, and apply its strategy from the first table. Write the sentence of the strategy.
3. Look at the givens. Use a given whose form fits the current goal, by the second table.
4. Repeat until the goal is an equation, an inequality or a fact that a calculation or a known result settles.
5. Collect the sentences in order. This is the final proof, which the page [[writing proofs|8JQHJK94RZ]] then polishes.

### Examples

1. [[Givens and goals for the composition of surjections|DP58D5CBPF]]: the goal $\forall c \, \exists a$ and two givens $\forall \exists$ produce the whole proof, one strategy at each stage.
2. [[Strategies for an equivalence with a disjunction, x² = x|RCNCZQXA1F]]: a [[biconditional|0S6GMNJ62Y]] splits into two directions. A goal "or" becomes "assume one part fails", and a given "or" becomes two cases.
3. [[Set equality by double inclusion|MKHVRNW68H]]: the goal $A = B$ unpacks to $\forall x \, (x \in A \iff x \in B)$, which gives "let $x \in A$" in each direction.
4. The goal $\forall \varepsilon > 0 \; \exists \delta > 0 \; \forall x \, (\ldots \Rightarrow \ldots)$ of a limit gives the skeleton of every [[epsilon-delta proof|RXG5JTZ2PZ]]: "Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Let $\delta = \ldots$. Let $x$ satisfy $\ldots$. Then $\ldots$."
5. A goal "exactly one $x$ with $P(x)$" splits into existence and uniqueness, as the page [[existence and uniqueness statements|ACSC75R7R5]] explains. Uniqueness has the form $\forall y \, \forall z \, (P(y) \wedge P(z) \Rightarrow y = z)$.

### Non-examples

1. [[One letter for two existential witnesses|FDT6332G8D]]: the givens "$m$ is even" and "$n$ is even" use the same letter $k$. So the text assumes $m = n$.
2. A "proof" of a goal $\forall n \, P(n)$ that checks $P(1)$, $P(2)$ and $P(3)$ does not apply the strategy for $\forall$. The strategy needs an arbitrary $n$, about which nothing else is assumed.
3. A "proof" of $P \Rightarrow Q$ that starts "Suppose $Q$" and ends with $P$ proves the converse $Q \Rightarrow P$. The strategy for $\Rightarrow$ adds $P$ to the givens, not $Q$.

### Basic properties

1. Each goal strategy is sound: if the new goal is proved from the new givens, then the old goal follows from the old givens. These are the introduction rules of [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]].
2. Each given strategy is sound for the same reason. These are the elimination rules of natural deduction, such as [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] for $\Rightarrow$.
3. The strategy for $\exists$ goals needs an idea: the witness. The other goal strategies are mechanical, which is why the search for witnesses is the main part of scratch work.
4. The order of the strategies follows the order of the quantifiers. In a goal $\forall x \, \exists y$, the witness $y$ is chosen after $x$ and may depend on $x$; in $\exists y \, \forall x$ it may not.
5. The strategy for a goal $\neg P$ and the "any goal" strategy are the start of a [[proof by contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]]. The strategy "suppose $\neg Q$, prove $\neg P$" for a goal $P \Rightarrow Q$ is a [[proof by contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]].
6. A goal of the form $\neg \forall x \, P(x)$ becomes $\exists x \, \neg P(x)$ after [[negation|029JBBF693]] is pushed inward, as on the page [[negating mathematical statements|K930V33BSC]]. So a disproof of a universal claim is the strategy for $\exists$: name a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]].

### When it fails

The strategies give the frame of a proof, but they do not find its creative parts.
They do not find the witness of an $\exists$ goal, the right case split, or the lemma that closes the last goal.
For a statement such as "there are infinitely many primes" the frame is short, and the content is [[Euclid's idea|0292EPPPT0]] of the number $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1$.
The strategies also do not say which given to use when several fit, and a wrong choice can lead to a long detour.

### Related methods

1. [[Unpacking definitions|41JZ39GE3F]]: makes the logical form of a goal visible.
2. [[Scratch work and the final proof|E97XXFW9MV]]: the place where the strategies are applied and the witnesses are found.
3. The forward-backward method of Solow: backward questions from the goal and forward steps from the givens, until the two meet.
4. [[Basic proof methods|958EF46EYJ]]: direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction and cases, which are strategies of this table used as whole proofs.

### History

Gerhard Gentzen introduced natural deduction in 1934 and 1935, with one introduction rule and one elimination rule for each connective and each quantifier.
The goal and given strategies of this page are the informal form of these rules.
Daniel Velleman's "How to Prove It", first published in 1994, organized the teaching of proof around the givens and the goal of each stage.
Proof assistants such as Lean make the same scheme visible.
The goal and the givens are shown on the screen, and each tactic is one strategy.

### Common errors

1. The student applies a strategy to a part of the goal that is not the outermost form. For example, in $\forall x \, (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))$, the first step is "let $x$ be arbitrary", and only then "suppose $P(x)$".
2. The student chooses the witness of an $\exists$ before the arbitrary element of an earlier $\forall$, or lets it depend on a later one. This breaks the order of the quantifiers.
3. The student uses an existential given as if it were universal, for example "since there is $k$ with $n = 2k$, take $k = 5$".

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every claim in mathematics is built from a few small words: "for every", "there is", "if ... then", "and", "or", "not".
Each small word tells the writer how the proof must start.
To show "every even number squared is even", we start with "Take any even number".
To show "there is a number whose square is $9$", we name one: $3$, since $3 \times 3 = 9$.
To show "if it rains, the street is wet", we start with "Suppose it rains" and show that the street is wet.
Learning these starts is like learning the opening moves of a game.

### L2 human

We plan a proof that, for all sets $A$, $B$, $C$, if $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq C$, then $A \subseteq C$.

1. Goal: $\forall A, B, C \, (A \subseteq B \wedge B \subseteq C \Rightarrow A \subseteq C)$. Strategy for $\forall$: "Let $A$, $B$, $C$ be sets."
2. Goal: an implication. Strategy: "Suppose that $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq C$." The given "and" becomes two givens.
3. Goal: $A \subseteq C$. Unpacked by the definition of a [[subset|0BJR9QR08E]], it is $\forall x \, (x \in A \Rightarrow x \in C)$.
4. Strategy for $\forall$ and then for $\Rightarrow$: "Let $x \in A$." The goal is now $x \in C$.
5. Given $A \subseteq B$, that is, $\forall y \, (y \in A \Rightarrow y \in B)$. Applied to $x$, with $x \in A$, it gives $x \in B$.
6. Given $B \subseteq C$, applied to $x$, with $x \in B$, it gives $x \in C$. This is the goal, so $A \subseteq C$.

The typical use of the tables is to write the skeleton of a proof in the first minute.
The skeleton shows at once which step needs an idea.
A trap is to stop at a goal that still contains a defined term, such as "$A \subseteq C$".
The strategies apply only after the definition shows the form.

### L3 human

The two tables are the introduction and elimination rules of intuitionistic natural deduction, with the rule of contradiction added for classical logic.
Under the Curry-Howard correspondence each strategy is a step in the construction of a program.
An introduction of $\forall$ or $\Rightarrow$ is a function, and a witness of $\exists$ is the first part of a pair.
A case split on $\vee$ is a branch of the program.
This is why the tactics of Lean and other proof assistants correspond almost one to one with the rows of the tables.
The page [[a proof checked by the Lean proof assistant|QF0Q7B5Q73]] shows an example.
The structured proofs of Lamport write the tree of strategies with numbered levels, as the page [[structured and hierarchical proofs|H2W8D7BMSW]] shows.
In a research paper the strategies are invisible, because the reader applies them silently; their use is in planning and in [[checking a proof|4M98ZZNK0E]].
