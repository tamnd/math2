## Proof

We prove part 1 by induction on the number $k$ of vectors: from a relation among $k$ eigenvectors we make a relation among $k - 1$ of them that has no $v_k$.
Parts 2 and 3 then follow from part 1.

*Part 1.*

1. For $k \ge 1$, let $P(k)$ be the claim that eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_k$ of $T$ for pairwise distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ are [[linearly independent|0EVHJFTG9Z]].
   For $k = 0$ the empty list is independent, so only $k \ge 1$ needs a proof.
2. We use one fact on [[vector spaces|05EGHEQZAW]]: if $a \in F$, $v \ne 0$ and $a v = 0$, then $a = 0$.
   Indeed, if $a \ne 0$, then $v = a^{-1}(a v) = a^{-1} \cdot 0 = 0$, a contradiction.
3. Base case $k = 1$.
   An [[eigenvector|0B5H4208Z9]] $v_1$ is nonzero, so $a_1 v_1 = 0$ forces $a_1 = 0$ by step 2, and $P(1)$ holds.
4. Induction step.
   Let $k \ge 2$, assume $P(k - 1)$, take $v_1, \dots, v_k$ as in $P(k)$, and suppose that
$$a_1 v_1 + \dots + a_k v_k = 0, \qquad a_i \in F.$$
5. Apply the [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] $T$ and use $T(v_i) = \lambda_i v_i$:
$$a_1 \lambda_1 v_1 + \dots + a_k \lambda_k v_k = 0.$$
6. Multiply the relation of step 4 by $\lambda_k$ and subtract it from the relation of step 5.
   The terms with $v_k$ cancel, and we get
$$a_1 (\lambda_1 - \lambda_k) v_1 + \dots + a_{k-1} (\lambda_{k-1} - \lambda_k) v_{k-1} = 0.$$
7. The vectors $v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}$ are eigenvectors for the distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{k-1}$.
   So $P(k - 1)$ gives $a_i (\lambda_i - \lambda_k) = 0$ for each $i < k$.
8. For $i < k$ we have $\lambda_i \ne \lambda_k$, so $\lambda_i - \lambda_k$ is invertible in the field $F$, and $a_i = 0$.
9. The relation of step 4 is now $a_k v_k = 0$, and $v_k \ne 0$, so step 2 gives $a_k = 0$.
10. All coefficients are $0$, so $v_1, \dots, v_k$ are independent.
    This proves $P(k)$, and by induction $P(k)$ holds for every $k \ge 1$.

*Part 2.*

11. Let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ be pairwise distinct.
    The sum of the eigenspaces is an [[internal direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] when $w_1 + \dots + w_k = 0$ with $w_i \in E_{\lambda_i}$ forces every $w_i = 0$.
12. Take such $w_i$, and suppose that the set $I$ of indices with $w_i \ne 0$ is not empty.
13. For $i \in I$, the vector $w_i$ is a nonzero vector of $E_{\lambda_i}$, so it is an eigenvector for $\lambda_i$, by the definition of the [[eigenspace|0AYEPBQMAW]].
14. Then $\sum_{i \in I} 1 \cdot w_i = 0$ is a relation with nonzero coefficients among eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues, and this contradicts part 1.
15. So $I$ is empty, every $w_i$ is $0$, and the sum is direct.

*Part 3.*

16. Let $\dim V = n$, and let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ be distinct eigenvalues with eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_k$.
    By part 1 these vectors are independent.
17. An independent list in $V$ has at most $n$ vectors, by the [[Steinitz exchange lemma|08ZDRN6H41]] applied with a basis of $V$, so $k \le n$.
18. By part 2, the subspace $E_{\lambda_1} \oplus \dots \oplus E_{\lambda_k}$ of $V$ has dimension $\sum_i \dim E_{\lambda_i}$, and a subspace of $V$ has dimension at most $n$.

## Idea

Apply $T - \lambda_k \operatorname{id}_V$ to a relation among the vectors.
It multiplies each $v_i$ by $\lambda_i - \lambda_k$, so it removes $v_k$ and keeps every other term with a nonzero factor.
The induction hypothesis then kills the other coefficients, and the last coefficient dies because $v_k \ne 0$.
For example, from $a_1 v_1 + a_2 v_2 = 0$ with eigenvalues $1$ and $3$, the map $T - 3\operatorname{id}$ gives $-2 a_1 v_1 = 0$, so $a_1 = 0$ and then $a_2 = 0$.

## Variants

A Vandermonde proof handles all coefficients at once.
From $\sum_i a_i v_i = 0$, apply $T^j$ for $j = 0, \dots, k - 1$ to get $\sum_i \lambda_i^j (a_i v_i) = 0$ for each $j$.
The coefficient matrix $(\lambda_i^j)$ is a [[Vandermonde matrix|0P84B554AS]] with the nonzero determinant $\prod_{i < l} (\lambda_l - \lambda_i)$, so it is invertible, and the system forces every vector $a_i v_i$ to be $0$; then each $a_i = 0$.

A proof by a shortest counterexample takes a dependent list of eigenvectors with distinct eigenvalues of least length and applies $T - \lambda_k \operatorname{id}_V$ to get a shorter one; Axler's book uses this form.

A polynomial proof uses $q_i = \prod_{l \ne i} (x - \lambda_l)$.
Then $q_i(T) v_l = 0$ for $l \ne i$ and $q_i(T) v_i = q_i(\lambda_i) v_i$ with $q_i(\lambda_i) \ne 0$, so applying $q_i(T)$ to a relation leaves only $a_i q_i(\lambda_i) v_i = 0$, and $a_i = 0$.
