## Proof

Part 1.
We argue by induction on $k$.
For $k = 0$ the empty family is linearly independent, so we treat $k \ge 1$.

1. For $k \ge 1$, let $P(k)$ say: eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_k$ of $T$ for pairwise distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ are [[linearly independent|0EVHJFTG9Z]].
2. A fact on [[vector spaces|05EGHEQZAW]]: if $a \in F$, $v \in V$, $v \ne 0$ and $a v = 0$, then $a = 0$. Otherwise $v = a^{-1} (a v) = a^{-1} 0 = 0$.
3. Base case $k = 1$: an [[eigenvector|0B5H4208Z9]] $v_1$ is not $0$. If $a_1 v_1 = 0$, then $a_1 = 0$ by step 2. So $P(1)$ holds.
4. Induction step: let $k \ge 2$ and assume $P(k-1)$. Let $v_1, \dots, v_k$ be as in $P(k)$, and let $a_1 v_1 + \cdots + a_k v_k = 0$ with $a_i \in F$.
5. Apply the [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] $T$, and use $T(v_i) = \lambda_i v_i$. This gives $a_1 \lambda_1 v_1 + \cdots + a_k \lambda_k v_k = 0$.
6. Multiply the relation of step 4 by $\lambda_k$, and subtract it from step 5. The terms with $v_k$ cancel.
7. This gives $\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} a_i (\lambda_i - \lambda_k) v_i = 0$.
8. The vectors $v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}$ are eigenvectors for the distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{k-1}$. By $P(k-1)$, $a_i (\lambda_i - \lambda_k) = 0$ for $i < k$.
9. For $i < k$, $\lambda_i \ne \lambda_k$, so $\lambda_i - \lambda_k$ is invertible in the field $F$. Hence $a_i = 0$ for $i < k$.
10. Then step 4 becomes $a_k v_k = 0$. Since $v_k \ne 0$, step 2 gives $a_k = 0$.
11. So all $a_i$ are $0$, and $v_1, \dots, v_k$ are linearly independent. This proves $P(k)$, and by induction $P(k)$ holds for all $k \ge 1$.

Part 2.
Let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ be pairwise distinct.

12. A sum of subspaces is an [[internal direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] when $w_1 + \cdots + w_k = 0$ with $w_i \in E_{\lambda_i}$ forces $w_i = 0$ for all $i$. Let $w_i$ be such vectors.
13. Suppose that some $w_i \ne 0$, and let $I$ be the set of $i$ with $w_i \ne 0$.
14. For $i \in I$, $w_i$ is a nonzero vector of $E_{\lambda_i}$, so it is an eigenvector for $\lambda_i$, by the definition of the [[eigenspace|0AYEPBQMAW]].
15. Then $\sum_{i \in I} 1 \cdot w_i = 0$ is a linear relation with nonzero coefficients among eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues. This contradicts part 1.
16. So $w_i = 0$ for all $i$, and the sum is direct.

Part 3.
Let $\dim V = n$.

17. Let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ be distinct eigenvalues, with eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_k$. By part 1 they are independent, and an independent list in $V$ has at most $n$ vectors, by the [[Steinitz exchange lemma|08ZDRN6H41]] applied with a basis of $V$. So $k \le n$.
18. By part 2, $\dim(E_{\lambda_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus E_{\lambda_k}) = \sum_i \dim E_{\lambda_i}$, and this subspace of $V$ has dimension at most $n$.

## Idea

Apply $T - \lambda_k \id_V$ to a linear relation.
It multiplies each $v_i$ by $\lambda_i - \lambda_k$, so it removes $v_k$ and keeps the other terms with nonzero factors.
The induction hypothesis then kills the other coefficients, and the last one follows.

## Variants

- Vandermonde proof of part 1. From $\sum_i a_i v_i = 0$, apply $T^j$ for $j = 0, \dots, k - 1$. This gives $\sum_i \lambda_i^j (a_i v_i) = 0$ for each $j$.
  The matrix $(\lambda_i^j)$ is a [[Vandermonde matrix|0P84B554AS]] with nonzero determinant $\prod_{i < l} (\lambda_l - \lambda_i)$.
  Multiplying by its inverse gives $a_i v_i = 0$, so $a_i = 0$.
- Minimal counterexample proof. Take a dependent list of eigenvectors with distinct eigenvalues of minimal length, and apply $T - \lambda_k \id_V$ to get a shorter one. This is Axler's form of the argument.
- Polynomial proof. Let $q_i = \prod_{l \ne i} (x - \lambda_l)$. Then $q_i(T) v_l = 0$ for $l \ne i$ and $q_i(T) v_i = q_i(\lambda_i) v_i \ne 0$. Applying $q_i(T)$ to a relation isolates $a_i$.
