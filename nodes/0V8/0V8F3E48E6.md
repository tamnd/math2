## Summary

A linear combination is what you get when you scale some vectors and add the results.
It is the only kind of expression that the two operations of a vector space can build, so every later idea of linear algebra, from span to bases and coordinates, is a question about linear combinations.
Let $V$ be a [[vector space|000AYPA9AJ]] over a field $F$, and let $v_1, \dots, v_k \in V$.
A *linear combination* of $v_1, \dots, v_k$ is a vector
$$a_1 v_1 + a_2 v_2 + \dots + a_k v_k \qquad \text{with } a_1, \dots, a_k \in F.$$
For an infinite family, a linear combination uses only finitely many of the vectors, as the [[formal definition|0JB3DP1BMF]] states.

## Motivation

A vector space lets you do two things: add two vectors and multiply a vector by a scalar.
If you start from a few vectors and use these operations as often as you like, the axioms let you collect terms, and the result is always one linear combination.
For example, $2(v_1 + 3v_2) - (v_2 - v_1)$ collects to $3v_1 + 5v_2$.
So the question "what can I build from these vectors?" is the question "which linear combinations are there?".

The same question hides inside every linear system.
The system $Ax = b$ asks whether $b$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$, and a solution $x$ lists the coefficients; [[linear systems as vector equations|E7SAHXQEVC]] works this out.
Mixing problems in chemistry and in diets have the same shape: each ingredient is a vector of amounts, and a recipe is a linear combination of ingredients.
In this sub-area, linear combinations lead at once to the [[span|0DTY8B9HMS]] and to [[linear independence|0CRMGPAH83]].

## Notation

Books write $a_1 v_1 + \dots + a_k v_k$ or $\sum_{i=1}^k a_i v_i$, and $\sum_{i \in I} a_i v_i$ for a family in which only finitely many $a_i$ are nonzero.
The scalars $a_i$ are the *coefficients*.
The combination with every coefficient $0$ is the *trivial* linear combination, and its value is the zero vector.
Watch the word "trivial": it describes the coefficients, not the value, so a nontrivial combination can still have value $0$.

## Remarks

### Three first computations

Is $(7, 2)$ a linear combination of $(1, 1)$ and $(2, -1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$?
We look for $a$ and $b$ with $a(1, 1) + b(2, -1) = (7, 2)$, which means $a + 2b = 7$ and $a - b = 2$.
Subtracting the second equation from the first gives $3b = 5$, so $b = \tfrac{5}{3}$ and $a = \tfrac{11}{3}$.
Check:
$$\tfrac{11}{3}(1, 1) + \tfrac{5}{3}(2, -1) = \left(\tfrac{11 + 10}{3}, \tfrac{11 - 5}{3}\right) = (7, 2).$$

In the [[space of polynomials|0JGZCDT5DN]] of degree at most $2$, every polynomial is a linear combination of $1$, $x$ and $x^2$, for example $3 - x + 4x^2 = 3 \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot x + 4 \cdot x^2$.

A matrix times a column is a linear combination of the columns.
By the [[matrix-vector product|0PDVQEP0ZC]],
$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -4 \\ 9/2 \end{pmatrix} = -4 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} + \frac{9}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -4 + 9 \\ -12 + 18 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}.$$
So $x_1 = -4$, $x_2 = \tfrac{9}{2}$ solves the system $x_1 + 2x_2 = 5$, $3x_1 + 4x_2 = 6$.

### What is not a linear combination

The vector $(1, 2, 3)$ is not a linear combination of $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$.
Every combination $a(1, 0, 0) + b(0, 1, 0) = (a, b, 0)$ has third entry $0$, so the third entry $3$ can never appear.

The series $1 + x + x^2 + x^3 + \dots$ is not a linear combination of the monomials in $F[x]$.
It would need infinitely many nonzero coefficients, and a linear combination is a finite sum.
In fact the series is not a polynomial at all.

A product $v_1 v_2$ of two vectors is not a linear combination either, because a vector space has no product of two vectors.

### The rules you can use

A linear combination of linear combinations of $v_1, \dots, v_k$ is again a linear combination of $v_1, \dots, v_k$.
The reason is the distributive and associative axioms, which let you multiply out and collect terms, as in the Motivation.
This one rule is why the set of all linear combinations of a set is a subspace, its [[span|0DTY8B9HMS]].
It also gives the [[subspace|00YC1D652K]] test in a new form: a nonempty subset is a subspace exactly when it contains every linear combination of its own vectors.

### Special combinations

Some coefficient patterns have names of their own.
When the coefficients add up to $1$, the combination is an [[affine combination|0KJH6SRFZK]]; for two points, $(1 - t)p + tq$ runs along the line through $p$ and $q$.
When the coefficients are also nonnegative, it is a [[convex combination|21SXYJYC59]]; for two points, $t$ in $[0, 1]$ gives the segment from $p$ to $q$.
For example, $\tfrac12 (0, 0) + \tfrac12 (4, 2) = (2, 1)$ is the midpoint of the segment.

### Watch out

The coefficients must come from the field $F$ of the vector space.
The vector $(1, \sqrt{2})$ equals $(1 - \sqrt{2})(1, 0) + \sqrt{2}(1, 1)$, a real combination of $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$.
It is not a rational combination, because any combination $a(1, 0) + b(1, 1)$ has second entry $b$, and $b = \sqrt{2}$ is [[irrational|0DZGSDHD92]].

The coefficients need not be unique.
For example, $2(1, 0) + 0(2, 0)$ and $0(1, 0) + 1(2, 0)$ both equal $(2, 0)$.
Uniqueness holds exactly when the vectors are [[linearly independent|0CRMGPAH83]].

### History

Grassmann built his *Ausdehnungslehre* of 1844 on sums of multiples of basic quantities, and he used the idea of linear dependence there.

### Try it

First, write $(4, 1)$ as a linear combination of $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$.
The second entry forces the coefficient of $(1, 1)$ to be $1$, and then $(4, 1) - (1, 1) = (3, 0)$, so $(4, 1) = 3(1, 0) + 1(1, 1)$.

Second, is $x^2$ a linear combination of $1 + x$ and $1 - x$ in $F[x]$?
No, because every combination $a(1 + x) + b(1 - x)$ has degree at most $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear combination is a recipe for mixing vectors.
You choose a number for each vector, stretch the vector by that number, and add up the results.
With $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, the recipe "$3$ of the first and $5$ of the second" gives $(3, 5)$.
Every point of the plane comes from some recipe of this kind.
A recipe may use only finitely many ingredients.

### L2 human

Here is a typical problem: is $(1, 2, 3)$ a linear combination of $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$?
Put the two vectors as the columns of a matrix and ask for $x_1$ and $x_2$ with
$$x_1 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + x_2 \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The first two rows give $x_1 = 1$ and $x_2 = 2$ at once.
The third row asks for $x_1 + x_2 = 3$, and $1 + 2 = 3$ holds, so the answer is yes: $(1, 2, 3) = (1, 0, 1) + 2(0, 1, 1)$.
If the target were $(1, 2, 4)$, the third row would fail, and no combination would exist.
In general you decide such questions with [[Gaussian elimination|0JVJ2WCXZH]]: a consistent system gives the coefficients, and an inconsistent one proves that none exist.

### L3 human

A family $(v_i)_{i \in I}$ in $V$ defines a linear map from the space $F^{(I)}$ of finitely supported functions $I \to F$ to $V$, sending $a$ to $\sum_i a_i v_i$.
Its image is the span of the family, and it is injective exactly when the family is independent, so span and independence are the surjectivity and injectivity of one map.
In Mathlib this map is `Finsupp.linearCombination`.
Over a ring instead of a field the same definition works for modules, and in topological vector spaces one adds infinite sums, which leads to Schauder bases in analysis.
