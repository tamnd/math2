## Statement

Over a finite field of odd order, a nondegenerate quadratic form is fixed up to equivalence by two pieces of data: its dimension and its determinant modulo squares.

Let $F$ be a [[finite field|0ZXZ82FTX2]] with $q$ elements, where $q$ is odd.
For $a_1, \dots, a_n \in F$, we write $\langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$ for the diagonal form $a_1 x_1^2 + \dots + a_n x_n^2$ on $F^n$.

1. Exactly $(q - 1)/2$ elements of $F^\times$ are squares, so the group of [[square classes|9160DW40TE]] $F^\times / F^{\times 2}$ has exactly two elements.
2. For all $a, b \in F^\times$ and all $c \in F$, the equation $a x^2 + b y^2 = c$ has a solution $(x, y) \in F^2$.
3. Every [[quadratic form|0HZNE5QZDJ]] $Q$ on $F^n$ with $n \ge 3$ is [[isotropic|MZHFGPV1YT]]: there is a vector $v \ne 0$ with $Q(v) = 0$.
4. Let $Q$ be a [[nondegenerate|01NHFZF2SK]] quadratic form on $F^n$ with $n \ge 1$, let $A$ be its symmetric matrix, and let $d = \det A$. Then $Q$ is equivalent to $\langle 1, \dots, 1, d \rangle$.
5. Two nondegenerate quadratic forms on $F^n$ are equivalent if and only if the determinants of their matrices lie in the same square class. So for each $n \ge 1$ there are exactly two equivalence classes of nondegenerate forms on $F^n$.

## Remarks

### A first case over the field with three elements

In $\mathbb{F}_3 = \{ 0, 1, 2 \}$ the nonzero squares are $1^2 = 1$ and $2^2 = 4 = 1$.
So $1$ is the only nonzero square, and $2 = -1$ is a nonsquare, as part 1 predicts with $(3 - 1)/2 = 1$.
Now take the two binary forms $x^2 + y^2$ and $x^2 - y^2$, with determinants $1$ and $-1 = 2$.
Their determinants lie in different square classes, so part 5 says that they are not equivalent.
You can see this directly.
The form $x^2 - y^2$ is zero at $(1, 1)$, while $x^2 + y^2$ takes only the values $1$ and $2$ at the eight nonzero vectors.
[[The classes of binary forms over F₃|R57XFVEDY0]] lists all $18$ nondegenerate matrices and sorts them into the two classes.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The order $q$ must be odd.
In characteristic $2$ every element of a finite field is a square, because $x \mapsto x^2$ is injective and so bijective.
Also, quadratic forms no longer correspond to symmetric bilinear forms there; the [[polar form|0HYFWKKY3C]] page explains why.
The classification in characteristic $2$ uses a different invariant, the Arf invariant, which we only name.

The field must be finite.
Over $\mathbb{R}$, the form $x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ has no nonzero zero, so part 3 fails.
Over $\mathbb{Q}$, the primes give infinitely many square classes, so part 5 fails, and even equal discriminants do not force equivalence.

The bound $n \ge 3$ in part 3 cannot be lowered.
Over $\mathbb{F}_3$, the binary form $x^2 + y^2$ is not isotropic, as the first case shows.

Nondegeneracy is needed in parts 4 and 5, since a degenerate form has $\det A = 0$ and no square class.
A degenerate form is an orthogonal sum of a nondegenerate form and a zero form.
It is classified by its rank and by the square class of its nondegenerate part.

### What it gives you

Count all quadratic forms on $F^n$, degenerate or not.
The rank $r$ runs from $0$ to $n$, rank $0$ gives one class, and each rank $r \ge 1$ gives two classes.
So there are exactly $2n + 1$ equivalence classes in all.

Part 3 and the theory of [[isotropic vectors|MZHFGPV1YT]] give the shape of each form.
A nondegenerate isotropic form contains a [[hyperbolic plane|1X5PZSK44P]] as an orthogonal summand.
So a nondegenerate form of dimension at least $3$ splits off hyperbolic planes until at most two dimensions remain, and its [[Witt decomposition|BPMRSPB6GD]] has an anisotropic part of dimension at most $2$.

The [[discriminant|MZFRXECCMC]] of a nondegenerate form is the square class of $\det A$.
So part 5 says that the dimension and the discriminant together are a complete invariant over $F$.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{Q}$ they are not, as the [[signature|07EZA80NEH]] and the example $x^2 + y^2$ against $-x^2 - y^2$ show.

### The converse

Part 5 is an equivalence, so it contains its own converse.
One direction holds over every field of characteristic not $2$: equivalent forms have determinants in the same square class, by [[the invariance theorem|WYQGZK8GFT]].
The other direction, from equal square classes to equivalence, is the special feature of finite fields.

### Generalizations

Part 3 also holds in characteristic $2$.
It is a case of Chevalley's theorem, a corollary of the [[Chevalley-Warning theorem|08895Q5W96]].
That theorem says that over a finite field, a homogeneous polynomial of degree $d$ in more than $d$ variables has a nonzero zero.
Over a $p$-adic field such as $\mathbb{Q}_p$, the classification needs a third invariant, the Hasse invariant; see [[quadratic forms over local and global fields|NQC5CJZY4J]].

### Watch out

"The same determinant" means the same square class, not the same number.
Over $\mathbb{F}_5$ the squares are $1$ and $4$, so the forms $x^2 + y^2$ and $x^2 + 4y^2$ have determinants $1$ and $4$, but they are equivalent: replace $y$ by $2y$.
The wrong move is to compare the numbers $\det A$; the correct move is to test whether their quotient is a square.

### Try it

First, decide whether $x^2 + y^2$ and $2xy$ are equivalent over $\mathbb{F}_5$.
The matrices have determinants $1$ and $-1 = 4$, and $4 = 2^2$ is a square, so the two forms are equivalent.

Second, how many equivalence classes of quadratic forms on $\mathbb{F}_7^3$ exist?
There are $2 \cdot 3 + 1 = 7$: one of rank $0$ and two of each rank $1$, $2$ and $3$.

## History

Chevalley and Warning proved their theorems on zeros of polynomials over finite fields in 1935.
Chevalley's result answered a question of Artin, and part 3 is its case of degree $2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In a finite field with an odd number of elements, exactly half of the nonzero elements are squares.
So a sum of two squares with nonzero coefficients reaches every value of the field.
For example, in $\mathbb{F}_5$ the squares are $0$, $1$ and $4$.
The form $x^2 + 2y^2$ gives $0$, $1$, $4$, then $1 + 2 = 3$ and $4 + 2 \cdot 4 = 12 = 2$.
That is every element of $\mathbb{F}_5$.
This one fact is what makes the classification so short.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: bring $Q = 2x^2 + 2y^2$ over $\mathbb{F}_5$ to the form $X^2 + dY^2$.
First find a vector where $Q$ takes the value $1$.
Try small vectors:
$$Q(1, 0) = 2, \quad Q(1, 1) = 4, \quad Q(2, 1) = 10 = 0, \quad Q(2, 2) = 16 = 1.$$
So $v_1 = (2, 2)$ works.
The proof uses $v_2 = (-by, ax)$ for the form $a x^2 + b y^2$ and the vector $(x, y)$, so here $v_2 = (-4, 4) = (1, 4)$.
Compute the value at $v_2$ and the polar form $B(u, w) = 2u_1 w_1 + 2u_2 w_2$ at the pair:
$$Q(v_2) = 2 + 32 = 34 = 4, \qquad B(v_1, v_2) = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = 20 = 0.$$
So in the basis $v_1, v_2$ the form is $X^2 + 4Y^2$, and since $4 = 2^2$, it is equivalent to $X^2 + Y^2$.
Check: $\det \operatorname{diag}(2, 2) = 4$ is a square, so part 5 gives the same answer at once.
The search for a value $1$ is the whole method, and part 2 says that it always succeeds.

### L3 human

The theorem computes the Witt ring $W(\mathbb{F}_q)$ for odd $q$.
It has order $4$, and it is $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ when $q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ and the group ring $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})[F^\times / F^{\times 2}]$ when $q \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.
The difference comes from whether $-1$ is a square, that is, whether $x^2 + y^2$ is isotropic.
Over the $p$-adic numbers with $p$ odd, forms with unit coefficients reduce to forms over $\mathbb{F}_p$, and this is one input of the [[Hasse-Minkowski theorem|R8HCWA981S]].
Counting the solutions of $Q(v) = c$ leads to the quadratic character of $\mathbb{F}_q$ and to Gauss sums.
