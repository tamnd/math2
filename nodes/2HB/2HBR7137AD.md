## Statement

Let $F$ be a [[finite field|0ZXZ82FTX2]] with $q$ elements, where $q$ is odd.

1. Exactly $(q - 1)/2$ elements of $F^\times$ are squares. So the group of [[square classes|9160DW40TE]] $F^\times / F^{\times 2}$ has two elements.
2. For all $a, b \in F^\times$ and all $c \in F$, the equation $a x^2 + b y^2 = c$ has a solution $(x, y) \in F^2$.
3. Every [[quadratic form|0HZNE5QZDJ]] $Q$ on $F^n$ with $n \ge 3$ is [[isotropic|MZHFGPV1YT]]: there is $v \ne 0$ with $Q(v) = 0$.
4. Let $Q$ be a [[nondegenerate|01NHFZF2SK]] quadratic form on $F^n$, $n \ge 1$, with symmetric matrix $A$, and let $d = \det A$. Then $Q$ is equivalent to $x_1^2 + \cdots + x_{n-1}^2 + d x_n^2$.
5. Two nondegenerate quadratic forms on $F^n$ are equivalent if and only if the determinants of their matrices lie in the same square class. So for each $n \ge 1$ there are exactly two equivalence classes of nondegenerate forms.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $q$ odd: in characteristic $2$ every element of a finite field is a square, and quadratic forms do not correspond to symmetric bilinear forms. There the classification uses the Arf invariant, which is not treated here.
- $F$ finite: over $\R$, the form $x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ is not isotropic, so part 3 fails. Over $\Q$, there are infinitely many square classes, so part 5 fails.
- $n \ge 3$ in part 3: over $\F_3$, the form $x^2 + y^2$ is not isotropic, since $-1$ is not a square in $\F_3$.
- Nondegenerate in parts 4 and 5: a degenerate form has $\det A = 0$. Such a form is the orthogonal sum of a nondegenerate form and a zero form. It is classified by its rank and the class of the nondegenerate part.

### Consequences

- Each quadratic form on $F^n$ is classified by its rank $r$ and, for $r \ge 1$, one square class. So there are $2n + 1$ classes in all.
- Every nondegenerate form of dimension at least $3$ is an orthogonal sum of a hyperbolic plane $xy$ and a form of smaller dimension.
- [[The binary forms over $\F_3$|R57XFVEDY0]] show the two classes in dimension $2$ with explicit matrices.
- The [[discriminant|MZFRXECCMC]] is the class of $\det A$ modulo squares. Over a finite field of odd order, the dimension and the discriminant are a complete invariant.

### Converse

Part 5 contains its converse: equal dimension and equal square class of the determinant imply equivalence.

### Generalizations

- Part 3 holds in characteristic $2$ too. It is a case of Chevalley's theorem, a corollary of the [[Chevalley-Warning theorem|08895Q5W96]]. Over a finite field, a homogeneous polynomial of degree $d$ in more than $d$ variables has a nonzero zero.
- Over a local field such as $\Q_p$, the classification needs one more invariant, the Hasse invariant; see [[the local and global theory|NQC5CJZY4J]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

In a finite field with an odd number of elements, half of the nonzero elements are squares.
That is why a sum of two squares with coefficients takes every value.
For example, in $\F_5$ the squares are $0, 1, 4$, and $x^2 + 2y^2$ gives $1 + 2 = 3$ and $4 + 2 \cdot 4 = 12 = 2$.

### L2 human

Worked example over $\F_5$: we bring $Q = 2x^2 + 2y^2$ to the form $X^2 + dY^2$.

1. We need $x, y$ with $2x^2 + 2y^2 = 1$. Try small vectors: $Q(1, 0) = 2$, $Q(1, 1) = 4$, $Q(2, 1) = 10 = 0$ and $Q(2, 2) = 16 = 1$. So $(x, y) = (2, 2)$.
2. Then $v_1 = (2, 2)$ and $v_2 = (-by, ax) = (-4, 4) = (1, 4)$, by the formula of [[the proof|3P1QM7V8T7]].
3. $Q(v_2) = 2 + 32 = 34 = 4 = ab$, and $B(v_1, v_2) = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = 20 = 0$.
4. So $Q \cong X^2 + 4Y^2$, and $4 = 2^2$ is a square, so $Q \cong X^2 + Y^2$.

A trap: "same determinant" means same square class; over $\F_5$, the determinants $1$ and $4$ give the same class.

### L3 human

The theorem computes the Witt ring of $\F_q$ for $q$ odd: it has order $4$, and it is $\Z/4\Z$ when $q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ and $(\Z/2\Z)[F^\times/F^{\times 2}]$ when $q \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.
It is the local input at primes $p \ne 2$ in [[the Hasse-Minkowski theorem|R8HCWA981S]], through reduction modulo $p$.
The same counting idea gives the number of solutions of $Q(v) = c$ in terms of a quadratic character, which leads to Gauss sums.
