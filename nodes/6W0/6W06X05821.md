## Problem

Let $(M, g)$ be a complete connected [[Riemannian manifold|7CBT4SM83Y]] of dimension $n \ge 2$, with Riemannian volume $\mathrm{vol}$.
For a region $\Omega \subseteq M$, let $P(\Omega)$ be the $(n-1)$-dimensional area of its boundary.
For $0 < v < \mathrm{vol}(M)$, the *isoperimetric profile* of $M$ is
$$I_M(v) = \inf \set{P(\Omega) : \Omega \subseteq M, \ \mathrm{vol}(\Omega) = v},$$
where $\Omega$ runs over sets of finite perimeter, or over compact domains with smooth boundary.
The *isoperimetric problem* in $M$ asks to compute $I_M$ and to decide whether the infimum is attained for each $v$.
It also asks to describe the minimising regions, called isoperimetric regions.

## Remarks

The problem is open in general and is solved only for special manifolds.
In $\R^n$ the minimisers are the balls and $I(v) = n \omega_n^{1/n} v^{(n-1)/n}$, where $\omega_n$ is the volume of the unit ball; this is the [[isoperimetric inequality|KBX8RBZ626]].
On the round sphere $S^n$ and in hyperbolic space $\mathbb{H}^n$ the minimisers are the geodesic balls.

If $M$ is compact, isoperimetric regions exist for every $v$, by compactness theorems of [[geometric measure theory|JW431VDN08]].
The boundary of an isoperimetric region has constant mean curvature.
It is a smooth hypersurface outside a closed singular set of Hausdorff dimension at most $n - 8$.
On a noncompact manifold minimisers may fail to exist, because a minimising sequence can escape to infinity.

Curvature bounds give comparison results.
The Lévy–Gromov inequality says: if $M$ is compact and its [[Ricci curvature|4GTNM0WHDH]] satisfies $\mathrm{Ric} \ge (n-1) g$, then $I_M(\beta \, \mathrm{vol}(M)) / \mathrm{vol}(M) \ge I_{S^n}(\beta \, \mathrm{vol}(S^n)) / \mathrm{vol}(S^n)$ for all $\beta \in (0, 1)$.
The Cartan–Hadamard conjecture says that a simply connected complete manifold with [[sectional curvature|1X42JAA349]] $\le 0$ satisfies the Euclidean inequality $I_M(v) \ge n \omega_n^{1/n} v^{(n-1)/n}$.
It is known in dimensions $2$, $3$ and $4$.

## History

The classical isoperimetric problem in the plane goes back to the geometers of ancient Greece.
The question in curved spaces grew out of it in the twentieth century, and it has no single author.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In a flat space, the shape that encloses a given volume with the least surface is a round ball.
On a curved space, such as a sphere, a saddle-shaped space or a doughnut, the best shape can be different.
On a sphere the answer is still a round cap.
On a thin flat torus, made by gluing opposite edges of a long rectangle, the best region for a large area is a band around it, not a disc.
The problem asks for these best shapes and for the least surface area as a function of the volume.
The answer reflects the curvature of the space, which is why it is a central tool in geometry and analysis.
