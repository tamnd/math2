## Statement

Let $G$ be a connected [[semisimple Lie group|XJS07AJ50Y]] without compact factors, and let $\Gamma$ be a [[lattice|BTYF5Y5PFY]] in $G$.
Let $\rho \colon G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)$ be a continuous representation on a finite-dimensional real vector space $V$.
Then:

1. every linear subspace of $V$ that is [[invariant|0ACX5QT8TE]] under $\rho(\Gamma)$ is invariant under $\rho(G)$;
2. the closure of $\rho(\Gamma)$ in the [[Zariski topology|8SQK7RTB63]] of $\mathrm{GL}(V)$ contains $\rho(G)$.

## Remarks

Furstenberg's proof for clause 1 goes as follows.
View a $\Gamma$-invariant subspace $W$ of dimension $k$ as a point of the projective space $P$ of $\Lambda^k V$.
The map $g\Gamma \mapsto \rho(g) W$ pushes the invariant probability measure on $G/\Gamma$ to a $G$-invariant probability measure on $P$.
If $\rho(u)$ is unipotent, the points $\rho(u)^m x$ converge to fixed points of $\rho(u)$, so such a measure lives on these fixed points.
As $G$ is generated by such elements $u$, the measure lives on $G$-fixed points, and this forces $W$ to be $G$-invariant.

Example: $\Gamma = \mathrm{SL}_2(\Z)$ in $G = \mathrm{SL}_2(\R)$, with the standard representation on $\R^2$.
A line invariant under $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ must be $\R e_1$, and a line invariant under $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ must be $\R e_2$.
So no line is invariant under $\Gamma$, as clause 1 predicts, since no line is invariant under $G$.

The hypothesis on compact factors is necessary.
In the compact group $G = \mathrm{SO}(3)$, the trivial subgroup is a lattice, and every subspace of $\R^3$ is invariant under it.
But the axis $\R e_3$ is not invariant under $\mathrm{SO}(3)$.

Consequences: the centraliser of $\Gamma$ in $G$ is the centre of $G$, and $\Gamma$ is not contained in any proper algebraic subgroup.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A lattice is a discrete subgroup with a quotient of finite volume, such as $\mathrm{SL}_2(\Z)$ in $\mathrm{SL}_2(\R)$.
It is a tiny, countable part of the big group.
Still, the theorem says that no polynomial equation can tell the lattice apart from the big group.
Any linear structure preserved by the lattice is preserved by the whole group.

### L2 human

The Borel density theorem is the first step of many rigidity results.
It lets one pass from statements about $\Gamma$ to statements about $G$, for instance in the [[Margulis superrigidity theorem|DSKV65NHZZ]], where Zariski density is part of the setup.
It also shows that the adjoint representation of $G$ restricted to $\Gamma$ is irreducible when $G$ is simple, which is used for local rigidity of lattices.
