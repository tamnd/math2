## Example

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product, turn the standard axes by $\theta = \pi/6$, so that $\cos\theta = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ and $\sin\theta = \tfrac12$.
The new axes are
$$e_1 = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = \left(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \tfrac12\right), \qquad e_2 = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta) = \left(-\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right).$$

1. Both vectors have length $1$:
$$\norm{e_1}^2 = \tfrac34 + \tfrac14 = 1, \qquad \norm{e_2}^2 = \tfrac14 + \tfrac34 = 1.$$
2. They are orthogonal:
$$e_1 \cdot e_2 = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} = 0.$$
3. Two orthonormal vectors in a space of dimension $2$ form a basis, so $(e_1, e_2)$ is an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
4. Take $v = (2, 0)$.
   Its coordinates are two dot products:
$$v \cdot e_1 = 2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = \sqrt3, \qquad v \cdot e_2 = 2 \cdot \left(-\tfrac12\right) = -1.$$
5. Check that they rebuild $v$:
$$\sqrt3\, e_1 - e_2 = \left(\tfrac32 + \tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = (2, 0).$$
6. Check the length: $(\sqrt3)^2 + (-1)^2 = 4 = \norm{v}^2$.

## Remarks

Nothing in the computation depends on $\theta = \pi/6$.
For every angle, $\norm{e_1}^2 = \norm{e_2}^2 = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$ and $e_1 \cdot e_2 = -\cos\theta \sin\theta + \sin\theta \cos\theta = 0$.

The matrix $Q = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ has columns $e_1$ and $e_2$, and it is an [[orthogonal matrix|0W02QESPJQ]].
The coordinate vector of $v$ is $Q^{\mathsf{T}} v$, because its entries are the dot products of the columns of $Q$ with $v$:
$$Q^{\mathsf{T}} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} & \tfrac12 \\ -\tfrac12 & \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
So the inverse of $Q$ is its transpose, and no linear system is needed.

The second coordinate of $v$ is negative.
The reason is that $e_2$ points up and to the left, while $v$ points to the right, so the angle between them is more than a right angle.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Turn the usual axes of the plane by $30$ degrees.
The new axes still have length $1$ and still meet at a right angle.
The vector $(2, 0)$ reaches $\sqrt3$, about $1.73$, along the first new axis and $-1$ along the second one.
The squares $3$ and $1$ add up to $4$, the squared length of the vector, just as for the old axes.

### L2 human

Here are the coordinates of $w = (0, 1)$ in the same basis.
The dot products are
$$w \cdot e_1 = \tfrac12, \qquad w \cdot e_2 = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}.$$
Check the vector:
$$\tfrac12 e_1 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} e_2 = \left(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}, \tfrac14 + \tfrac34\right) = (0, 1).$$
Check the length: $\tfrac14 + \tfrac34 = 1 = \norm{w}^2$.
This is the typical use of a rotated basis: changing axes in mechanics or in computer graphics costs one transpose.
Watch out: the coordinates come from $Q^{\mathsf{T}} v$, not from $Q v$.
For $v = (2, 0)$, the product $Q v = (\sqrt3, 1)$ turns $v$ forward by $\theta$, which is the opposite of what the coordinates need.
