## Example

Let $D_4$ be the [[dihedral group|0JZ27DMJ5D]] of order $8$, the group of symmetries of a square with corners $1, 2, 3, 4$ in counterclockwise order.
The product $xy$ means "first $y$, then $x$".
Let $r = (1\,2\,3\,4)$ be the rotation by $90$ degrees, and let $s = (2\,4)$ be the reflection in the diagonal through $1$ and $3$.
Then [[$D_4 = \langle r, s \rangle$|GPK3HYCGFR]], and the elements are $e, r, r^2, r^3, s, sr, sr^2, sr^3$.
Here $r^2 = (1\,3)(2\,4)$, $sr = (1\,4)(2\,3)$, $sr^2 = (1\,3)$ and $sr^3 = (1\,2)(3\,4)$.
We find the [[lattice of subgroups|0B8BW4YWTF]] of $D_4$ and show that it is not [[modular|QX7WZ92QAK]].

1. Orders: by [[Lagrange's theorem|3C53TD]], a subgroup has order $1$, $2$, $4$ or $8$.
2. Order $2$: such a subgroup is $\{e, x\}$ with $x$ of order $2$. The elements of order $2$ are $r^2$ and the four reflections $s, sr, sr^2, sr^3$. So there are $5$ subgroups of order $2$.
3. Cyclic of order $4$: the elements of order $4$ are $r$ and $r^3 = r^{-1}$. They give one subgroup $\langle r \rangle = \{e, r, r^2, r^3\}$.
4. Not cyclic of order $4$.
   1. By Lagrange's theorem, the order of each element of such a subgroup divides $4$, and no element has order $4$. So each of its three elements other than $e$ has order $2$.
   2. Let $x$ and $y$ be two different ones. The product $xy$ is not $e$, because $y \neq x^{-1} = x$. It is not $x$ or $y$, because $xy = x$ gives $y = e$ and $xy = y$ gives $x = e$. So $xy$ is the third element of order $2$.
   3. At least two of the three are reflections, and the product of two different reflections is a rotation other than $e$, because $(sr^j)(sr^k) = r^{k-j}$ by the relation $r^j s = s r^{-j}$. That rotation has order $2$, so it is $r^2$.
5. So a non-cyclic subgroup of order $4$ is $\{e, r^2, t, tr^2\}$ for a reflection $t$. This gives two subgroups: $V_1 = \{e, r^2, s, sr^2\}$ and $V_2 = \{e, r^2, sr, sr^3\}$.
6. So $D_4$ has $1 + 5 + 3 + 1 = 10$ subgroups.
7. Covering relations: a subgroup of order $2$ is covered by each subgroup of order $4$ that contains it, and each subgroup of order $4$ is covered by $D_4$. The lines of the Hasse diagram are
   - $\{e\} \prec$ each of the $5$ subgroups of order $2$,
   - $\langle s \rangle, \langle sr^2 \rangle \prec V_1$ and $\langle sr \rangle, \langle sr^3 \rangle \prec V_2$,
   - $\langle r^2 \rangle \prec \langle r \rangle, V_1, V_2$,
   - $\langle r \rangle, V_1, V_2 \prec D_4$.
8. Choose $A = \langle s \rangle$, $C = V_1$ and $B = \langle sr \rangle$. Then $A \leq C$.
9. $B \wedge C = \langle sr \rangle \cap V_1 = \{e\}$, because $sr \notin V_1$. So $A \vee (B \wedge C) = A \vee \{e\} = \langle s \rangle$.
10. $A \vee B$ contains $s$ and $sr$, so it contains $s \cdot sr = s^2 r = r$. It contains $r$ and $s$, so $A \vee B = D_4$. So $(A \vee B) \wedge C = D_4 \cap V_1 = V_1$.
11. By steps 9 and 10, $A \vee (B \wedge C) = \langle s \rangle$ and $(A \vee B) \wedge C = V_1$. These differ, so the modular law fails, and the lattice of subgroups of $D_4$ is not modular.

## Remarks

The five subgroups $\{e\}$, $\langle s \rangle$, $V_1$, $\langle sr \rangle$ and $D_4$ form a pentagon inside the lattice.
The joins and meets among them stay among them: $\langle s \rangle \vee \langle sr \rangle = V_1 \vee \langle sr \rangle = D_4$ and $\langle s \rangle \wedge \langle sr \rangle = V_1 \wedge \langle sr \rangle = \{e\}$.
Dedekind proved that a lattice is modular exactly when it contains no such pentagon.

The failure comes from subgroups that are not normal.
The [[product set|0PT52XQZD7]] $\langle s \rangle \langle sr \rangle = \{e, sr, s, r\}$ has $4$ elements, but it is not a subgroup, because $r^2$ is not in it.
So the join $\langle s \rangle \vee \langle sr \rangle$ is not the product, and Dedekind's law for normal subgroups does not apply.
Indeed $\langle s \rangle$ is not normal: $r s r^{-1} = sr^2$.

The normal subgroups of $D_4$ are $\{e\}$, $\langle r^2 \rangle$, $\langle r \rangle$, $V_1$, $V_2$ and $D_4$.
They form a modular lattice, as for every group.
The [[quaternion group|0NRJGW864D]] $Q_8$ also has order $8$, but all of its subgroups are normal, so its lattice of subgroups is modular.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The square has $8$ symmetries, and they form $10$ smaller and larger groups inside the full group.
For lattices there is a rule, the modular law, that holds in many nice cases.
Here it fails.
Take the flip $s$ over one diagonal and the flip $sr$ over the line through the midpoints of two sides.
Each flip alone gives a group of $2$ elements, but the two flips together give all $8$ symmetries.
The modular law does not allow this pattern.

### L2 human

We compute $s \cdot sr$ and the product set $\langle s \rangle \langle sr \rangle$ as permutations.

1. $s \cdot sr = s^2 r = r$, because $s^2 = e$. As permutations, $(2\,4)(1\,4)(2\,3) = (1\,2\,3\,4)$.
2. The product set $\langle s \rangle \langle sr \rangle$ is $\{e \cdot e, \ e \cdot sr, \ s \cdot e, \ s \cdot sr\} = \{e, sr, s, r\}$.
3. It contains $r$ but not $r^2 = (1\,3)(2\,4)$, so it is not closed and not a subgroup.
4. The join must contain $r$ and $s$, so it is $D_4$, with $8$ elements.

The typical use of this example is as the smallest group whose lattice of subgroups is not modular.
It shows why Dedekind's law needs a normal subgroup.

A trap: the lattice of normal subgroups is always modular, but the lattice of all subgroups need not be.
The modular law for all subgroups is a much stronger condition.
