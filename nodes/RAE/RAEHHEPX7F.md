## Statement

Let $(M, g, F)$ be a four-dimensional, stationary, [[asymptotically flat|3HTF65XMF7]] [[electrovacuum solution|GAZKQEMJJ6]] of the Einstein–Maxwell equations, with $F$ the electromagnetic field, which contains a black hole.
Assume that $g$ is real analytic, that the domain of outer communications is globally hyperbolic, and that the [[event horizon|D8NZ32Z3SQ]] is connected and non-degenerate.
Then the domain of outer communications is isometric to that of a [[Kerr–Newman metric|NM34PFSE8A]].
In particular it is determined by three numbers: the mass $m$, the angular momentum $J = am$ and the electric charge $Q$, with $a^2 + Q^2 < m^2$.

## Remarks

The proof combines Hawking's rigidity theorem with the [[black hole uniqueness theorems|4RZDN10VCN]].
Rigidity says that a rotating stationary black hole is also axisymmetric, and a non-rotating one is static; this is where analyticity is used.
The uniqueness theorems then identify the spacetime in the static and in the axisymmetric case.
The hypotheses on the field content matter.
For the Einstein–Yang–Mills equations there are static, spherically symmetric black holes that are not determined by their mass and charge.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A star has many features: its shape, its mountains, its chemistry, its magnetic field.
When it collapses to a black hole and settles down, all of this is lost except three numbers: mass, spin and electric charge.
Physicists say that black holes have no hair.

### L2 human

The no-hair theorem is the reason why gravitational wave observations of the final ringing of a merged black hole can test general relativity.
The [[quasinormal mode|8X3E8122BJ]] frequencies of a Kerr black hole depend only on its mass and spin, so measuring two or more of them over-determines the black hole.
The theorem concerns exactly stationary black holes; whether dynamical black holes do settle down to Kerr–Newman is the [[black hole stability problem|6R0BJ6WS5A]].
