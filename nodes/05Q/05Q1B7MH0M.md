## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, and let $U$, $V$, $W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces over $F$ with ordered bases $A$, $B$, $C$.
Let $S \colon U \to V$ and $T \colon V \to W$ be linear maps.
Then
$$[T \circ S]_{C,A} = [T]_{C,B}\, [S]_{B,A},$$
where $[\,\cdot\,]$ denotes the [[matrix of a linear map|0YQSGNYE3K]] and the right side is a [[matrix product|032Q9FB8MT]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The middle basis $B$ must be the same on both factors. If $[T]$ uses a basis $B'$ of $V$ and $[S]$ uses $B$, the product $[T]_{C,B'}[S]_{B,A}$ is in general not the matrix of $T \circ S$. It differs by a [[change-of-basis matrix|0MKRHZB3WS]] in the middle.
- The order matters. $[S]_{B,A}[T]_{C,B}$ is defined only when $\dim U = \dim W$, and even for $U = V = W$ it is in general a different matrix.

### Consequences

- Matrix multiplication is associative, since composition of maps is associative. The theorem on [[associativity of matrix multiplication|073E4XC016]] also has a direct proof by sums.
- For $V$ with $\dim V = n$ and a basis $B$, the map $T \mapsto [T]_B$ is an isomorphism of algebras $\End(V) \to M_n(F)$. This is part 2 of the theorem on the [[algebra of endomorphisms|7BDGQ1Q46E]], and its proof uses the present theorem.
- If $S$ is an isomorphism, then $[S^{-1}]_{A,B} = [S]_{B,A}^{-1}$, since $[S^{-1}]_{A,B}[S]_{B,A} = [\id_U]_{A,A} = I$.
- If $T \colon V \to V$, then $[T^k]_B = [T]_B^k$ for each $k \ge 0$. So $[p(T)]_B = p([T]_B)$ for each polynomial $p$.
- The [[change-of-basis formula|0MQXVZEBMT]] is the case where $S$ or $T$ is an identity map.

### Converse

The rule for matrix products is forced by this theorem.
If a product $\ast$ of matrices satisfies $[T \circ S] = [T] \ast [S]$ for all linear maps between coordinate spaces with the standard bases, then $\ast$ is the usual product, since every matrix is the matrix of a map.

### Generalizations

The same formula holds for free modules of finite rank over a commutative ring.
In Mathlib this is `LinearMap.toMatrix_comp`.

## History

Cayley defined the product of matrices in his memoir of 1858 so that it matches the composition of linear substitutions.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Doing one linear map after another corresponds to multiplying their matrices.
The order is the same as in $T \circ S$: the matrix of the map that acts first is on the right.
This is why matrix multiplication has its row-times-column rule.

### L2 human

Column $k$ of $[T \circ S]$ lists the coordinates of $T(S(a_k))$.
First $S(a_k)$ is the combination of the $b_j$ with coefficients from column $k$ of $[S]$.
Then $T$ sends each $b_j$ to column $j$ of $[T]$.
The resulting combination of the columns of $[T]$ is the product $[T]$ times column $k$ of $[S]$.
A rotation by $\alpha$ followed by a rotation by $\beta$ is the rotation by $\alpha + \beta$, and the product of the two rotation matrices gives the addition formulas for sine and cosine, as in the [[example of rotations|3ZRXBNHM19]].

### L3 human

The theorem says that the choice of bases is a functor from finite-dimensional vector spaces with ordered bases to the category whose objects are natural numbers and whose arrows $n \to m$ are $m \times n$ matrices.
This functor is an equivalence of categories.
For $V = W$ it gives the algebra isomorphism $\End(V) \cong M_n(F)$, which is the starting point of the theory of representations by matrices.
