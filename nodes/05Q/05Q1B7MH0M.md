## Statement

Doing one linear map after another corresponds to multiplying their matrices, with the matrix of the first map on the right.
Let $F$ be a field, and let $U$, $V$, $W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces over $F$ with ordered bases $A$, $B$, $C$.
Let $S \colon U \to V$ and $T \colon V \to W$ be linear maps.
Then the [[composite|05YBKMRVAR]] $T \circ S$ satisfies
$$[T \circ S]_{C,A} = [T]_{C,B}\, [S]_{B,A},$$
where $[\,\cdot\,]$ denotes the [[matrix of a linear map|0YYMY95FWS]] and the right side is a [[matrix product|076JGP6R3T]].
The [[proof|84XDSB1RJ7]] follows each basis vector of $U$ through $S$ and then $T$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The middle basis $B$ must be the same in both factors.
Take $U = V = W = \mathbb{R}^2$, $S = T = \id$, the standard basis $E$ for $A$ and $C$, and compare $B = E$ with $B' = ((1, 1), (1, -1))$.
Then $[\id]_{E,B'} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, while $[\id]_{E,E} = I$, so the mixed product $[\id]_{E,B'}[\id]_{E,E}$ is not $[\id]_{E,E} = I$.
The two choices differ by a [[change of basis matrix|0MKRHZB3WS]] in the middle.

The order of the factors matters.
The product $[S]_{B,A}[T]_{C,B}$ is defined only when $\dim U = \dim W$, and even for $U = V = W$ it is in general a different matrix, as the [[rotation example|3ZRXBNHM19]] shows with a rotation and a reflection.

### A worked composite

Let $D \colon P_2 \to P_1$ be the derivative and let $T \colon P_1 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be $T(q) = (q(0), q(1))$, with the bases $(1, x, x^2)$, $(1, x)$ and the standard basis.
Their matrices are $[D] = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $[T] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so the theorem gives
$$[T \circ D] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $T \circ D$ sends $p$ to $(p'(0), p'(1))$, so $1 \mapsto (0, 0)$, $x \mapsto (1, 1)$ and $x^2 \mapsto (0, 2)$, which are the three columns.

### What it gives you

Matrix multiplication is associative, since composition of maps is associative; the theorem on [[associativity of matrix multiplication|073E4XC016]] also has a direct proof by sums.
For a basis $B$ of $V$ with $\dim V = n$, the map $T \mapsto [T]_B$ is an isomorphism of algebras $\End(V) \to M_n(F)$, as in the theorem on the [[algebra of endomorphisms|7BDGQ1Q46E]].
If $S$ is an isomorphism, then $[S^{-1}]_{A,B} = [S]_{B,A}^{-1}$, since $[S^{-1}]_{A,B}[S]_{B,A} = [\id_U]_{A,A} = I$.
For $T \colon V \to V$, $[T^k]_B = [T]_B^k$ for each $k \ge 0$, and so $[p(T)]_B = p([T]_B)$ for each polynomial $p$.
The [[change of basis formula|0MQXVZEBMT]] is the case where some of the maps are identity maps.

### The converse

The rule for multiplying matrices is forced by this theorem.
Suppose a product $\ast$ of matrices satisfies $[T \circ S] = [T] \ast [S]$ for all linear maps between coordinate spaces with the standard bases.
Every matrix is the matrix of such a map, so $\ast$ must be the usual product.
This is the reason for the row-times-column rule.

### Generalizations

The same formula holds for free modules of finite rank over a commutative ring, with the same proof.
In Mathlib it is `LinearMap.toMatrix_comp`.

## History

Cayley's memoir on matrices of 1858 defined the product of two matrices so that it matches the composition of the linear substitutions that they represent.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Doing one linear map after another corresponds to multiplying their matrices.
The order is the same as in $T \circ S$: the matrix of the map that acts first stands on the right.
For example, turning the plane by $90$ degrees twice turns it by $180$ degrees, and indeed $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$.
This is why matrix multiplication has its row-times-column rule.

### L2 human

Why does the product come out right?
Column $k$ of $[T \circ S]$ lists the coordinates of $T(S(a_k))$.
First, $S(a_k)$ is the combination of the $b_j$ with the coefficients in column $k$ of $[S]$.
Then $T$ sends each $b_j$ to the vector with coordinates in column $j$ of $[T]$.
So the result is the combination of the columns of $[T]$ with the coefficients from column $k$ of $[S]$, which is $[T]$ times column $k$ of $[S]$.
A rotation by $\alpha$ followed by a rotation by $\beta$ is the rotation by $\alpha + \beta$, and the product of the two rotation matrices gives the addition formulas for sine and cosine, as in the [[rotation example|3ZRXBNHM19]].
Watch out: $[T]_{C,B}[S]_{B,A}$ needs the same basis $B$ in both places, so the inner subscripts must match.

### L3 human

The theorem says that taking matrices is a functor from the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces with chosen ordered bases to the category whose objects are the integers $n \ge 0$ and whose arrows $n \to m$ are the $m \times n$ matrices.
This functor is an equivalence of categories.
For $V = W$ it gives the algebra isomorphism $\End(V) \cong M_n(F)$, which is the starting point for representing groups and algebras by matrices.
