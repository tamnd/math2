## Statement

Let $R$ be a ring and let $m, n, p, q \ge 0$ be integers.
Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix, $B$ an $n \times p$ matrix and $C$ a $p \times q$ matrix over $R$.
Then $(AB)C = A(BC)$, where the products are [[matrix products|076JGP6R3T]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The sizes must chain: if $B$ is $n \times p$ and $C$ is $p' \times q$ with $p' \neq p$, neither side is defined.
- Associativity of the multiplication of $R$ is used. Over a nonassociative algebra such as the octonions, $1 \times 1$ matrices already fail the law.
- Distributivity in $R$ is used to move a factor into a sum.
- Commutativity of $R$ is not needed for the [[proof by sums|1QVWFSF796]].

### Consequences

- A product $A_1 A_2 \cdots A_k$ has the same value for every placement of the parentheses.
- For a square matrix, $A^j A^k = A^{j+k}$ and $(A^j)^k = A^{jk}$.
- With the [[distributive law|0RXD6EZYBK]], the square matrices $M_n(R)$ form a [[ring|~de637a64]].
- $(AB)x = A(Bx)$ for column vectors $x$; this is the case $q = 1$.

### Generalizations

- The same proof works for matrices over a semiring, for example the $(\max, +)$ semiring used in path problems.
- It works for infinite matrices when each sum is finite, for example for matrices with finitely many nonzero entries in each row.

## History

Cayley stated the law in his memoir of 1858, where he defined matrix multiplication by the composition of linear substitutions.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When we multiply three matrices, we may multiply the first two first, or the last two first.
The result is the same in both cases.
This is like $(2 \cdot 3) \cdot 4 = 2 \cdot (3 \cdot 4)$ for numbers.

### L2 human

Both sides have entry $(i, l)$ equal to $\sum_{j} \sum_{k} a_{ij} b_{jk} c_{kl}$, the sum over all paths $i \to j \to k \to l$.
The [[proof by composition|~c239e1a3]] gives a second reason: $AB$ acts as "first $B$, then $A$", and composition of maps is associative.
The cost can differ: for sizes $10 \times 100$, $100 \times 5$ and $5 \times 50$, the order $(AB)C$ uses $7500$ multiplications and $A(BC)$ uses $75000$.

### L3 human

Associativity says that matrices form a category, with composition given by the product.
The proof by composition shows that this category is equivalent to the category of finite free modules with chosen bases.
The law is the reason that the [[matrix ring|~de637a64]] is an associative ring, which is the setting of [[ring theory|0B1261B5ED]].
