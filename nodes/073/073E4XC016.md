## Statement

In a product of three matrices, it does not matter which two we multiply first.
Let $R$ be a ring and let $m, n, p, q \ge 1$ be integers.
Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix, $B$ an $n \times p$ matrix and $C$ a $p \times q$ matrix, all over $R$.
Then the two products $(AB)C$ and $A(BC)$ are defined, and
$$(AB)C = A(BC),$$
where each product is the [[matrix product|076JGP6R3T]].

## Remarks

### A check with numbers

Take the real matrices
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Multiply the first two, then multiply by $C$:
$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (AB)C = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Now multiply the last two first, then multiply by $A$:
$$BC = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A(BC) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The intermediate products $AB$ and $BC$ are different matrices, but the final results agree, as the theorem says.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The sizes must chain.
If $B$ is $n \times p$ and $C$ is $p' \times q$ with $p' \ne p$, then $BC$ is not defined, so neither side of the law makes sense.

The [[proof by sums|1QVWFSF796]] uses the associativity of the multiplication in $R$, in the form $(a b) c = a (b c)$ for entries.
For an algebra whose own product is not associative, such as the octonions, the law already fails for $1 \times 1$ matrices, since those multiply like their entries.
The proof also uses the distributive law of $R$, to move a factor into a sum, and the fact that a finite sum can be added in any order.

Commutativity of $R$ is not needed.
So the law holds for matrices over the quaternions, or over a matrix ring, just as well as over $\mathbb{R}$.

### What it gives you

By induction on the number of factors, a product $A_1 A_2 \cdots A_k$ has the same value for every way of placing the brackets.
So we may drop the brackets, and for a square matrix the powers obey $A^j A^k = A^{j + k}$ and $(A^j)^k = A^{jk}$.
The case $q = 1$ is the rule $(AB)x = A(Bx)$ for a column $x$, which says that $AB$ acts as "first $B$, then $A$".
Together with the [[distributive law|0RXD6EZYBK]], associativity makes the square matrices of one size into the [[matrix ring|AB2MD5CD13]].

### The same result, different costs

Associativity says the answer does not depend on the brackets, but the work does.
Let $A$ be $10 \times 100$, $B$ be $100 \times 5$ and $C$ be $5 \times 50$.
A product of an $m \times n$ and an $n \times p$ matrix needs $mnp$ multiplications of entries, so
$$(AB)C: \ 10 \cdot 100 \cdot 5 + 10 \cdot 5 \cdot 50 = 5000 + 2500 = 7500,$$
$$A(BC): \ 100 \cdot 5 \cdot 50 + 10 \cdot 100 \cdot 50 = 25000 + 50000 = 75000.$$
The first order is ten times cheaper, and programs that multiply chains of matrices choose the order with this count.

### Generalizations

The proof by sums uses only the laws of addition and multiplication, so it works over any semiring, for example the $(\max, +)$ semiring that appears in shortest-path and scheduling problems.
It also works for infinite matrices in which each row has only finitely many nonzero entries, because every sum in the proof is then finite.

## History

Cayley defined the product of matrices in his memoir on the theory of matrices of 1858, as the rule for composing linear substitutions, and he stated the associative law there.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When you multiply three matrices, you may multiply the first two first, or the last two first.
The answer is the same either way, just as $(2 \cdot 3) \cdot 4 = 2 \cdot (3 \cdot 4) = 24$ for numbers.
The difference from numbers is that you must keep the order $A$, $B$, $C$ from left to right.
You may move the brackets, but not the matrices.

### L2 human

Why should the law hold?
Look at one entry of each side.
Expanding the definition twice gives
$$\bigl((AB)C\bigr)_{il} = \sum_{k} \Bigl(\sum_{j} a_{ij} b_{jk}\Bigr) c_{kl}, \qquad \bigl(A(BC)\bigr)_{il} = \sum_{j} a_{ij} \Bigl(\sum_{k} b_{jk} c_{kl}\Bigr).$$
After multiplying out, both are the sum of $a_{ij} b_{jk} c_{kl}$ over all pairs $(j, k)$.
You can read each term as a path $i \to j \to k \to l$, and both sides add up the same paths in a different order.
The [[proof by sums|1QVWFSF796]] writes this out, and the [[proof by composition|KC9SCPKS2G]] gives a second reason: $AB$ is the matrix of "first $B$, then $A$", and composition of maps is associative.
In practice, the typical use is silent: every time you write $A^3$ or $ABx$ without brackets, you use this theorem.

### L3 human

Associativity is the statement that matrices over $R$ form a category, with the natural numbers as objects, the $m \times n$ matrices as arrows from $n$ to $m$, and the product as composition.
The proof by composition shows that this category is equivalent to the category of finitely generated free $R$-modules, so associativity of the product is associativity of composition seen in coordinates.
This is the reason that the [[matrix ring|AB2MD5CD13]] is an associative ring, the basic setting of [[ring theory|0B1261B5ED]], and the sub-area on [[linear maps|9080WQTZ3M]] returns to the same correspondence.
