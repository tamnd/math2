## Statement

Let $G$ be a [[cyclic group|0KVGH0J5B3]].

1. Every cyclic group is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ or to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$: if $G$ is infinite, then $G \cong (\mathbb{Z}, +)$, and if $G$ has finite [[order|0B85S2V2DR]] $n$, then $G \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, +)$.
2. Two cyclic groups of the same order are isomorphic. More precisely, let $G = \langle g \rangle$ and $H = \langle h \rangle$ with $|G| = |H|$, finite or infinite. Then $\varphi(g^k) = h^k$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ defines a [[group isomorphism|08MG7H8JW5]] $\varphi \colon G \to H$.

So, up to isomorphism, the cyclic groups are the [[infinite cyclic group|02B1T50JD1]] $\mathbb{Z}$ and the [[cyclic groups of order $n$|0G0B3DRG65]] $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for $n \geq 1$, and no two of these are isomorphic.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. Both groups must be cyclic. The groups $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and the [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]] both have order $4$, but they are not isomorphic: $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has an element of order $4$, and the Klein four-group has none.
2. For infinite groups, "the same order" means that both groups are infinite. The groups $\mathbb{Z}$ and [[$(\mathbb{Q}, +)$|E1EFH8EV3G]] are both infinite, but they are not isomorphic, because $\mathbb{Q}$ is not cyclic.
3. The isomorphism in part 2 must send a generator to a generator. The formula $g^k \mapsto (h^2)^k$ is not an isomorphism when $h^2$ does not generate $H$, for example for $H = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and $h = 1$.

### Consequences

1. The [[group $\{1, -1\}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$|7FCN2KNWC0]].
2. The integers modulo $n$, the $n$th roots of unity and the rotations of a regular $n$-gon are [[isomorphic models|0G0B3DRG65]] of one group.
3. Any two groups of the same prime order $p$ are isomorphic, because [[both are cyclic|05MM4EK4SA]].
4. Each infinite cyclic group, such as $2\mathbb{Z}$ or $\{2^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}^\times$, is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$.
5. A property of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ that is stated in terms of the group operation holds in each cyclic group of order $n$. For example, the [[subgroups of a cyclic group of order $n$|0423TXR9AN]] correspond to the divisors of $n$.

### Converse

The converse holds: each group isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ or to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic.
If $\psi$ is an isomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ onto $G$, then $\psi(1)$ generates $G$, because $\psi(k \cdot 1) = \psi(1)^k$.
Also, isomorphic groups have the same order, so cyclic groups of different orders are not isomorphic.

### Generalizations

The [[structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups|0RY0B72XAS]] extends the classification.
It writes each finitely generated abelian group as a direct product of cyclic groups, $\mathbb{Z}^r \times \mathbb{Z}/d_1\mathbb{Z} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}/d_s\mathbb{Z}$ with $r \geq 0$ and $1 < d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_s$.
Under these conditions, $r$ and $d_1, \ldots, d_s$ are unique.
The cyclic groups are the case of at most one factor.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Up to the names of the elements, there is only one cyclic group of each size.
There is one with $n$ elements for each $n$, the clock with $n$ hours, and one infinite one, the integers.
To match two cyclic groups of the same size, send a generator of one to a generator of the other, and each power to the same power.
For example, the turns of a regular hexagon and the clock with $6$ hours are the same group with different names.

### L2 human

We match $C = \{1, -1, i, -i\} \subseteq \mathbb{C}^\times$ with $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.

1. The number $i$ generates $C$, and $1$ generates $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$. Both groups have order $4$.
2. The isomorphism $\varphi \colon \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \to C$ sends $k$ to $i^k$: $0 \mapsto 1$, $1 \mapsto i$, $2 \mapsto -1$ and $3 \mapsto -i$.
3. Check one product: $2 + 3 = 5 \equiv 1$, and $\varphi(2)\varphi(3) = (-1)(-i) = i = \varphi(1)$.

The typical use: to answer a question about a cyclic group, move it to $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, where we compute with integers.
A trap: the isomorphism is not unique.
Sending $1$ to the other generator $-i$ gives a second isomorphism.

### L3 human

The theorem is the case of one generator of a general principle: a group generated by $g$ is a quotient of the free group on one generator, which is $\mathbb{Z}$.
The quotients of $\mathbb{Z}$ are the groups $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ with $n \geq 0$, one for each subgroup $n\mathbb{Z}$.
In the language of modules, the theorem classifies the cyclic modules over the [[principal ideal domain|0CMD0BCH8T]] $\mathbb{Z}$, and the same statement holds for cyclic modules over any principal ideal domain.
The isomorphisms between two cyclic groups of order $n$ correspond to the choices of a generator of the target, so there are $\varphi(n)$ of them, where $\varphi$ is [[Euler's totient function|0DAMHCT17R]].
