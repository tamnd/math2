## Summary

Gradient-index optics studies media whose [[refractive index|9VGK0SP6EQ]] varies continuously from point to point.
In such a medium light rays bend gradually instead of only at surfaces, following the curves of stationary optical length given by [[Fermat's principle|RKX9XJ0RVW]].
Mathematically the rays are the geodesics of the metric $n^2 \abs{dx}^2$, so gradient-index optics is a meeting point of optics and Riemannian geometry.
Classical examples are [[Maxwell's fish-eye lens|CMBA0BC77W]] and the [[Luneburg lens|FP62YWDZRV]], and practical uses include rod lenses and graded-index optical fibres.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Light slows down in glass, and it slows down more where the glass is denser.
If the density changes smoothly, a light ray bends smoothly toward the slower region, like a car that turns when one wheel runs on sand.
A mirage is a natural example.
Hot air near a road has a lower index than the cooler air above, and rays from the sky curve back up to the eye.
A rod of glass whose index is highest on its axis can focus light with flat ends, without any curved surface.
