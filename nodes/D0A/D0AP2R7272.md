## Summary

Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over a field $F$.
An eigendecomposition of $A$ is a factorization $A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}$, where $Q$ is an [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] $n \times n$ matrix over $F$ and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ is a [[diagonal matrix|0Q9PGXD9E4]] over $F$.
Then column $q_i$ of $Q$ is an [[eigenvector|0B5H4208Z9]] of $A$ for $\lambda_i$, and $A$ has an eigendecomposition exactly when it is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]].
If $r_i$ is row $i$ of $Q^{-1}$, then $E_i = q_i r_i$ is a rank-one matrix, and $A = \lambda_1 E_1 + \cdots + \lambda_n E_n$ is the spectral sum.
The eigendecomposition turns powers and other functions of $A$ into functions of numbers.

## Motivation

A linear system $x_{k+1} = A x_k$ couples all coordinates.
In the coordinates $y = Q^{-1} x$, it becomes $y_{k+1} = \Lambda y_k$, which is $n$ separate scalar equations $y_{k+1, i} = \lambda_i y_{k, i}$.
So the eigendecomposition reduces a matrix problem to $n$ problems about numbers.
The same holds for the differential equation $x' = A x$ and for the [[matrix exponential|020M04FN1H]].

## Notation

Books write $A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}$, $A = P D P^{-1}$ or $A = V \Lambda V^{-1}$.
The order matters: $P^{-1} A P = D$ is the same equation as $A = P D P^{-1}$.
The matrices $E_i$ are called spectral projections, or Frobenius covariants when the $\lambda_i$ are distinct.
For a real symmetric matrix, $Q$ can be chosen orthogonal, and one writes $A = Q \Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ has the [[eigendecomposition with eigenvalues 5 and 2|306FBDECMB]], and $A = 5 E_1 + 2 E_2$ with explicit rank-one matrices $E_1$ and $E_2$.
- $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = I + J$, where $J$ is the all-ones matrix, has the [[eigendecomposition with eigenvalues 4, 1, 1|K5NXSFJV50]]. Grouped by eigenvalue, $A = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} J + 1 \cdot \big(I - \tfrac{1}{3} J\big)$.
- A diagonal matrix $D$ has the eigendecomposition $D = I D I^{-1}$, with $Q = I$.
- A [[projection|033DA1WE8Y]] $P$ with $P^2 = P$ has an eigendecomposition with eigenvalues in $\{0, 1\}$, by the [[minimal polynomial criterion|0MPAJRB23T]].
- A real symmetric matrix has an eigendecomposition with $Q$ orthogonal, by the [[spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]].

### Non-examples

- The [[shear|SBDF8APDF7]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has only one independent eigenvector, so it has no eigendecomposition over any field.
- The [[rotation by a right angle|XS5H42KEP5]] has no eigendecomposition over $\R$, since it has no real eigenvalue. It has one over $\C$, with $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(i, -i)$.
- A nonzero [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]] matrix has no eigendecomposition, because it is a [[defective matrix|5F2AV5855Y]].

### Basic properties

- $A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}$ holds exactly when $A Q = Q \Lambda$ with $Q$ invertible, that is, when the columns of $Q$ form a basis of eigenvectors. This is the matrix form of the [[characterization by eigenvectors|056SW1E0AC]].
- From $Q^{-1} Q = I$, we get $r_i q_j = \delta_{ij}$. So $E_i E_j = \delta_{ij} E_i$, $E_1 + \cdots + E_n = I$ and $A E_i = \lambda_i E_i$.
- For each polynomial $f$, $f(A) = Q f(\Lambda) Q^{-1} = \sum_i f(\lambda_i) E_i$. For powers, see [[powers of a diagonalizable matrix|0TNR9BQ871]].
- When an eigenvalue $\mu$ is repeated, the sum of the $E_i$ with $\lambda_i = \mu$ is the projection onto the eigenspace of $\mu$ along the other eigenspaces. These projections do not depend on the choice of $Q$, while the single $E_i$ do.
- The decomposition is unique up to the order of the eigenvalues and the choice of bases of the eigenspaces.

### History

The formula $f(A) = \sum_i f(\lambda_i) E_i$ for a matrix with distinct eigenvalues is known as Sylvester's formula.
For real symmetric matrices, the eigendecomposition with an orthogonal $Q$ is the matrix form of the principal axis theorem for quadratic forms.

### Common errors

- Writing $A = Q^{-1} \Lambda Q$ when the columns of $Q$ are the eigenvectors. With eigenvectors as columns, the correct form is $A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}$.
- Replacing $Q^{-1}$ by $Q^{\mathsf T}$ for a matrix that is not symmetric. This only works when $Q$ is orthogonal.
- Listing the eigenvalues in $\Lambda$ in a different order than the eigenvectors in $Q$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An eigendecomposition splits a matrix into three simpler pieces.
The middle piece is diagonal and holds the stretch factors.
The outer pieces change to the special directions and back.
Many hard questions about the matrix become easy questions about the stretch factors.

### L2 human

Find the eigendecomposition of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $p_A = (t - 2)^2 - 1 = (t - 3)(t - 1)$, so the eigenvalues are $3$ and $1$.
2. $A(1, 1) = (3, 3)$ and $A(1, -1) = (1, -1)$. So $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(3, 1)$.
3. $Q^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, since $Q^2 = 2I$.
4. $E_1 = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $E_2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
5. Check: $3 E_1 + E_2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = A$.
6. Then $A^k = 3^k E_1 + E_2$. For $k = 2$, this gives $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, which equals $A^2$.

The typical use is a formula for $A^k$, $e^{tA}$ or $\sqrt{A}$ through the eigenvalues.
The trap is to forget $Q^{-1}$: the product $Q \Lambda$ alone is not $A$.

### L3 human

For a diagonalizable operator on a finite-dimensional space, $T = \sum_\mu \mu \, \pi_\mu$, where $\pi_\mu$ are the projections onto the eigenspaces.
The projections are polynomials in $T$, by the [[kernels lemma|ACBNK1VFVG]].
For normal operators on a Hilbert space, the spectral theorem replaces the sum by an integral against a projection-valued measure.
For matrices that are not diagonalizable, the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]] keeps the spectral sum for the semisimple part and adds a nilpotent part.
