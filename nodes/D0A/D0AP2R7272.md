## Summary

An eigendecomposition writes a matrix as a change to a basis of eigenvectors, a diagonal stretch, and a change back.
It is a [[diagonalization|07SQCNV233]] written as a factorization, and it reduces questions about the matrix to questions about its eigenvalues.
Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over a field $F$.
An eigendecomposition of $A$ is a factorization
$$A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1},$$
where $Q$ is an [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] $n \times n$ matrix over $F$ and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ is a [[diagonal matrix|0Q9PGXD9E4]] over $F$.
Then column $q_i$ of $Q$ is an [[eigenvector|0B5H4208Z9]] of $A$ for $\lambda_i$, and $A$ has an eigendecomposition exactly when it is diagonalizable.
If $r_i$ is row $i$ of $Q^{-1}$, then $E_i = q_i r_i$ is a matrix of rank one, and the equivalent form
$$A = \lambda_1 E_1 + \dots + \lambda_n E_n$$
is the spectral sum of $A$.

## Motivation

A linear system $x_{k+1} = A x_k$ couples all the coordinates of $x$.
In the new coordinates $y = Q^{-1} x$ it becomes $y_{k+1} = \Lambda y_k$, which is $n$ separate equations $y_{k+1, i} = \lambda_i y_{k, i}$.
So the eigendecomposition turns one matrix problem into $n$ problems about numbers.
The same reduction solves the differential equation $x' = A x$ through the [[matrix exponential|020M04FN1H]], and it computes [[powers of a diagonalizable matrix|0TNR9BQ871]].

The spectral sum shows a second picture.
Each $E_i$ picks out the part of a vector along $q_i$, and $A$ stretches that part by $\lambda_i$.
This picture returns in the spectral theorem of the sub-area on operators on inner product spaces, where the $E_i$ become orthogonal projections.

## Notation

Books write $A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}$, $A = P D P^{-1}$ or $A = V \Lambda V^{-1}$, and $P^{-1} A P = D$ is the same equation.
The matrices $E_i$, or their sums over equal eigenvalues, are called spectral projections, and the name Frobenius covariants is used for them when the eigenvalues are distinct.
For a real symmetric matrix one can choose $Q$ orthogonal and write $A = Q \Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}$.
Keep the order of the eigenvalues in $\Lambda$ the same as the order of the eigenvectors in $Q$.

## Remarks

### A first decomposition

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, as in the [[2 × 2 diagonalization example|306FBDECMB]].
Its eigenvalues are $5$ and $2$, with the eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -2)$.
So
$$Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Multiply out: $Q \Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 5 & -4 \end{pmatrix}$, and
$$Q \Lambda Q^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 10 + 2 & 5 - 2 \\ 10 - 4 & 5 + 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

### The spectral sum

Each column of $Q$ times the matching row of $Q^{-1}$ gives a rank-one matrix.
For the example above,
$$E_1 = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_2 = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $E_1 + E_2 = I$, and $5 E_1 + 2 E_2 = \tfrac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 12 & 3 \\ 6 & 9 \end{pmatrix} = A$.

Why do these rules hold in general?
From $Q^{-1} Q = I$ we get $r_i q_j = \delta_{ij}$, so $E_i E_j = q_i (r_i q_j) r_j = \delta_{ij} E_i$.
From $Q Q^{-1} = I$ we get $E_1 + \dots + E_n = I$, and $A q_i = \lambda_i q_i$ gives $A E_i = \lambda_i E_i$.
So the $E_i$ are projections that sum to $I$ and kill each other, and for every polynomial $f$
$$f(A) = Q f(\Lambda) Q^{-1} = f(\lambda_1) E_1 + \dots + f(\lambda_n) E_n.$$

### Repeated eigenvalues

The matrix $A = I + J$ of size $3$, where $J$ is the all-ones matrix, has the [[eigenvalues 4, 1, 1|K5NXSFJV50]].
Here the single $E_i$ for the eigenvalue $1$ depend on the choice of basis of the eigenspace, so they are not determined by $A$.
Their sum is determined: grouping by eigenvalue gives
$$A = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} J + 1 \cdot \big(I - \tfrac{1}{3} J\big).$$
Check: $\tfrac{4}{3} J + I - \tfrac{1}{3} J = I + J$.
In general the sum of the $E_i$ with $\lambda_i = \mu$ is the projection onto the eigenspace of $\mu$ along the other eigenspaces, and it does not depend on $Q$.
So the decomposition is unique up to the order of the eigenvalues and the choice of a basis in each eigenspace.

### Where no eigendecomposition exists

A matrix has an eigendecomposition only when it has a basis of eigenvectors, by the [[characterization by eigenvectors|056SW1E0AC]], since $A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}$ is the same as $A Q = Q \Lambda$.
The [[shear|SBDF8APDF7]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has only the eigenvectors on the line of $e_1$, so it has no eigendecomposition over any field.
A nonzero [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]] matrix is a [[defective matrix|5F2AV5855Y]] for the same reason.
The [[rotation by a right angle|XS5H42KEP5]] has no real eigenvalue, so it has no eigendecomposition over $\R$, while over $\C$ it has one with $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(i, -i)$.
On the other side, a diagonal matrix $D$ has the eigendecomposition $D = I D I^{-1}$, and a [[projection|033DA1WE8Y]] has one with eigenvalues $0$ and $1$ by the [[minimal polynomial criterion|0MPAJRB23T]].

### Watch out

With the eigenvectors as the columns of $Q$, the factorization is $A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1}$, not $Q^{-1} \Lambda Q$.
For the matrix $A$ above, $Q^{-1} \Lambda Q = \tfrac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 12 & 6 \\ 3 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$, which is $A^{\mathsf T}$ and not $A$.
A second slip is to use $Q^{\mathsf T}$ in place of $Q^{-1}$.
That is correct only when $Q$ is orthogonal, as for a real symmetric matrix with the [[real spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]].

### History

For a matrix with distinct eigenvalues, the formula $f(A) = \sum_i f(\lambda_i) E_i$ is known as Sylvester's formula.
For real symmetric matrices, the eigendecomposition with an orthogonal $Q$ is the matrix form of the [[principal axis theorem|0PR5P9Z4KM]] for quadratic forms.

### Try it

First, find $A^2$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ from the spectral sum.
It is $25 E_1 + 4 E_2 = \tfrac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 54 & 21 \\ 42 & 33 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 18 & 7 \\ 14 & 11 \end{pmatrix}$, and direct multiplication agrees.

Second, find a matrix $B$ with $B^2 = A$.
Take $B = \sqrt 5 \, E_1 + \sqrt 2 \, E_2$, since $E_1^2 = E_1$, $E_2^2 = E_2$ and $E_1 E_2 = E_2 E_1 = 0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An eigendecomposition splits a matrix into three simpler pieces.
The middle piece is diagonal and holds the stretch factors.
The outer pieces turn the usual coordinates into coordinates along the special directions, and back again.
For a stretch by $3$ along one direction and by $1$ along another, everything about the matrix is in the two numbers $3$ and $1$ and the two directions.

### L2 human

Let us decompose $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
The characteristic polynomial is
$$p_A(t) = (t - 2)^2 - 1 = (t - 3)(t - 1),$$
so the eigenvalues are $3$ and $1$.
Since $A(1, 1) = (3, 3)$ and $A(1, -1) = (1, -1)$, we take $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(3, 1)$.
Then $Q^2 = 2I$, so $Q^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2} Q$, and the rank-one pieces are
$$E_1 = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_2 = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $3 E_1 + E_2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = A$.
Now $A^k = 3^k E_1 + E_2$, and for $k = 2$ this gives $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, which is $A^2$.
The same pattern gives $e^{tA} = e^{3t} E_1 + e^{t} E_2$.
The trap is to stop at $Q \Lambda$: without the factor $Q^{-1}$ the product is not $A$.

### L3 human

For a diagonalizable operator $T$ on a finite-dimensional space, $T = \sum_\mu \mu \, \pi_\mu$, where $\pi_\mu$ is the projection onto the eigenspace of $\mu$ along the other eigenspaces.
The $\pi_\mu$ are polynomials in $T$, by the [[kernels lemma|ACBNK1VFVG]] and the [[primary decomposition theorem|49ZHBMYKVE]].
For normal operators on a Hilbert space, the spectral theorem replaces the sum by an integral against a projection-valued measure.
For matrices that are not diagonalizable, the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]] keeps the spectral sum for the semisimple part and adds a nilpotent part.
