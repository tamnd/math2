## Summary

An *adaptive logic* is a [[paraconsistent logic|C7YWCR87QJ]] that reasons as classical logic does until a contradiction is shown, and then withdraws the inferences that the contradiction affects.
It has three parts: a *lower limit logic* that is always valid, a set of *abnormalities* such as $A \land \neg A$, and a *strategy* for handling them.
Rules such as [[disjunctive syllogism|6EFJF9NAYX]] are used on the condition that some abnormalities are false.
Proofs are *dynamic*: a line derived at one stage can be marked as withdrawn at a later stage.
Adaptive logics were pioneered by Batens.

## Motivation

Inconsistency is usually rare.
A reasoner should treat a set of premises "as consistently as possible": use classical rules where no contradiction is involved, and only drop them where one is.
A fixed paraconsistent logic, such as the logic of paradox, drops disjunctive syllogism everywhere, even for consistent premises.
Adaptive logics keep the rule wherever it is safe.
They also model the way a person changes conclusions during reasoning, without new information.

## Notation

We write LLL for the lower limit logic and $\Omega$ for the set of abnormalities.
A *Dab-formula* $\mathrm{Dab}(\Delta)$ is the disjunction of a finite set $\Delta \subseteq \Omega$.
A line of a dynamic proof has a number, a formula, a justification and a *condition*, a finite subset of $\Omega$.
The *upper limit logic* ULL is LLL plus the requirement that no abnormality is true.

## Remarks

### Examples

- In the lower limit logic CLuN, [[negation|029JBBF693]] is weakened: $A \lor \neg A$ holds, but $\neg A$ may be true when $A$ is true.
- [[A premise set from the SEP|R6KHZTWNVK]] shows a conclusion $t$ that is first derived and then withdrawn.
- [[Consistent premises give disjunctive syllogism|QV7H0Y3XDW]]: for a consistent premise set, the adaptive logic gives all classical consequences.

### Non-examples

- [[The logic of paradox is not adaptive|C2ZGZYF53H]]: it rejects disjunctive syllogism even for consistent premises.
- Classical logic is the upper limit logic, not an adaptive logic: it never withdraws a line, and it is explosive.

### Basic properties

- Adaptive consequence lies between LLL and ULL: every LLL consequence is an adaptive consequence, and every adaptive consequence is a ULL consequence.
- Adaptive consequence is nonmonotonic: new premises can add a Dab-formula and withdraw a conclusion; see [[nonmonotonic logic|BKDFH4PKRP]].
- The two standard strategies are *reliability* and *minimal abnormality*. Reliability withdraws a line when its condition meets the set of unreliable formulas, those in some minimal Dab-consequence. Under minimal abnormality, $A$ follows when $A$ holds in every LLL model of the premises whose set of true abnormalities is minimal.
- *Final derivability* is the stable notion. A formula is finally derived when it is on an unmarked line, and every extension of the proof that marks the line can be extended further to unmark it.
- The SEP entry calls the change of status during a proof "internal dynamics", and links it to the fact that the set of valid inferences need not be recursively enumerable.

### History

Adaptive logics were pioneered by Batens in Belgium.
The SEP entry cites Batens (2001) as a representative work.

### Common errors

- To think that adaptive logics drop classical rules. They keep them on condition and drop only unsafe uses.
- To confuse the conditional status of a line with its final status. A line can be unmarked now and marked later.
- To think that "as consistent as possible" means "pick a maximal consistent subset". Adaptive logics do not choose; reliability takes all minimal Dab-formulas into account.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose you reason from a set of facts and use the rule "$A$ or $B$, not $A$, so $B$".
Later you find that one of the facts you used is contradicted.
An adaptive logic then cancels exactly the steps that relied on that fact being consistent.
All other steps stay.

### L2 human

We use disjunctive syllogism on a condition.

1. From $\neg s$ and $s \lor t$, derive $t$ on the condition $\set{s \land \neg s}$.
2. If later the proof derives $s \land \neg s$, or a minimal disjunction of abnormalities that contains it, then line 1 is marked.
3. A marked line is not a conclusion at that stage.

A trap is to forget minimal disjunctions: the line is withdrawn even if $s \land \neg s$ is never derived alone.

### L3 human

In the standard format, an adaptive logic is a triple of a lower limit logic, a set of abnormalities characterized by a logical form, and a strategy.
The lower limit logic is a monotonic, compact, reflexive and transitive consequence relation, usually with classical disjunction.
Soundness and completeness results then hold uniformly: dynamic proofs with reliability or minimal abnormality match the semantics of reliable or minimally abnormal LLL models.
Beyond inconsistency, the same format handles inductive generalization, abduction and defeasible reasoning.
