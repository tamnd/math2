## Example

Let $\mathbb{P}$ be the set of prime numbers, and let $\mathcal{U}$ be a nonprincipal [[ultrafilter|J64Y3JC0RE]] on $\mathbb{P}$.
For each $p \in \mathbb{P}$ let $\F_p$ be the [[finite field|0ZXZ82FTX2]] $\Z / p\Z$, as a structure for the signature $(+, \cdot, -, 0, 1)$ of rings.
Let $K = \prod_{p \in \mathbb{P}} \F_p / \mathcal{U}$ be their [[ultraproduct|AX9Q86GB8M]].
We check that $K$ is an infinite field of [[characteristic|1ZB13N78XW]] $0$.

1. A nonprincipal ultrafilter contains every cofinite set: a finite set is a finite union of singletons, and no singleton lies in $\mathcal{U}$.
2. The field axioms are first-order sentences, and they hold in every $\F_p$. Their truth set is $\mathbb{P} \in \mathcal{U}$, so $K$ is a field by [[Łoś's theorem|AGYBSTD7BG]].
3. Fix a prime $q$, and let $\chi_q$ be the sentence $\neg (1 + 1 + \dots + 1 = 0)$ with $q$ summands.
4. $\chi_q$ holds in $\F_p$ exactly when $p \neq q$. So its truth set is $\mathbb{P} \setminus \set{q}$, which is cofinite and lies in $\mathcal{U}$ by step 1.
5. By Łoś's theorem $\chi_q$ holds in $K$ for every prime $q$, so $K$ has characteristic $0$.
6. For each $n \ge 1$, the sentence "there are at least $n$ elements" holds in $\F_p$ for all $p \ge n$, a cofinite set. So $K$ is infinite.

## Remarks

The example shows that an ultraproduct can have a property that no factor has: each $\F_p$ has prime characteristic, and $K$ has characteristic $0$.
This does not contradict Łoś's theorem, since "characteristic $0$" is an infinite set of sentences, not one sentence.
Some properties of $K$ depend on $\mathcal{U}$.
The element $-1$ is a square in $\F_p$ exactly when $p = 2$ or $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, and both this set and its complement are infinite.
So $-1$ is a square in $K$ for some choices of $\mathcal{U}$ and not for others.
Nonprincipal ultraproducts of finite fields are pseudofinite fields, and Ax used them to describe the theory of all finite fields.
If $\mathcal{U}$ is principal at a prime $q$, then $K \cong \F_q$ and the characteristic is $q$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For each prime $p$ there is a small number system with $p$ elements, where adding $p$ ones gives $0$.
We glue all these systems into one by an ultraproduct.
In the glued system, adding $5$ ones never gives $0$, because this is true in every system except the one with $5$ elements.
The same holds for every number of ones.
So in the glued system no sum of ones is ever $0$, as in the rational numbers.

### L2 human

We decide whether the equation $x^2 = 2$ has a solution in $K$, for one family of choices of $\mathcal{U}$.

1. By the second supplement to the law of quadratic reciprocity, $2$ is a square mod an odd prime $p$ exactly when $p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8$.
2. Let $X = \set{p : p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8}$. By Dirichlet's theorem on primes in progressions, both $X$ and $\mathbb{P} \setminus X$ are infinite.
3. Each infinite subset of $\mathbb{P}$ lies in some nonprincipal ultrafilter: the cofinite sets together with $X$ have the finite intersection property.
4. If $X \in \mathcal{U}$, then the sentence $\exists x \, (x \cdot x = 1 + 1)$ holds in $K$ by Łoś's theorem.
5. If $X \notin \mathcal{U}$, then its complement is in $\mathcal{U}$, and the sentence fails in $K$.

The typical use is to turn a statement about all large primes into a statement about a field of characteristic $0$.
The trap is to think that $K$ is determined by the family of factors alone; it also depends on $\mathcal{U}$.
