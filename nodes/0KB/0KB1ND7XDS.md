## Statement

Let $G = \langle g \rangle$ be a [[cyclic group|0KVGH0J5B3]] with generator $g$ and identity $e$.

1. (Subgroups.) Every [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] $H$ of $G$ is cyclic. If $H = \set{e}$, then $H = \langle e \rangle$. If $H \neq \set{e}$, then there is a least positive integer $m$ with $g^m \in H$, and $H = \langle g^m \rangle$.
2. (Quotients.) Every subgroup $N$ of $G$ is [[normal|0CB0DX5BVV]] in $G$, and the [[quotient group|0KYX2N69J0]] $G/N$ is cyclic. The coset $gN$ is a generator: $G/N = \langle gN \rangle$.

In additive notation, for example in $(\Z, +)$ with $g = 1$, the power $g^m$ is the multiple $m \cdot 1 = m$.
So part 1 says that a nonzero subgroup of $\Z$ is generated by its least positive element.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. The group $G$ must be cyclic. In the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_4$, the subgroup $V = \set{e, (1\,2)(3\,4), (1\,3)(2\,4), (1\,4)(2\,3)}$ has $4$ elements. Each element of $V$ other than $e$ has [[order|0WE2NHZJ7B]] $2$, so no element generates $V$, and $V$ is not cyclic.
2. The quotient part also needs a cyclic group. The [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]] $V$ is a quotient of itself by $\set{e}$, and it is not cyclic.
3. The exponent $m$ must be the least positive one. In $(\Z, +)$, the subgroup $4\Z$ contains $8$, but $\langle 8 \rangle = 8\Z$ does not contain $4$. So $8$ does not generate $4\Z$.

### Consequences

1. Every subgroup of $(\Z, +)$ has the form $n\Z$. This is the [[classification of the subgroups of the integers|0SRATCFF1W]].
2. The subgroups of a finite cyclic group are the cyclic subgroups $\langle g^{n/d} \rangle$, one for each divisor $d$ of the order $n$. This is the [[fundamental theorem of cyclic groups|0423TXR9AN]].
3. Each quotient $\Z / n\Z$ of the integers, the group of [[integers modulo n|03VFZ2WETT]], is cyclic. The coset $1 + n\Z$ generates it.
4. A group with a subgroup that is not cyclic is not cyclic. For example, $\Z/2 \times \Z/4$ contains the subgroup $\set{0, 1} \times \set{0, 2}$, which is isomorphic to the Klein four-group. So $\Z/2 \times \Z/4$ is not cyclic.
5. A group with a quotient that is not cyclic is not cyclic, by part 2.
6. If $\varphi \colon G \to K$ is a [[homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]] and $G = \langle g \rangle$, then the image $\varphi(G)$ is cyclic, generated by $\varphi(g)$. The reason is the same as for part 2: $\varphi(g^k) = \varphi(g)^k$.

### Converse

The converse of part 1 is true for a trivial reason: $G$ is a subgroup of itself.
A stronger converse is false: a group whose proper subgroups are all cyclic need not be cyclic.
In $V$, the proper subgroups are $\set{e}$ and three subgroups of order $2$, and all of them are cyclic.
The converse of part 2 is false too: one cyclic quotient $G/N$, even with $N$ cyclic, does not make $G$ cyclic.
In $V = \set{e, a, b, c}$, the subgroup $N = \set{e, a}$ is normal and cyclic.
$V/N$ has $2$ elements, so it is cyclic.
But $V$ is not cyclic.

### Generalizations

1. A subgroup of $\Z^r$ is isomorphic to $\Z^s$ for some $s \leq r$. So a subgroup of a [[free abelian group|0WJX8MGG37]] of rank $r$ is free abelian of rank at most $r$.
2. The proof of part 1 uses only division with remainder. The same proof shows that every ideal of the polynomial ring $F[x]$ over a field $F$ is generated by one polynomial. In this proof the degree takes the place of the exponent.
3. For groups that are not abelian, the number of generators can grow in a subgroup. The free group on $2$ generators has a subgroup that is not generated by any finite set.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A cyclic group is a group in which every element is a repeat of one basic step.
An example is the $12$ hours of a clock, with the basic step of $1$ hour.
Now start at $0$ and move in steps of $4$ hours.
We visit $0$, $4$ and $8$, and then we are back at $0$.
These three hours form a smaller group inside the clock.
The theorem says that every smaller group inside a cyclic group is of this kind.
It has its own basic step, and that step is the smallest step that the smaller group contains.

### L2 human

We take $G = \Z/12$ under addition, with generator $g = 1$, and the subgroup $H = \set{0, 3, 6, 9}$.

1. Among the positive integers $1, 2, 3$, only $3$ lies in $H$. So the least positive exponent is $m = 3$.
2. The multiples of $3$ in $\Z/12$ are $0, 3, 6, 9$. So $\langle 3 \rangle = H$, as part 1 says.
3. The cosets of $H$ are $H$, $1 + H = \set{1, 4, 7, 10}$ and $2 + H = \set{2, 5, 8, 11}$.
4. The multiples of the coset $1 + H$ are $1 + H$, $2 + H$ and $3 + H = H$. So $G/H = \langle 1 + H \rangle$ is cyclic of order $3$, as part 2 says.

The typical use is a search for all subgroups of a cyclic group: we only need to look at the subgroups generated by one element.
A trap: an element of $H$ other than $g^m$ can fail to generate $H$.
In $\Z$, the element $8$ lies in $4\Z$, but $\langle 8 \rangle = 8\Z$ is smaller than $4\Z$.

### L3 human

Every cyclic group is a quotient of $\Z$, because $k \mapsto g^k$ is a surjective homomorphism from $\Z$ onto $\langle g \rangle$.
By the [[correspondence theorem|0S9ZVZ34MD]], the subgroups of $\Z/n\Z$ match the subgroups of $\Z$ that contain $n\Z$.
So both parts of the theorem come down to one fact about $\Z$.
Each additive subgroup of $\Z$ is $m\Z$ for one $m$, so $\Z$ is a [[principal ideal domain|0CMD0BCH8T]].

The class of cyclic groups is closed under subgroups and quotients, but not under extensions.
The group $V$ has the normal subgroup $\set{e, a}$ of order $2$ with quotient of order $2$, and $V$ is not cyclic.
The class is not closed under direct products either, and the [[criterion for products of cyclic groups|08FDFEF8D1]] says exactly when a product is cyclic.
A group in which every finitely generated subgroup is cyclic is a [[locally cyclic group|RXWA8TF2RM]], such as $(\Q, +)$.
