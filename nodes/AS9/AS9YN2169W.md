## Statement

Let $V \subset \R^3$ be a bounded Lipschitz domain, the volume element, and write $\langle g \rangle = \frac{1}{\abs{V}} \int_V g \, dx$.
Let $\sigma$ be a symmetric [[stress field|KG2XWEMJ30]] on $V$ with $\operatorname{div} \sigma = 0$.
Let $\varepsilon = \frac{1}{2}(\nabla u + \nabla u^T)$ be the [[strain|8QS6QMJF5Z]] of a displacement field $u$ on $V$.
Assume that $\sigma$ and $u$ are smooth enough for the divergence theorem.
Let $n$ be the outer unit normal on $\partial V$.
Then (Hill's lemma)
$$\langle \sigma : \varepsilon \rangle - \langle \sigma \rangle : \langle \varepsilon \rangle = \frac{1}{\abs{V}} \int_{\partial V} \big( u - \langle \varepsilon \rangle x \big) \cdot \big( \sigma n - \langle \sigma \rangle n \big) \, dS.$$
In particular the *Hill–Mandel condition* $\langle \sigma : \varepsilon \rangle = \langle \sigma \rangle : \langle \varepsilon \rangle$ holds in each of these three cases:

1. affine boundary displacements: $u = E x$ on $\partial V$ for a constant symmetric matrix $E$;
2. uniform boundary tractions: $\sigma n = \Sigma n$ on $\partial V$ for a constant symmetric matrix $\Sigma$;
3. $V$ is a box, $u - E x$ is periodic and $\sigma n$ is antiperiodic on opposite faces.

## Remarks

The proof uses the divergence theorem twice: $\langle \varepsilon \rangle$ and $\langle \sigma \rangle$ are boundary integrals of $u \otimes n$ and $x \otimes \sigma n$, and $\langle \sigma : \varepsilon \rangle$ is the boundary integral of $u \cdot \sigma n$ because $\operatorname{div} \sigma = 0$.
Expanding the right-hand side and using these formulas gives the identity.
In case 1 the first factor vanishes, since $\langle \varepsilon \rangle = E$; in case 2 the second factor vanishes, since $\langle \sigma \rangle = \Sigma$; in case 3 the boundary integral cancels between opposite faces.
For general boundary data the condition fails: the boundary term need not vanish.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The work done inside a small sample of material, added up point by point, should equal the work computed from its average stress and its average strain.
This is not true in general, because the fluctuations of stress and strain can interact.
It is true when the sample is loaded at its boundary in one of the three standard ways.

### L2 human

The Hill–Mandel condition is the energy consistency requirement of multiscale mechanics.
It allows one to define the macroscopic stress and strain at a point as averages over a [[representative elementary volume|ZMZY201H89]].
With this choice, the macroscopic stress power equals the average microscopic one.
The three boundary conditions give ordered apparent stiffnesses.
Affine displacements give the stiffest response, uniform tractions the softest, and periodic conditions lie in between.
For a large volume element they agree.
