## Statement

An independent list can never be longer than a spanning list, and its vectors can be traded into the spanning list one at a time.
Let $F$ be a field and let $V$ be a vector space over $F$.
Let $u_1, \dots, u_m \in V$ be [[linearly independent|0EVHJFTG9Z]], and let $w_1, \dots, w_n \in V$ [[span|0BJKVF90Z6]] $V$.
Then the following hold.

1. We have $m \le n$.
2. (Replacement form.) After a renumbering of the $w_j$, the list $u_1, \dots, u_m, w_{m+1}, \dots, w_n$ spans $V$.

## Remarks

### A trade in three dimensions

Take $V = \mathbb{R}^3$ with the spanning list $e_1, e_2, e_3$, and the independent vectors $u_1 = (1, 1, 0)$ and $u_2 = (0, 1, 1)$.
First write $u_1$ in the spanning list:
$$u_1 = 1 e_1 + 1 e_2 + 0 e_3.$$
The coefficient of $e_1$ is not zero, so we may solve for $e_1 = u_1 - e_2$, and the list $u_1, e_2, e_3$ still spans.
Next write $u_2$ in the new list:
$$u_2 = 0 u_1 + 1 e_2 + 1 e_3.$$
The coefficient of $e_2$ is not zero, so $e_2 = u_2 - e_3$, and the list $u_1, u_2, e_3$ spans $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Each trade used up one $e_j$, which is why the $u_i$ cannot outnumber the $e_j$.

### Watch out

You may not trade away any vector you like.
Take $u_1 = (1, 1, 0)$ as above and $u_2 = (1, 0, 0)$, so that after the first trade $u_2 = u_1 - e_2 + 0 e_3$.
If you swap out $e_3$, whose coefficient is $0$, you get $(1, 1, 0), (0, 1, 0), (1, 0, 0)$, and all three lie in the plane $z = 0$.
The correct move is to remove a vector with a nonzero coefficient, here $e_2$, and then $u_1, u_2, e_3$ spans.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Without independence, part 1 fails.
The three vectors $(1, 0)$, $(2, 0)$ and $(3, 0)$ are more than the two vectors $e_1, e_2$ that span $\mathbb{R}^2$, but they are dependent.

Without spanning, part 1 fails as well.
The vector $(1, 0)$ alone does not span $\mathbb{R}^2$, and the independent list $e_1, e_2$ is longer than it.

The field is used in the proof, which divides by a nonzero coefficient.
Over the ring $\mathbb{Z}$, the replacement form fails: $2$ and $3$ span $\mathbb{Z}$, the single element $6$ is independent, but $6, 3$ spans only $3\mathbb{Z}$ and $2, 6$ spans only $2\mathbb{Z}$.
Part 1 still holds for modules over a commutative ring, by a proof with determinants.

### What it gives you

Any two finite bases of $V$ have the same number of vectors, which is the [[dimension theorem|0WK0Y8RH1M]].
So in a space of dimension $n$, each independent list has at most $n$ vectors and each spanning list has at least $n$.
Part 2 says more: an independent list extends to a basis by vectors taken from any finite spanning list, as in the [[extension to a basis|0M4SXE5WW3]].
In $F^m$, part 1 gives again that more than $m$ vectors are dependent, which the [[column test|76GG9B25A1]] proves by row reduction.

### The converse

The converse of part 1 is false.
A list with $m \le n$ vectors need not be independent or spanning: the list $(1, 0), (2, 0)$ has two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and is neither.

### Generalizations

The exchange property is one of the axioms of a [[matroid|PX31ZNYS3Z]], so the lemma holds in every matroid, for example for algebraic independence in field extensions.
For infinite families, an independent set has cardinality at most that of each spanning set, and the proof uses cardinal arithmetic as in the [[proof for infinite bases|C6V1B2MC0X]].

## History

The lemma carries the name of Ernst Steinitz, who used it in a paper of 1913.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose three arrows are enough to build every arrow in space.
Then you can never find four arrows in space that are truly independent.
The reason is a trade: each independent arrow can take the place of one of the building arrows, and the building still works.
With only three places to fill, a fourth independent arrow has nowhere to go.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: show that $p_1 = 1 + x$, $p_2 = x + x^2$, $p_3 = 1 + x^2$ and $p_4 = x$ are dependent in the space $P_2$ of polynomials of degree at most $2$.
The three monomials $1, x, x^2$ span $P_2$, so by part 1 every independent list in $P_2$ has at most $3$ vectors.
Four polynomials are too many, so they are dependent, and we did not need to solve any equations.
Check: $p_1 + p_2 - p_3 = 2x = 2p_4$, so $p_1 + p_2 - p_3 - 2p_4 = 0$.
The lemma gives a bound from counting alone, and this is how it is used in almost every proof about dimension.

### L3 human

The lemma is the step that makes dimension a well-defined number, and the [[proof by induction|155KAD3BRS]] is the standard route to the dimension theorem.
In the language of matroids, the subsets of a vector space that are independent satisfy the exchange axiom, and the lemma says that all maximal independent sets have the same size.
The same pattern gives the transcendence degree of a field extension, where algebraic independence replaces linear independence.
