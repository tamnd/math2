## Statement

Let $F$ be a field and let $V$ be a vector space over $F$.
Let $u_1, \dots, u_m \in V$ be [[linearly independent|0EVHJFTG9Z]], and let $w_1, \dots, w_n \in V$ [[span|0BJKVF90Z6]] $V$.

1. Then $m \le n$.
2. (Replacement form) After a renumbering of the $w_j$, the family $u_1, \dots, u_m, w_{m+1}, \dots, w_n$ spans $V$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Independence of the $u_i$ is needed: the dependent family $(1, 0), (2, 0), (3, 0)$ has three vectors in $\R^2$, which is spanned by two.
Spanning by the $w_j$ is needed: $(1, 0)$ alone does not span $\R^2$, and the two independent vectors $e_1, e_2$ are more than one.
The field is needed in the proof, which divides by a coefficient.
For free modules over a commutative ring, part 1 still holds, by a different proof.

### Consequences

Any two finite bases of $V$ have the same number of elements; see the [[dimension theorem|0WK0Y8RH1M]].
In a space of dimension $n$, each independent list has at most $n$ vectors and each spanning list has at least $n$ vectors.
By part 2, an independent list extends to a basis by vectors taken from any given finite spanning list; see [[extension to a basis|0M4SXE5WW3]].
In $F^m$, part 1 gives again that more than $m$ vectors are dependent; compare [[the column test|76GG9B25A1]].

### Converse

The converse of part 1 fails: $m \le n$ does not make a list independent or spanning.
The list $(1, 0), (2, 0)$ has two vectors in $\R^2$ and is neither.

### Generalizations

The exchange property holds in every [[matroid|PX31ZNYS3Z]]; Mac Lane stated it for abstract closure systems in 1936.
For infinite families, an independent set has cardinality at most that of each spanning set; the proof uses cardinal arithmetic, as in the [[dimension theorem|0WK0Y8RH1M]].

## History

The lemma is named after Steinitz, who used it in 1913.
The idea of exchanging vectors appears earlier in Grassmann's work.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An independent family can never have more vectors than a spanning family.
We can trade the vectors of the spanning family, one by one, for the independent vectors, and the family still spans.
This lemma is the reason why the dimension of a space is well defined.

### L2 human

Example in $\R^3$: $u_1 = (1, 1, 0)$, $u_2 = (0, 1, 1)$ and the spanning list $e_1, e_2, e_3$.
First, $u_1 = e_1 + e_2$ has a nonzero coefficient at $e_1$, so we replace $e_1$: the list $u_1, e_2, e_3$ spans.
Next, $u_2 = 0 \cdot u_1 + e_2 + e_3$ has a nonzero coefficient at $e_2$, so we replace $e_2$: the list $u_1, u_2, e_3$ spans.
At each step, the new vector must have a nonzero coefficient at some remaining $w_j$; otherwise the $u_i$ would be dependent.
