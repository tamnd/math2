## Statement

The adjoint follows the rules of the conjugate transpose of matrices: it is additive, it conjugates scalars, it reverses products, and taking it twice gives the map back.
Let $U$, $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional inner product spaces over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and write $T^*$ for the [[adjoint|0ST8QF9T3T]] of a linear map $T$.

1. $(S + T)^* = S^* + T^*$ for all linear maps $S, T \colon V \to W$.
2. $(aT)^* = \overline{a}\, T^*$ for each linear map $T \colon V \to W$ and each $a \in \mathbb{K}$.
3. $(T^*)^* = T$ for each linear map $T \colon V \to W$.
4. $(ST)^* = T^* S^*$ for all linear maps $T \colon V \to W$ and $S \colon W \to U$.
5. $I^* = I$, where $I$ is the identity map of $V$.
6. If $T \colon V \to W$ is invertible, then $T^*$ is invertible and $(T^*)^{-1} = (T^{-1})^*$.

So the map $T \mapsto T^*$ from the linear maps $V \to W$ to the linear maps $W \to V$ is a bijection, linear over $\mathbb{R}$ and [[antilinear|BNPDDM95KF]] over $\mathbb{C}$.

## Remarks

### A check with numbers

Take $S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ with the standard inner product, where the adjoint is the conjugate transpose.
Multiply first, then take the adjoint:
$$ST = \begin{pmatrix} 2 + i & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (ST)^* = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - i & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Now take the adjoints first and multiply in the reverse order:
$$T^* S^* = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - i & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The two results agree, as part 4 says.

### Why the conjugate and the reversed order are needed

Part 2 needs the conjugate over $\mathbb{C}$.
On $\mathbb{C}$ with $\ip{x}{y} = x \overline{y}$, we have $\ip{ix}{y} = i x \overline{y} = x \overline{(-iy)} = \ip{x}{-iy}$, so $(iI)^* = -iI$ and not $iI$.

Part 4 needs the reversed order.
For $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, the product $ST = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is its own adjoint.
But $S^* T^* = TS = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which is a different matrix.

Part 6 needs $T$ to be invertible, since otherwise $T^{-1}$ does not exist.
Then $T^*$ is not invertible either, because the [[kernel and image theorem|YPP8XT6B7Y]] gives $\rank T^* = \rank T$.

### What it gives you

The adjoint of a polynomial in $T$ is the polynomial with conjugate coefficients in $T^*$.
If $p(T) = \sum_k a_k T^k$, then parts 1, 2, 4 and 5 give $p(T)^* = \sum_k \overline{a_k} (T^*)^k$.
For example, $(T^2 + iT)^* = (T^*)^2 - i T^*$.

The maps $T^* T$ and $T T^*$ are [[self-adjoint|0NEGN5CRSC]], since parts 3 and 4 give $(T^* T)^* = T^* (T^*)^* = T^* T$.
Every operator $T$ on a complex space splits as $T = A + iB$ with $A = \tfrac{1}{2}(T + T^*)$ and $B = \tfrac{1}{2i}(T - T^*)$.
Parts 1 to 3 show that $A$ and $B$ are self-adjoint.
For $B$, part 2 turns the factor $\tfrac{1}{2i}$ into $\overline{\tfrac{1}{2i}} = -\tfrac{1}{2i}$, and $(T - T^*)^* = T^* - T$, so the two signs cancel.
This is the analogue of $z = x + iy$ for complex numbers.

### The rules do not determine the adjoint

The six rules hold for other maps too, so they do not replace the defining identity.
On $2 \times 2$ complex matrices, let $D = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\phi(T) = D T^* D$.
Since $D^2 = I$, the map $\phi$ satisfies parts 1 to 6 in place of the adjoint.
But $\phi$ sends $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ to $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, while the adjoint sends it to $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
In fact $\phi$ is the adjoint for the indefinite form $y^* D x$, which is not an inner product.

### Generalizations

The same rules hold for bounded operators on a Hilbert space, with the same proofs.
They are the axioms of an involution in a $C^*$-algebra.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The adjoint behaves like the transpose of a matrix, with one extra rule over the complex numbers.
The adjoint of a sum is the sum of the adjoints.
The adjoint of a product is the product of the adjoints in the reverse order, like taking off socks and shoes.
A complex number in front of a map becomes its conjugate, so the adjoint of $iT$ is $-iT^*$.
Taking the adjoint twice gives the original map back.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: is the product of two self-adjoint operators self-adjoint?
If $S^* = S$ and $T^* = T$, part 4 gives
$$(ST)^* = T^* S^* = TS.$$
So $ST$ is self-adjoint exactly when $ST = TS$.
For $S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, both symmetric, we get $ST = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is not symmetric, and indeed $TS = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \ne ST$.
Watch out: $(ST)^* = S^* T^*$ is false in general, so a product of two self-adjoint operators need not be self-adjoint.

### L3 human

Parts 1 to 4 say that the operators on $V$ form a $*$-algebra: an algebra with an antilinear involution that reverses products.
Finite-dimensional inner product spaces with the adjoint form a dagger category, and the unitary maps are the maps with $T^* = T^{-1}$.
The [[normal operators|0C8BR8XRTR]] and the [[self-adjoint operators|0NEGN5CRSC]] are defined by relations of this algebra, and the [[spectral theorems|04CW1P44VD]] describe them.
