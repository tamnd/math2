## Statement

In dual bases, the matrix of the dual map is the transpose of the matrix of the map.
Let $F$ be a field, and let $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces over $F$.
Let $B = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ be a basis of $V$ and $C = (c_1, \dots, c_m)$ a basis of $W$, with [[dual bases|0W6M3PSD08]] $B^* = (b_1^*, \dots, b_n^*)$ and $C^* = (c_1^*, \dots, c_m^*)$.
Let $T \colon V \to W$ be a linear map with [[dual map|0C2TQMW5A1]] $T^* \colon W^* \to V^*$.
Then
$$[T^*]_{B^*, C^*} = \left([T]_{C, B}\right)^{\mathsf{T}},$$
where $[T]_{C, B}$ is the [[matrix of T|0YYMY95FWS]] from the basis $B$ to the basis $C$, and ${}^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]].
In entries, the number $c_i^*(T b_j)$ is the entry in row $i$ and column $j$ of $[T]_{C, B}$, and the entry in row $j$ and column $i$ of $[T^*]_{B^*, C^*}$.

The [[proof|NPZX4CY60C]] reads off both entries by pairing a dual basis form with a basis vector.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The bases of $W^*$ and $V^*$ must be the dual bases $C^*$ and $B^*$.
In other bases the matrix of $T^*$ changes by the change of basis formula, and it is not $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ in general.
For example, take $T = \id$ on $\R^2$ with $B = C$ the standard basis, so $A = I$.
If we use the basis $(2e_1^*, e_2^*)$ of the dual space on the target side and $B^*$ on the source side, then $T^*(2e_1^*) = 2e_1^*$ has coordinates $(2, 0)$, and the matrix of $T^*$ is $\operatorname{diag}(2, 1) \ne I^{\mathsf{T}}$.

Finite dimension is needed so that $B^*$ and $C^*$ are bases of $V^*$ and $W^*$.
In infinite dimension the coordinate forms of a basis are independent, but they do not span the dual space, so the dual map has no matrix in this sense.

### What it gives you

The theorem turns every fact about dual maps into a fact about transposes.
With the [[functoriality lemma|XBKREPBM9D]], the rule $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$ becomes $(BA)^{\mathsf{T}} = A^{\mathsf{T}} B^{\mathsf{T}}$, the [[transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]].
Applied to the identity map with two different bases of $V$, it gives the [[change of dual basis lemma|SXWX10HSB6]]: dual bases change by the inverse transpose.
With the theorem on the [[kernel and image of the dual map|7FA499AW3T]], it gives a proof that [[row rank equals column rank|0TZAG60ZYT]], since $\operatorname{rank} T^* = \operatorname{rank} T$.

### The converse

The matrix in fixed bases determines the linear map.
So if a linear map $S \colon W^* \to V^*$ has the matrix $([T]_{C, B})^{\mathsf{T}}$ in the bases $C^*$ and $B^*$, then $S = T^*$.
In this sense the transpose of a matrix and the dual map are the same idea seen in coordinates and without them.

### Generalizations

The same statement and the same proof hold for free modules of finite rank over a commutative ring, where dual bases exist.
For an inner product space and orthonormal bases, the matrix of the inner product adjoint is also the transpose, or the conjugate transpose over $\mathbb{C}$.
In other bases this fails, as the example [[the inner product adjoint is not the dual map|16WRFVDYB0]] shows.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The dual map is the transpose of a matrix, described without coordinates.
Once you choose bases and their dual bases, the matrix of the dual map is the matrix of the original map with rows and columns exchanged.
For example, the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ becomes $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.
This explains why rows and columns play symmetric roles in linear algebra.

### L2 human

We check the theorem for $T \colon \R^2 \to \R^2$, $T(x, y) = (x + 2y, \, 3x + 4y)$, in the standard bases.
The matrix of $T$ has the coefficients of each output as its rows:
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The form $T^*(e_1^*)$ reads off the first output, so it is $(x, y) \mapsto x + 2y$, which is $1 \cdot e_1^* + 2 \cdot e_2^*$.
In the same way, $T^*(e_2^*)$ is $(x, y) \mapsto 3x + 4y$, which is $3 \cdot e_1^* + 4 \cdot e_2^*$.
These coordinate vectors are the columns of the matrix of $T^*$:
$$[T^*] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = A^{\mathsf{T}}.$$
The typical use is to compute a dual map by transposing a matrix, and to give the transpose a meaning that does not depend on coordinates.
Watch out: the theorem needs the dual bases; with another basis of $W^*$ or $V^*$, the matrix of $T^*$ is not $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.

### L3 human

In coordinates, the dual functor $V \mapsto V^*$ is the transpose of matrices, and the reversal of products under the transpose is the contravariance of this functor.
In index notation a linear map has components $a^i{}_j$, and the dual map has the same components, contracted with a form $\varphi_i$ on the upper index: $(T^* \varphi)_j = \varphi_i \, a^i{}_j$.
Transposing twice gives back $A$, and on the side of maps this is the [[naturality of the evaluation map|R19758JQWE]]: $T^{**}$ corresponds to $T$ under the identification of each finite-dimensional space with its [[double dual|0ZJM9D7NPS]].
