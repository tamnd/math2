## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, and let $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces over $F$.
Let $B = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ be a basis of $V$ and $C = (c_1, \dots, c_m)$ a basis of $W$, with [[dual bases|0W6M3PSD08]] $B^*$ and $C^*$.
Let $T \colon V \to W$ be a linear map with [[dual map|0C2TQMW5A1]] $T^* \colon W^* \to V^*$.
Then
$$[T^*]_{B^*, C^*} = \left([T]_{C, B}\right)^{\mathsf{T}},$$
where $[T]_{C, B}$ is the [[matrix of $T$|0YYMY95FWS]] from the basis $B$ to the basis $C$, and ${}^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]].
In terms of entries, both sides have entry $c_i^*(T b_j)$ at the places $(j, i)$ and $(i, j)$ respectively.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The bases of $V^*$ and $W^*$ must be the dual bases of $B$ and $C$. In other bases of $W^*$ and $V^*$, the matrix of $T^*$ is $Q^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}} P$ for change of basis matrices $P$, $Q$, which is not $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ in general.
- Finite dimension is needed for $V^*$ and $W^*$ to have the dual bases $B^*$ and $C^*$.

### Consequences

- The [[transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]], $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$, follows from $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$ in the [[functoriality lemma|~3163ec56]].
- The [[change of dual basis lemma|~6d879f22]] follows: the inverse transpose rule for dual bases is this theorem for the identity map.
- With the [[kernel and image of the dual map|~0b4dab92]], it gives a proof that the row rank of a matrix equals its column rank; see [[row rank equals column rank|0TZAG60ZYT]].

### Converse

If a linear map $S \colon W^* \to V^*$ has matrix $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ in the bases $C^*$, $B^*$, then $S = T^*$.
A linear map is fixed by its matrix.

### Generalizations

- The same formula holds for free modules of finite rank over a commutative ring.
- For an inner product space with orthonormal bases, the matrix of the adjoint is also the transpose, or the conjugate transpose over $\mathbb{C}$. In other bases it is not; see [[the inner product adjoint is not the dual map|~d9b85092]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The dual map is the linear algebra form of the transpose of a matrix.
In dual bases, its matrix is the transpose of the matrix of the original map.
This explains why rows and columns play symmetric roles.

### L2 human

We check the theorem for $T \colon \R^2 \to \R^2$, $T(x, y) = (x + 2y,\ 3x + 4y)$, in the standard bases.

1. $[T] = A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. $T^*(e_1^*)$ is the form $(x, y) \mapsto x + 2y$, that is, $1 \cdot e_1^* + 2 \cdot e_2^*$.
3. $T^*(e_2^*)$ is the form $(x, y) \mapsto 3x + 4y$, that is, $3 \cdot e_1^* + 4 \cdot e_2^*$.
4. The columns of $[T^*]$ are $(1, 2)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $(3, 4)^{\mathsf{T}}$. So $[T^*] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = A^{\mathsf{T}}$.

The typical use is to compute dual maps with matrices, and to give the transpose a meaning that does not depend on coordinates.
A trap: the theorem needs dual bases; with an arbitrary basis of $W^*$, the matrix of $T^*$ is not $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.

### L3 human

The theorem says that the functor $V \mapsto V^*$, in coordinates, is the transpose of matrices.
The fact that the transpose reverses products is the contravariance of this functor.
In tensor notation, a linear map is a tensor $a^i{}_j$, and its dual map is the same tensor with the roles of the indices exchanged.
