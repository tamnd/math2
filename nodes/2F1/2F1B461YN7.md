## Example

A linear discrete [[dynamical system|BBZP1R872N]] is a rule $x_{k+1} = A x_k$ with a fixed $n \times n$ matrix $A$, so that $x_k = A^k x_0$.
When $A$ has an eigenbasis $v_1, \dots, v_n$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$, write $x_0 = c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_n v_n$.
Then $x_k = c_1 \lambda_1^k v_1 + \dots + c_n \lambda_n^k v_n$, by the formula for [[powers of a diagonalizable matrix|0TNR9BQ871]].
The eigenvalue of largest absolute value, the dominant eigenvalue, decides the long-term behavior.
We work out a predator-prey model and a Leslie population model, and then classify the origin.

**Part 1: a predator-prey model.**
Let $x_k = (P_k, Q_k)$, where $P_k$ counts predators in hundreds and $Q_k$ counts prey in thousands, in month $k$.
The model is
$$P_{k+1} = 0.5 P_k + 0.4 Q_k, \qquad Q_{k+1} = -0.15 P_k + 1.2 Q_k, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.4 \\ -0.15 & 1.2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Without prey, half of the predators die each month.
Without predators, the prey grow by $20\%$ each month.
The entry $-0.15$ is the effect of predation.

1. Characteristic polynomial: $\lambda^2 - 1.7 \lambda + 0.66 = (\lambda - 1.1)(\lambda - 0.6)$, since the trace is $1.7$ and the determinant is $0.6 + 0.06 = 0.66$.
2. For $\lambda_1 = 1.1$: $A - 1.1 I = \begin{pmatrix} -0.6 & 0.4 \\ -0.15 & 0.1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $v_1 = (2, 3)$ spans its null space, so $v_1$ is an [[eigenvector|0B5H4208Z9]].
3. For $\lambda_2 = 0.6$: $A - 0.6 I = \begin{pmatrix} -0.1 & 0.4 \\ -0.15 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}$, and $v_2 = (4, 1)$ spans its null space.
4. The two [[eigenvalues|0ZND7NQ970]] are distinct, so $A$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] by [[n distinct eigenvalues imply diagonalizable|0Y95Z7MT02]].
5. Start with $x_0 = (10, 20)$. Solve $c_1 (2, 3) + c_2 (4, 1) = (10, 20)$: this gives $c_1 = 7$ and $c_2 = -1$.
6. So $x_k = 7 (1.1)^k (2, 3) - (0.6)^k (4, 1)$.
7. Check $k = 1$: $A x_0 = (5 + 8, -1.5 + 24) = (13, 22.5)$, and the formula gives $(15.4 - 2.4, 23.1 - 0.6) = (13, 22.5)$.
8. As $k \to \infty$, $(0.6)^k \to 0$. So $x_k \approx 7 (1.1)^k (2, 3)$: both populations grow by about $10\%$ each month, in the ratio $2$ hundred predators to $3$ thousand prey.

**Part 2: a Leslie model with three age classes.**
A population of females has the age classes $0$, $1$ and $2$ years.
Each year, a female of class $1$ has $4$ daughters on average, and a female of class $2$ has $3$.
Half of class $0$ survives to class $1$, and a quarter of class $1$ survives to class $2$.
The [[Leslie matrix|CHAYZAWENA]] is
$$L = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 4 & 3 \\ 0.5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.25 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. Characteristic polynomial: $\det(\lambda I - L) = \lambda^3 - 2 \lambda - 0.375 = (\lambda - 1.5)(\lambda^2 + 1.5 \lambda + 0.25)$.
2. The eigenvalues are $1.5$ and $(-1.5 \pm \sqrt{1.25})/2 \approx -0.191, -1.309$. They are distinct, so $L$ is diagonalizable.
3. The dominant eigenvalue is $1.5$, since $1.309 < 1.5$.
4. Its eigenvector is $v_1 = (18, 6, 1)$. Check: $L v_1 = (4 \cdot 6 + 3 \cdot 1,\ 0.5 \cdot 18,\ 0.25 \cdot 6) = (27, 9, 1.5) = 1.5\, v_1$.
5. The row vector $w = (1, 3, 2)$ satisfies $w L = 1.5\, w$. The coefficient of $v_1$ in $x_0$ is $c_1 = w \cdot x_0 / w \cdot v_1 = w \cdot x_0 / 38$, because $w$ is orthogonal to the other two eigenvectors.
6. All entries of $w$ are positive. So $c_1 > 0$ for every nonzero starting population $x_0$ with nonnegative entries.
7. So $x_k / 1.5^k \to c_1 v_1$. The population grows by $50\%$ a year, and the age distribution tends to $v_1 / 25 = (0.72, 0.24, 0.04)$.
8. For $x_0 = (0, 10, 0)$, the age distribution is $(0.613, 0.357, 0.030)$ after $10$ years and $(0.714, 0.247, 0.039)$ after $30$ years. The approach is slow, because $1.309 / 1.5 \approx 0.873$ is close to $1$.

**Part 3: attractors, repellers and saddles.**
For a $2 \times 2$ matrix with two real eigenvalues, the origin is classified as follows.

1. If $\abs{\lambda_1} < 1$ and $\abs{\lambda_2} < 1$, then $x_k \to 0$ for every $x_0$, and the origin is an attractor. Example: $\operatorname{diag}(0.8, 0.5)$.
2. If $\abs{\lambda_1} > 1$ and $\abs{\lambda_2} > 1$, then $\norm{x_k} \to \infty$ for every $x_0 \ne 0$, and the origin is a repeller. Example: $\operatorname{diag}(2, 1.5)$.
3. If $\abs{\lambda_1} > 1 > \abs{\lambda_2}$, the origin is a saddle. Example: $\operatorname{diag}(2, 0.5)$ sends $(1, 1)$ to $(2^k, 0.5^k)$, which moves towards the first axis and away from the origin.
4. The predator-prey matrix of part 1 is a saddle, with $\lambda_1 = 1.1$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.6$. The orbits with $c_1 = 0$ go to $0$ along the line through $(4, 1)$. All other orbits go to infinity along the direction $(2, 3)$.

## Remarks

The example shows that one eigenvalue controls the long run, and its eigenvector gives the shape of the state.

The predation parameter changes the outcome.
Replace $-0.15$ by $-p$.
For $p = 0.25$, the eigenvalues are $1$ and $0.7$, and the populations level off at a multiple of the eigenvector $(4, 5)$.
For $p = 0.3$, the eigenvalues are $0.9$ and $0.8$, the origin is an attractor, and both populations die out.

Complex eigenvalues give rotation.
If $A$ has the eigenvalues $r e^{\pm i \theta}$, the orbits turn around the origin by the angle $\theta$ at each step, and they spiral in, spiral out or stay on an ellipse when $r < 1$, $r > 1$ or $r = 1$.

The positive eigenvector of the Leslie matrix comes from the [[Perron-Frobenius theorem|62NS7PV1M9]], because $L$ is nonnegative and irreducible.
A Markov chain is the special case of a stochastic matrix, with dominant eigenvalue $1$, in [[steady-state vectors of Markov chains|Y9FRWY8YX4]].
The continuous analogue $x' = A x$ is treated in [[linear systems of ODEs by eigenvectors|DH7CQXQYJX]].
Nonlinear models and their stability theory belong to dynamical systems.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Many quantities change from one year to the next by fixed rules, such as the numbers of animals in different groups.
If each new number is a fixed mixture of the old numbers, the rule is a matrix.
After many years, the numbers usually grow or shrink by the same factor each year, and their proportions stop changing.
In the age model here, the population grows by half each year, and in the end about $72\%$ of the females are young.

### L2 human

To find the long-term behavior of $x_{k+1} = A x_k$, do these steps.

1. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A$.
2. Write $x_0$ in the eigenbasis: $x_0 = c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_n v_n$.
3. Write $x_k = \sum_j c_j \lambda_j^k v_j$.
4. Keep the terms with the largest $\abs{\lambda_j}$ and $c_j \ne 0$.

The typical use is population ecology and economics, where one wants a growth rate and a stable distribution.
The trap is to forget $c_1$: if $c_1 = 0$, the dominant term is absent, and the orbit follows the next eigenvalue.
In the saddle of part 1, the start $x_0 = (4, 1)$ has $c_1 = 0$, and $x_k = 0.6^k (4, 1) \to 0$, although the other orbits grow.
A second trap: two eigenvalues of the same absolute value, such as $1$ and $-1$, give no single limit shape.

### L3 human

The general statement for nonnegative matrices is the Perron-Frobenius theorem: a primitive nonnegative matrix has a simple positive dominant eigenvalue with a positive eigenvector.
For matrices without an eigenbasis, the Jordan form gives terms $k^j \lambda^k$, and the spectral radius still decides whether $A^k \to 0$.
The dominant eigenvector is also what the power method computes, by iterating $x_{k+1} = A x_k / \norm{A x_k}$.
Linear systems are the linearizations of nonlinear maps at a fixed point, and the eigenvalues of the derivative there decide local stability in the hyperbolic case.
