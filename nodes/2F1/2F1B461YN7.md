## Example

An ecologist counts predators and prey each month, and a demographer counts a population by age each year.
In both cases next period's numbers are fixed mixtures of this period's numbers, so the rule is $x_{k+1} = A x_k$ with a fixed $n \times n$ matrix $A$.
This is a linear discrete [[dynamical system|BBZP1R872N]], and its states are $x_k = A^k x_0$.
When $A$ has an eigenbasis $v_1, \dots, v_n$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$, we write $x_0 = c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_n v_n$.
The formula for [[powers of a diagonalizable matrix|0TNR9BQ871]] then gives
$$x_k = c_1 \lambda_1^k v_1 + \dots + c_n \lambda_n^k v_n.$$
So the eigenvalue that is strictly largest in absolute value, the dominant eigenvalue, decides the long run.
We work out a predator-prey model and a Leslie age model, and then we classify the origin.

**Part 1: a predator-prey model.**
Let $x_k = (P_k, Q_k)$, where $P_k$ counts predators in hundreds and $Q_k$ counts prey in thousands, in month $k$.
The model is
$$P_{k+1} = 0.5 P_k + 0.4 Q_k, \qquad Q_{k+1} = -0.15 P_k + 1.2 Q_k, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.4 \\ -0.15 & 1.2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Without prey, half of the predators die each month, and without predators, the prey grow by $20\%$ each month.
The entry $-0.15$ is the effect of predation.

1. The trace of $A$ is $1.7$ and the determinant is $0.6 + 0.06 = 0.66$, so the characteristic polynomial is
$$\lambda^2 - 1.7 \lambda + 0.66 = (\lambda - 1.1)(\lambda - 0.6).$$
2. For $\lambda_1 = 1.1$ we get $A - 1.1 I = \begin{pmatrix} -0.6 & 0.4 \\ -0.15 & 0.1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $v_1 = (2, 3)$ spans its null space, so $v_1$ is an [[eigenvector|0B5H4208Z9]].
3. For $\lambda_2 = 0.6$ we get $A - 0.6 I = \begin{pmatrix} -0.1 & 0.4 \\ -0.15 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}$, and $v_2 = (4, 1)$ spans its null space.
4. The two [[eigenvalues|0ZND7NQ970]] are distinct, so $A$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] because [[n distinct eigenvalues imply diagonalizable|0Y95Z7MT02]].
5. Start with $x_0 = (10, 20)$.
   Solving $c_1 (2, 3) + c_2 (4, 1) = (10, 20)$ gives $c_1 = 7$ and $c_2 = -1$, so
$$x_k = 7 \cdot 1.1^k\, (2, 3) - 0.6^k\, (4, 1).$$
6. Check $k = 1$: $A x_0 = (5 + 8,\ -1.5 + 24) = (13, 22.5)$, and the formula gives $(15.4 - 2.4,\ 23.1 - 0.6) = (13, 22.5)$.
7. As $k \to \infty$, $0.6^k \to 0$, so $x_k \approx 7 \cdot 1.1^k\, (2, 3)$.
   Both populations grow by about $10\%$ a month, in the ratio of $2$ hundred predators to $3$ thousand prey.

**Part 2: a Leslie model with three age classes.**
A population of females has the age classes $0$, $1$ and $2$ years.
Each year a female of class $1$ has $4$ daughters on average, and a female of class $2$ has $3$.
Half of class $0$ survives to class $1$, and a quarter of class $1$ survives to class $2$.
The [[Leslie matrix|CHAYZAWENA]] is
$$L = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 4 & 3 \\ 0.5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.25 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. The characteristic polynomial is
$$\det(\lambda I - L) = \lambda^3 - 2 \lambda - 0.375 = (\lambda - 1.5)(\lambda^2 + 1.5 \lambda + 0.25).$$
2. The eigenvalues are $1.5$ and $(-1.5 \pm \sqrt{1.25})/2 \approx -0.191, -1.309$.
   They are distinct, so $L$ is diagonalizable, and $1.5$ is dominant because $1.309 < 1.5$.
3. An eigenvector for $1.5$ is $v_1 = (18, 6, 1)$.
   Check: $L v_1 = (24 + 3,\ 9,\ 1.5) = (27, 9, 1.5) = 1.5\, v_1$.
4. The row vector $w = (1, 3, 2)$ satisfies $w L = (1.5, 4.5, 3) = 1.5\, w$.
   A left eigenvector is orthogonal to the right eigenvectors of the other eigenvalues, so the coefficient of $v_1$ in $x_0$ is $c_1 = w \cdot x_0 / w \cdot v_1 = w \cdot x_0 / 38$.
5. All entries of $w$ are positive, so $c_1 > 0$ for every nonzero starting population with nonnegative entries.
6. Hence $x_k / 1.5^k \to c_1 v_1$.
   The population grows by $50\%$ a year, and the age distribution tends to $v_1 / 25 = (0.72, 0.24, 0.04)$.
7. For $x_0 = (0, 10, 0)$ the age distribution is about $(0.613, 0.356, 0.030)$ after $10$ years and $(0.714, 0.247, 0.039)$ after $30$ years.
   The approach is slow because $1.309 / 1.5 \approx 0.873$ is close to $1$.

**Part 3: attractors, repellers and saddles.**
For a $2 \times 2$ matrix with two real eigenvalues and an eigenbasis, the origin is of one of three kinds.

1. If $\abs{\lambda_1} < 1$ and $\abs{\lambda_2} < 1$, then $x_k \to 0$ for every $x_0$, and the origin is an attractor.
   An example is $\operatorname{diag}(0.8, 0.5)$.
2. If $\abs{\lambda_1} > 1$ and $\abs{\lambda_2} > 1$, then $\norm{x_k} \to \infty$ for every $x_0 \ne 0$, and the origin is a repeller.
   An example is $\operatorname{diag}(2, 1.5)$.
3. If $\abs{\lambda_1} > 1 > \abs{\lambda_2}$, the origin is a saddle.
   For example, $\operatorname{diag}(2, 0.5)$ sends $(1, 1)$ to $(2^k, 0.5^k)$, which moves towards the first axis and away from the origin.
4. The predator-prey matrix of part 1 is a saddle, with $\lambda_1 = 1.1$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.6$.
   The orbits with $c_1 = 0$ go to $0$ along the line through $(4, 1)$, and all other orbits go to infinity along the direction $(2, 3)$.

## Remarks

### What the example shows

One eigenvalue controls the long run, and its eigenvector gives the shape of the state.
In part 1 the shape is the ratio $2 : 3$, and in part 2 it is the stable age distribution $(0.72, 0.24, 0.04)$.
The other eigenvalues only decide how fast the state settles into that shape.

### Change the predation rate

Replace $-0.15$ in part 1 by $-p$.
The trace stays $1.7$, and the determinant becomes $0.6 + 0.4 p$.
For $p = 0.25$ the determinant is $0.7$, the eigenvalues are $1$ and $0.7$, and the populations level off at a multiple of the eigenvector $(4, 5)$.
For $p = 0.3$ the determinant is $0.72$, the eigenvalues are $0.9$ and $0.8$, the origin is an attractor, and both populations die out.
So a small change in one rate turns growth into extinction.

### Complex eigenvalues

If a real $2 \times 2$ matrix has the eigenvalues $r e^{\pm i \theta}$ with $\theta \ne 0$, it has no real eigenvector.
In a suitable real basis it acts as a rotation by $\theta$ followed by a scaling by $r$.
So the orbits turn around the origin, and they spiral in when $r < 1$, spiral out when $r > 1$, and stay on an ellipse when $r = 1$.
For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & -0.6 \\ 0.6 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix}$ has $r = 1$ and rotates every point on its circle.

### Watch out

The coefficient $c_1$ of the dominant eigenvector can be $0$.
In the saddle of part 1, the start $x_0 = (4, 1)$ has $c_1 = 0$, so $x_k = 0.6^k (4, 1) \to 0$, although almost every other start grows.
A second trap is two eigenvalues of the same absolute value, such as $1$ and $-1$ for $\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$.
Then no eigenvalue is strictly dominant, and the start $(1, 1)$ gives the orbit $(1, 1), (1, -1), (1, 1), \dots$, with no single limit shape.

### Where the theory lives

The positive eigenvector of the Leslie matrix comes from the [[Perron-Frobenius theorem|62NS7PV1M9]].
It applies because $L$ is nonnegative and primitive: its graph has cycles of lengths $2$ ($0 \to 1 \to 0$) and $3$ ($0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 0$), and $\gcd(2, 3) = 1$.
A Markov chain is the case of a stochastic matrix with dominant eigenvalue $1$, in [[steady-state vectors of Markov chains|Y9FRWY8YX4]].
The continuous analogue $x' = A x$ is in [[linear systems of ODEs by eigenvectors|DH7CQXQYJX]], and nonlinear models belong to the theory of dynamical systems.

### Try it

First, find the long-run growth rate of $x_{k+1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0.25 & 1 \end{pmatrix} x_k$.
The eigenvalues are $1 \pm \sqrt{0.25} = 1.5$ and $0.5$, so most orbits grow by $50\%$ each step.

Second, which start makes that system decay?
The vector $(2, -1)$ is an eigenvector for $0.5$, because $(A - 0.5 I)(2, -1) = (0.5 \cdot 2 - 1,\ 0.25 \cdot 2 - 0.5) = (0, 0)$.
So the starts on the line through $(2, -1)$ decay to $0$, and all other starts grow.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Many quantities change from one year to the next by fixed rules, such as the numbers of animals of different ages.
If each new number is a fixed mixture of the old numbers, the rule is a matrix.
After many years the numbers usually grow or shrink by the same factor each year, and their proportions stop changing.
In the age model here, the population grows by half each year, and in the end about $72\%$ of the females are in their first year.

### L2 human

Here is a typical question: a population follows $x_{k+1} = L x_k$, and you want its growth rate and its long-run age mix.
You find the eigenvalues of $L$, pick the one that is strictly largest in absolute value, and find its eigenvector.
For the Leslie matrix of part 2 this gave $1.5$ and $(18, 6, 1)$.
Then you make sure that the start has a nonzero component along that eigenvector, which the positive left eigenvector $w = (1, 3, 2)$ guaranteed.
The answer is that the population grows by $50\%$ a year with the age mix $(0.72, 0.24, 0.04)$, whatever the start.
The same steps give the growth rate and the stable shape in economics and in any model of the form $x_{k+1} = A x_k$.

### L3 human

The general statement for nonnegative matrices is the Perron-Frobenius theorem: a primitive nonnegative matrix has a simple positive eigenvalue that is strictly larger in absolute value than all other eigenvalues, with a positive eigenvector.
For matrices without an eigenbasis, the Jordan form gives terms $k^j \lambda^k$, and the spectral radius still decides whether $A^k \to 0$.
The dominant eigenvector is also what [[power iteration|H4MQD841BR]] computes, by repeating $x_{k+1} = A x_k / \norm{A x_k}$.
Linear systems are the linearizations of nonlinear maps at a fixed point, and in the hyperbolic case the eigenvalues of the derivative there decide local stability.
