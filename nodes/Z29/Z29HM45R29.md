## Summary

Some mathematical work aims to settle one hard question, and some aims to understand a whole area, and each kind keeps producing the other.
Timothy Gowers made this contrast well known in his essay "The two cultures of mathematics" (2000).
A *problem solver* works to settle specific questions and values a theory mainly as a tool for that purpose.
A *theory builder* works to understand the structure of an area and values a specific problem mainly for what its solution reveals.
The distinction is about priorities, not about two kinds of people: most mathematicians do both.
It explains why different fields value different kinds of results, and why both concrete problems and general theories drive the growth of mathematics.

## Motivation

Mathematicians do not all judge results in the same way.
In some fields a new theorem is valued for the general framework that it creates, even when it settles no old question.
In other fields a result is valued for the hard problem that it solves, even when its methods look special to that problem.
These two judgments can lead to real disagreement about which work matters.
The distinction gives words for the difference, and the history of almost every field shows the two kinds of work depending on each other.
It connects to the [[methodology of mathematics|3FJ3XMV9KD]], which asks how problems are chosen, and to [[abstraction and generalization|976RZZCT1H]], the typical move of theory building.

## Remarks

### A puzzle that started a field

In Königsberg, seven bridges joined four land masses, and the question was whether a walk could cross every bridge exactly once.
Euler answered it in 1736 with an argument about counting, not about the map.
A walk enters and leaves each land mass that it passes through, so it uses an even number of bridge ends there, and only the start and the end of the walk can break this rule.
So a walk is possible only if at most two land masses touch an odd number of bridges.
In Königsberg the island touched $5$ bridges and the other three land masses touched $3$ each, so all four counts were odd, and no walk exists.
Check: $(5 + 3 + 3 + 3)/2 = 7$ bridges.
The [[seven bridges of Königsberg|0YCW781Q9D]] are often taken as the start of [[graph theory|A167XK4YTF]], where the counts become the degrees of a graph.

### A problem that needed a new notion

Unique factorization into primes holds in the integers, but not in every number system of the same kind.
In the numbers $a + b\sqrt{-5}$ with integers $a$ and $b$, the number $6$ has two essentially different factorizations:
$$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5}).$$
Check: $(1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5}) = 1 - (-5) = 6$.
Ernst Kummer met this failure in the 1840s for the integers of cyclotomic fields, and he introduced "ideal numbers" to restore a form of unique factorization.
He used them to prove [[Fermat's Last Theorem|0Y5X3SDBCZ]] for all regular prime exponents.
Richard Dedekind later recast ideal numbers as ideals, which are now a basic notion of algebra far beyond the original problem.

### Theories that settle problems

The traffic also runs the other way.
The [[theory of Galois|WNQDKQYZ2R]] explains which polynomial equations can be solved by radicals, and the [[Abel-Ruffini theorem|163A2WQQJX]] on the general quintic becomes one case of a general criterion.
Alexander Grothendieck developed étale cohomology as part of a program to prove the [[Weil conjectures|81TXP32VDM]], and Pierre Deligne completed their proof in 1974 with this theory.
In such cases a problem stays open until a theory, often built for broader reasons, reaches it.

### What the distinction is not

It is not the distinction between pure and applied mathematics, since both cultures live inside pure mathematics.
It is not a ranking: Gowers argued against the view that problem solving is less deep than theory building.
And "problem solving" here means research problems whose solution needs new ideas, not routine exercises.

### How the two aims feed each other

Hard problems test theories, and a theory that settles an old open problem gains authority, as the modularity methods did when Andrew Wiles proved Fermat's Last Theorem in 1995.
Theories organize solutions, because when many problems fall to the same idea, the idea is made into a general method or theorem.
Gowers observed that in a problem-solving field such as combinatorics, knowledge is often stored as general principles, such as the probabilistic method, and not as large theorems.
A good problem is a [[conjecture|WMEMMHZ3BH]] or question that is precise, hard and connected to other questions, so that its solution changes more than itself.

### Watch out

The most common misreading is to take the distinction as a sorting of people, while Gowers presented it as a difference in what mathematicians value most.
A second misreading is that a theory with no application to an old problem has no value, although many theories, group theory among them, were developed for one purpose and later used in many others.
A third is that a solution by a special trick is a lesser solution, when a trick that works is often the first form of a later general method.

### History

David Hilbert presented a list of open problems at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris in 1900.
He discussed ten problems in the lecture, and the published version contains twenty-three.
The first problem is the [[continuum hypothesis|0MN2MER9WN]]: Kurt Gödel showed in 1940 that it is consistent with the axioms of set theory, and Paul Cohen showed in 1963 that its negation is consistent too.
The tenth problem asked for an algorithm that decides whether a Diophantine equation has an integer solution, and [[Hilbert's tenth problem|X8TVJSVTD4]] was settled in the negative in 1970.
The eighth problem contains the [[Riemann hypothesis|VCGF9YR36X]], which is still open.
Gowers published "The two cultures of mathematics" in the American Mathematical Society volume "Mathematics: Frontiers and Perspectives" in 2000.

### Try it

First, suppose one bridge between the island and a bank of Königsberg is removed; is a walk over every remaining bridge exactly once now possible?
The island now touches $4$ bridges and that bank touches $2$, so only two land masses have odd counts, and a walk between those two exists.

Second, why does the factorization $6 = 2 \cdot 3$ not settle unique factorization in the numbers $a + b\sqrt{-5}$?
Because $6 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$ is a second factorization into numbers that cannot be split further, and neither $2$ nor $3$ is a unit multiple of $1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some mathematicians like to attack one hard puzzle, such as "can a walk cross each of these seven bridges exactly once?".
Others like to build a general picture that explains many puzzles at once.
Euler answered the bridge puzzle, and his answer grew into the theory of networks, which is now called graph theory.
In the other direction, a general theory of equations explained at once why some old puzzles about equations have no solution.
Both kinds of work are needed, and most mathematicians do some of each.

### L2 human

We follow one problem through both kinds of work: Fermat's Last Theorem, which says that $x^n + y^n = z^n$ has no solution in positive integers for $n \ge 3$.

1. Fermat gave a proof for $n = 4$ by infinite descent.
   The method works only for that exponent, so it settles one case by a special argument.
2. Kummer studied the equation for a prime exponent $p$ by factoring $x^p + y^p$ over the integers of the cyclotomic field.
   Unique factorization fails there, and his ideal numbers led to ideal theory and to a proof for all regular primes.
3. Ideal theory grew into algebraic number theory, far beyond the needs of the original problem.
4. In the twentieth century the theory of elliptic curves and modular forms grew as a theory for its own sake.
5. Work of Gerhard Frey, Jean-Pierre Serre and Kenneth Ribet showed that a solution of Fermat's equation would give an elliptic curve that is not modular.
6. Wiles, with a step completed jointly with Richard Taylor, proved enough of the [[modularity theorem|CS1NVJBZMH]] in 1995 to rule out such a curve.

The lesson is that a hard problem can stay open until a theory built for other reasons reaches it.
Watch out: the last step is not the whole story, since the final proof used the work of many theory builders and was also a feat of problem solving.

### L3 human

Gowers argued that the two cultures differ in how they store knowledge.
In a theory-building field, knowledge sits in definitions and large theorems, and a newcomer learns the theory first.
In a problem-solving field such as combinatorics, much knowledge sits in principles too flexible to state as single theorems, such as the probabilistic method, and a newcomer learns them by solving problems.
So a field can look loosely organized from outside while it has a dense structure of methods inside.

The relation between the cultures returns in later parts of the area: the heuristics of discovery are the typical tools of problem solving, and abstraction is the typical tool of theory building.
Competitions train the problem-solving side in a pure form, and [[problem solving and competitions|2HNEG88WT8]] treats that side as a subject of its own.
