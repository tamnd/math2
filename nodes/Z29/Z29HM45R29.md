## Summary

"Problem solvers and theory builders" names a distinction between two aims of mathematical research, made well known by Timothy Gowers in his essay "The two cultures of mathematics" (2000).
A problem solver works to settle specific questions, and values a theory mainly as a tool for that purpose.
A theory builder works to understand a whole area, and values a specific problem mainly for what its solution reveals about the structure of the area.
The distinction is about priorities, not about two kinds of people: most mathematicians do both, and the two aims feed each other.
It matters because it explains why different fields value different kinds of results, and why both concrete problems and general theories drive the growth of mathematics.

## Motivation

Mathematicians judge results in different ways.
In some fields a new theorem is valued for the general framework that it creates, even when it settles no old question.
In other fields a result is valued for the hard problem that it solves, even when its methods look special to that problem.
These two judgements can lead to disagreement about which work is important.
The distinction between problem solvers and theory builders gives words for this difference, and it shows how the two kinds of work depend on each other.

## Remarks

### Examples

- A problem that produced a theory. In the 1840s Ernst Kummer introduced "ideal numbers" in his work on [[Fermat's Last Theorem|0Y5X3SDBCZ]]. Richard Dedekind later recast them as ideals, which are now a basic notion of algebra.
- A problem that started a field. Leonhard Euler's solution of the problem of the [[seven bridges of Königsberg|0YCW781Q9D]] in 1736 is often seen as the start of [[graph theory|A167XK4YTF]].
- A theory that solved problems. The [[theory of Galois|WNQDKQYZ2R]] explains which polynomial equations can be solved by radicals. The [[Abel-Ruffini theorem|163A2WQQJX]] on the general quintic becomes one case of a general criterion.
- A theory built for a problem. Alexander Grothendieck developed étale cohomology as part of a program to prove the [[Weil conjectures|81TXP32VDM]]. Pierre Deligne completed their proof in 1974 with this theory.
- A list of problems that steered research. David Hilbert's problems of 1900 guided much of the mathematics of the twentieth century, as the History section describes.

### Non-examples

- The distinction is not the distinction between pure and applied mathematics. A theory builder and a problem solver can both work in pure mathematics.
- The distinction is not a ranking. Gowers argued against the view that problem solving is less deep than theory building.
- A routine exercise is not "problem solving" in this sense. The term refers to research problems, whose solution needs new ideas.

### Basic properties

- Hard problems test theories. A theory that settles an old open problem gains authority, as the modularity methods did with the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem by Andrew Wiles in 1995.
- Theories organize solutions. When many problems are solved by the same idea, the idea is often made into a general method or a general theorem.
- In fields with a problem-solving culture, knowledge is often stored as general principles and methods, not as large theorems. Gowers gave combinatorics as the main example.
- A good problem is a [[conjecture|WMEMMHZ3BH]] or a question that is precise, hard, and connected to other questions. Its solution then changes more than the problem itself.

### History

David Hilbert presented a list of open problems at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris in 1900.
He discussed ten problems in the lecture, and the published version contains twenty-three.
The first problem is the [[continuum hypothesis|0MN2MER9WN]].
Kurt Gödel showed in 1940 that it is consistent with the axioms of set theory, and Paul Cohen showed in 1963 that its negation is consistent too.
The tenth problem asked for an algorithm that decides whether a Diophantine equation has an integer solution, and [[Hilbert's tenth problem|X8TVJSVTD4]] was settled in the negative in 1970.
The eighth problem contains the [[Riemann hypothesis|VCGF9YR36X]], which is still open as of 2026-11.
Timothy Gowers published "The two cultures of mathematics" in the volume "Mathematics: Frontiers and Perspectives" of the American Mathematical Society in 2000.

### Common errors

- Readers take the distinction as a classification of people. Gowers presented it as a difference in what mathematicians value most, and most research mixes both aims.
- Readers think that a theory without an application to an old problem has no value. Many theories, such as group theory, were first developed for one purpose and later used in many others.
- Readers think that a solution by a special trick is a lesser solution. A special trick that works is often the first form of a later general method.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some mathematicians like to attack one hard puzzle, such as "can a walk cross each of these seven bridges exactly once?".
Others like to build a general picture that explains many puzzles at once.
Euler answered the bridge puzzle, and his answer grew into the theory of networks, which is now called graph theory.
In the other direction, a general theory of equations explained at once why some old puzzles about equations have no solution.
Both kinds of work are needed, and most mathematicians do some of each.

### L2 human

We follow one problem through the two kinds of work: Fermat's Last Theorem, "$x^n + y^n = z^n$ has no solution in positive integers for $n \geq 3$".

1. Fermat himself gave the idea of a proof for $n = 4$. The method works only for that exponent, so it is a solution of one case by a special argument.
2. Kummer tried to factor $x^p + y^p$ over the integers of the cyclotomic field. Unique factorization fails there, so he introduced ideal numbers, which led to ideal theory.
3. Ideal theory became part of algebraic number theory, which was developed far beyond the needs of the original problem.
4. In the twentieth century the theory of elliptic curves and modular forms grew as a theory for its own sake.
5. Work of Gerhard Frey, Jean-Pierre Serre and Kenneth Ribet linked Fermat's equation to the modularity of elliptic curves.
6. Wiles, with a step completed with Richard Taylor, proved enough of the [[modularity theorem|CS1NVJBZMH]] in 1995 to settle the problem.

The typical lesson is that a hard problem can stay open until a theory that was built for other reasons reaches it.
A trap is to see only the last step.
The final proof used the work of many theory builders, and it was also an achievement of problem solving.

### L3 human

Gowers argued that the two cultures differ in how they store knowledge.
In a theory-building field, knowledge is stored in definitions and large theorems, and a newcomer learns the theory first.
In a problem-solving field such as combinatorics, much knowledge is stored in principles that are too flexible to state as single theorems, such as the probabilistic method, and a newcomer learns them by solving problems.
So a field can look less organized from outside while it has a dense structure of methods inside.

The [[methodology of mathematics|3FJ3XMV9KD]] studies how mathematicians choose problems and theories.
The relation between the two cultures shows up in the later sub-areas: [[abstraction and generalization|976RZZCT1H]] is the typical move of theory building, and the heuristics of discovery are the typical tools of problem solving.
Competitions train the problem-solving side in a pure form, and the topic [[problem solving and competitions|2HNEG88WT8]] treats that side as a subject of its own.
