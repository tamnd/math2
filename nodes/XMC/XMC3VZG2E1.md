## Definition

Let $Q$ be a [[risk-neutral measure|858BHBY2BD]] and let $X$ be the discounted payoff of a derivative, a random variable with $E_Q[X^2] < \infty$.
Its price is the [[expected value|0WQNRCNY3M]] $V = E_Q[X]$.
Let $X_1, \dots, X_N$ be independent samples of $X$ under $Q$, generated by simulation.
The *Monte Carlo estimate* of $V$ is the sample mean
$$\hat V_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i.$$

## Remarks

By the [[law of large numbers|0ZVEAYVRC3]], $\hat V_N \to V$ almost surely as $N \to \infty$.
By the [[central limit theorem|0EPZC4Z1FC]], the error $\hat V_N - V$ is approximately normal with standard deviation $s / \sqrt{N}$, where $s^2$ is the variance of $X$.
Example: in the [[Black–Scholes model|FFT8TF6QP2]] take $X = e^{-rT} \max(S_T - K, 0)$ with $S_T = S_0 \exp((r - \sigma^2/2) T + \sigma \sqrt{T} Z)$ and $Z$ standard normal.
Sampling $Z$ gives samples of $X$, and $\hat V_N$ converges to the Black–Scholes call price.
Non-example: an American option is not priced by this plain average, since its value is a supremum over stopping times.
Regression methods, such as that of Longstaff and Schwartz, are needed.
Variance reduction, such as antithetic variates and control variates, lowers $s$ while keeping the estimator unbiased.
