## Example

Fix a core radius $a > 0$ and a circulation $\Gamma > 0$.
In plane polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, the *Rankine vortex* is the planar flow with zero radial velocity and the following azimuthal velocity.
$$u_\theta(r) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\Gamma r}{2 \pi a^2}, & r \le a, \\[2mm] \dfrac{\Gamma}{2 \pi r}, & r > a. \end{cases}$$
The [[vorticity|B3SXT9TWFA]] of a circular flow is $\zeta = \frac{1}{r} \frac{d}{dr}(r u_\theta)$.
Inside the core, $\zeta = \frac{1}{r} \cdot \frac{2 \Gamma r}{2 \pi a^2} = \Gamma / (\pi a^2)$, a rigid rotation.
Outside, $r u_\theta = \Gamma / (2\pi)$ is constant, so $\zeta = 0$: the flow is irrotational there.
The [[circulation|BTHH4Y71QR]] around any circle of radius $r \ge a$ is $2 \pi r \, u_\theta(r) = \Gamma$, which is the total vorticity $\pi a^2 \cdot \Gamma / (\pi a^2)$ in the core.
With the pressure $p(r)$ given by $dp/dr = \rho \, u_\theta^2 / r$, the flow is a steady solution of the [[Euler equations|RW0JK9VDZE]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Rankine vortex is the simplest model of a whirlpool or the core of a tornado.
In the middle, the fluid turns like a solid disc, with speed growing in proportion to the distance from the centre.
Outside, the speed falls off like $1/r$, as for a point vortex, and the fluid there does not spin locally even though it goes around the centre.
The fastest speed is at the edge of the core, $r = a$.
