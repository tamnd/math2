## Summary

Ann and Bob do not know whether the meeting is at noon.
Their boss says in front of both: "The meeting is at noon."
Now both know it, both know that the other knows it, and so on: one sentence has made the fact [[common knowledge|7BCMKF2P7X]].
Dynamic epistemic logic is the extension of [[epistemic logic|EA34W4ERRE]] that describes such changes of knowledge inside the logic.

The simplest event is a truthful public announcement.
The formula $[!\varphi] \psi$ says "after the public announcement of $\varphi$, $\psi$ holds", and its clause deletes the worlds where $\varphi$ is false:
$$M, w \models [!\varphi] \psi \iff (M, w \models \varphi \implies M|\varphi, w \models \psi).$$
Here $M|\varphi$ keeps only the worlds where $\varphi$ is true, with the agents' relations restricted to them.
Action models, due to Baltag, Moss and Solecki, describe events that different agents perceive differently, and they update a model by a product.

## Motivation

Epistemic logic describes a snapshot of what agents know.
Puzzles like the [[muddy children|BG9TYCJ7HP]] turn on how knowledge changes, since each answer, even "no", tells the others something.
We want to reason about the effect of such events inside the logic, as [[propositional dynamic logic|VJ78A0Q22R]] reasons about the effect of programs.
Possible worlds give a direct model of information: learning $\varphi$ means ruling out the worlds where $\varphi$ is false.

Real communication is often not public.
A letter read by one person while another watches, or a message that may have been intercepted, changes knowledge differently for different agents.
Action models handle these cases, and they are now used in the analysis of security protocols, card games and epistemic planning.

## Notation

We write $[!\varphi] \psi$ for "after announcing $\varphi$, $\psi$", and $\langle !\varphi \rangle \psi = \neg [!\varphi] \neg \psi$ for "$\varphi$ is true and $\psi$ holds after its announcement".
Some books write $[\varphi] \psi$ without the exclamation mark.
$\mathsf{PAL}$ is public announcement logic, and $\mathsf{PAC}$ is $\mathsf{PAL}$ with common knowledge.
An action model is $\mathsf{E} = (E, (\to_a)_a, \mathrm{pre})$, with a set $E$ of events, a relation $\to_a$ for each agent, and a precondition $\mathrm{pre}(e)$ for each event.
The product update is $M \otimes \mathsf{E}$, and $[\mathsf{E}, e] \psi$ says "after event $e$, $\psi$".

## Remarks

### A first update

Let $W = \{w, v\}$ with $p$ true at $w$ only, and let both $a$ and $b$ be unable to tell $w$ from $v$.
At $w$, $K_a p$ is false, because $a$ considers $v$ possible.
Announcing $p$ at $w$ is truthful, so we pass to $M|p$, which has the single world $w$.
In $M|p$ both agents know $p$, and since only $w$ is left, $C_{\{a, b\}} p$ holds as well.
$$M, w \models [!p] (K_a p \wedge K_b p \wedge C_{\{a, b\}} p).$$
At $v$ the announcement cannot be made, so every formula $[!p] \psi$ is true there.
[[The announcement example|YDAHGYKQZN]] does the same on a larger model where some links survive.

### Reduction axioms

Plaza found that announcements can always be pushed inward and removed, by [[the reduction theorem|MFGHDHGV1D]].
The axioms for atoms, negation, conjunction and knowledge are these.

- $[!\varphi] p \leftrightarrow (\varphi \to p)$
- $[!\varphi] \neg \psi \leftrightarrow (\varphi \to \neg [!\varphi] \psi)$
- $[!\varphi] (\psi \wedge \chi) \leftrightarrow ([!\varphi] \psi \wedge [!\varphi] \chi)$
- $[!\varphi] K_a \psi \leftrightarrow (\varphi \to K_a [!\varphi] \psi)$

So every $\mathsf{PAL}$ formula is equivalent to an [[S5|VC3A2VWXND]]$_n$ formula without announcements, and $\mathsf{PAL}$ is complete and decidable.
[[The translation example|JRHH7Z42A4]] runs the rewriting by hand.
With common knowledge the reduction fails: some formulas $[!\varphi] C_G \psi$ have no equivalent in epistemic logic with $C_G$, and one adds relativized common knowledge to restore it.

### Private events and action models

A private message to agent $a$ is not a public announcement.
Deleting worlds would tell every agent the content, while in fact only $a$ learns it.
An action model lists the possible events, which agent can tell which events apart, and when each event can happen.
The product $M \otimes \mathsf{E}$ has a world $(w, e)$ for each world $w$ where $\mathrm{pre}(e)$ holds, and $(w, e)$ is linked to $(v, f)$ for $a$ when $w R_a v$ and $e \to_a f$.
[[The letter example|SYVGPVYNCP]] works this out for a letter read by one agent in front of another.

### What it is not

An announced formula need not stay true.
Announcing the Moore sentence $p \wedge \neg K_a p$ makes it false, as [[the unsuccessful update|Z13C7EBPXB]] shows.
A change of facts, such as flipping a coin, is not an announcement either, because announcements never change the valuation; ontic change needs postconditions in action models.

### Watch out

The most common slip is to write $[!\varphi] K_a \psi \leftrightarrow K_a [!\varphi] \psi$.
Take a world $w$ where $\varphi$ is false and $a$ sees a world where $\varphi$ is true and $\psi$ is false after the update.
Then $[!\varphi] K_a \psi$ is true at $w$ vacuously, but $K_a [!\varphi] \psi$ is false, so the antecedent $\varphi \to$ is needed.

A second slip is to update agent by agent.
The model $M|\varphi$ is computed once, from the truth of $\varphi$ in the whole model, and every agent's relation is then restricted to the same set of worlds.

### History

Plaza introduced public announcement logic, with the reduction axioms, in "Logics of public communications" (1989).
Gerbrandy and Groeneveld treated announcements independently in 1997.
Baltag, Moss and Solecki introduced action models in 1998.
Bolander and Andersen proved in 2011 that multi-agent epistemic planning with action models is undecidable.
The textbook of van Ditmarsch, van der Hoek and Kooi (2007) collected the field.

### Try it

First, in the first update, is $[!\neg K_a p] K_a p$ true at $w$?
The formula $\neg K_a p$ is true at both worlds, so the announcement deletes nothing, and $K_a p$ stays false: the answer is no.

Second, simplify $[!p] K_a q$ with the reduction axioms.
The knowledge axiom gives $p \to K_a [!p] q$, and the atom axiom turns $[!p] q$ into $p \to q$, so the result is $p \to K_a (p \to q)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Dynamic epistemic logic is about how knowledge changes when something is said or done.
Think of all the ways the world might be.
When someone announces a fact to everyone, the ways in which it is false drop out.
After that, everyone knows the fact, and everyone knows that everyone knows it.
Secret messages are harder, because one person learns something while the others learn only that something happened.

### L2 human

Here is a typical problem: three cards $0$, $1$, $2$ are dealt, one each to Ann and Bob, and one stays face down.
Ann holds $0$ and announces "I do not hold $2$".
The worlds are the six deals $(x, y)$ with $x \ne y$, where $x$ is Ann's card and $y$ is Bob's.
The announcement deletes $(2, 0)$ and $(2, 1)$, and leaves four deals.
Bob holds $1$, so he now considers only $(0, 1)$, since $(2, 1)$ is gone, and he knows Ann's card.
Ann still considers $(0, 1)$ and $(0, 2)$, so she does not know Bob's card.
The typical use is to compute what agents know after a sequence of announcements by deleting worlds, one announcement at a time.

### L3 human

$\mathsf{PAL}$ is a logic of model transformers, close to [[PDL|VJ78A0Q22R]], with announcements as programs and the reduction axioms as weakest preconditions.
Its reduction axioms show that it has the expressive power of epistemic logic, but Lutz showed in 2006 that it can be exponentially more succinct.
In the same work he showed that $\mathsf{PAL}$ satisfiability is PSPACE-complete for two or more agents, as for $\mathsf{S5}_n$ in [[the complexity theorem|1P2B0C7CQ4]].
Plausibility models extend the method from knowledge to belief, and they connect dynamic epistemic logic with [[AGM belief revision|Y05YQN7HGQ]].
