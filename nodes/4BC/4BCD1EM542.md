## Summary

The no-counterexample interpretation, or n.c.i., is Kreisel's way to read a classical theorem in [[prenex form|CS82V969DE]] as a statement about computable functionals.
For a sentence $A = \exists x\, \forall y\, \varphi(x, y)$ with $\varphi$ quantifier-free, it reads $A$ as $\forall f\, \exists x\, \varphi(x, f(x))$.
The function $f$ is a proposed counterexample: it claims that $\varphi(x, f(x))$ fails for every $x$.
The interpretation asks for a functional $X$ with $\varphi(X(f), f(X(f)))$ for every $f$: a refutation of every proposed counterexample.
Kreisel proved that every theorem of [[Peano arithmetic|0QFT7Z76N5]] has such functionals, and that they can be chosen $\varepsilon_0$-recursive.
The idea matters because it gives computational content to every arithmetic theorem, even when the theorem itself has no computable witness.

## Motivation

A classical proof of $\exists x\, \forall y\, \varphi(x, y)$ often gives no way to compute $x$.
The least value of a function $g$ is an example: it exists, but it cannot be computed from finitely many values of $g$.
Still, the proof says something finite: for each finite set of tests $y$, some $x$ passes them.
The n.c.i. makes this precise.
A counterexample function $f$ assigns to each candidate $x$ a test $f(x)$ that $x$ is claimed to fail.
A finite search through candidates, guided by $f$, then finds an $x$ that passes its test.
So the classical theorem becomes a statement about computable search procedures, which is the goal of Hilbert's program and of Kreisel's unwinding of proofs.

## Notation

Let $A = \exists x_1\, \forall y_1\, \cdots \exists x_n\, \forall y_n\, \varphi(x_1, y_1, \ldots, x_n, y_n)$ with $\varphi$ quantifier-free; empty blocks are allowed.
Its no-counterexample form, also called its Herbrand normal form, is
$$A^H = \forall f_1 \cdots \forall f_n\, \exists x_1 \cdots \exists x_n\, \varphi(x_1, f_1(x_1), x_2, f_2(x_1, x_2), \ldots, x_n, f_n(x_1, \ldots, x_n)).$$
A tuple of functionals $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ *satisfies the n.c.i.* of $A$ when $\varphi(X_1, f_1(X_1), \ldots, X_n, f_n(X_1, \ldots, X_n))$ holds for all $f_1, \ldots, f_n$, where $X_i = X_i(f_1, \ldots, f_n)$.
Classically $A$ and $A^H$ are equivalent: $\neg A$ is $\forall x_1\, \exists y_1 \cdots \neg \varphi$, and its [[Skolem functions|GCPQGMJYEV]] $f_i$ are counterexample functions.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The minimum of a function|EMMGBY1KYH]]: $\exists n\, \forall m\, (g(n) \le g(m))$ has no computable witness, but its n.c.i. is satisfied by a search of at most $g(0) + 1$ steps.
- [[The drinker formula|MXRRV8J67S]]: $\exists x\, \forall y\, (P(x) \to P(y))$ has the n.c.i. witness $X(f) = 0$ if $P(f(0))$ holds and $X(f) = f(0)$ otherwise.
- The Cauchy property of a sequence, $\forall \varepsilon\, \exists n\, \forall i, j \ge n\, (|a_i - a_j| \le \varepsilon)$, has as its n.c.i. a form of [[metastability|8WH2SKSAZD]].

### Non-examples

- [[A Skolem function for the halting formula|7RFM1XPJQV]]: a witness of $\forall x\, \exists y\, \forall z\, \psi$ that depends on $x$ alone is not an n.c.i. functional, and here none is computable.
- The constant functional $X(f) = 0$ for the minimum principle of $g(m) = |m - 5|$. The condition that fails: for $f(n) = 5$, $g(0) = 5 > 0 = g(f(0))$.

### Basic properties

- For pure first-order logic, [[Herbrand's theorem|VZ21F10WRS]] gives n.c.i. functionals of a special form: finitely many candidate terms built from $f$, and a case distinction among them.
- For PA, Kreisel's theorem gives functionals defined by recursion on well-orderings of order type less than [[epsilon nought|KT50SMH3EC]]. The bound $\varepsilon_0$ cannot be lowered for all theorems of PA.
- The n.c.i. of $A$ is the [[Dialectica interpretation|HZ17ES9PDY]] of the [[negative translation|HGJVM49PA4]] of $A$, for prenex $A$. For $A = \exists x\, \forall y\, \varphi$ this gives $\exists X\, \forall f\, \varphi(X f, f(X f))$, with $X$ a term of [[system T|A4WR995XZP]].

### History

Kreisel introduced the no-counterexample interpretation in 1951, in his work on the interpretation of non-finitist proofs.
He obtained the $\varepsilon_0$-recursive functionals from Ackermann's 1940 consistency proof of arithmetic by the epsilon substitution method.
Kreisel's program of unwinding proofs, and later Kohlenbach's [[proof mining|XR1JXQG1CD]], used the interpretation to extract bounds.

### Common errors

- To read $A^H$ as giving a witness for $A$. It gives a witness only relative to the counterexample function, and the actual witness is in general not computable.
- To forget the dependence of $f_2$ on $x_1$. With $f_2(x_2)$ in place of $f_2(x_1, x_2)$, the form is no longer classically equivalent to $A$.
- To take the n.c.i. of a formula that is not prenex. First move the quantifiers out in classical logic.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some theorems say "there is a number with a property", but no program finds the number.
The no-counterexample interpretation offers a weaker game that a program can win.
An opponent claims: "every number you name fails, and here is the test that shows it."
A program wins if it names a number that passes the test the opponent chose for it.
For example, for "the function $g$ has a smallest value", the opponent maps each $n$ to some $m$ with $g(m) < g(n)$.
The program starts at $0$, follows the opponent's tests downward, and must stop after at most $g(0)$ steps, at a number whose test fails.

### L2 human

We compute the no-counterexample form of $A = \exists x\, \forall y\, \exists u\, \forall v\, \varphi(x, y, u, v)$.

1. The negation is $\forall x\, \exists y\, \forall u\, \exists v\, \neg \varphi(x, y, u, v)$.
2. Its Skolem form is $\exists f\, \exists g\, \forall x\, \forall u\, \neg \varphi(x, f(x), u, g(x, u))$.
3. Negating again, $A$ is classically equivalent to $\forall f\, \forall g\, \exists x\, \exists u\, \varphi(x, f(x), u, g(x, u))$.
4. So the n.c.i. asks for functionals $X(f, g)$ and $U(f, g)$ with $\varphi(X, f(X), U, g(X, U))$.

The typical use is to give finite content to a theorem with a $\forall \exists \forall$ form, such as the convergence of a sequence.
A trap is step 2: $g$ depends on both $x$ and $u$, and the form is wrong without this dependence.

### L3 human

The n.c.i. is the Herbrand form of a theorem, and Kreisel's theorem is a version of Herbrand's theorem for PA in which the finite disjunction is replaced by a transfinite search.
It is the Dialectica interpretation after the negative translation, restricted to prenex formulas, and it comes with a weaker treatment of modus ponens.
In terms of games, it is the Gödel interpretation of a two-player game in which the opponent commits in advance to a counterexample strategy.
In analysis it becomes metastability, the form in which Tao and later work in [[proof mining|XR1JXQG1CD]] state finite convergence results.
