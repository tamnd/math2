## Summary

The no-counterexample interpretation, or n.c.i., is Kreisel's way to get a computable answer out of every classical theorem of arithmetic, even when the theorem names an object that no program can find.
For a theorem $\forall x\, \exists y\, \varphi(x, y)$ the answer is a bound or a witness for $y$.
Euclid's proof that every $n$ has a larger prime gives one: the least prime factor of $n! + 1$ is a prime above $n$, so some prime lies in the interval $(n, n! + 1]$.
For $n = 3$ the bound is $3! + 1 = 7$, and indeed $5$ is a prime with $3 < 5 \le 7$.

A theorem $\exists x\, \forall y\, \varphi(x, y)$ is harder, because a computable $x$ may not exist.
Kreisel's reading replaces it by a game against a counterexample function $f$, which claims that each candidate $x$ fails the test $f(x)$:
$$\forall f\, \exists x\, \varphi(x, f(x)).$$
A functional $X$ *satisfies the n.c.i.* when $\varphi(X(f), f(X(f)))$ holds for every $f$, so that $X$ refutes every proposed counterexample.
Kreisel proved that every theorem of [[Peano arithmetic|0QFT7Z76N5]] in [[prenex form|CS82V969DE]] has such functionals, and that they can be chosen $\alpha$-recursive for some $\alpha < \varepsilon_0$.

## Motivation

A classical proof of $\exists x\, \forall y\, \varphi(x, y)$ often gives no way to compute $x$.
The place where a function $g$ takes its least value is an example: it exists, but no finite set of values of $g$ determines it.
Still the proof says something finite: for each finite set of tests $y$, some $x$ passes them.
The n.c.i. makes this precise, because a counterexample function $f$ proposes the tests and a finite search guided by $f$ finds an $x$ that passes its test.

This turned Hilbert's question about the meaning of classical proofs into a question about computable search procedures.
Kreisel called the extraction of such procedures the unwinding of proofs, and the method returns in Kohlenbach's [[proof mining|XR1JXQG1CD]].
In analysis the n.c.i. of the Cauchy property is [[metastability|8WH2SKSAZD]], the form in which finite convergence results are now stated.

## Notation

Let $A = \exists x_1\, \forall y_1\, \cdots \exists x_n\, \forall y_n\, \varphi(x_1, y_1, \ldots, x_n, y_n)$ with $\varphi$ quantifier-free, where empty blocks are allowed.
Its no-counterexample form, also called its Herbrand normal form, is the following formula:
$$A^H = \forall f_1 \cdots \forall f_n\, \exists x_1 \cdots \exists x_n\, \varphi(x_1, f_1(x_1), x_2, f_2(x_1, x_2), \ldots, x_n, f_n(x_1, \ldots, x_n)).$$
Each $f_i$ sees all the $x_j$ with $j \le i$, and the functionals $X_i(f_1, \ldots, f_n)$ must satisfy the matrix for every choice of the $f_i$.
Classically $A$ and $A^H$ are equivalent, because $\neg A$ is $\forall x_1\, \exists y_1 \cdots \neg \varphi$, and its [[Skolem functions|GCPQGMJYEV]] are exactly counterexample functions.

## Remarks

### A first refutation

Take the minimum principle $\exists n\, \forall m\, (g(n) \le g(m))$ for a function $g$ on the natural numbers.
Its n.c.i. asks for $X(f)$ with $g(X) \le g(f(X))$.
Follow $f$ from $0$: the sequence $0, f(0), f(f(0)), \ldots$ must stop going down in $g$ within $g(0)$ steps, because values of $g$ are natural numbers.
So $X(f)$ is the first point of the sequence with $g(n) \le g(f(n))$, found by at most $g(0) + 1$ evaluations.
The [[minimum principle example|EMMGBY1KYH]] checks this and runs it on numbers.

### Two more cases

The [[drinker formula|MXRRV8J67S]] $\exists x\, \forall y\, (P(x) \to P(y))$ has the witness $X(f) = 0$ if $P(f(0))$ holds and $X(f) = f(0)$ otherwise.
The two candidates $0$ and $f(0)$ form a Herbrand disjunction, the shape that pure logic always gives.

For a sequence $(a_i)$, the Cauchy property $\forall \varepsilon\, \exists n\, \forall i, j \ge n\, (|a_i - a_j| \le \varepsilon)$ has an n.c.i. that is equivalent to $\forall \varepsilon\, \forall F\, \exists n\, \forall i, j \in [n, F(n)]\, (|a_i - a_j| \le \varepsilon)$.
This is the [[metastability|8WH2SKSAZD]] of the sequence, and for monotone bounded sequences the search for $n$ has an explicit bound.

### What it is not

A Skolem function that gives $y$ from $x$ alone is not an n.c.i. functional.
For the [[halting formula|7RFM1XPJQV]] $\forall x\, \exists y\, \forall z\, (T(x, x, y) \vee \neg T(x, x, z))$ no Skolem function is computable, while the n.c.i. functional $Y(x, f)$ needs one evaluation of $f$.

A functional that ignores $f$ fails as soon as the theorem has no computable witness.
For the minimum principle of $g(m) = |m - 5|$ the constant $X(f) = 0$ fails against $f(n) = 5$, because $g(0) = 5 > 0 = g(f(0))$.

### What Herbrand and Kreisel give

For pure first-order logic, [[Herbrand's theorem|VZ21F10WRS]] gives n.c.i. functionals of a special form: finitely many candidate terms built from $f$, and a case distinction among them.
For PA, Kreisel's theorem gives functionals defined by recursion along well-orderings of order type less than [[epsilon nought|KT50SMH3EC]], and no smaller bound works for all theorems of PA.
For a sentence $\exists x\, \forall y\, \varphi$ the n.c.i. agrees with the [[Dialectica interpretation|HZ17ES9PDY]] of its [[negative translation|HGJVM49PA4]].
That interpretation is $\exists X\, \forall f\, \neg \neg \varphi(X f, f(X f))$, and it gives $X$ as a term of [[system T|A4WR995XZP]].

### Watch out

The first slip is to read $A^H$ as giving a witness for $A$.
It gives an answer only relative to the counterexample function: for the minimum principle and $f(n) = 0$, the functional stops at $X = 0$, which need not be the minimum.

The second slip is to drop the dependence of $f_2$ on $x_1$.
With $f_2(x_2)$ in place of $f_2(x_1, x_2)$ the form is no longer classically equivalent to $A$, because the Skolem function of $\neg A$ for $y_2$ depends on both $x_1$ and $x_2$.

The third slip is to apply the definition to a formula that is not prenex.
First move the quantifiers to the front with classical logic, and then read off the form.

### History

Kreisel introduced the no-counterexample interpretation in 1951, in his work on the interpretation of non-finitist proofs.
He obtained the $\varepsilon_0$-recursive functionals from Ackermann's 1940 consistency proof of arithmetic by the epsilon substitution method.
Kohlenbach later used the interpretation, together with the Dialectica interpretation, in [[proof mining|XR1JXQG1CD]].

### Try it

First, write the n.c.i. of $\exists x\, \forall y\, (x \ge y \to x = y)$ and give a witness.
It asks for $X(f)$ with $X \ge f(X) \to X = f(X)$, and $X(f) = 0$ works, since $0 \ge f(0)$ forces $f(0) = 0$.

Second, why is $X(f) = f(0)$ not a witness for the minimum principle of $g(m) = |m - 5|$?
Against $f(n) = n + 1$ it gives $X = 1$, and $g(1) = 4 > 3 = g(2)$, so the test fails.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some theorems say "there is a number with a property", but no program finds the number.
The no-counterexample interpretation offers a weaker game that a program can win.
An opponent claims: "every number you name fails, and here is the test that shows it."
A program wins if it names a number that passes the test the opponent chose for it.
For "the function $g$ has a smallest value", the opponent maps each $n$ to some $m$ with $g(m) < g(n)$.
The program starts at $0$ and follows the opponent's tests downward, and after at most $g(0)$ steps it reaches a number whose test fails.

### L2 human

We compute the no-counterexample form of $A = \exists x\, \forall y\, \exists u\, \forall v\, \varphi(x, y, u, v)$.
The negation is $\forall x\, \exists y\, \forall u\, \exists v\, \neg \varphi(x, y, u, v)$.
Its Skolem functions are $f$ for $y$ and $g$ for $v$, and $g$ sees both $x$ and $u$:
$$\exists f\, \exists g\, \forall x\, \forall u\, \neg \varphi(x, f(x), u, g(x, u)).$$
Negating again, $A$ is classically equivalent to $\forall f\, \forall g\, \exists x\, \exists u\, \varphi(x, f(x), u, g(x, u))$.
So the n.c.i. asks for functionals $X(f, g)$ and $U(f, g)$ with $\varphi(X, f(X), U, g(X, U))$.

The typical use is to give finite content to a theorem of the form $\forall \exists \forall$, such as the convergence of a sequence.
Watch out for the Skolem step: $g$ depends on both $x$ and $u$, and the form is wrong without this dependence.

### L3 human

The n.c.i. is the Herbrand form of a theorem, and Kreisel's theorem is a version of Herbrand's theorem for PA in which the finite disjunction is replaced by a search along a well-ordering below $\varepsilon_0$.
It is closely related to the Dialectica interpretation after the negative translation, and for sentences $\exists x\, \forall y\, \varphi$ the two agree.
Kreisel's proof did not go by induction on derivations, since it used Ackermann's consistency proof, but the functionals can also be obtained through the Dialectica interpretation.
In analysis the n.c.i. of convergence is metastability, which Tao used for finite convergence results and which [[proof mining|XR1JXQG1CD]] bounds explicitly.
