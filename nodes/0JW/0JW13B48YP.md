## Summary

Let $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $v \neq 0$.
The vector projection of $u$ along $v$ is the vector
$$p = \operatorname{proj}_v u = \frac{u \cdot v}{v \cdot v} \, v,$$
where $u \cdot v$ is the [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]].
The scalar component of $u$ along $v$ is the number $\operatorname{comp}_v u = \frac{u \cdot v}{\|v\|}$, the signed [[length|0XKBKGTCPC]] of $p$.
The projection is the only multiple of $v$ such that $u - p$ is [[perpendicular|03NK6RSRQ1]] to $v$, so $u = p + (u - p)$ splits $u$ into a part along $v$ and a part across $v$.
The [[orthogonal decomposition theorem|~16119a5a]] proves this, and the [[formal definition|0G78GP5TF1]] states the formula.
Projections matter because they split forces into components, give distances to lines and planes, and are the first case of least squares.

## Motivation

A box rests on a ramp, and gravity pulls it straight down.
Only the part of gravity along the ramp makes the box slide, and the part perpendicular to the ramp presses it on the ramp.
So we want to write the force $u$ as a sum $p + q$, with $p$ along the direction $v$ of the ramp and $q$ perpendicular to it.
If $p = cv$, then $q = u - cv$ must satisfy $(u - cv) \cdot v = 0$.
This is one linear equation for $c$, and its solution is $c = \frac{u \cdot v}{v \cdot v}$.
In a picture, $p$ is the shadow of $u$ on the line of $v$ when the light falls perpendicular to that line.

## Notation

- Books write $\operatorname{proj}_v u$, $\operatorname{proj}_{v}(u)$ or $P_v u$ for the vector projection.
- The scalar component is $\operatorname{comp}_v u$ or $u_v$; some books call it the scalar projection.
- The vector $u - \operatorname{proj}_v u$ is the vector rejection, or the component of $u$ orthogonal to $v$.
- For a unit vector $\hat v$, the formula becomes $\operatorname{proj}_{\hat v} u = (u \cdot \hat v)\, \hat v$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- $u = (3, 4)$ along $v = (1, 0)$: $p = (3, 0)$ and $u - p = (0, 4)$.
- $u = (1, 2, 3)$ along $v = (1, 1, 1)$: $p = (2, 2, 2)$ and $u - p = (-1, 0, 1)$. The [[worked example|~5636957a]] checks the decomposition.
- $u = (2, 3)$ along $v = (4, 1)$: $u \cdot v = 11$ and $v \cdot v = 17$, so $p = (\tfrac{44}{17}, \tfrac{11}{17})$ and $u - p = (-\tfrac{10}{17}, \tfrac{40}{17})$. Indeed $(u - p) \cdot v = \tfrac{-40 + 40}{17} = 0$.
- A weight of $10$ newtons on a ramp in the direction $(4, 3)$ has a component of $6$ newtons down the ramp. The [[ramp example|~b8317e79]] computes it.
- If $u \perp v$, then $p = 0$; if $u = cv$, then $p = u$.

### Non-examples

- The vector $(u \cdot v)\, v$ is not the projection unless $\|v\| = 1$. The [[non-example|~c9ead3ef]] gets $(6, 6, 6)$ instead of $(2, 2, 2)$.
- The projection of $u$ along the zero vector is not defined: the formula divides by $0 \cdot 0 = 0$.
- The coordinate $(u_1, 0)$ of $u$ is the projection along $\mathbf{i}$, but in general it is not the projection along another axis or line.

### Basic properties

- Decomposition: $u = p + q$ with $p \parallel v$ and $q \perp v$, and this pair is unique, by the [[orthogonal decomposition theorem|~16119a5a]].
- Pythagoras: $\|u\|^2 = \|p\|^2 + \|q\|^2$, since $p \cdot q = 0$.
- Closest point: $p$ is the multiple of $v$ nearest to $u$, and $\|q\|$ is the distance from $u$ to the line $\mathbb{R}v$.
- Scaling: the projection along $cv$ is the same as along $v$ for each $c \neq 0$, because $c$ cancels in the formula.
- Linearity: $\operatorname{proj}_v (au + bw) = a \operatorname{proj}_v u + b \operatorname{proj}_v w$, by the linearity of the dot product.
- Idempotence: the projection of $p$ along $v$ is $p$ again.
- Angle: for $u \neq 0$, $\operatorname{comp}_v u = \|u\| \cos \theta$, where $\theta$ is the [[angle|0ZEHZY85E6]] between $u$ and $v$.

### History

Newton stated the parallelogram rule for forces in the Principia of 1687, and mechanics has split forces into components since then.
The formula with the dot product belongs to the vector analysis of Gibbs and Heaviside in the 1880s.

### Common errors

- Students forget to divide by $v \cdot v$, and write $(u \cdot v)\, v$. This is correct only for unit vectors.
- Students mix the order: $\operatorname{proj}_v u$ lies on the line of $v$, while $\operatorname{proj}_u v$ lies on the line of $u$. For $u = (1, 2, 3)$ and $v = (1, 1, 1)$ these are $(2, 2, 2)$ and $\tfrac{3}{7}(1, 2, 3)$.
- Students think the scalar component is a length and so never negative. It is negative when the angle is obtuse.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Shine a light from straight above onto a stick that leans over a floor line.
The shadow of the stick on the line is the projection.
For the vector $(3, 4)$ and the floor direction $(1, 0)$, the shadow is $(3, 0)$.
The rest of the vector, $(0, 4)$, points straight up, at a right angle to the floor.

### L2 human

Worked example: split $u = (4, 2, 1)$ along $v = (0, 1, 1)$.

1. $u \cdot v = 0 + 2 + 1 = 3$ and $v \cdot v = 2$.
2. $p = \tfrac{3}{2} v = (0, \tfrac{3}{2}, \tfrac{3}{2})$.
3. $q = u - p = (4, \tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2})$.
4. Check: $q \cdot v = 0 + \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} = 0$.
5. Check: $\|u\|^2 = 21$ and $\|p\|^2 + \|q\|^2 = \tfrac{9}{2} + \tfrac{33}{2} = 21$.

The typical use is the decomposition of a force, and the distance from a point to a line.
A trap: the projection depends only on the line of $v$, so the sign and the length of $v$ do not matter, but $v$ must not be $0$.

### L3 human

The map $u \mapsto \operatorname{proj}_v u$ is linear, and its matrix is $P = \dfrac{v v^{\mathsf{T}}}{v^{\mathsf{T}} v}$.
This matrix satisfies $P^2 = P$ and $P^{\mathsf{T}} = P$, the two properties of an [[orthogonal projection|0B8KZRT28M]].
Projection onto a subspace with an orthogonal basis is the sum of the projections along the basis vectors, and the [[Gram-Schmidt process|0VT6P0GNQR]] subtracts such projections one by one.
The closest point property is the one-dimensional case of least squares, treated in the sub-area on [[orthogonality and least squares|8GQVME4SZZ]].
