## Summary

Projecting a vector onto a direction splits it into the part that points along that direction and the part that points straight across it.
For [[vectors|0QN3D80AWP]] $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with $v \neq 0$, the vector projection of $u$ along $v$ is
$$p = \operatorname{proj}_v u = \frac{u \cdot v}{v \cdot v} \, v,$$
where $u \cdot v$ is the [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]].
It is the only multiple of $v$ that leaves a remainder $u - p$ [[perpendicular|03NK6RSRQ1]] to $v$, a fact proved in the [[orthogonal decomposition theorem|NA6Q7ME9G0]].
The same $p$ is the point of the line through $0$ and $v$ that lies nearest to $u$.
That is why projections give components of forces, distances to lines and planes, and the first case of least squares.

## Motivation

A box rests on a ramp, and gravity pulls it straight down.
Only the part of gravity that runs along the ramp makes the box slide, while the part across the ramp presses it into the surface.
So we want to write the force $u$ as a sum $p + q$, with $p$ along the direction $v$ of the ramp and $q$ perpendicular to it.

Try $p = cv$ for an unknown number $c$.
Then $q = u - cv$, and the condition $q \perp v$ becomes $(u - cv) \cdot v = 0$.
By the linearity of the dot product this is $u \cdot v - c\,(v \cdot v) = 0$, one linear equation for one unknown.
Its solution is $c = \frac{u \cdot v}{v \cdot v}$, and the formula for the projection follows.
In a picture, $p$ is the shadow that $u$ casts on the line of $v$ when the light falls at a right angle to that line.

## Notation

Books write $\operatorname{proj}_v u$, $P_v u$ or $u_{\parallel v}$ for the vector projection.
The scalar component, also called the scalar projection, is the signed [[length|0XKBKGTCPC]]
$$\operatorname{comp}_v u = \frac{u \cdot v}{\|v\|}.$$
The remainder $u - \operatorname{proj}_v u$ is called the vector rejection, or the component of $u$ orthogonal to $v$.
For a unit vector $\hat v$ the formula shortens to $\operatorname{proj}_{\hat v} u = (u \cdot \hat v)\, \hat v$, since $\hat v \cdot \hat v = 1$.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take $u = (1, 2, 3)$ and $v = (1, 1, 1)$.
The two dot products are $u \cdot v = 6$ and $v \cdot v = 3$, so
$$p = \tfrac{6}{3}\,(1, 1, 1) = (2, 2, 2), \qquad q = u - p = (-1, 0, 1).$$
The remainder passes the test: $q \cdot v = -1 + 0 + 1 = 0$.
The [[worked example|3S2MYEVFB2]] reads this split as the mean and the deviations of the numbers $1, 2, 3$.

In the plane, $u = (2, 3)$ along $v = (4, 1)$ gives $u \cdot v = 11$ and $v \cdot v = 17$.
So $p = \tfrac{11}{17}(4, 1)$, and $q = \tfrac{1}{17}(-10, 40)$, which again has dot product $0$ with $v$.
Fractions like these are normal; the projection is rarely a vector with integer entries.

### Forces on a ramp

The motivating problem has a clean answer.
A weight of $10$ newtons, $F = (0, -10)$, on a ramp in the direction $(4, 3)$ splits into $6$ newtons down the ramp and $8$ newtons into it.
The [[ramp example|Q9NG7HNKZH]] carries out the computation and checks it against the school formulas with $\sin$ and $\cos$.

### What the projection does

The two parts satisfy Pythagoras, $\|u\|^2 = \|p\|^2 + \|q\|^2$, because $p \cdot q = 0$.
So the projection is never longer than $u$.
Among all multiples $tv$, the projection is the one nearest to $u$, and $\|q\|$ is the distance from $u$ to the line $\mathbb{R}v$.
The projection depends only on the line of $v$: replacing $v$ by $cv$ with $c \neq 0$ changes nothing, since $c$ cancels in the formula.
The map $u \mapsto \operatorname{proj}_v u$ is linear, and projecting $p$ a second time gives $p$ again.
Two extreme cases help as checks: if $u \perp v$ then $p = 0$, and if $u$ is a multiple of $v$ then $p = u$.

### The scalar component and the angle

For $u \neq 0$ the scalar component equals $\|u\| \cos \theta$, where $\theta$ is the [[angle|0ZEHZY85E6]] between $u$ and $v$.
It is positive for an acute angle, zero for a right angle and negative for an obtuse angle.
For $u = (1, 2, 3)$ and $v = (1, 1, 1)$ it is $6 / \sqrt{3} = 2\sqrt{3}$, the length of $(2, 2, 2)$.

### Watch out

The most common slip is to forget the division by $v \cdot v$ and write $(u \cdot v)\, v$.
For $u = (1, 2, 3)$ and $v = (1, 1, 1)$ that gives $(6, 6, 6)$, three times too long, as the [[non-example|DMMBEWWVJD]] shows.
The shortcut is correct only when $v$ is a unit vector.

The order also matters.
The projection $\operatorname{proj}_v u$ lies on the line of $v$, while $\operatorname{proj}_u v$ lies on the line of $u$.
For the same pair, $\operatorname{proj}_u v = \tfrac{6}{14}(1, 2, 3) = \tfrac{3}{7}(1, 2, 3)$, which is not $(2, 2, 2)$.
Finally, there is no projection along the zero vector, because the formula would divide by $0 \cdot 0 = 0$.

### History

Newton stated the parallelogram rule for combining forces in the *Principia* of 1687, and mechanics has split forces into components ever since.
The compact formula with the dot product belongs to the vector analysis that Gibbs and Heaviside developed in the 1880s.

### Try it

Project $u = (4, 2, 1)$ along $v = (0, 1, 1)$.
Here $u \cdot v = 3$ and $v \cdot v = 2$, so $p = (0, \tfrac{3}{2}, \tfrac{3}{2})$ and $q = (4, \tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2})$.
Check that $q \cdot v = 0$ and that $\|p\|^2 + \|q\|^2 = \tfrac{9}{2} + \tfrac{33}{2} = 21 = \|u\|^2$.

What is the distance from the point $(5, 0)$ to the line through $0$ in the direction $(1, 2)$?
The projection is $(1, 2)$, the remainder is $(4, -2)$, and the distance is $\sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Shine a light from straight above onto a stick that leans over a line on the floor.
The shadow of the stick on the line is the projection.
For the vector $(3, 4)$ and the floor direction $(1, 0)$, the shadow is $(3, 0)$.
The rest of the vector, $(0, 4)$, points straight up, at a right angle to the floor.
Shadow plus the upright part gives back the stick: $(3, 0) + (0, 4) = (3, 4)$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: split $u = (4, 2, 1)$ into a part along $v = (0, 1, 1)$ and a part across it.
The coefficient is
$$c = \frac{u \cdot v}{v \cdot v} = \frac{0 + 2 + 1}{0 + 1 + 1} = \frac{3}{2},$$
so the part along $v$ is $p = (0, \tfrac{3}{2}, \tfrac{3}{2})$ and the part across is $q = u - p = (4, \tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2})$.
Check: $q \cdot v = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} = 0$.
The length $\|q\| = \sqrt{33/2}$ is the distance from the point $(4, 2, 1)$ to the line through $0$ with direction $(0, 1, 1)$.
This is the pattern for the [[distance from a point to a line|C6GAS92QHX]]: project, subtract, and take the length of what is left.

### L3 human

The map $u \mapsto \operatorname{proj}_v u$ is linear, and its matrix is $P = \dfrac{v v^{\mathsf{T}}}{v^{\mathsf{T}} v}$.
This matrix satisfies $P^2 = P$ and $P^{\mathsf{T}} = P$, the two properties of an [[orthogonal projection|0B8KZRT28M]].
Projection onto a subspace with an orthogonal basis is the sum of the projections along the basis vectors, and the [[Gram-Schmidt process|0VT6P0GNQR]] subtracts such projections one at a time.
The nearest-point property is the one-dimensional case of least squares, which the sub-area on [[orthogonality and least squares|8GQVME4SZZ]] develops in full.
