## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2i \\ -2i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ act on $\mathbb{C}^2$ with the standard inner product $\ip{x}{y} = x_1 \overline{y_1} + x_2 \overline{y_2}$.
We check that $A$ is Hermitian and find a unitary matrix that makes it diagonal.

1. In the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]] $A^*$, the entry in place $(1, 2)$ is $\overline{a_{21}} = \overline{-2i} = 2i$, the entry in place $(2, 1)$ is $\overline{a_{12}} = -2i$, and the diagonal entries $1$ are real. So $A^* = A$, the matrix is [[Hermitian|0EKZCX4K3P]], and the operator $x \mapsto Ax$ is [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]].
2. The characteristic polynomial is $(1 - t)^2 - (2i)(-2i) = (1 - t)^2 - 4$. Its roots are $t = 1 \pm 2$, that is, $3$ and $-1$, and both are real.
3. For $t = 3$, the matrix $A - 3I = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 2i \\ -2i & -2 \end{pmatrix}$ sends $(i, 1)$ to $(-2i + 2i, 2 - 2) = (0, 0)$.
4. For $t = -1$, the matrix $A + I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2i \\ -2i & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ sends $(1, i)$ to $(2 - 2, -2i + 2i) = (0, 0)$.
5. The two eigenvectors are orthogonal for the complex inner product:
$$\ip{(i, 1)}{(1, i)} = i \cdot \overline{1} + 1 \cdot \overline{i} = i - i = 0.$$
6. Each has length $\sqrt{\abs{i}^2 + 1} = \sqrt{2}$. So $u_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(i, 1)$ and $u_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, i)$ form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^2$.
7. Let $U = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} i & 1 \\ 1 & i \end{pmatrix}$ have the columns $u_1$ and $u_2$. Then $U$ is [[unitary|0D6RMJ10M9]], and
$$U^* A U = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

## Remarks

The example shows the general theorems at work on one matrix.
Step 2 gives [[real eigenvalues|0C4NT4GT3X]], and step 5 gives [[orthogonal eigenvectors|0JHGAV9H25]], as the theorems predict for every self-adjoint operator.

The same data give the spectral decomposition $A = 3 u_1 u_1^* - u_2 u_2^*$, with the two orthogonal projections
$$u_1 u_1^* = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad u_2 u_2^* = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $\tfrac{3}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix} - \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2i \\ -2i & 1 \end{pmatrix} = A$, and the two projections add up to $I$.

For $x = (a, b)$, the quadratic form is
$$x^* A x = \abs{a}^2 + \abs{b}^2 + 2i\, \overline{a} b - 2i\, a \overline{b} = \abs{a}^2 + \abs{b}^2 + 2 \operatorname{Re}(2i\, \overline{a} b).$$
It is real for every $x$, as [[the quadratic form statement|WAYMK9GR5S]] says, and at $x = (1, 1)$ it equals $2$.
Its largest value on unit vectors is $3$, at $u_1$, and its smallest is $-1$, at $u_2$.

The matrix is not symmetric: $A^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2i \\ 2i & 1 \end{pmatrix} \neq A$.
Over $\mathbb{C}$ the right condition is $A^* = A$.
If you replace $2i$ by any complex number $z$ and $-2i$ by $\overline{z}$, the eigenvalues become $1 \pm \abs{z}$, still real.

## Explanations

### L1 human

This complex matrix equals its own conjugate transpose.
It stretches one direction by $3$ and flips another direction, with the factor $-1$.
The two directions are perpendicular for the complex inner product.
In these two directions the matrix acts by the plain numbers $3$ and $-1$.

### L2 human

The computation follows the standard recipe for a Hermitian matrix.
First find the eigenvalues from the characteristic polynomial; for a Hermitian matrix they are real, here $3$ and $-1$.
Next find a unit eigenvector for each eigenvalue.
Then check orthogonality with the complex inner product, which conjugates the second vector.
Last put the unit eigenvectors as the columns of $U$, and $U^* A U$ is diagonal.
Watch out: in the orthogonality check, without the conjugation the product would be $i \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot i = 2i \neq 0$, and the vectors would wrongly look not orthogonal.
