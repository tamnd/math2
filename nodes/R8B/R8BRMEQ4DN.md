## Summary

The apparent magnitude of a celestial object measures how bright it looks from the Earth, on a reversed logarithmic scale.
A difference of $5$ magnitudes is a factor of exactly $100$ in received flux, and brighter objects have smaller magnitudes.
It is the basic photometric quantity of observational astronomy.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Ancient astronomers sorted stars into classes, from the brightest of the first magnitude to the faintest of the sixth.
The modern scale keeps this idea and makes it exact: each step of one magnitude is a factor of about $2.512$ in brightness.
Because the scale is reversed, very bright objects get negative numbers.
Sirius has magnitude about $-1.5$, the full Moon about $-12.7$ and the Sun about $-26.7$.
The faintest stars visible to the eye in a dark sky are near magnitude $6$.
