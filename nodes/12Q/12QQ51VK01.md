## Summary

A connective of mathematical language is a word or a phrase that makes a new [[statement|S9MHMRAX88]] from one or two statements, so that the truth value of the new statement depends only on the truth values of the parts.
The main connectives of mathematical English are "and", "or", "not", "but" and "neither ... nor"; "if ... then" and "if and only if" are on the page [[conditional statements|FA6YZGR2SS]].
Mathematics gives each of them one fixed meaning.
"$P$ and $Q$" is true when both $P$ and $Q$ are true.
"$P$ or $Q$" is true when at least one of $P$ and $Q$ is true, so "or" is inclusive.
"Not $P$" is true when $P$ is false.
"$P$ but $Q$" has the meaning of "$P$ and $Q$".
"Neither $P$ nor $Q$" means "not $P$ and not $Q$".
These fixed meanings let a reader check a [[compound statement|EBEMSMMDFE]] from its parts, and they are the informal side of the [[logical connectives|QS0XVAN9BG]] of logic.

## Motivation

Ordinary English uses these words loosely.
On a menu, "soup or salad" usually means one of the two, not both.
In "she got up and left", the word "and" also says that one event came first.
In "the test is short but hard", the word "but" adds a contrast that "and" does not.
A proof must be checkable by every reader in the same way.
So mathematics fixes one meaning for each connective, and it ignores the extra shades of ordinary speech.
The most important choice is that "or" is inclusive: "$P$ or $Q$" stays true when both parts are true.

## Notation

In symbols, "and" is $\land$, "or" is $\lor$, and "not $P$" is $\neg P$.
Older books write $P \,\&\, Q$ or $P \cdot Q$ for "and", and $\sim P$ or $\overline{P}$ for "not".
The exclusive "or" ("one of the two, but not both") is written $\oplus$ or XOR.
For sets, "and" corresponds to the intersection $\cap$, "or" to the union $\cup$, and "not" to the complement.
The pages of math2 read "or" as inclusive, as almost all mathematical texts do.
Some books read "or" as exclusive in examples from daily life, so a reader must check the convention of the book.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The inclusive "or" in "$n$ is even or $n$ is a multiple of $3$"|CER1EHMKW6]]: for $n = 6$ both parts are true, and the statement is true.
2. [[The word "but" in "the absolute value is continuous but not differentiable at $0$"|HW5E2DSZH3]]: the statement means "continuous and not differentiable", and both parts are true.
3. "$5$ is neither even nor a perfect square" is true. The number $5$ is odd, and $5$ is not a perfect square, because $2^2 = 4 < 5 < 9 = 3^2$.
4. "$x^2 = 1$ exactly when $x = 1$ or $x = -1$." The short form $x = \pm 1$ hides the word "or".
5. In set-builder notation, $\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x > 0 \text{ and } x < 1\}$ is the open interval $(0, 1)$, and $\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x < 0 \text{ or } x > 1\}$ is the union $(-\infty, 0) \cup (1, \infty)$.

### Non-examples

1. [[In "$2$ and $3$ are coprime", the word "and" is not a connective|R4D9KAT2QH]]. It joins two numbers in one relation, not two statements.
2. "Square the number and add $1$." Here "and" orders two steps of a command. For the number $3$, this order gives $10$, and the other order gives $16$. A connective of statements does not depend on order in this way.
3. "Because" is not a connective in this sense. "$4$ is even because $4 = 2 \cdot 2$" is a good reason, but "$4$ is even because $7$ is prime" is not, although both parts are true in each case. So the truth values of the parts do not decide the whole.

### Basic properties

1. "$P$ and $Q$" has the same truth value as "$Q$ and $P$", and "$P$ or $Q$" has the same truth value as "$Q$ or $P$".
2. "Not (not $P$)" has the same truth value as $P$.
3. "Neither $P$ nor $Q$" has the same truth value as "not ($P$ or $Q$)". This is one of [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]].
4. "Not ($P$ and $Q$)" has the same truth value as "not $P$ or not $Q$". This is the other law of De Morgan.
5. "$P$ but $Q$" has the same truth value as "$P$ and $Q$".
6. The exclusive "or" is a compound of the other connectives: "($P$ or $Q$) and not ($P$ and $Q$)".

The formal proofs of these facts use [[truth tables|05RSK4Q657]], and they belong to logic.

### History

In his book The Laws of Thought of 1854, George Boole added two classes only when they had no common member, so his "or" behaved like the exclusive "or".
William Stanley Jevons argued in 1864 for the inclusive reading, and later logic followed him.
Gottlob Frege, Giuseppe Peano and Bertrand Russell wrote the connectives as symbols, and the inclusive "or" became the standard of mathematics.

### Common errors

1. A student reads "or" as exclusive. The statement "$x \geq 0$ or $x \leq 0$" is true for $x = 0$, because both parts are true.
2. A student negates "and" part by part. "It is not true that $6$ is divisible by $4$ and by $3$" is true, but "$6$ is not divisible by $4$ and not divisible by $3$" is false.
3. A student writes "$x \neq 1$ or $2$" for "$x$ is neither $1$ nor $2$". The correct form is "$x \neq 1$ and $x \neq 2$".

## Explanations

### L1 human

Words such as "and", "or" and "not" join small claims into larger claims.
"$6$ is even and $6$ is bigger than $5$" is true, because both small claims are true.
"$7$ is even or $7$ is bigger than $5$" is true, because one small claim is true.
In mathematics, "or" also allows both: "$6$ is even or $6$ is bigger than $5$" is true.
"Not" turns a claim around: "$7$ is not even" is true, because "$7$ is even" is false.

### L2 human

We find the truth value of "$12$ is a multiple of $8$ or $12$ is not a multiple of $5$, and $12$ is a perfect square".
The commas group it as "($A$ or not $B$) and $C$".

1. $A$: "$12$ is a multiple of $8$". The multiples of $8$ near $12$ are $8$ and $16$, so $A$ is false.
2. $B$: "$12$ is a multiple of $5$". The multiples of $5$ near $12$ are $10$ and $15$, so $B$ is false, and "not $B$" is true.
3. "$A$ or not $B$" is true, because one of its parts is true.
4. $C$: "$12$ is a perfect square". We have $3^2 = 9 < 12 < 16 = 4^2$, so $C$ is false.
5. "($A$ or not $B$) and $C$" is false, because its second part $C$ is false.

The typical use is to break a long hypothesis into parts and to check each part.
In a proof, a hypothesis "$P$ and $Q$" gives two facts, and a goal "$P$ or $Q$" can be reached by proving one part.

A trap: English has no brackets, so the grouping is not always clear.
If the sentence were grouped as "$A$ or (not $B$ and $C$)", then it would be false here as well, but for other numbers the two groupings differ.
For example, for "$24$ is a multiple of $8$ or $24$ is not a multiple of $5$, and $24$ is a perfect square", the first grouping gives false and the second gives true.

### L3 human

A connective with $n$ inputs is a function from $\{\text{true}, \text{false}\}^n$ to $\{\text{true}, \text{false}\}$.
There are $2^4 = 16$ connectives with two inputs, and "not" with "and" can express all of them.
The single [[Sheffer stroke|1NNYWRN6B8]] ("not both") can also express all of them.
The laws of the connectives are the laws of a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]], and the same laws hold for the union, the intersection and the complement of sets.

Many words of English are not truth-functional, for example "because", "necessarily" and "before".
Modal and temporal logics study some of them.
In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], a proof of "$P$ or $Q$" must give a proof of one part, so the inclusive "or" has a stronger meaning there.
The formal treatment of connectives is in the topic [[propositions, connectives and quantifiers|PM1AYWABA7]].
