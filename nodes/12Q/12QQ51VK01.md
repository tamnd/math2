## Summary

The words "and", "or" and "not" join small claims into larger ones, and mathematics fixes their meanings so that the truth of the whole depends only on the truth of the parts.
Is "$6$ is even or $6$ is a multiple of $3$" true?
In mathematics it is, because both parts are true, and "or" allows both.

A *connective* of mathematical language is a word or phrase that makes a new [[statement|S9MHMRAX88]] from one or two given statements, so that the truth value of the new statement depends only on the truth values of the given ones.
The main connectives of mathematical English have these meanings:

- "$P$ and $Q$" is true when both $P$ and $Q$ are true, and "$P$ but $Q$" means the same;
- "$P$ or $Q$" is true when at least one of $P$ and $Q$ is true, so "or" is *inclusive*;
- "not $P$" is true when $P$ is false;
- "neither $P$ nor $Q$" means "not $P$ and not $Q$".

The connectives "if ... then" and "if and only if" have their own page, [[conditional statements|FA6YZGR2SS]].
These fixed meanings are the informal side of the [[logical connectives|QS0XVAN9BG]] of logic.

## Motivation

Ordinary English uses these words loosely.
On a menu, "soup or salad" means one of the two and not both.
In "she got up and left", the word "and" also says which event came first.
In "the test is short but hard", the word "but" adds a contrast that "and" does not.
A proof must be checked by every reader in the same way, so mathematics keeps one meaning for each word and drops the extra shades.

The connectives return on almost every later page.
A hypothesis "$P$ and $Q$" gives a proof two facts to use, and a goal "$P$ or $Q$" can be reached by proving one part.
[[Negating mathematical statements|K930V33BSC]] shows how "not" turns "and" into "or" and back.

## Notation

In symbols, "and" is $\land$, "or" is $\lor$, and "not $P$" is $\neg P$.
Older books write $P \,\&\, Q$ or $P \cdot Q$ for "and", and $\sim P$ or $\overline{P}$ for "not".
The [[exclusive or|PTMR3P9J6C]], "one of the two but not both", is written $\oplus$ or XOR.
For sets, "and" matches the intersection $\cap$, "or" matches the union $\cup$, and "not" matches the complement.
Almost all mathematical texts read "or" as inclusive, and math2 does too.
Some books read "or" as exclusive in examples from daily life, so check the convention of a book before you use its exercises.

## Remarks

### A first look at and, or and not

Each connective can be checked with numbers.
"$5$ is neither even nor a perfect square" is true: $5$ is odd, and $5$ is not a square, because
$$2^2 = 4 < 5 < 9 = 3^2.$$
The short form $x = \pm 1$ hides an "or": it means "$x = 1$ or $x = -1$", and these are exactly the solutions of $x^2 = 1$.
In set-builder notation, "and" gives an intersection and "or" a union:
$$\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x > 0 \text{ and } x < 1\} = (0, 1), \qquad \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x < 0 \text{ or } x > 1\} = (-\infty, 0) \cup (1, \infty).$$

### The inclusive or

The one case where mathematics and the menu disagree is the case where both parts are true.
In [[the inclusive or in "$n$ is even or $n$ is a multiple of $3$"|CER1EHMKW6]], the value $n = 6$ makes both parts true, and the statement is true.
The exclusive reading would call it false.
A real theorem shows why the inclusive reading is the useful one.
"If a prime $p$ divides $ab$, then $p$ divides $a$ or $p$ divides $b$" must allow both, since $2$ divides $2 \cdot 4 = 8$ and divides both $2$ and $4$.
When a mathematician wants the exclusive sense, the text says so: "exactly one of", or "but not both".

### But, although and neither nor

The word "but" has the truth value of "and".
It only tells the reader that the second part is a surprise after the first.
In [[the word "but" in "the absolute value is continuous but not differentiable at $0$"|HW5E2DSZH3]], the statement is the conjunction of two true parts, and a proof must prove both.
Words such as "although", "whereas" and "yet" work the same way.
"Neither $P$ nor $Q$" denies both parts, so "$x$ is neither $1$ nor $2$" means "$x \ne 1$ and $x \ne 2$".

### When "and" is not a connective

The same English word can join two statements in one sentence and two nouns in another.
"$2$ and $3$ are prime" splits into "$2$ is prime and $3$ is prime", so here "and" is a connective.
"$2$ and $3$ are coprime" does not split, because "coprime" is a relation between two numbers, as [[the "and" in "$2$ and $3$ are coprime"|R4D9KAT2QH]] shows.
In "square the number and add $1$", the word "and" orders two steps: for $3$ this gives $10$, and the other order gives $16$.
"Because" is not a connective either: "$4$ is even because $4 = 2 \cdot 2$" is a good reason, and "$4$ is even because $7$ is prime" is not, although all four parts are true.

### The rules you can use

The order of the parts does not matter: "$P$ and $Q$" has the truth value of "$Q$ and $P$", and the same holds for "or".
"Not (not $P$)" has the truth value of $P$.
[[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]] move "not" across the other two connectives:
$$\text{not } (P \text{ and } Q) \;\equiv\; \text{not } P \text{ or not } Q, \qquad \text{not } (P \text{ or } Q) \;\equiv\; \text{not } P \text{ and not } Q.$$
The second law says that "neither $P$ nor $Q$" is "not ($P$ or $Q$)".
The exclusive or is a compound of the others: "($P$ or $Q$) and not ($P$ and $Q$)".
The formal proofs of these rules use [[truth tables|05RSK4Q657]], and they belong to logic.

### Watch out

The first error is to read "or" as exclusive.
"$x \ge 0$ or $x \le 0$" is true for $x = 0$, because both parts hold.
The second error is to negate "and" part by part.
"It is not true that $6$ is divisible by $4$ and by $3$" is true, but "$6$ is not divisible by $4$ and not divisible by $3$" is false, since $3$ divides $6$.
The third error is to write "$x \ne 1$ or $2$" for "$x$ is neither $1$ nor $2$".
The correct form is "$x \ne 1$ and $x \ne 2$".

### History

In The Laws of Thought of 1854, George Boole added two classes only when they had no common member, so his "or" behaved like the exclusive or.
William Stanley Jevons argued for the inclusive reading in 1864, and later logic followed him.
Frege, Peano and Russell wrote the connectives as symbols, and the inclusive or became the standard of mathematics.

### Try it

First, is "$9$ is a perfect square or $9$ is odd" true, and does the answer depend on the reading of "or"?
It is true on the inclusive reading, since both parts hold, and false on the exclusive one.

Second, write "$x$ is not between $0$ and $1$" with connectives, for real $x$, where "between" means $0 < x < 1$.
It is "not ($x > 0$ and $x < 1$)", which De Morgan turns into "$x \le 0$ or $x \ge 1$".

## Explanations

### L1 human

Words such as "and", "or" and "not" join small claims into larger claims.
"$6$ is even and $6$ is bigger than $5$" is true, because both small claims are true.
"$7$ is even or $7$ is bigger than $5$" is true, because one small claim is true.
In mathematics, "or" also allows both: "$6$ is even or $6$ is bigger than $5$" is true.
"Not" turns a claim around: "$7$ is not even" is true, because "$7$ is even" is false.

### L2 human

Find the truth value of "$12$ is a multiple of $8$ or $12$ is not a multiple of $5$, and $12$ is a perfect square".
The commas group it as "($A$ or not $B$) and $C$".
The multiples of $8$ near $12$ are $8$ and $16$, so $A$ is false.
The multiples of $5$ near $12$ are $10$ and $15$, so $B$ is false and "not $B$" is true.
So "$A$ or not $B$" is true, because one of its parts is true.
Finally $3^2 = 9 < 12 < 16 = 4^2$, so $C$ is false, and the whole statement is false.

Watch out: English has no brackets, so the grouping is not always clear.
The other grouping, "$A$ or (not $B$ and $C$)", is also false for $12$.
For $24$ the two groupings differ: $A$ is true and $C$ is false, so the first grouping gives false and the second gives true.
In a proof, break a long hypothesis into its parts and check the grouping before you use it.

### L3 human

A connective with $n$ inputs is a function from $\{\text{true}, \text{false}\}^n$ to $\{\text{true}, \text{false}\}$.
There are $2^4 = 16$ connectives with two inputs, and "not" together with "and" can express all of them.
The single [[Sheffer stroke|1NNYWRN6B8]], "not both", can also express all of them.
The laws of the connectives are the laws of a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]], and the same laws hold for the union, the intersection and the complement of sets.

Many words of English are not truth-functional, for example "because", "necessarily" and "before".
Modal and temporal logics study some of them.
In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], a proof of "$P$ or $Q$" must give a proof of one of the parts, so "or" has a stronger meaning there.
The formal treatment is in the topic [[propositions, connectives and quantifiers|PM1AYWABA7]].
