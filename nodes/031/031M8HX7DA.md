## Statement

Let $G$ be a [[group|G5S1M9]] with identity $e$, let $g \in G$, and let $\langle g \rangle$ be the [[cyclic subgroup generated by $g$|08ABT85339]].

1. Suppose $g$ has finite [[order|0WE2NHZJ7B]] $n$. Then $g^a = g^b$ if and only if $n$ divides $a - b$. Thus $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \ldots, g^{n-1}\}$, these $n$ elements are distinct, and $|\langle g \rangle| = n$.
2. Suppose $g$ has infinite order. Then $g^a \neq g^b$ for all integers $a \neq b$, so the map $k \mapsto g^k$ from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\langle g \rangle$ is injective, and $\langle g \rangle$ is infinite.

In both cases, the [[order of the group|0B85S2V2DR]] $\langle g \rangle$ equals the order of the element $g$.

## Remarks

### Consequences

1. Let $G$ be finite. Then each $g \in G$ has finite order, because $\langle g \rangle$ is a finite set and part 2 would make it infinite.
2. Let $G$ be finite. By [[Lagrange's theorem|3C53TD]] applied to $\langle g \rangle$, the order of $g$ divides $|G|$. This is [[the corollary on element orders|08V7JHS3QZ]].
3. Then $g^{|G|} = e$ for each $g$ in a finite group $G$, by [[the divisibility criterion for powers|0F5QC101Q7]].
4. A finite group $G$ is cyclic if and only if it has an element of order $|G|$.
5. To compute $g^k$ for $g$ of order $n$, replace $k$ by its remainder modulo $n$. For example, $i^{2026} = i^2 = -1$ in $\mathbb{C}^\times$, because $2026 = 4 \cdot 506 + 2$.

### Converse

Each part of the theorem is an equivalence in disguise.
If $\langle g \rangle$ is finite with $n$ elements, then $g$ has finite order by part 2, and this order is $n$ by part 1.
If $\langle g \rangle$ is infinite, then $g$ has infinite order by part 1.

### Generalizations

In a monoid, the powers $x, x^2, x^3, \ldots$ of an element can repeat without returning to the identity.
For example, in the multiplicative monoid of residues modulo $8$, the powers of $2$ are $2, 4, 0, 0, \ldots$.
So the list of powers has a tail followed by a cycle, and the theorem needs the inverses of a group.
For a subset $S$ with more than one element, the order of the [[subgroup generated by $S$|0RP7VWQ0YN]] is not determined by the orders of the elements of $S$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The word "order" has two meanings: the size of a group, and the number of steps for an element to return to the identity.
This theorem says that the two meanings agree for the group made by one element.
For example, a rotation by $120$ degrees returns to the start after $3$ steps.
Its powers are the rotations by $0$, $120$ and $240$ degrees, so they form a group with $3$ elements.

### L2 human

We take $g = (1\,2\,3\,4)$ in the symmetric group $S_4$, with products of permutations read from right to left.

1. The powers are $g^1 = (1\,2\,3\,4)$, $g^2 = (1\,3)(2\,4)$, $g^3 = (1\,4\,3\,2)$ and $g^4 = e$.
2. So $g$ has order $4$, and the theorem gives $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, g^3\}$ with $4$ distinct elements.
3. For example, $g^{-1} = g^3$ and $g^{10} = g^2$, because $-1 \equiv 3$ and $10 \equiv 2$ modulo $4$.

The typical use: to find the size of $\langle g \rangle$, compute the order of $g$, and conversely.
A trap: the list $e, g, \ldots, g^{n-1}$ has no repeats only when $n$ is the least positive integer with $g^n = e$.
For a larger $m$ with $g^m = e$, such as $m = 8$ here, the list $e, g, \ldots, g^{m-1}$ repeats.

### L3 human

The map $k \mapsto g^k$ is a homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$ onto $\langle g \rangle$, and its kernel is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$, so it is $n\mathbb{Z}$ for one $n \geq 0$.
Part 1 is the case $n \geq 1$, where the [[first isomorphism theorem|0CXXJNG5B6]] gives $\langle g \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.
Part 2 is the case $n = 0$, where $\langle g \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}$.
For an element of a topological group, the analogous question asks for the closure of $\langle g \rangle$, which can be a torus or a solenoid instead of a finite set.
