## Example

We prove: if a right triangle has legs of lengths $a$ and $b$, a hypotenuse of length $c$, and area $\frac{c^2}{4}$, then the triangle is isosceles.
We find the proof by the [[forward-backward method|V8ZB838JY5]].

1. Backward: the goal is that the triangle is isosceles.
   The hypotenuse is longer than each leg, so the two equal sides must be the legs, and the new goal is $a = b$.
2. Backward: how can we show that two numbers are equal?
   It suffices to show $(a - b)^2 = 0$, so the new goal is $a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = 0$.
3. Forward: the [[area of the triangle|065JWMZW86]] with legs $a$ and $b$ at the right angle is $\frac{ab}{2}$.
   With the hypothesis,
   $$\frac{ab}{2} = \frac{c^2}{4}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad 2ab = c^2.$$
4. Forward: by the [[Pythagorean theorem|0VP3A1290K]], $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, and with step 3 this gives $2ab = a^2 + b^2$.
5. The chains meet, because step 4 is the equation $a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = 0$ of step 2.

Written forward, the proof reads: the area gives $2ab = c^2$, the Pythagorean theorem gives $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, so $(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = 0$, so $a = b$, and the triangle is isosceles.

## Remarks

The backward steps chose which equation to aim for.
Without them, the forward process could produce many true equations, such as $c^4 = 4a^2b^2$, that do not lead to the goal.

The converse also holds.
An isosceles right triangle with legs $a = b$ has $c^2 = 2a^2$ and area $\frac{a^2}{2} = \frac{c^2}{4}$.
For the right triangle with legs $3$ and $4$, the hypotenuse is $5$, the area is $6$, and $\frac{c^2}{4} = 6.25$, so the hypothesis fails, as it must for a triangle that is not isosceles.
In fact $\frac{ab}{2} \le \frac{c^2}{4}$ for every right triangle, since $2ab \le a^2 + b^2$, so the hypothesis picks out the right triangles of largest area for a given hypotenuse.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Fold a square sheet of paper along a diagonal to get a right triangle with two equal sides.
Its area is half of the square, and a little calculation shows that it is a quarter of the square of the long side.
The statement says the reverse: a right triangle with exactly this area must have two equal sides.
To find the proof, we asked what we need at the end, two equal sides, and what the facts give us at the start, and we met in the middle.

### L2 human

Use the same plan for a related claim: if a right triangle has legs $a$, $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, then $a + b \le c\sqrt{2}$.
Backward: both sides are positive, so it suffices to show $(a + b)^2 \le 2c^2$.
Forward: the Pythagorean theorem gives $2c^2 = 2a^2 + 2b^2$.
So the goal becomes
$$a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \le 2a^2 + 2b^2, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad 0 \le (a - b)^2,$$
which is true, with equality exactly when $a = b$.
Check: legs $3$ and $4$ give $7 \le 5\sqrt{2} \approx 7.07$.
The typical use is to square a goal whose sides are positive and then bring in a known identity forward.
Watch out: squaring keeps the direction of the inequality only because both sides are positive, and the final write-up must start from $(a - b)^2 \ge 0$ and go forward to the claim.
