## Example

We look at two short [[proofs|0B94TKFQJX]] about the [[natural numbers|0MWKJ44V5A]], written in the language of the proof assistant Lean 4, and we follow how the program checks them.
In Lean the natural numbers form the type `Nat`, and a statement is a type whose elements are its proofs.
The first proof is the line `example : 2 + 2 = 4 := rfl`.
The second proof is the line `theorem add_comm_example (a b : Nat) : a + b = b + a := Nat.add_comm a b`.

1. The first line states $2 + 2 = 4$.
   The text after `:=` is the proof, here the term `rfl`, which claims that the two sides are the same value.
2. Lean computes both sides from the definition of addition on `Nat`.
   It gets the same natural number on each side, so it accepts `rfl` as a proof.
3. The second line states $a + b = b + a$ for all natural numbers $a$ and $b$.
   The part `(a b : Nat)` fixes two arbitrary natural numbers, as "let $a$ and $b$ be natural numbers" does in a written proof.
4. The proof term is `Nat.add_comm a b`.
   The name `Nat.add_comm` refers to a theorem that the Lean core library proves once for all $a$ and $b$, and the term applies it to our $a$ and $b$.
5. Lean computes the type of `Nat.add_comm a b`, which is $a + b = b + a$.
   This type is exactly the statement in step 3, so Lean accepts the theorem.
6. Now change the first line to `example : 2 + 2 = 5 := rfl`.
   Lean computes $4$ on the left and $5$ on the right, finds two different values, and rejects the line with an error.

## Remarks

A written proof asks the reader to trust that the gaps can be filled.
A proof assistant fills nothing in on trust: a small trusted part of the program, the kernel, checks every proof term against its statement.
Step 6 shows the other side of this: a false claim gets no proof, however it is written.

The proof in step 4 is one line because it cites a result that is already proved, in the same way that a written proof cites a lemma.
Longer proofs are written with tactics, which are commands that build the proof term piece by piece, such as `induction` or `simp`.
The match between statements and types, and between proofs and terms, is the [[Curry-Howard correspondence|A77FER1Q5H]].

Watch out.
Lean checks that the proof proves the statement as it is written, not the statement that the author meant.
If someone writes `a + b = a + b` where they meant `a + b = b + a`, the proof `rfl` is accepted, and nothing about commutativity has been shown.
So a formal proof is only as good as the formal statement, and a human must still read the statement.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A proof assistant is a computer program that checks proofs.
You type a statement, such as "two plus two is four", and then a proof.
The program checks each step and either accepts the proof or shows an error.
For "two plus two is four" the program computes both sides and sees the same number.
For "two plus two is five" it computes, sees two different numbers, and refuses.

### L2 human

Read the second line as you would read a theorem in a book.
The part `(a b : Nat)` is the hypothesis: $a$ and $b$ are natural numbers, with no other condition.
The part `a + b = b + a` is the conclusion.
The part after `:=` is the proof, and it says "apply the general theorem `Nat.add_comm` to $a$ and $b$".
Lean compares the statement of the applied theorem with the conclusion, finds that they agree, and accepts.
The typical use of such a program is to check a long or delicate proof, where a human reader can miss an error in one of many steps.
