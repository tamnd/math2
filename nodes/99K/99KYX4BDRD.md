## Definition

Let $d \ge 2$ and work on Euclidean space $\R^d$.
Let a [[quantum field theory|W031YNVRE3]] be given by the correlation functions $\langle \mathcal{O}_1(x_1) \cdots \mathcal{O}_n(x_n) \rangle$ of a family of local fields at distinct points.
It is a *conformal field theory* on $\R^d$ if it has the following properties.

1. The theory is invariant under translations and rotations, and it has a stress tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$ that is symmetric and traceless.
2. The fields are spanned by primary fields and their derivatives.
   Each primary field $\mathcal{O}$ has a scaling dimension $\Delta \in \R$ and a transformation law under the rotation group.
3. Let $f$ be a global [[conformal map|0YYT36R4YD]], a composition of translations, rotations, dilations and inversions, defined at the points $x_i$, and set $\Omega(x) = \abs{\det Df(x)}^{1/d}$.
   For scalar primary fields $\mathcal{O}_i$ of dimensions $\Delta_i$,
$$\langle \mathcal{O}_1(f(x_1)) \cdots \mathcal{O}_n(f(x_n)) \rangle = \prod_{i=1}^n \Omega(x_i)^{-\Delta_i} \, \langle \mathcal{O}_1(x_1) \cdots \mathcal{O}_n(x_n) \rangle,$$
with the analogous rule for primary fields of non-zero spin.

## Remarks

For $d \ge 3$, Liouville's theorem says that the conformal maps are generated by translations, rotations, dilations $x \mapsto \lambda x$ and inversion, and they form a group locally isomorphic to $SO(d + 1, 1)$.
For $d = 2$, every holomorphic map is locally conformal, but clause 3 is required only for the global ones, the Möbius transformations.
The local conformal maps act infinitesimally through the stress tensor, which gives two commuting copies of the [[Virasoro algebra|R9WFXME6GC]] with a central charge $c$.

Consequence: invariance under translations, rotations, dilations and inversion forces the two-point function of a scalar primary to be $\langle \mathcal{O}(x)\mathcal{O}(y) \rangle = C \abs{x - y}^{-2\Delta}$ for a constant $C$.
Check with the dilation $f(x) = \lambda x$, $\Omega = \lambda$: the left side is $C \lambda^{-2\Delta} \abs{x - y}^{-2\Delta}$, which is the right side.

Example: the free massless scalar field in $d = 4$, with $\langle \phi(x)\phi(y) \rangle = \frac{1}{4\pi^2 \abs{x - y}^2}$, is a conformal field theory, and $\phi$ is a primary of dimension $1$.

Non-example: the free scalar field of mass $m > 0$ has a two-point function that decays like $e^{-m\abs{x - y}}$ at large distance.
This is not a power of $\abs{x - y}$, so clause 3 fails for dilations.

There are several rigorous frameworks for conformal field theory, such as [[vertex operator algebras|GHRPQAJY43]] and Segal's functorial definition in dimension two, and conformal nets of operator algebras.
