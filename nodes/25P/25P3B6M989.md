## Definition

Let a linear elastic material obey [[Hooke's law|V56XAESDNE]] $\sigma_{ij} = C_{ijkl} \varepsilon_{kl}$, where $C$ is its [[elasticity tensor|SX32X1D537]] with the symmetries $C_{ijkl} = C_{jikl} = C_{klij}$.
For a rotation $Q \in SO(3)$, let $(Q \star C)_{ijkl} = Q_{ip} Q_{jq} Q_{kr} Q_{ls} C_{pqrs}$.
The symmetry group of the material is the set of $Q \in SO(3)$ with $Q \star C = C$.
The material is *anisotropic* if its symmetry group is not all of $SO(3)$, that is, if $Q \star C \ne C$ for some rotation $Q$.

## Remarks

Example: take an orthotropic material whose symmetry planes are the coordinate planes.
In Voigt notation its stiffness matrix is nonzero only in the upper left $3 \times 3$ block and on the last three diagonal places.
This gives $9$ independent constants.
If $C_{22} \ne C_{33}$, the rotation by $\pi/2$ about the $x_1$-axis maps $C$ to a tensor with $C_{22}$ and $C_{33}$ exchanged, so it is not a symmetry, and the material is anisotropic.
Non-example: an isotropic material, $C_{ijkl} = \lambda \delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \mu(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl} + \delta_{il}\delta_{jk})$, satisfies $Q \star C = C$ for every rotation.
A general anisotropic (triclinic) material has $21$ independent constants.
Up to conjugation, the possible symmetry groups give exactly $8$ classes of elasticity tensors: triclinic, monoclinic, orthotropic, trigonal, tetragonal, transversely isotropic, cubic and isotropic.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The elasticity tensor tells how a material stretches under a load.
The material is anisotropic when turning a sample changes its response, so some directions are stiffer than others.
