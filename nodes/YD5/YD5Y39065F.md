## Statement

Let $f \in \R[x, y]$ be a nonzero polynomial with zero set $C = \set{(x, y) \in \R^2 : f(x, y) = 0}$, a real plane [[algebraic curve|XJM2MT4S3Y]], and let $D \subset \R^2$ be a closed disc.
A planar linkage is a [[framework|V5YS9BZX0N]] of rigid bars joined at revolute joints, with some joints pinned to the plane.
Then there are a planar linkage and a joint $v$ of it with this property.
The set of positions of $v$ over all configurations of the linkage is exactly $C \cap D$.

## Remarks

Idea of the proof: write the position of a point $v$ with two arms of fixed length from a pinned joint, through two angles.
Then $f$ becomes a trigonometric polynomial in the two angles, that is, a sum of cosines of integer combinations of them.
Gadget linkages copy angles, add angles, multiply angles by integers and add vectors, and together they build a linkage that forces the sum to be $0$.
Alfred Kempe published the argument in 1876; his gadgets could move into degenerate positions, and complete proofs were given later with braced gadgets.

Example: a single bar of length $1$ pinned at the origin realises the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$; the [[Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage|PP2CAZ7QKE]] realises a segment of a straight line.

The disc $D$ is needed: a linkage has bars of bounded length, so all its joints stay in a bounded region.
So an unbounded curve such as a line cannot be traced entirely.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Any curve given by a polynomial equation can be drawn, within any bounded window, by a machine made only of rigid rods and hinges.
A circle needs only one rod pinned at one end; a curve of high degree may need a very large number of rods.

### L2 human

The theorem says that planar linkages are as expressive as real algebraic geometry in the plane.
Extensions show that every compact real algebraic set arises, up to suitable equivalence, from configuration spaces of linkages.
It connects mechanism design with [[rigidity of frameworks|V5YS9BZX0N]] and with the study of configuration spaces in topology.
