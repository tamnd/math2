## Summary

Let $U$ be a [[subspace|00YC1D652K]] of a vector space $V$.
A *complement* of $U$ in $V$ is a subspace $W$ with $V = U \oplus W$, that is, $U + W = V$ and $U \cap W = \{0\}$.
Every subspace of a vector space has a complement, by the [[existence theorem|0P9T8WHNMH]], but the complement is almost never unique.
All complements of $U$ are isomorphic to each other, since each is [[isomorphic to the quotient|244KTXDJHW]] $V / U$.

## Motivation

Given a subspace $U$, we often want to describe the rest of $V$.
The set difference $V \setminus U$ is not a subspace, and the quotient $V / U$ is not inside $V$.
A complement is a subspace of $V$ that plays the role of "the rest": each vector splits uniquely as a part in $U$ plus a part in $W$.
With a complement, we can extend linear maps from $U$ to $V$, build [[projections|033DA1WE8Y]] onto $U$, and choose bases of $V$ that start with a basis of $U$.

## Notation

We say that $U$ and $W$ are *complementary*, or that $W$ is a complement of $U$, and we write $V = U \oplus W$.
Mathlib writes `IsCompl U W`, the lattice-theoretic notion of complements in the lattice of subspaces.
The [[orthogonal complement|0XJXTBCB2T]] $U^\perp$ of an inner product space is one particular complement, and the notation $U^\perp$ is reserved for it.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The complements of the x-axis in the plane|S8TRB7953Y]] are exactly the lines $\R(m, 1)$ for $m \in \R$.
- [[A complement of a plane in R³|DVP2D04EZD]]: the line $\R(1, 0, 0)$ and the line $\R(1, 1, 1)$ are both complements of the plane $x + y + z = 0$.
- In $\R^3$, the $z$-axis is a complement of the $xy$-plane.
- In the space of square matrices, the skew-symmetric matrices form a complement of the symmetric matrices, when $2 \ne 0$ in the field.
- The subspaces $\{0\}$ and $V$ are complements of each other.

### Non-examples

- In $\R^3$, the $xz$-plane is not a complement of the $xy$-plane: the two planes meet in the $x$-axis.
- In $\R^3$, the $z$-axis is not a complement of the $x$-axis: they meet only in $0$, but their sum is a plane.
- [[The subgroup 2Z of Z has no complement|XK7YECSHR9]]. Over a ring that is not a field, complements can fail to exist.

### Basic properties

- If $W$ is a complement of $U$, then $W \cong V / U$, by the [[theorem on complements and quotients|244KTXDJHW]].
- In finite dimension, a complement $W$ of $U$ has $\dim W = \dim V - \dim U$.
- A subspace $W$ with $U \cap W = \{0\}$ and $\dim U + \dim W = \dim V$ is a complement, by the [[dimension criterion|P4JG9DJ2HH]].
- Complements correspond to [[projections|033DA1WE8Y]] with image $U$: the complement is the kernel of the projection.
- If $U \ne \{0\}$ and $U \ne V$, then $U$ has infinitely many complements over an infinite field.

### History

Complements appear in the early abstract treatments of finite-dimensional spaces, for example in Halmos's *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces* of 1942.
The existence of complements in infinite dimension depends on the axiom of choice.
In functional analysis, the question of which closed subspaces of a Banach space have closed complements became a central topic.

### Common errors

- Saying "the complement" of $U$. Complements are not unique, unlike the orthogonal complement in an inner product space.
- Confusing the complement subspace with the set complement $V \setminus U$, which does not contain $0$.
- Checking only $U \cap W = \{0\}$. The sum $U + W$ must also be all of $V$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A complement of a subspace $U$ fills the space together with $U$, without overlap.
In the plane, a complement of a line is any other line through the origin.
Every vector then splits into a piece along $U$ and a piece along the complement.

### L2 human

To find a complement of $U$ in $\R^n$, take a basis of $U$ and extend it to a basis of $\R^n$.

1. Let $U = \R(1, 1, 0)$ in $\R^3$.
2. The vectors $(1, 1, 0)$, $e_2 = (0, 1, 0)$ and $e_3 = (0, 0, 1)$ form a basis, since the matrix with these columns has determinant $1$.
3. So $W = \operatorname{span}(e_2, e_3)$, the $yz$-plane, is a complement of $U$.
4. Check: $(x, y, z) = x(1, 1, 0) + (0, y - x, z)$, and $U \cap W = \{0\}$ since $t(1, 1, 0)$ has first entry $t$.

Another extension, such as $(1, 1, 0)$, $e_1$, $e_3$, gives a different complement, the $xz$-plane.

### L3 human

In the lattice of subspaces of $V$, a complement of $U$ is a lattice complement: $U \wedge W = 0$ and $U \vee W = V$.
This lattice is modular and complemented, which is a key property of vector spaces among modules.
For modules over a ring, a submodule with a complement is a direct summand, and the ring is semisimple exactly when every submodule of every module has a complement.
In topological vector spaces one also asks for closed complements, which can fail to exist: $c_0$ has no closed complement in $\ell^\infty$.
