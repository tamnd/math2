## Summary

A complement of a subspace $U$ is a second subspace that supplies exactly the directions that $U$ is missing, with no overlap.
Together the two subspaces rebuild the whole space, and every vector splits in one way into a part in each.
Precisely, let $U$ be a [[subspace|00YC1D652K]] of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$.
A *complement* of $U$ in $V$ is a subspace $W$ of $V$ with
$$V = U \oplus W, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad U + W = V \quad \text{and} \quad U \cap W = \{0\}.$$
Every subspace has a complement, by the [[existence theorem|0P9T8WHNMH]], but a complement is almost never unique.
All complements of $U$ are still isomorphic to each other, because each one is [[isomorphic to the quotient|244KTXDJHW]] $V / U$.

## Motivation

Once we fix a subspace $U$, we often want to talk about "the rest of $V$".
The set difference $V \setminus U$ does not help, because it does not even contain $0$.
The [[quotient space|0BR3FY5Q8M]] $V / U$ describes the rest well, but its elements are cosets, not vectors of $V$.
A complement is a way to keep the rest inside $V$: it is a subspace whose vectors stand for the directions that $U$ does not cover.

With a complement in hand, several constructions become possible.
We can build the [[projection|033DA1WE8Y]] onto $U$ along $W$, which keeps the $U$-part of each vector.
We can extend a linear map from $U$ to all of $V$ by sending $W$ to $0$.
We can choose a basis of $V$ that starts with a basis of $U$, so that the matrices of maps that preserve $U$ have a block of zeros.
Complements return in the theory of [[exact sequences|1GYTT98CC5]], where they make every short exact sequence split, and in inner product spaces, where the [[orthogonal complement|0XJXTBCB2T]] is a preferred choice.

## Notation

We say that $U$ and $W$ are *complementary* subspaces, or that $W$ is a complement of $U$, and we write $V = U \oplus W$.
The relation is symmetric, so $U$ is then also a complement of $W$.
Mathlib writes `IsCompl U W`, which is the notion of complements in the lattice of subspaces.
The notation $U^\perp$ is reserved for the [[orthogonal complement|0XJXTBCB2T]], which is one particular complement and needs an inner product.

## Remarks

### All the complements of a line

Take the $x$-axis $U = \R(1, 0)$ in the plane.
A complement must be a line through $0$, because the dimensions must add up to $2$.
Any line other than $U$ itself works, so the complements are the lines $\R(m, 1)$, one for each real number $m$.
For the line with slope parameter $m$ the splitting of a vector is
$$(x, y) = (x - m y, 0) + y (m, 1).$$
For $(3, 2)$ this gives $(3, 0) + (0, 2)$ when $m = 0$, and $(1, 0) + (2, 2)$ when $m = 1$.
So the $U$-part of a vector depends on the complement that we choose.
[[The complements of the x-axis in the plane|S8TRB7953Y]] works this out in full.

### A complement of a plane in space

Let $U$ be the plane $x + y + z = 0$ in $\R^3$.
Any line through $0$ that does not lie in $U$ is a complement.
The $x$-axis is one, with the splitting
$$(x, y, z) = (x + y + z)(1, 0, 0) + (-y - z, y, z),$$
where the second vector lies in $U$ because $(-y - z) + y + z = 0$.
The line $\R(1, 1, 1)$ is another, and it is the orthogonal complement of $U$.
[[Two complements of a plane in R³|DVP2D04EZD]] checks both, and [[a complementary subspace that is not the orthogonal complement|NFHJSAH156]] compares a complement with $U^\perp$.

More examples follow the same pattern.
In $\R^3$ the $z$-axis is a complement of the $xy$-plane.
The subspaces $\{0\}$ and $V$ are complements of each other.
Over a field in which $2 \ne 0$, the skew-symmetric $n \times n$ matrices form a complement of the symmetric ones, by the [[decomposition into symmetric and skew-symmetric parts|K284W6D15Z]].

### What is not a complement

Both conditions of the definition can fail on their own.
The $xz$-plane is not a complement of the $xy$-plane in $\R^3$: the sum is $\R^3$, but the two planes meet in the $x$-axis.
The $z$-axis is not a complement of the $x$-axis: they meet only in $0$, but their sum is the $xz$-plane.
Over a ring that is not a field, a complement can fail to exist at all, and [[the subgroup 2Z of Z has no complement|XK7YECSHR9]] gives the smallest case.

### Why all complements look alike

Different complements are different subspaces, but they all have the same size.
Each coset $v + U$ meets a complement $W$ in exactly one vector, so the vectors of $W$ label the cosets, and this labeling is linear.
That is the theorem that [[every complement is isomorphic to the quotient|244KTXDJHW]], $W \cong V / U$.
In finite dimension it gives
$$\dim W = \dim V - \dim U$$
for every complement $W$.
In the other direction, a subspace $W$ with $U \cap W = \{0\}$ and $\dim U + \dim W = \dim V$ is a complement, by the [[dimension criterion|P4JG9DJ2HH]].

### Complements and projections

A complement $W$ of $U$ gives the projection onto $U$ along $W$, which sends $u + w$ to $u$.
Its image is $U$ and its kernel is $W$.
By the [[theorem on projections and decompositions|2ZX5Q7V4VN]], this sets up a bijection between the complements of $U$ and the projections with image $U$.
So choosing a complement and choosing a projection onto $U$ are the same act.

### Watch out

Do not say "the complement" of $U$.
If $U \ne \{0\}$ and $U \ne V$, then $U$ has more than one complement, and over an infinite field it has infinitely many.
For example, the lines $\R(m, 1)$ above are all different.
The phrase "the complement" is correct only for the orthogonal complement in an inner product space.

Do not confuse the complement with the set complement $V \setminus U$.
The set complement is never a subspace, since it misses $0$.

### Try it

First, is the line $\R(1, 2, 3)$ a complement of the plane $z = 0$ in $\R^3$?
Yes: the dimensions are $1 + 2 = 3$, and $t(1, 2, 3)$ has third entry $3t$, which is $0$ only for $t = 0$.

Second, find a complement of $U = \R(1, 1)$ in $\R^2$ that contains $(1, 0)$.
The line $\R(1, 0)$ works, since $(1, 0)$ is not a multiple of $(1, 1)$, and the decomposition is $(x, y) = y(1, 1) + (x - y)(1, 0)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A complement of a subspace fills the space together with it, without any overlap except the origin.
In the plane, a complement of a line through the origin is any other line through the origin.
Every arrow in the plane is then one step along the first line plus one step along the second.
For the horizontal axis and the line through $(1, 1)$, the arrow $(3, 2)$ is $(1, 0)$ plus $(2, 2)$.

### L2 human

The standard way to find a complement in $\R^n$ is to extend a basis.
Take $U = \R(1, 1, 0)$ in $\R^3$.
We add vectors to $(1, 1, 0)$ until we have a basis of $\R^3$, and we try $e_2 = (0, 1, 0)$ and $e_3 = (0, 0, 1)$.
The matrix with columns $(1, 1, 0)$, $e_2$, $e_3$ is
$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
which is lower triangular with diagonal entries $1$, so its determinant is $1$ and the three vectors form a basis.
So $W = \operatorname{span}(e_2, e_3)$, the $yz$-plane, is a complement of $U$.
Check: $(x, y, z) = x(1, 1, 0) + (0, y - x, z)$, and $t(1, 1, 0)$ lies in $W$ only when its first entry $t$ is $0$.
If we add $e_1$ and $e_3$ instead, we get the $xz$-plane, a different complement.
This is the usual way complements appear in practice: as the span of the vectors that complete a basis.

### L3 human

In the lattice of subspaces of $V$, a complement of $U$ is a lattice complement: $U \wedge W = 0$ and $U \vee W = V$.
The existence theorem says that this lattice is complemented, and it is also modular, so it is a complemented modular lattice.
For modules over a ring, a submodule with a complement is a direct summand, and a ring is [[semisimple|NY25HXA478]] exactly when every submodule of every module has a complement.
In a [[Banach space|06GZ956657]] one asks for a closed complement, and this can fail: the space $c_0$ of null sequences has no closed complement in $\ell^\infty$.
That analytic notion is the [[complemented subspace|Q0TY362K0V]].
