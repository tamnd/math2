## Definition

Let $T$ be a [[theory|92SWXST1ZM]] in a signature $L$.
A *chain of models* of $T$ is a family $(M_i)_{i \in I}$ of [[models|MVCH6N9SNW]] of $T$, indexed by a nonempty [[total order|0FRXBVVR03]] $(I, <)$, such that $M_i$ is a [[substructure|676H7EGTKJ]] of $M_j$ whenever $i < j$.
Its union $\bigcup_{i \in I} M_i$ is the $L$-structure on the union of the domains that contains each $M_i$ as a substructure, as in the [[elementary chain theorem|NHCRZ74JHH]].

The theory $T$ is *inductive* if the union of every chain of models of $T$ is a model of $T$.

A sentence is $\forall\exists$ if it has the form $\forall \bar x \, \exists \bar y \, \theta(\bar x, \bar y)$ with $\theta$ quantifier-free.
By the Chang-Łoś-Suszko theorem, $T$ is inductive if and only if $T$ is equivalent to a set of $\forall\exists$ sentences.

## Notation

Many books define an inductive theory as a theory axiomatized by $\forall\exists$ sentences, and then prove the equivalence with the chain condition.
Other names are $\forall\exists$ theory, $\forall_2$ theory and $\Pi_2$ theory.
One writes $T_{\forall\exists}$ for the set of $\forall\exists$ consequences of $T$; then $T$ is inductive if and only if $T_{\forall\exists}$ and $T$ have the same models.

## Remarks

It suffices to check chains indexed by ordinals, since every total order has a well-ordered cofinal subset, and the union over it is the same.

The easy half of the Chang-Łoś-Suszko theorem is part 2 of the elementary chain theorem: a theory axiomatized by $\forall\exists$ sentences is inductive.

Example: the theory of fields is inductive; see [[this example|25T9H3722Z]].

Example: every model complete theory is inductive, for instance the theory of dense linear orders; see [[this example|3NGJGNEB85]].

Non-example: the theory of discrete linear orders without endpoints is not inductive; see [[this example|7PSHWG43AG]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a growing sequence of structures, each one inside the next, like the fields $\Q \subseteq \Q(\sqrt 2) \subseteq \Q(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3) \subseteq \cdots$.
A theory is inductive if, whenever each structure in such a sequence obeys the axioms, the union of all of them obeys the axioms too.
Axioms of the form "for all $x$ there is a $y$ with ..." have this property, because each witness $y$ appears at some finite stage.

### L2 human

We check that the theory of fields is inductive, using $\forall\exists$ axioms.

1. The field axioms are universal, except the inverse axiom $\forall x \, \exists y \, (x = 0 \lor x \cdot y = 1)$, which is $\forall\exists$.
2. Let $(K_i)_{i \in I}$ be a chain of fields with union $K$, and let $a \in K$. Then $a \in K_i$ for some $i$.
3. If $a \neq 0$, the inverse $b$ of $a$ in $K_i$ satisfies $a \cdot b = 1$ in $K_i$, hence in $K$.
4. A universal axiom, like commutativity, involves finitely many elements. They all lie in one $K_j$, where the axiom holds, and the operations of $K$ restrict to those of $K_j$.

So $K$ is a field.
The typical use of the definition is the construction of [[existentially closed models|60HQMM380F]] by chains.
A trap is to look only at the given axioms: a theory whose axioms are not $\forall\exists$ can still be inductive, since it may be equivalent to $\forall\exists$ axioms.
