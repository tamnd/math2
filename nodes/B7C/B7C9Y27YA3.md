## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Its entries are integers, so we can read $A$ as a matrix over $\mathbb{R}$, over $\mathbb{C}$ or over the field $\mathbb{F}_5$ of integers modulo $5$.
We find its [[spectrum|04EHB7YDQS]] over each of the three fields.

1. By the [[definition of the characteristic polynomial|039HF58KQ5]],
$$p_A(t) = \det \begin{pmatrix} t & 1 \\ -1 & t \end{pmatrix} = t^2 + 1.$$
   Check: $\tr A = 0$ and $\det A = 0 \cdot 0 - (-1) \cdot 1 = 1$, so $t^2 - 0 \cdot t + 1$ agrees.
2. Over $\mathbb{R}$, we have $t^2 + 1 \ge 1 > 0$ for every real $t$.
   So $p_A$ has no real root, and the spectrum of $A$ over $\mathbb{R}$ is empty.
3. Over $\mathbb{C}$, the polynomial factors as $t^2 + 1 = (t - i)(t + i)$, so the spectrum is $\{i, -i\}$.
4. Check for $i$ with $v = (1, -i)$:
$$Av = (0 - (-i), 1 + 0) = (i, 1), \qquad iv = (i, -i^2) = (i, 1).$$
5. Check for $-i$ with $w = (1, i)$: $Aw = (-i, 1)$ and $-iw = (-i, -i^2) = (-i, 1)$.
6. Over $\mathbb{F}_5$, we have $2^2 + 1 = 5 = 0$ and $3^2 + 1 = 10 = 0$.
   So $t^2 + 1 = (t - 2)(t - 3)$ in $\mathbb{F}_5[t]$, and the spectrum is $\{2, 3\}$.
7. Check for $2$ with $u = (2, 1)$: $Au = (-1, 2) = (4, 2)$ modulo $5$, and $2u = (4, 2)$.
8. Check for $3$ with $x = (3, 1)$: $Ax = (-1, 3) = (4, 3)$ modulo $5$, and $3x = (9, 3) = (4, 3)$.

## Remarks

The characteristic polynomial $t^2 + 1$ is the same over every field, but its roots depend on where we look for them.
The [[eigenvalues|0ZND7NQ970]] are the roots in the field of scalars, so the spectrum changes with the field.

Two more fields show the other cases.
Over $\mathbb{F}_3$, the values $0^2 + 1 = 1$, $1^2 + 1 = 2$ and $2^2 + 1 = 5 = 2$ are all nonzero, so the spectrum is empty, just as over $\mathbb{R}$.
Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, we have $t^2 + 1 = (t + 1)^2$, so $1$ is the only eigenvalue, and it is a double root.
Check: $A(1, 1) = (-1, 1) = (1, 1)$ modulo $2$.

Over $\mathbb{C}$ the spectrum can never be empty for a square matrix of size at least $1$, by the [[existence of an eigenvalue over an algebraically closed field|08CJB2NQ92]].
The empty real spectrum is the reason why real matrices are often studied over $\mathbb{C}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

As a map of the real plane, $A$ is a quarter turn about the origin.
No line through the origin stays where it is, so there is no real stretch factor.
If complex numbers are allowed, the same matrix has the two eigenvalues $i$ and $-i$.
So the answer to "what are the eigenvalues?" depends on which numbers you allow.

### L2 human

The typical use of the example is to explain why a spectrum must always come with its field.
The steps are the same over every field: write $p_A$, then look for its roots in that field.
Watch out in $\mathbb{F}_5$: an answer such as $Au = (-1, 2)$ looks different from $2u = (4, 2)$ until you reduce modulo $5$.
