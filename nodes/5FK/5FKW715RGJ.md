## Summary

Let $L$ be a [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]] and let $M$ be an $L$-[[structure|983GZBQYT4]].
The *age* of $M$ is the class of all finitely generated $L$-structures that [[embed|SCRB74SWQN]] into $M$.
Equivalently, it is the class of structures [[isomorphic|PZMGQMDN25]] to a finitely generated [[substructure|676H7EGTKJ]] of $M$.
A class $\mathcal{K}$ of finitely generated structures has the *hereditary property* (HP) when every finitely generated substructure of a member of $\mathcal{K}$ is isomorphic to a member of $\mathcal{K}$.
It has the *joint embedding property* (JEP) when any two members embed into a common member.
Every age has HP and JEP, and in a countable signature a class with HP, JEP and at most countably many isomorphism types is the age of a [[countable|01TQB88H4C]] structure.
The age records the finite pieces of a structure, and Fraïssé theory rebuilds a homogeneous structure from its age.

## Motivation

A countable structure is often too large to describe directly.
Its finite pieces are easy to list: for $(\Q, <)$ they are the finite linear orders, and for the Rado graph they are the finite graphs.
Two structures can have the same finite pieces and still differ, as $(\Z, <)$ and $(\Q, <)$ show.
The age is the precise name for the list of finite pieces.
Fraïssé asked which lists arise as ages, and when the list determines the structure up to isomorphism.
The answer uses the age together with the [[amalgamation property|XSCSX39GEZ]] and [[ultrahomogeneity|H8VM37SSAX]].

## Notation

We write $\operatorname{Age}(M)$ for the age of $M$.
Hodges uses this notation, and some authors write $\mathcal{K}(M)$.
The age is closed under isomorphism, so one often fixes one representative of each isomorphism type and speaks of a set.
In a finite relational signature "finitely generated" means "finite", and the age is the class of finite structures that embed into $M$.
The formal definition is [[this definition|HBE89GZ79M]].

## Remarks

### Examples

- The age of $(\Q, <)$ is the class of all finite linear orders, and so is the age of $(\Z, <)$; see [[this example|1B07YPD0SC]].
- The age of the [[Rado graph|RMXR0FEF54]] is the class of all finite graphs, because every finite graph is an induced subgraph of it.
- The age of the additive group $(\Z, +, -, 0)$ is the class of trivial and infinite cyclic groups; see [[this example|MXTQVT7SQ7]].
- The age of an infinite set in the empty signature is the class of all finite sets, including the empty set.
- The age of a finite structure $M$ is the class of structures isomorphic to substructures of $M$.

### Non-examples

- The finite linear orders with an even number of elements are not an age: HP fails, because a $2$-element order has a $1$-element substructure; see [[this non-example|VDQCA78MNQ]].
- The finite graphs that are complete or have no edges are not an age: JEP fails, since no such graph contains both an edge and a non-edge.

### Basic properties

- $\operatorname{Age}(M)$ has HP, since a finitely generated substructure of a finitely generated substructure of $M$ is again one of $M$.
- $\operatorname{Age}(M)$ has JEP: if $A$ and $B$ embed into $M$, the substructure generated by the two images is finitely generated and contains copies of both.
- If $L$ is countable and $M$ is countable, the age has at most countably many isomorphism types, since $M$ has countably many finite subsets.
- If $M$ embeds into $N$, then $\operatorname{Age}(M) \subseteq \operatorname{Age}(N)$.
- In a finite relational signature, $\operatorname{Age}(M) \subseteq \operatorname{Age}(N)$ holds exactly when every existential sentence true in $M$ is true in $N$.

### History

Roland Fraïssé introduced the age (French *âge*) of a relational structure in work of the early 1950s.
He used it to characterize the countable structures in which isomorphisms between finite substructures extend to automorphisms.

### Common errors

- To take only finite substructures in a signature with function symbols. The age of $(\Z, +, -, 0)$ contains the infinite group $\Z$, which is finitely generated.
- To expect the age to determine the structure. $(\Z, <)$ and $(\Q, <)$ have the same age; only among ultrahomogeneous countable structures does the age determine the structure.
- To forget the empty structure. When $L$ has no constant symbols, the empty set generates the empty substructure, and it belongs to every age under the usual convention.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The age of a structure is the collection of all small pieces that one can find inside it.
For the rational numbers with their order, a small piece is a finite set of rationals in increasing order, such as $1 < 2 < 7$.
Every finite chain of any length can be found among the rationals, so the age is "all finite chains".
The integers have exactly the same small pieces, even though the integers have gaps and the rationals do not.
So the age is a list of ingredients, not a full recipe.

### L2 human

We compute the age of the graph $C_4$, the cycle on the vertices $1, 2, 3, 4$ with edges $12, 23, 34, 41$.

1. The signature is one binary relation, so substructures are induced subgraphs on subsets of $\set{1, 2, 3, 4}$.
2. Size $0$ and $1$: the empty graph and one vertex.
3. Size $2$: the pair $\set{1, 2}$ gives an edge, and $\set{1, 3}$ gives a non-edge.
4. Size $3$: every $3$-subset, such as $\set{1, 2, 3}$, induces a path with two edges.
5. Size $4$: the whole cycle $C_4$.
6. So $\operatorname{Age}(C_4)$ consists of the graphs isomorphic to one of these six graphs.

The typical use of the age is as the input of Fraïssé's theorem: one starts from a class of finite structures and asks for a countable structure with exactly that age.
A trap is to include the triangle: $C_4$ has no three pairwise adjacent vertices, so the triangle is not in its age.

### L3 human

In a finite relational signature, the age of $M$ and the universal theory of $M$ determine each other, because each finite structure is described by one existential sentence.
Classes with HP, JEP, the [[amalgamation property|XSCSX39GEZ]] and countably many isomorphism types are exactly the ages of countable [[ultrahomogeneous structures|H8VM37SSAX]], by [[Fraïssé's theorem|V5CKNFP3HC]].
Many countable structures can share one age, as $(\Z, <)$ and $(\Q, <)$ do, and among them at most one is ultrahomogeneous.
In topological dynamics, the age of an ordered structure carries the Ramsey property of the [[Kechris-Pestov-Todorcevic correspondence|96Q4P7PZVY]].
