## Statement

Every linear map whose characteristic polynomial splits is, in exactly one way, a diagonalizable map plus a nilpotent map that commutes with it.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear.
Assume that the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] $p_T$ splits into linear factors over $F$, which holds for every $T$ when $F$ is [[algebraically closed|0PF6TM8TWT]], for example $F = \C$.
Then there is exactly one pair of linear maps $S, N \colon V \to V$ with these three properties:

1. $T = S + N$;
2. $S$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] and $N$ is [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]];
3. $S N = N S$.

In addition, $S = g(T)$ and $N = h(T)$ for some polynomials $g, h \in F[t]$, in the sense of [[polynomials of an endomorphism|0E3MV6E6MG]].
Since $p_T$ splits, "diagonalizable" in property 2 is the same as [[semisimple|08VRX3Q4PB]].

## Remarks

### A first decomposition

Take $T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
The obvious split is the scalar part plus the rest:
$$T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The first matrix is diagonal, the second squares to $0$, and they commute because the first is $2I$.
So $S = 2I$ and $N = T - 2I$, and both are polynomials in $T$: the constant $2$ and the polynomial $t - 2$.
For a larger matrix, the [[3 × 3 worked example|4P9QZRCJND]] finds $S = -A^2 + 4A - 2I$, a polynomial in $A$ that no one would guess from the entries.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Commutation is what makes the pair unique.
For $T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, the split $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2) + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has a diagonal part and a nilpotent part, but $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ times the second matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, while the product in the other order is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Since $T$ has the distinct eigenvalues $1$ and $2$, it is diagonalizable itself, and the true decomposition is $S = T$ and $N = 0$.

The splitting hypothesis is needed for $S$ to be diagonalizable over $F$.
The rotation by a right angle over $\R$ has no real eigenvalue, so it is not diagonalizable over $\R$, and its decomposition in the semisimple sense is $S = T$, $N = 0$.

Without the splitting hypothesis, the semisimple version below needs a perfect field.
Over the imperfect field $\mathbb{F}_2(s)$, the [[imperfect field example|C720K6S84D]] shows that multiplication by $x$ on $F[x]/((x^2 + s)^2)$ has no decomposition with $S$ semisimple, $N$ nilpotent and $S N = N S$.

### What it gives you

The decomposition separates two kinds of behavior: $S$ stretches along eigenvectors, and $N$ shears.
So $T$ is diagonalizable exactly when $N = 0$, and $T$ is nilpotent exactly when $S = 0$.
Since $S$ and $N$ are polynomials in $T$, they commute with every map that commutes with $T$.
The eigenvalues of $S$ are those of $T$, with the same algebraic multiplicities, and in a Jordan basis $S$ is the diagonal of the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].

The commuting parts make powers and exponentials easy.
If $N^2 = 0$, the [[binomial theorem for commuting matrices|DS9TS6AFMW]] gives
$$T^k = (S + N)^k = S^k + k S^{k-1} N,$$
and in the same way the [[matrix exponential|020M04FN1H]] is $e^T = e^S e^N = e^S (I + N)$.

### The converse

Suppose that $T = S + N$ with $S$ diagonalizable over $F$, $N$ nilpotent and $S N = N S$.
Then $N$ maps each eigenspace $E_\mu$ of $S$ into itself, and on $E_\mu$ the map $T$ is $\mu$ plus a nilpotent map, with characteristic polynomial $(t - \mu)^{\dim E_\mu}$.
So $p_T = p_S$, and $p_T$ splits.
The splitting hypothesis is therefore necessary as well as sufficient.

### Generalizations

Let $F$ be a [[perfect field|VWBVKT794W]], for example a field of characteristic $0$, a finite field or an algebraically closed field.
Then every $T$ has exactly one decomposition $T = S + N$ with $S$ semisimple, $N$ nilpotent and $S N = N S$, and again $S$ and $N$ are polynomials in $T$.
Here $S$ is diagonalizable over an algebraic closure of $F$.
The Mathlib theorem `Module.End.exists_isNilpotent_isSemisimple` gives the existence part of this version, with $S$ and $N$ in the subalgebra generated by $T$.
For linear algebraic groups there is a multiplicative form $g = g_s g_u$ with $g_u$ unipotent, and semisimple Lie algebras have an abstract Jordan decomposition; both belong to later areas.

## History

The theorem is named after Camille Jordan and Claude Chevalley.
In Jordan's normal form of a matrix the two parts are already visible, as the diagonal and the part above it.
Chevalley used the decomposition into semisimple and nilpotent parts in his work on Lie algebras and algebraic groups.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every square matrix over the complex numbers splits into two parts.
One part only stretches, by different amounts in different directions.
The other part becomes zero after a few powers, like a shear that slides a stack of cards.
The two parts commute, and there is only one way to split the matrix like this.

### L2 human

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, with $p_A = (t - 1)(t - 2)^2$, as in the [[worked example|4P9QZRCJND]].

1. The polynomial $g(t) = -t^2 + 4t - 2$ has $g(1) = 1$, $g(2) = 2$ and $g'(2) = 0$, so it is $1$ on the generalized eigenspace for $1$ and $2$ on the generalized eigenspace for $2$.
2. So
$$S = g(A) = -A^2 + 4A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
3. Then
$$N = A - S = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad N^2 = 0.$$
4. The two products agree:
$$S N = N S = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 2 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
5. $S$ has the eigenvectors $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$ for $2$, and $(1, 1, -1)$ for $1$, so it is diagonalizable.

The typical use is to separate the stretching part $S$ from the shearing part $N$, and then to compute powers and exponentials.
The trap is to take the diagonal entries of $A$ as $S$: the matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1, 3, 1)$ does not commute with $A - \operatorname{diag}(1, 3, 1)$.

### L3 human

In module language, $S$ acts on each primary component $\ker (T - \lambda_i)^{m_i}$ as the scalar $\lambda_i$, so the theorem is a corollary of the [[decomposition into generalized eigenspaces|0Z3GDY89T5]].
The polynomial $g$ with $g(T) = S$ solves the congruences $g \equiv \lambda_i \bmod (t - \lambda_i)^{m_i}$, which have a solution by the Chinese remainder theorem.
Over a perfect field, the semisimple part can be computed by a Newton iteration on the squarefree part of $m_T$, without factoring $m_T$.
The same idea gives the Jordan form of the sub-area on canonical forms: once $S$ is known, only the nilpotent map $N$ on each eigenspace of $S$ remains to be classified.
