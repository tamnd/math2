## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear.
Assume that the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] of $T$ splits over $F$.
This holds for every $T$ when $F$ is [[algebraically closed|0PF6TM8TWT]], for example $F = \C$.
Then there are unique linear maps $S, N \colon V \to V$ with these properties:

1. $T = S + N$.
2. $S$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] and $N$ is [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]].
3. $S N = N S$.

In addition, $S$ and $N$ are [[polynomials in $T$|0E3MV6E6MG]].
Since $p_T$ splits, "diagonalizable" in part 2 is the same as [[semisimple|08VRX3Q4PB]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Commutation is needed for uniqueness. For $T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, the splitting $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2) + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has a diagonalizable and a nilpotent part, but they do not commute. Since $T$ is diagonalizable, the true decomposition is $S = T$ and $N = 0$.
- The splitting hypothesis is needed for $S$ to be diagonalizable over $F$. The rotation by a right angle over $\R$ has $S = T$, which is semisimple but not diagonalizable over $\R$.
- Without the splitting hypothesis, the field must be perfect for the semisimple version below. Over the imperfect field $\mathbb{F}_2(s)$, the [[companion matrix of (x² + s)²|~d837395a]] has no such decomposition with $S$ semisimple.

### Consequences

- $T$ is diagonalizable exactly when $N = 0$, and $T$ is nilpotent exactly when $S = 0$.
- $S$ and $N$ commute with every map that commutes with $T$, since they are polynomials in $T$.
- The eigenvalues of $S$ are those of $T$, with the same algebraic multiplicities. In a Jordan basis, $S$ is the diagonal part of the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].
- Since $S N = N S$, the matrix exponential satisfies $e^T = e^S e^N$, and $e^N$ is a finite sum. This helps to compute the [[matrix exponential|020M04FN1H]].
- The polynomial $g$ with $g(T) = S$ can be chosen without constant term. If $0$ is not an eigenvalue, add the congruence $g \equiv 0 \bmod t$ in the construction of $g$, by the Chinese remainder theorem.

### Converse

If $T = S + N$ with $S$ diagonalizable, $N$ nilpotent and $S N = N S$, then $p_T = p_S$, and $p_T$ splits.
So the splitting hypothesis is also necessary for a decomposition with diagonalizable $S$.

### Generalizations

Let $F$ be a [[perfect field|VWBVKT794W]], for example a field of characteristic $0$, a finite field or an algebraically closed field.
Then every $T$ has a unique decomposition $T = S + N$ with $S$ semisimple, $N$ nilpotent and $S N = N S$, and $S$, $N$ are polynomials in $T$.
Here $S$ is diagonalizable over the algebraic closure.
The Mathlib statement `Module.End.exists_isNilpotent_isSemisimple` gives the existence part of this version over a perfect field, with $S$ and $N$ in the subalgebra generated by $T$.
For algebraic groups there is a multiplicative version $g = g_s g_u$ with $g_u$ unipotent, and for Lie algebras there is an abstract Jordan decomposition; these belong to the theory of algebraic groups and to Lie theory.

## History

Jordan's normal form of 1870 contains the decomposition for a single matrix in the split case, as its diagonal and off-diagonal parts.
Chevalley used the decomposition into semisimple and nilpotent parts systematically in his work on algebraic groups and Lie algebras, which gave the theorem its double name.
The proof by polynomials in $T$ is in Hoffman and Kunze, Section 6.8.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every square matrix over the complex numbers splits into two parts.
One part is diagonal in a suitable basis.
The other part becomes zero after a few powers.
The two parts commute, and there is only one way to split the matrix like this.

### L2 human

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, with $p_A = (t - 1)(t - 2)^2$, as in the [[worked example|~502fc18d]].

1. $S = -A^2 + 4 A - 2 I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, a polynomial in $A$.
2. $N = A - S = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and $N^2 = 0$.
3. $S N = N S = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 2 & 0 \\ -2 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. $S$ has the eigenvectors $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$ for $2$, and $(1, 1, -1)$ for $1$. So $S$ is diagonalizable.

The typical use is to separate the "stretching" part $S$ of $T$ from the "shearing" part $N$, for powers and exponentials.
The trap is to take the diagonal entries of $A$ as $S$: $\operatorname{diag}(1, 3, 1)$ does not commute with $A - \operatorname{diag}(1, 3, 1)$.

### L3 human

In module language, $S$ acts on each primary component $\ker (T - \lambda_i)^{m_i}$ as the scalar $\lambda_i$.
The polynomial $g$ with $g(T) = S$ solves the congruences $g \equiv \lambda_i \bmod (t - \lambda_i)^{m_i}$, by the Chinese remainder theorem.
Over a perfect field, the semisimple part is found by a Newton iteration on the squarefree part of $m_T$, without factoring $m_T$.
