## Proof

*Part 1: linearity.*

1. Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, and let $V^*$ be its [[dual space|07JBSZE0V4]]. By the [[definition|0M856Q1AMZ]], $V^*$ has pointwise operations.
2. Fix $v \in V$. For $f, g \in V^*$ and $a, b \in F$, we have $\operatorname{ev}(v)(af + bg) = (af + bg)(v) = a f(v) + b g(v)$.
3. The right side of step 2 equals $a \operatorname{ev}(v)(f) + b \operatorname{ev}(v)(g)$. So $\operatorname{ev}(v)$ is a [[linear form|07YQ1C78G0]] on $V^*$, that is, $\operatorname{ev}(v) \in V^{**}$.
4. Let $v, w \in V$ and $a, b \in F$. For every $f \in V^*$, linearity of $f$ gives $\operatorname{ev}(av + bw)(f) = f(av + bw) = a f(v) + b f(w)$.
5. The right side of step 4 equals $(a \operatorname{ev}(v) + b \operatorname{ev}(w))(f)$. Since this holds for all $f$, the map $\operatorname{ev}$ is [[linear|0NRYHJPQG2]].

*Part 2: injectivity.*

6. We show that the kernel of $\operatorname{ev}$ is $\set{0}$. Let $v \in V$ with $v \ne 0$.
7. The set $\set{v}$ is [[linearly independent|0CRMGPAH83]], because $v \ne 0$. So it [[extends to a basis|0M4SXE5WW3]] $B$ of $V$ with $v \in B$.
8. By the [[extension property of bases|0R8X9XA83A]], there is a linear map $f \colon V \to F$ with $f(v) = 1$ and $f(b) = 0$ for $b \in B \setminus \set{v}$. Then $f \in V^*$.
9. Then $\operatorname{ev}(v)(f) = f(v) = 1 \ne 0$. So $\operatorname{ev}(v) \ne 0$.
10. By steps 6 to 9, the kernel of $\operatorname{ev}$ is $\set{0}$. So $\operatorname{ev}$ is injective, by the [[kernel criterion|0BJBYCGMWE]].

*Part 3, "if": finite dimension.*

11. Now let $V$ be finite-dimensional with $n = \dim V$. By the [[dimension of the dual space|00889YD15K]], $V^*$ is finite-dimensional with $\dim V^* = n$.
12. Applying the same result to $V^*$, the space $V^{**}$ is finite-dimensional with $\dim V^{**} = \dim V^* = n$.
13. By the [[rank-nullity theorem|0W4FSW1TVB]] and step 10, $\dim \im(\operatorname{ev}) = \dim V - 0 = n$.
14. Let $u_1, \dots, u_n$ be a basis of $\im(\operatorname{ev})$. These are $n$ linearly independent vectors of $V^{**}$, a space of dimension $n$.
15. By the [[criterion for bases|096V7H13H9]], $u_1, \dots, u_n$ is a basis of $V^{**}$. So $\im(\operatorname{ev})$ contains their span $V^{**}$, and $\operatorname{ev}$ is surjective.
16. By steps 5, 10 and 15, $\operatorname{ev}$ is a bijective linear map. So it is a [[linear isomorphism|0B8T10CE7N]].

*Part 3, "only if": infinite dimension.*

17. Let $V$ be infinite-dimensional, with a basis $B$. For $b \in B$, let $b^* \in V^*$ be the coordinate form with $b^*(b) = 1$ and $b^*(c) = 0$ for $c \in B \setminus \set{b}$, by step 8.
18. The family $(b^*)_{b \in B}$ is linearly independent. If $\sum_{b \in S} a_b b^* = 0$ with $S$ finite, then evaluation at $c \in S$ gives $a_c = 0$.
19. Let $s \in V^*$ be the form with $s(b) = 1$ for all $b \in B$, which exists by the extension property of bases.
20. $s$ is not in the span of the $b^*$. A finite combination $\sum_{b \in S} a_b b^*$ is $0$ at each $c \in B \setminus S$, and this set is not empty because $B$ is infinite. But $s(c) = 1$.
21. So the family $(b^*)_{b \in B}$ together with $s$ is linearly independent. It extends to a basis $D$ of $V^*$, by the theorem on [[extension to a basis|0M4SXE5WW3]].
22. By the extension property of bases, applied to $V^*$ and $D$, there is $\Phi \in V^{**}$ with $\Phi(b^*) = 0$ for all $b \in B$, $\Phi(s) = 1$, and $\Phi = 0$ on the rest of $D$.
23. Suppose $\Phi = \operatorname{ev}(v)$ for some $v \in V$. Write $v = \sum_{b \in B} x_b b$ with finitely many $x_b \ne 0$.
24. Then $x_b = b^*(v) = \operatorname{ev}(v)(b^*) = \Phi(b^*) = 0$ for every $b \in B$. So $v = 0$.
25. Then $\Phi = \operatorname{ev}(0) = 0$, which contradicts $\Phi(s) = 1$.
26. So $\Phi$ is not in the image of $\operatorname{ev}$, and $\operatorname{ev}$ is not surjective. This proves the "only if" of part 3, by contraposition.

## Idea

To see that $v \ne 0$ is not sent to zero, we need one linear form that does not vanish at $v$.
Extending $v$ to a basis gives such a form.
In finite dimension, $V$ and $V^{**}$ have the same dimension, so an injective linear map between them is onto.
In infinite dimension, the coordinate forms do not span $V^*$.
A form on $V^*$ that kills all coordinate forms but not the sum of coordinates cannot come from a vector.

## Variants

- Steps 13 to 15 can be replaced by the fact that an injective linear map between spaces of the same finite dimension is surjective.
- The "only if" also follows from the [[Erdős-Kaplansky theorem|S5ET1HG2ZX]]: $\dim V^{**} > \dim V$ for infinite-dimensional $V$, so no linear map $V \to V^{**}$ is surjective.
- Injectivity can be proved without a basis of all of $V$ if a complement of the line $Fv$ is given; the existence of a complement in general also needs Zorn's lemma.
