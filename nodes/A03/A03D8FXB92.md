## Proof

We check linearity from the definitions, prove injectivity with a form that does not vanish at a given vector, and then compare dimensions.
For the failure in infinite dimension we build an element of $V^{**}$ that comes from no vector.

*Part 1: linearity.*

1. Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$; by the [[definition of the dual space|0M856Q1AMZ]], the operations of $V^*$ are pointwise.
2. Fix $v \in V$; for $f, g \in V^*$ and $a, b \in F$, we have $\operatorname{ev}(v)(af + bg) = (af + bg)(v) = a f(v) + b g(v)$.
3. The right side of step 2 is $a \operatorname{ev}(v)(f) + b \operatorname{ev}(v)(g)$, so $\operatorname{ev}(v)$ is a [[linear form|07YQ1C78G0]] on $V^*$, that is, $\operatorname{ev}(v) \in V^{**}$.
4. Let $v, w \in V$ and $a, b \in F$; for every $f \in V^*$, linearity of $f$ gives $\operatorname{ev}(av + bw)(f) = f(av + bw) = a f(v) + b f(w)$.
5. The right side of step 4 is $(a \operatorname{ev}(v) + b \operatorname{ev}(w))(f)$, and this holds for all $f$, so $\operatorname{ev}$ is [[linear|0NRYHJPQG2]].

*Part 2: injectivity.*

6. We show that the kernel of $\operatorname{ev}$ is $\{0\}$, so let $v \in V$ with $v \ne 0$.
7. The set $\{v\}$ is [[linearly independent|0CRMGPAH83]] because $v \ne 0$, so it [[extends to a basis|0M4SXE5WW3]] $B$ of $V$ with $v \in B$.
8. By the theorem that [[linear maps are determined by a basis|0R8X9XA83A]], there is a form $f \in V^*$ with $f(v) = 1$ and $f(b) = 0$ for $b \in B \setminus \{v\}$.
9. Then $\operatorname{ev}(v)(f) = f(v) = 1 \ne 0$, so $\operatorname{ev}(v) \ne 0$.
10. So the kernel of $\operatorname{ev}$ is $\{0\}$, and $\operatorname{ev}$ is injective by the [[kernel criterion|0BJBYCGMWE]].

*Part 3, "if": finite dimension.*

11. Now let $V$ be finite-dimensional with $n = \dim V$; by the [[dimension of the dual space|00889YD15K]], $\dim V^* = n$.
12. The same theorem applied to $V^*$ gives $\dim V^{**} = \dim V^* = n$.
13. By the [[rank-nullity theorem|0W4FSW1TVB]] and step 10, $\dim \im(\operatorname{ev}) = \dim V - 0 = n$.
14. So $\im(\operatorname{ev})$ contains $n$ independent vectors of the $n$-dimensional space $V^{**}$, which form a basis of $V^{**}$ by the [[right-size criterion for a basis|096V7H13H9]].
15. Hence $\im(\operatorname{ev}) = V^{**}$, and $\operatorname{ev}$ is surjective.
16. By steps 5, 10 and 15, $\operatorname{ev}$ is a bijective linear map, so it is a [[linear isomorphism|0B8T10CE7N]].

*Part 3, "only if": infinite dimension.*

17. Let $V$ be infinite-dimensional with a basis $B$, and for $b \in B$ let $b^* \in V^*$ be the coordinate form with $b^*(b) = 1$ and $b^*(c) = 0$ for $c \in B \setminus \{b\}$.
18. The family $(b^*)_{b \in B}$ is independent, since evaluating $\sum_{b \in S} a_b b^* = 0$ at $c \in S$ gives $a_c = 0$.
19. Let $s \in V^*$ be the form with $s(b) = 1$ for all $b \in B$, which exists by the theorem on bases.
20. A finite combination $\sum_{b \in S} a_b b^*$ is $0$ at each $c \in B \setminus S$, and this set is not empty because $B$ is infinite, while $s(c) = 1$.
21. So $s$ is not in the span of the $b^*$, and the family $(b^*)_{b \in B}$ together with $s$ is independent.
22. By the same extension theorem as in step 7, this family extends to a basis $D$ of $V^*$.
23. By the theorem on bases applied to $V^*$ and $D$, there is $\Phi \in V^{**}$ with $\Phi(b^*) = 0$ for all $b \in B$, $\Phi(s) = 1$, and $\Phi = 0$ on the rest of $D$.
24. Suppose for a contradiction that $\Phi = \operatorname{ev}(v)$, and write $v = \sum_{b \in B} x_b b$ with finitely many $x_b \ne 0$.
25. Then $x_b = b^*(v) = \operatorname{ev}(v)(b^*) = \Phi(b^*) = 0$ for every $b \in B$, so $v = 0$.
26. But then $\Phi = \operatorname{ev}(0) = 0$, which contradicts $\Phi(s) = 1$.
27. So $\Phi$ is not in the image of $\operatorname{ev}$, and $\operatorname{ev}$ is not surjective, which proves the "only if" of part 3.

## Idea

To see that a nonzero vector $v$ is not sent to zero, we need one linear form that does not vanish at $v$.
Extending $v$ to a basis gives such a form, because a form may take any values on a basis.
In finite dimension, $V$ and $V^{**}$ have the same dimension, so an injective linear map between them is onto.
In infinite dimension, the coordinate forms do not span $V^*$, and that leaves room for a form on $V^*$ that kills every coordinate form but not their "sum" $s$.
Such a form cannot be evaluation at a vector, because a vector is determined by its coordinates.

## Variants

Steps 13 to 15 can be replaced by one fact: an injective linear map between spaces of the same finite dimension is surjective.

The "only if" also follows from the [[Erdős-Kaplansky theorem|S5ET1HG2ZX]].
For infinite-dimensional $V$ it gives $\dim V^{**} > \dim V^* > \dim V$, so no linear map $V \to V^{**}$ is surjective.
That route is shorter to state but uses much more cardinal arithmetic.

For injectivity one does not need a basis of all of $V$; a complement of the line $Fv$ is enough, since a form can be $1$ on $v$ and $0$ on the complement.
In general, the existence of such a complement also needs Zorn's lemma.
