## Definition

A *test of general relativity* is an observation or experiment whose result is predicted by [[general relativity|7M7M55TKXA]] and is compared with the measured value.
The test is informative when the prediction of general relativity differs from that of [[Newtonian gravity|29WRN5J730]] or of a competing theory of gravity.
The *classical tests* are the three tests proposed by Einstein:

1. the [[perihelion precession of Mercury|VS3C49R7T0]];
2. the [[gravitational deflection of light|JJCKCYTBXD]] by the Sun;
3. the [[gravitational redshift|TJ74QPATFP]] of light.

## Remarks

The [[Shapiro time delay|FT8T5PHDVQ]] of radar signals is often called the fourth classical test.
Tests of [[frame-dragging|WFCMWBVAEB]] and of the [[geodetic effect|31ZYBXSC50]] probe the rotation of gyroscopes in orbit.
Strong-field tests use binary pulsars and [[gravitational waves|SJ0A4TZTFR]] from merging black holes.
Weak-field results are often given as bounds on the parameters of the [[parameterized post-Newtonian formalism|2TAW8GAMM1]].
For example, the deflection of light and the Shapiro delay both measure the parameter $\gamma$, which equals $1$ in general relativity.
A non-example is the measurement of the orbit of a planet to Newtonian accuracy only: it cannot tell the theories apart, so it does not test general relativity.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A test of general relativity is any measurement where the theory makes a definite prediction that could have come out wrong.
The best tests are those where Newton's theory and general relativity disagree.
The three historical tests are the turning of Mercury's orbit, the bending of starlight by the Sun, and the slowing of clocks deep in a gravitational field.
