## Example

The [[equation of a plane|07SDJ5DNE4]] needs a nonzero normal, and the two cases below show what goes wrong without one.
The second case shows that three points on one line lead straight back to the first.

**Case 1: a zero normal.**
Consider the equation $0x + 0y + 0z = d$.

1. For every point $(x, y, z)$, the left side is $0$.
2. If $d = 5$, no point satisfies the equation, so the set is empty.
3. If $d = 0$, every point satisfies it, so the set is all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
4. Neither set is a plane, so the condition $n \neq 0$ in the definition is needed.

**Case 2: three points on one line.**
Let $A = (1, 1, 1)$, $B = (2, 3, 4)$ and $C = (3, 5, 7)$.

1. The edge vectors are $u = B - A = (1, 2, 3)$ and $v = C - A = (2, 4, 6)$, and $v = 2u$.
2. The [[cross product|0N5N15HWZ7]] is
   $$u \times v = (2 \cdot 6 - 3 \cdot 4, \; 3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 6, \; 1 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 2) = (0, 0, 0).$$
3. The usual method then gives the equation $0x + 0y + 0z = 0$, which is Case 1 with $d = 0$.
4. Indeed $A$, $B$ and $C$ lie on the line $A + t u$, and infinitely many planes contain this line.

## Remarks

Two of those planes are $x - 2y + z = 0$ and $3x - z = 2$.
Each contains all three points.
For the first, $1 - 2 + 1 = 0$, $2 - 6 + 4 = 0$ and $3 - 10 + 7 = 0$.
For the second, $3 - 1 = 2$, $6 - 4 = 2$ and $9 - 7 = 2$.
So the trouble in Case 2 is not that there is no plane through the points, but that there are too many.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A plane needs a direction that sticks out of it, and the zero arrow has no direction.
Three points on one line are like a stool with all three legs in a row: it can tip in many ways.
So the three points do not fix one plane.

### L2 human

Before using the cross product method, make sure the three points are not on one line.
The method gives this check for free: the points are on one line exactly when the cross product of the edge vectors is $0$.
Watch out: a computed normal $(0, 0, 0)$ is not an arithmetic slip to repair; it means that the plane is not unique.
