## Statement

The dual map sees the image and the kernel of a linear map the other way round: its kernel comes from the image, and its image comes from the kernel.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over $F$, and let $f \colon V \to W$ be a linear map.
Let $f^* \colon W^* \to V^*$ be its [[dual map|0C2TQMW5A1]], and write $S^0$ for the [[annihilator|0R6M158SQ9]] of a subspace $S$.

1. $\ker f^* = (\im f)^0$, where $\ker$ is the [[kernel|02YFME3RGW]] and $\im$ is the [[image|0JB228DCWB]].
2. $\im f^* = (\ker f)^0$.
3. $f$ is injective if and only if $f^*$ is surjective, and $f$ is surjective if and only if $f^*$ is injective.
4. If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $\rank f^* = \rank f$, where $\rank$ is the [[rank|0JGB7A6GTS]].
5. Let $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_k, \psi \in V^*$. If $\ker \varphi_1 \cap \dots \cap \ker \varphi_k \subseteq \ker \psi$, then $\psi$ is a linear combination of $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_k$.

Parts 1, 2, 3 and 5 hold in every dimension, and $W$ may be infinite-dimensional in part 4.
The [[proof|2EZNHS66FE]] uses the fact that every linear form on a subspace extends to the whole space.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Part 4 needs $\dim V < \infty$.
Take $V = W$ infinite-dimensional and $f = \id_V$.
Then $\rank f = \dim V$, while $f^* = \id_{V^*}$ has rank $\dim V^*$, and $\dim V^* > \dim V$ by the [[Erdős-Kaplansky theorem|S5ET1HG2ZX]].

Part 5 needs finitely many forms.
On the space $V = F^{(\N)}$ of finitely supported sequences, the coordinate forms $e_i^*$ have common kernel $\{0\}$, which lies in the kernel of every form.
The form $\psi$ with $\psi(e_i) = 1$ for all $i$ is not a finite combination of them, since such a combination vanishes on $e_i$ for all large $i$.

The field matters as well.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$, multiplication by $2$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ is injective, and its dual map is again multiplication by $2$ on $\Hom(\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, which is not surjective.
So part 3 fails for modules over a ring, because a form on $2\mathbb{Z}$ such as $2n \mapsto n$ does not extend to a form on $\mathbb{Z}$ with integer values.

### What it gives you

In finite dimension, part 4 and the theorem on the [[matrix of the dual map|0ZPB3180AY]] show that a matrix and its transpose have the same rank.
This is the [[proof by duality|7DQXA4AY1D]] that [[row rank equals column rank|0TZAG60ZYT]].

Part 5 with $k = 1$ says that $\ker \varphi_1 \subseteq \ker \psi$ forces $\psi = c\varphi_1$, which is the main step in the theorem on [[hyperplanes and linear forms|XR0EQN0H8T]].

Part 1 gives a solvability test for a linear system $Ax = b$: there is a solution exactly when $yb = 0$ for every row $y$ with $yA = 0$.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the row $y = (1, -1)$ has $yA = 0$.
So $b = (1, 2)^{\mathsf{T}}$ gives no solution, since $yb = -1$, while $b = (3, 3)^{\mathsf{T}}$ passes the test and $x = (3, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$ solves the system.
This is the [[Fredholm alternative|4HH8C9V8JQ]] in finite dimension.

### The converse

Part 5 has a converse: if $\psi = \sum c_i \varphi_i$, then $\psi$ vanishes wherever all the $\varphi_i$ vanish.
So $\ker \varphi_1 \cap \dots \cap \ker \varphi_k \subseteq \ker \psi$ holds exactly when $\psi$ lies in the span of $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_k$.

### Generalizations

For bounded operators between Banach spaces, functional analysis proves the analogue $\ker T' = (\im T)^\perp$ for the Banach space adjoint $T'$, where $(\im T)^\perp$ is the annihilator in the [[continuous dual|4A82S5FVRT]].
The analogue of part 2 holds with equality when the range of $T$ is closed, by the closed range theorem, and only up to a closure in general.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The dual map turns measuring rules on $W$ into measuring rules on $V$.
A rule on $W$ becomes the zero rule exactly when it gives zero on every output of $f$.
The rules on $V$ that come from $W$ are exactly those that give zero on every vector that $f$ sends to zero.
For example, if $f(x, y) = (x, 0)$, the rule "take the second coordinate" on the target becomes zero, and only rules that ignore $y$ come back to the source.

### L2 human

We check parts 1 and 2 for $f \colon \R^3 \to \R^2$, $f(x, y, z) = (x + y, \, x + y)$.
The image of $f$ is the line of vectors $(t, t)$, and the forms on $\R^2$ that vanish on it are the multiples of $(a, b) \mapsto a - b$.
For a form $\varphi(a, b) = pa + qb$, substitute to get the pulled back form:
$$f^*(\varphi)(x, y, z) = p(x + y) + q(x + y) = (p + q)(x + y).$$
So $f^*(\varphi) = 0$ exactly when $q = -p$, that is, when $\varphi$ is a multiple of $a - b$, and this is part 1.
The kernel of $f$ is the plane $x + y = 0$, spanned by $(1, -1, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 1)$.
A form $rx + sy + tz$ vanishes on both vectors exactly when $r = s$ and $t = 0$, so $(\ker f)^0$ is the set of multiples of $x + y$.
By the display, $\im f^*$ is the same set, and this is part 2.
Both ranks are $1$, as part 4 says.
The typical use is to prove that $f$ is surjective by showing that $f^*$ is injective, which is often easier.
Watch out: $\im f^*$ lies in $V^*$, not in $W^*$, so compare it with the annihilator of $\ker f \subseteq V$.

### L3 human

Parts 1 and 2 say that the dual functor $V \mapsto V^*$ is exact over a field: it turns an exact sequence $U \to V \to W$ into an exact sequence $W^* \to V^* \to U^*$.
Part 2 rests on the extension of forms from a subspace, which uses a basis and so, in infinite dimension, the axiom of choice.
Over a commutative ring $R$, the functor $\Hom(-, R)$ is only left exact, and part 2 can fail, as the example over $\mathbb{Z}$ above shows.
In finite dimension, the theorem and the [[double dual|0ZJM9D7NPS]] also give $\ker f = (\im f^*)^0$ inside $V^{**} \cong V$.
