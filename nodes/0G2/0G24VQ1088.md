## Statement

The products $AB$ and $BA$ of two rectangular matrices can have very different sizes, but after we add the identity they have the same determinant.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $m, n \ge 1$, let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix over $K$, and let $B$ be an $n \times m$ matrix over $K$.
Then
$$\det(I_m + AB) = \det(I_n + BA),$$
where $I_m$ and $I_n$ are the [[identity matrices|06DW9BAWB1]] of sizes $m$ and $n$, and $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].

## Remarks

### A first check with a column and a row

Take the column $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and the row $B = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, so $m = 2$ and $n = 1$.
The product $AB$ is $2 \times 2$, and we add $I_2$:
$$I_2 + AB = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 6 & 9 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(I_2 + AB) = 36 - 24 = 12.$$
The product $BA$ is the $1 \times 1$ matrix $(3 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 2) = (11)$, so
$$\det(I_1 + BA) = 1 + 11 = 12.$$
The two sides agree, and the right side needed no $2 \times 2$ determinant at all.

### Two identities with one name

Watch out for the name "Sylvester's determinant theorem".
Some books use it for the identity $\det(I_m + AB) = \det(I_n + BA)$.
Others use it for [[Sylvester's determinant identity|7SSKBQYFW1]], a different statement about determinants of bordered minors.
To avoid the clash we call the present result the Weinstein-Aronszajn identity, after Alexander Weinstein and Nachman Aronszajn.
When you meet the name "Sylvester" in a source, check which of the two formulas it means.

### Why the identity matrices are needed

Both products $AB$ and $BA$ exist because the inner sizes match.
Without the identity matrices the statement fails when $m \ne n$.
For the column $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and the row $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ we get
$$\det(AB) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = 0, \qquad \det(BA) = \det(1) = 1.$$
In fact $\det(AB) = 0$ whenever $m > n$, by the [[Cauchy-Binet formula|08WE2W7DYS]], since $A$ has no $m \times m$ minors.
The ring must be commutative, as in every use of the determinant.

### What it gives you

For a column $u$ and a row $v^{\mathsf{T}}$, the right side is a $1 \times 1$ determinant, and the identity reads $\det(I_m + u v^{\mathsf{T}}) = 1 + v^{\mathsf{T}} u$.
This is the heart of the [[matrix determinant lemma|0BC3Q6K92H]].
In general the identity trades a determinant of size $m$ for one of size $n$, which pays off when one of the two sizes is small.

The identity also compares eigenvalues.
Apply it over the ring $K[t, t^{-1}]$ with $-A/t$ in place of $A$, and multiply both sides by $t^{m+n}$:
$$t^n \det(t I_m - AB) = t^m \det(t I_n - BA).$$
So the [[characteristic polynomials|0VSSPAFM2C]] of $AB$ and $BA$ differ only by a power of $t$.
Over a field this means that $AB$ and $BA$ have the same nonzero [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]], with the same algebraic multiplicities.

### Generalizations

Let $X$ be an invertible $m \times m$ matrix.
Factor $X + AB = X (I_m + X^{-1} A B)$ and apply the identity to the pair $X^{-1} A$ and $B$:
$$\det(X + AB) = \det X \cdot \det(I_n + B X^{-1} A).$$
This is the general form of the matrix determinant lemma.
The identity also holds for Fredholm determinants of bounded operators $A$ and $B$ between Hilbert spaces when $AB$ and $BA$ are trace class.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Multiply a column of two numbers by a row of two numbers, and you get a square table of four numbers.
Multiply them in the other order, and you get a single number.
These two results look nothing alike.
Yet if you add $1$ along the diagonal of each, the determinant of the table equals the single number plus $1$.
For the column $(1, 2)$ and the row $(1, 1)$, the table plus the identity has determinant $2 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 2 = 4$, and the number is $1 + 1 + 2 = 4$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: compute $\det(I_3 + u v^{\mathsf{T}})$ for $u = (1, 2, 3)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $v = (1, 0, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
The direct way builds a $3 \times 3$ matrix:
$$I_3 + u v^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Expand along the second column, whose only nonzero entry is the $1$ in the middle:
$$\det(I_3 + u v^{\mathsf{T}}) = 1 \cdot \det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = 8 - 3 = 5.$$
The identity gives the same number with one line:
$$\det(I_1 + v^{\mathsf{T}} u) = 1 + (1 + 0 + 3) = 5.$$
So the identity turns a determinant of size $m$ into one of size $n$, and this is how it is used for low-rank matrices.
Watch out: the version $\det(I_m + AB) = \det(I_m + BA)$ makes no sense when $m \ne n$, because $BA$ has size $n$.

### L3 human

The identity is a polynomial identity in the entries of $A$ and $B$, so it is enough to prove it over the polynomial ring in those entries, and then every commutative ring follows.
The [[proof by two block factorizations|PYJ4W9RDFE]] avoids even that step.
In operator theory the version for [[Fredholm determinants|PPX0AK81QV]] is a basic tool of the perturbation theory of eigenvalues.
In statistics and machine learning it replaces $\det(I_m + A A^{\mathsf{T}})$ for a tall matrix $A$ with $m$ rows and $n$ columns by the much smaller $\det(I_n + A^{\mathsf{T}} A)$.
