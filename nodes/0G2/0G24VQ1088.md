## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $m, n \ge 1$, and let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix and $B$ an $n \times m$ matrix over $K$.
Then
$$\det(I_m + AB) = \det(I_n + BA),$$
where $I_m$ and $I_n$ are [[identity matrices|06DW9BAWB1]] and $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].

## Remarks

### A warning about the name

The name "Sylvester's determinant theorem" refers to two different identities in the literature.
Some authors use it for this identity.
Others use it for [[Sylvester's determinant identity|7SSKBQYFW1]] on bordered minors, which is a different statement.
This page uses the name Weinstein-Aronszajn identity to avoid confusion.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The ring must be commutative, as in every use of the determinant.
The sizes must match so that both $AB$ and $BA$ are defined.
The identity matrices cannot be dropped: $\det(AB) = \det(BA)$ is false for $m \ne n$.
For $A = (1, 0)^T$ and $B = (1, 0)$, $\det(AB) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = 0$, but $\det(BA) = 1$.

### Consequences

- For a column $u$ and a row $v^T$, it gives $\det(I_m + u v^T) = 1 + v^T u$; see the [[matrix determinant lemma|0BC3Q6K92H]].
- The two sides are determinants of different sizes, $m$ and $n$, so the identity can turn a large determinant into a small one.
- Replace $A$ by $-A/t$ and clear denominators: $t^n \det(t I_m - AB) = t^m \det(t I_n - BA)$.
- So $AB$ and $BA$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues, with the same algebraic multiplicities.

### Converse

The identity holds for all $A$ and $B$, so a converse in the usual sense does not apply.

### Generalizations

The same proof gives $\det(X + AB) = \det X \cdot \det(I_n + B X^{-1} A)$ for invertible $X$; this is the generalized [[matrix determinant lemma|0BC3Q6K92H]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The products $AB$ and $BA$ can have very different sizes.
Still, adding the identity to them gives the same determinant.
This can turn a large determinant into a small one.

### L2 human

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, so $m = 2$ and $n = 1$.

1. $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$, so $I_2 + AB = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 6 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $36 - 24 = 12$.
2. $BA = (3 + 8) = (11)$, so $\det(I_1 + BA) = 12$.

The two sides agree, and the right side needs no $2 \times 2$ determinant.
The typical use is to replace $\det(I_m + AB)$ by a smaller determinant when $n < m$.
A trap: $\det(I_m + AB) = \det(I_m + BA)$ has no meaning when $m \ne n$, since $BA$ is $n \times n$.

### L3 human

The identity holds for Fredholm determinants of operators $A$, $B$ with $AB$ and $BA$ of trace class.
It is a tool in the perturbation theory of eigenvalues.
