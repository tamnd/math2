## Summary

A symmetric matrix is a square matrix that does not change when you flip it across its main diagonal.
Symmetric matrices are the matrices that can move from one side of a dot product to the other, and they are the matrices of quadratic forms.
Exactly, a [[square matrix|0JXZKX526R]] $A$ is symmetric when it equals its [[transpose|09DXWYRD43]],
$$A^{\mathsf{T}} = A, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad a_{ij} = a_{ji} \text{ for all } i, j.$$
The [[formal definition|0N28QN4CYA]] states this rule.
Sums and scalar multiples of symmetric matrices are symmetric, but a product of two symmetric matrices is symmetric only when the two factors commute.

## Motivation

Many tables in practice are symmetric by nature.
A table of distances between cities, a covariance matrix in statistics and the Hessian matrix of a smooth function all have $a_{ij} = a_{ji}$.
In each case the quantity in position $(i, j)$ does not depend on the order of $i$ and $j$.

In algebra, the same condition says that $A$ moves across the [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]] without change:
$$(Ax) \cdot y = x \cdot (A^{\mathsf{T}} y) = x \cdot (Ay).$$
This is why symmetric matrices describe [[symmetric bilinear forms|0AXZNE5XNH]] and [[quadratic forms|RHVKMCPGPN]].
Over $\mathbb{R}$, the [[spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]] says that a symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

## Notation

The condition is written $A = A^{\mathsf{T}}$, and with the other notations for the transpose also $A = A^t$, $A = {}^t\!A$ or, in statistics, $A = A'$.
Some books write $\operatorname{Sym}_n(F)$ or $S_n(F)$ for the set of symmetric $n \times n$ matrices over $F$.
In Mathlib, `Matrix.IsSymm A` means `Aᵀ = A`; since Mathlib indexes matrices by arbitrary finite types, the condition is `A j i = A i j` for all indices `i` and `j` of the one index type.
For complex matrices, the condition $A^* = A$ with the [[conjugate transpose|0DXKE10PHF]] defines [[Hermitian|0P17NGQQMQ]] matrices, which are a different class.

## Remarks

### Three symmetric matrices

The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 7 \\ 7 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is symmetric, since its two entries off the diagonal are both $7$.
Every [[diagonal matrix|044JNC7ZAA]] is symmetric, because all its entries off the diagonal are $0$.

Every square matrix gives symmetric matrices, as in [[this example|VR8VKQA9BN]].
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, which is not symmetric,
$$A^{\mathsf{T}} A = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 14 \\ 14 & 20 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A + A^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 5 & 8 \end{pmatrix}.$$
In general $(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{\mathsf{T}} = A^{\mathsf{T}} (A^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}} = A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ by the [[transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]], and $(A + A^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}} = A^{\mathsf{T}} + A$.

### What is not symmetric

The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not symmetric, because $a_{12} = 2$ and $a_{21} = 0$.
A matrix that is not square is never symmetric, because an $m \times n$ matrix with $m \neq n$ has a transpose of the other size $n \times m$.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is not symmetric over $\mathbb{R}$; it is [[skew-symmetric|0PBY4E9GQF]], since $A^{\mathsf{T}} = -A$.

### Sums, powers and inverses

Let $A$ and $B$ be symmetric $n \times n$ matrices over a commutative ring and $c$ a scalar.
Then $A + B$ and $cA$ are symmetric, so over a field the symmetric matrices form a subspace of dimension $n(n+1)/2$: the entries on and above the diagonal can be chosen freely, and they fix the rest.
Each power $A^k$ is symmetric, since $(A^k)^{\mathsf{T}} = (A^{\mathsf{T}})^k = A^k$.
If $A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]], then $(A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} = (A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1} = A^{-1}$, so $A^{-1}$ is symmetric.
For every $n \times m$ matrix $P$, the matrix $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$ is symmetric, since its transpose is $P^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} P$.
Outside characteristic $2$, every square matrix is in exactly one way the sum of a symmetric and a skew-symmetric matrix, by [[this theorem|K284W6D15Z]].

### Watch out

A product of symmetric matrices is symmetric only when the factors commute: $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} = BA$, so $AB$ is symmetric exactly when $AB = BA$, by [[this theorem|YX9Z3E9SGN]].
In [[this example|3MD62SJJEP]], the symmetric matrices $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ have the product $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is not symmetric.

A second trap: the spectral theorem needs real entries.
The complex symmetric matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ has square $\begin{pmatrix} 1 + i^2 & i - i \\ i - i & i^2 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = 0$, so its only eigenvalue is $0$, and since it is not the zero matrix it cannot be diagonalized.

### History

In 1829 Cauchy proved, in the language of quadratic forms, that the characteristic roots of a real symmetric matrix are real.

### Try it

First, for which $a$ and $b$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & a \\ 4 & b \end{pmatrix}$ symmetric?
The condition $a_{12} = a_{21}$ forces $a = 4$, and $b$ can be any number, since diagonal entries never matter.

Second, compute $A A^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and check that it is symmetric.
$A A^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which is symmetric, while $A$ itself is not.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A symmetric matrix looks the same after you flip it over its main diagonal, the line from the top left corner to the bottom right corner.
The number in row $i$, column $j$ equals the number in row $j$, column $i$.
A table of distances between cities is symmetric, because the distance from one city to another is the same in both directions.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: the condition $A^{\mathsf{T}} = A$ lets you move $A$ across a dot product, $(Ax) \cdot y = x \cdot (Ay)$.
Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, $x = (1, 0)$ and $y = (1, 1)$.
Then $Ax = (2, 1)$ and $(Ax) \cdot y = 3$, while $Ay = (3, 4)$ and $x \cdot (Ay) = 3$.
Conversely, if this rule holds for all $x$ and $y$, then $A$ is symmetric, because with standard basis vectors $e_i \cdot (A e_j) = a_{ij}$ and $(A e_i) \cdot e_j = a_{ji}$.
This is the step that starts the proof of the spectral theorem: for eigenvectors $Au = \lambda u$ and $Av = \mu v$ with $\lambda \neq \mu$, we get $\lambda (u \cdot v) = (Au) \cdot v = u \cdot (Av) = \mu (u \cdot v)$, so $u \cdot v = 0$.

### L3 human

For a bilinear form $\beta$ on a free module $V$ with matrix $A$ in a basis, $\beta$ is symmetric if and only if $A$ is symmetric.
A change of basis by an invertible $P$ replaces $A$ by the congruent matrix $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$, and congruence keeps symmetry; classifying symmetric matrices up to congruence is the classification of quadratic forms, as in Sylvester's law of inertia over $\mathbb{R}$.
Outside characteristic $2$, the symmetric matrices are the $(+1)$ eigenspace of the transpose on $M_n(F)$, and they form a Jordan algebra under $A \circ B = (AB + BA)/2$, though not an associative subalgebra.
