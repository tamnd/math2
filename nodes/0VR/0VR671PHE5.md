## Summary

A symmetric matrix is a [[square matrix|0JXZKX526R]] that is equal to its [[transpose|09DXWYRD43]]: $A^{\mathsf{T}} = A$.
The [[formal definition|0N28QN4CYA]] says $a_{ij} = a_{ji}$ for all $i$ and $j$.
Symmetric matrices form a subspace of $M_n(F)$ of dimension $n(n+1)/2$, but they are not closed under products.
The [[product of two symmetric matrices|~85664979]] is symmetric exactly when the two matrices commute.

## Motivation

Symmetric matrices are the matrices of [[symmetric bilinear forms|0AXZNE5XNH]] and of [[quadratic forms|RHVKMCPGPN]].
Distance tables, covariance matrices and the Hessian matrix of a smooth function are symmetric.
Over $\mathbb{R}$, the [[spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]] says that a symmetric matrix has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The matrices AᵀA, AAᵀ and A + Aᵀ|~fca1333e]] are symmetric for every square $A$.
- Every [[diagonal matrix|044JNC7ZAA]] is symmetric.
- $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 7 \\ 7 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is symmetric.

### Non-examples

- The [[product of two symmetric matrices that do not commute|~10cd1d09]] is not symmetric.
- $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not symmetric, because $a_{12} = 2$ and $a_{21} = 0$.
- A non-square matrix is never symmetric, because $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ has a different size.

### Basic properties

Let $A$ and $B$ be symmetric $n \times n$ matrices over a commutative ring $R$, and let $c \in R$.

- $A + B$ and $cA$ are symmetric.
- $A^k$ is symmetric for each $k \ge 0$, because $(A^k)^{\mathsf{T}} = (A^{\mathsf{T}})^k$ by the [[transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]].
- If $A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]], then $A^{-1}$ is symmetric.
- $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$ is symmetric for every $n \times m$ matrix $P$.
- $AB$ is symmetric if and only if $AB = BA$, by [[this theorem|~85664979]].
- Outside characteristic $2$, each square matrix is the sum of a symmetric and a [[skew-symmetric|0PBY4E9GQF]] matrix in one way only; see [[this theorem|~3a63c44b]].

### Common errors

- Assuming that $AB$ is symmetric when $A$ and $B$ are.
- Using "symmetric" for complex matrices where [[Hermitian|0P17NGQQMQ]] is meant. A complex symmetric matrix need not have real eigenvalues.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A symmetric matrix looks the same after we flip it over its main diagonal.
The entry in row $i$, column $j$ equals the entry in row $j$, column $i$.
A table of distances between cities is symmetric, because the distance from one city to another is the same both ways.

### L2 human

The equation $A^{\mathsf{T}} = A$ means that $(Ax) \cdot y = x \cdot (Ay)$ for all column vectors $x$ and $y$.
So a symmetric matrix is a matrix that moves freely across the dot product.
The rule $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$ reverses the order, so the product $AB$ is symmetric only when the order does not matter.

### L3 human

For a bilinear form $\beta$ on $V$ with matrix $A$ in a basis, $\beta$ is symmetric if and only if $A$ is symmetric.
A change of basis by $P$ replaces $A$ by $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$, a congruence, and congruence keeps symmetry.
The symmetric matrices form a Jordan algebra under $A \circ B = (AB + BA)/2$ outside characteristic $2$.
