## Summary

The *adjugate* $\operatorname{adj} A$ of a square matrix $A$ over a commutative ring is the [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]] of its matrix of [[cofactors|0XPKR18APA]]: $(\operatorname{adj} A)_{ij} = C_{ji}$.
It satisfies $A \operatorname{adj} A = \operatorname{adj} A \cdot A = (\det A) I_n$.
So it is the inverse up to the factor $\det A$, and it exists for every square matrix, also for singular ones and over rings where one cannot divide.

## Motivation

Solving $Ax = b$ by Cramer's rule gives $x_i$ as a ratio of determinants, and the numerators are linear in $b$ with cofactors as coefficients.
Collecting these coefficients in a matrix gives the adjugate, and $x = (\det A)^{-1} \operatorname{adj} A \, b$.
Since the entries of $\operatorname{adj} A$ are polynomials in the entries of $A$, the adjugate replaces the inverse in arguments over a commutative ring, where row reduction is not available.

## Notation

We write $\operatorname{adj} A$ or $\operatorname{adj}(A)$; some books write $A^{\operatorname{adj}}$ or $\tilde{A}$.
Older texts call it the *classical adjoint*; it is not the adjoint of an operator on an inner product space.
In Mathlib it is `Matrix.adjugate`.

## Remarks

### Examples

- $\operatorname{adj} \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$.
- The adjugate of an invertible $3 \times 3$ matrix and the inverse it gives; see the [[worked example|BDHT6DBGC9]].
- The adjugate of a singular $3 \times 3$ matrix of rank $2$, which has rank $1$; see the [[singular example|H98NXA00NZ]].
- $\operatorname{adj} I_n = I_n$, and $\operatorname{adj} 0 = 0$ for $n \ge 2$.

### Non-examples

- The cofactor matrix itself, without the transpose, is not the adjugate, and $A C \neq (\det A) I_n$ in general; see the [[non-example|2BJC4DHCMK]].
- The conjugate transpose $A^{*}$ is the adjoint of $A$ for the standard inner product, not the adjugate.

### Basic properties

- $A \operatorname{adj} A = \operatorname{adj} A \cdot A = (\det A) I_n$, by the [[adjugate identity|0528HKWZ1Z]].
- If $\det A$ is a unit, then $A^{-1} = (\det A)^{-1} \operatorname{adj} A$, by the [[inverse via the adjugate|0ENVM493VK]].
- $\operatorname{adj}(AB) = \operatorname{adj} B \operatorname{adj} A$, $\operatorname{adj}(A^{T}) = (\operatorname{adj} A)^{T}$ and $\det \operatorname{adj} A = (\det A)^{n-1}$; see the [[product rule for adjugates|3KDKBZ0WK2]].
- Over a field, $\operatorname{adj} A$ has rank $n$, $1$ or $0$ when $A$ has rank $n$, $n - 1$ or less; see the [[rank of the adjugate|9CJME5W96T]].
- $\operatorname{adj} A$ is a polynomial in $A$, by the Cayley-Hamilton theorem; it is the derivative of $\det$ in [[Jacobi's formula|357FEAZ0Q4]].

### Common errors

- Forgetting the transpose: $(\operatorname{adj} A)_{ij}$ is $C_{ji}$, not $C_{ij}$.
- Forgetting the signs of the cofactors.
- Believing that a singular matrix has adjugate $0$; this holds only when the rank is at most $n - 2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To build the adjugate, replace each entry by its cofactor, and then flip the result across the diagonal.
The adjugate is almost the inverse: it is the inverse times the determinant.
For $2 \times 2$ matrices, swap the two diagonal entries and change the sign of the other two.

### L2 human

To compute $\operatorname{adj} A$, compute all $n^2$ cofactors, place $C_{ij}$ in position $(j, i)$, and check that $A \operatorname{adj} A$ is a multiple of $I_n$.
The typical uses are the formula for the inverse, Cramer's rule, and proofs over rings such as $\mathbb{Z}$ or $K[x]$.
A trap: the adjugate costs $n^2$ determinants of order $n - 1$, so it is not a good way to invert a large matrix.

### L3 human

The adjugate is the matrix of $\Lambda^{n-1} A$, read through the isomorphism $\Lambda^{n-1} K^n \cong (K^n)^{*} \otimes \Lambda^n K^n$; this explains the transpose and the signs.
For the generic matrix over $\mathbb{Z}[x_{ij}]$, $\operatorname{adj} X = (\det X) X^{-1}$ in the field of fractions, and identities for $\operatorname{adj}$ follow from identities for $X^{-1}$.
