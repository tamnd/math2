## Summary

The adjugate of a square matrix is the matrix that inverts it up to one scalar factor, the determinant, and it exists even when the matrix has no inverse.
For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over a commutative ring, the *adjugate* $\operatorname{adj} A$ is the [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]] of the matrix of [[cofactors|0XPKR18APA]]:
$$(\operatorname{adj} A)_{ij} = C_{ji} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ji},$$
where $M_{ji}$ is the minor that deletes row $j$ and column $i$.
The [[formal definition|0GMPYM0NSR]] states it, with the convention $\operatorname{adj}(a) = (1)$ for $n = 1$.
Its main property is the identity
$$A \operatorname{adj} A = \operatorname{adj} A \cdot A = (\det A) I_n.$$

## Motivation

How do you write down the inverse of a matrix by a formula, and not by an algorithm?
For a $2 \times 2$ matrix, most students learn to swap the diagonal entries, change the signs of the other two, and divide by $ad - bc$.
The adjugate is the general form of that recipe.
Its entries are signed determinants of size $n - 1$, so they are polynomials in the entries of $A$, and no division happens until the last step.

This matters wherever division is not available or not wanted.
Over the integers, or over a polynomial ring such as $K[x]$, the adjugate still exists and still satisfies $A \operatorname{adj} A = (\det A) I_n$.
That is why it appears in proofs over rings: [[Cramer's rule|0977KZ6PQB]], the test for [[invertibility over a commutative ring|03Q7RE2HXR]], and a standard proof of the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]].
It returns in calculus as the derivative of the determinant, in [[Jacobi's formula|357FEAZ0Q4]].

## Notation

Most books write $\operatorname{adj} A$ or $\operatorname{adj}(A)$, and some write $A^{\operatorname{adj}}$ or $\tilde{A}$.
Older texts call it the *classical adjoint* or simply the *adjoint*.
Watch out: that adjoint is not the adjoint $A^{*}$ of an operator on an inner product space, which for real matrices is the transpose $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
In Mathlib the adjugate is `Matrix.adjugate`.

## Remarks

### The 2 by 2 case

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ the cofactors are $C_{11} = d$, $C_{12} = -c$, $C_{21} = -b$ and $C_{22} = a$.
Transposing places $C_{21} = -b$ in position $(1, 2)$, so
$$\operatorname{adj} A = \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}.$$
For example, $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\det A = 24 - 14 = 10$, and
$$A \operatorname{adj} A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 6 & -7 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 24 - 14 & -28 + 28 \\ 12 - 12 & -14 + 24 \end{pmatrix} = 10 I_2.$$
So $A^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{10} \operatorname{adj} A$, which is the school formula.

### Larger examples

For the invertible matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$ with determinant $-3$, the [[worked example|BDHT6DBGC9]] computes all nine cofactors, checks that $A \operatorname{adj} A = -3 I_3$, and reads off the inverse.
For the singular matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$, the [[singular example|H98NXA00NZ]] finds an adjugate that is not zero: it has rank $1$, and its columns lie in the kernel.
Two extreme cases are quick to check: $\operatorname{adj} I_n = I_n$, because every cofactor of $I_n$ off the diagonal is $0$, and $\operatorname{adj} 0 = 0$ for $n \ge 2$, because every minor of the zero matrix is $0$.

### What it is not

The cofactor matrix $C = (C_{ij})$ itself is not the adjugate.
For the $3 \times 3$ invertible matrix above, the product $AC$ has the entry $-2$ in position $(1, 2)$, so it is not a multiple of $I_3$; the [[non-example|2BJC4DHCMK]] works this out.
The conjugate transpose $A^{*}$ is not the adjugate either.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ it is $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 7 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, and $A A^{*}$ has the entry $8 + 42 = 50$ off the diagonal.

### The rules you can use

The identity $A \operatorname{adj} A = \operatorname{adj} A \cdot A = (\det A) I_n$ is the [[adjugate identity|0528HKWZ1Z]].
When $\det A$ is a unit, it gives $A^{-1} = (\det A)^{-1} \operatorname{adj} A$, the [[inverse via the adjugate|0ENVM493VK]].
The adjugate reverses products, $\operatorname{adj}(AB) = \operatorname{adj} B \operatorname{adj} A$, it commutes with the transpose, and $\det \operatorname{adj} A = (\det A)^{n-1}$; the [[product rule for adjugates|3KDKBZ0WK2]] proves these.
Over a field, $\operatorname{adj} A$ has rank $n$, $1$ or $0$ when $A$ has rank $n$, $n - 1$ or at most $n - 2$, by the [[rank of the adjugate|9CJME5W96T]].

### The adjugate is a polynomial in the matrix

For $n = 2$ you can check that $\operatorname{adj} A = (\operatorname{tr} A) I_2 - A$, since $(a + d) I_2 - A = \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$.
For $n = 3$ the Cayley-Hamilton theorem gives $\operatorname{adj} A = A^2 - (\operatorname{tr} A) A + c I_3$, where $c$ is the sum of the three principal $2 \times 2$ minors.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$ we have $\operatorname{tr} A = 16$ and $c = -3 - 11 + 2 = -12$, and
$$A^2 - 16 A - 12 I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 30 & 36 & 45 \\ 66 & 81 & 102 \\ 109 & 134 & 169 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 16 & 32 & 48 \\ 64 & 80 & 96 \\ 112 & 128 & 160 \end{pmatrix} - 12 I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 & -3 \\ 2 & -11 & 6 \\ -3 & 6 & -3 \end{pmatrix},$$
which is the adjugate found in the worked example.

### Watch out

Two slips are common.
The first is to forget the transpose: $(\operatorname{adj} A)_{12}$ is $C_{21}$, not $C_{12}$, and the two differ unless the cofactor matrix is symmetric.
The second is to think that a singular matrix has adjugate $0$.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $0$, the adjugate is $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and only the product $A \operatorname{adj} A$ is $0$.

### Try it

Find the adjugate of $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and use it to invert the matrix.
The adjugate is $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, and since the determinant is $6 - 5 = 1$, it is also the inverse.

What is $\operatorname{adj}(2 I_3)$?
Each diagonal cofactor is $\det(2 I_2) = 4$ and the others are $0$, so $\operatorname{adj}(2 I_3) = 4 I_3$, and indeed $2 I_3 \cdot 4 I_3 = 8 I_3 = \det(2 I_3) I_3$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To build the adjugate, replace each entry by its cofactor, and then flip the whole table across the main diagonal.
The result is almost the inverse: it is the inverse multiplied by the determinant.
For a $2 \times 2$ matrix the recipe is short: swap the two diagonal entries and change the sign of the other two.
For $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ this gives $\begin{pmatrix} 6 & -7 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, and dividing by the determinant $10$ gives the inverse.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use over the integers: invert
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
which has $\det A = 1$ because it is triangular with ones on the diagonal.
The cofactors of row $1$ are $C_{11} = 1$, $C_{12} = 0$ and $C_{13} = 0$.
The cofactors of row $2$ are $C_{21} = -(2 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0) = -2$, $C_{22} = 1$ and $C_{23} = -(1 \cdot 0 - 2 \cdot 0) = 0$.
The cofactors of row $3$ are $C_{31} = 2 \cdot 3 - 0 \cdot 1 = 6$, $C_{32} = -(1 \cdot 3 - 0 \cdot 0) = -3$ and $C_{33} = 1$.
Transposing gives
$$\operatorname{adj} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 & 6 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: row $1$ of $A$ times column $3$ of $\operatorname{adj} A$ is $6 - 6 + 0 = 0$, and the other entries of $A \operatorname{adj} A$ match $I_3$ in the same way.
Since $\det A = 1$, the inverse is $\operatorname{adj} A$, and it has integer entries.
The problem shows why the adjugate is the tool of choice over a ring: it needs no division, so integer matrices stay integer.
For a large numerical matrix it is a poor method, because it costs $n^2$ determinants of size $n - 1$.

### L3 human

Over the generic matrix $X = (x_{ij})$ with entries in $\mathbb{Z}[x_{ij}]$, the adjugate is $(\det X) X^{-1}$ in the field of fractions.
Every identity for the adjugate that holds there holds over every commutative ring, by substitution, and this is the usual way to prove such identities.
In the [[exterior algebra|0MDMZ965VE]], the entries of $\operatorname{adj} A$ are, up to signs and a transpose, the entries of the matrix of $\Lambda^{n-1} A$ in the basis of wedge products of $n - 1$ standard vectors.
Over $\mathbb{R}$, the gradient of $\det$ at $A$ is $(\operatorname{adj} A)^{\mathsf{T}}$, so the adjugate measures how the determinant changes when the entries move.
