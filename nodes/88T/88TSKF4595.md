## Example

Let $K_{1,3}$ be the [[star|NJB31J8DZT]] with a centre $c$ and three leaves $a_1, a_2, a_3$, and with the three edges $c a_1$, $c a_2$, $c a_3$.
We test two false conclusions that students often draw from an average.

1. The [[degrees|0KS0KRDV4Y]] are $d(c) = 3$ and $d(a_1) = d(a_2) = d(a_3) = 1$.
2. The sum of the degrees is $3 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 6 = 2 \cdot 3$, as the [[handshaking lemma|0XAP8WHNES]] says for $3$ edges.
3. The average degree is $6/4 = 3/2$.
4. False conclusion 1: "every vertex has degree at least $3/2$". The leaf $a_1$ has degree $1 < 3/2$.
5. False conclusion 2: "some vertex has degree exactly $3/2$". Each degree is an integer, and $3/2$ is not an integer.
6. The correct conclusions of the [[averaging argument|HXP9VMXV8S]] hold: the centre has degree $3 \ge 3/2$, and each leaf has degree $1 \le 3/2$.

## Remarks

The example shows the exact content of an averaging argument.
The average gives one element on each side of it, and nothing about the other elements.
An argument that claims more is not an averaging argument, even when it starts with an average.

The example changes with the number of leaves.
The star $K_{1,r}$ with $r \ge 2$ leaves has degree sum $2r$ and average degree $2r/(r + 1)$, which lies strictly between $1$ and $2$.
So no vertex has the average degree, and the $r$ leaves are all below it.
The average is reached only when all the values are equal, as in a regular graph.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A star has one centre joined to three outer points.
The centre has three lines and each outer point has one line, so the average is one and a half.
No point has one and a half lines, and three of the four points are below the average.
The average only tells us that some point is at least as large as it, here the centre.

### L2 human

We show the false step in a wrong proof.
The wrong claim is: "in a graph with $m$ edges and $n$ vertices, every vertex has degree at least $2m/n$".

1. The wrong proof says: the average degree is $2m/n$, so each vertex has degree at least $2m/n$.
2. The step from "the average is $2m/n$" to "each value is at least $2m/n$" has no reason.
3. The star $K_{1,3}$ refutes the claim: $2m/n = 3/2$, and a leaf has degree $1$.

The typical correct use of the average is the statement "some vertex has degree at least $2m/n$".
A trap is the minimum degree: it can be much smaller than the average, and a bound on it needs another argument.
