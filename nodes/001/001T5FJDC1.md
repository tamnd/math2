## Summary

A block diagonal matrix is a matrix that falls apart into smaller square matrices that do not talk to each other.
It is a square [[block matrix|0ASDFEQS6P]] whose diagonal blocks $A_1, \dots, A_k$ are square and whose other blocks are all zero:
$$\operatorname{diag}(A_1, \dots, A_k) = \begin{pmatrix} A_1 & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & A_k \end{pmatrix}.$$
It is also called the direct sum $A_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus A_k$ of the blocks, and the [[formal definition|0M0SNKZ482]] fixes the sizes.
With all blocks of size $1 \times 1$ it is a [[diagonal matrix|044JNC7ZAA]].
Sums, products, powers and inverses are computed one block at a time.

## Motivation

Suppose that $V = U_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_k$ and that a linear map $T$ sends each $U_i$ into itself.
Take a basis of $V$ made of a basis of $U_1$, then a basis of $U_2$, and so on.
The image of a basis vector of $U_i$ stays in $U_i$, so its coordinates on the other groups are zero, and the matrix of $T$ is block diagonal.
So a block diagonal matrix is the matrix form of a map that splits into $k$ independent maps, one on each $U_i$.
This is why the search for [[invariant subspaces|0ACX5QT8TE]] is a search for a basis with small diagonal blocks.
Normal forms such as the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]] are block diagonal for this reason.

## Notation

The forms $\operatorname{diag}(A_1, \dots, A_k)$, $A_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus A_k$ and $\bigoplus_i A_i$ are all common.
In Mathlib, `Matrix.blockDiagonal` takes a family of blocks of one shape indexed by a type `o`, and its rows are indexed by the product type `m × o`.
`Matrix.blockDiagonal'` allows blocks of different sizes, and its rows are indexed by a sigma type.
So a reindexing is needed to compare these matrices with a matrix indexed by `Fin n`.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take $A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $A_2 = (5)$, and let $M = \operatorname{diag}(A_1, A_2)$.
By the [[block multiplication rule|WR3RCF5PSQ]], the off-diagonal blocks of $M^2$ are $A_1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot A_2 = 0$, so
$$M^2 = \operatorname{diag}(A_1^2, A_2^2) = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 10 & 0 \\ 15 & 22 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 25 \end{pmatrix}.$$
A $3 \times 3$ product became a $2 \times 2$ product and a $1 \times 1$ product.
[[Powers and the inverse of a block diagonal matrix|D5885TV9MW]] carries this out for all powers, and [[a rotation about an axis|TZCTBFEM13]] is $\operatorname{diag}(R_\theta, 1)$.

### What it is not

[[A block anti-diagonal matrix|PCAKQ72AW3]], with nonzero blocks only off the diagonal, is not block diagonal for any partition with two or more blocks.
A block upper triangular matrix with a nonzero block above the diagonal is not block diagonal either.
Its first block of coordinates is invariant, but the second one is not.

### The rules you can use

Let $A = \operatorname{diag}(A_1, \dots, A_k)$ and $B = \operatorname{diag}(B_1, \dots, B_k)$ with $A_i$ and $B_i$ of the same order $n_i$.

- $A + B = \operatorname{diag}(A_1 + B_1, \dots, A_k + B_k)$ and $AB = \operatorname{diag}(A_1 B_1, \dots, A_k B_k)$.
- $A^m = \operatorname{diag}(A_1^m, \dots, A_k^m)$, and $p(A) = \operatorname{diag}(p(A_1), \dots, p(A_k))$ for each polynomial $p$.
- $A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] if and only if each $A_i$ is, and then $A^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(A_1^{-1}, \dots, A_k^{-1})$.
- $A^{\mathsf{T}} = \operatorname{diag}(A_1^{\mathsf{T}}, \dots, A_k^{\mathsf{T}})$ and $\operatorname{tr} A = \sum_i \operatorname{tr} A_i$.
- Over a commutative ring, $\det A = \prod_i \det A_i$.

For the inverse, a two-sided inverse $N$ of $A$, cut in the same way, satisfies $A_i N_{ii} = I$ and $N_{ii} A_i = I$ by the block rule, so each $A_i$ is invertible.

### Watch out

The blocks must be square, or they do not sit on the main diagonal.
Block diagonal matrices need not commute: $\operatorname{diag}(A_1, A_2)$ and $\operatorname{diag}(B_1, B_2)$ commute exactly when $A_1 B_1 = B_1 A_1$ and $A_2 B_2 = B_2 A_2$.
A matrix can also split without looking block diagonal, because the groups of coordinates need not be consecutive.
For example,
$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$$
keeps the span of $e_1, e_3$ and the span of $e_2$, and in the basis order $e_1, e_3, e_2$ it becomes $\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, (3)\right)$.

### Try it

First, invert $\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, (4)\right)$.
The first block has determinant $1$ and inverse $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, so the answer is $\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, (1/4)\right)$.

Second, find the determinant and the trace of $M$ from the first computation.
They are $\det M = (4 - 6) \cdot 5 = -10$ and $\operatorname{tr} M = (1 + 4) + 5 = 10$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A block diagonal matrix has square blocks along the diagonal and zeros everywhere else.
Each block acts on its own group of coordinates and leaves the other groups alone.
So you can square it, invert it or find its determinant one block at a time.
For example, $\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, (2)\right)$ cubed is $\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, (8)\right)$.

### L2 human

The matrix of a map is block diagonal exactly when the basis splits into consecutive groups and the map keeps the span of each group.
Here is why.
Let $V = U \oplus W$ with bases $u_1, \dots, u_r$ of $U$ and $w_1, \dots, w_s$ of $W$, and use the basis $u_1, \dots, u_r, w_1, \dots, w_s$.
Column $j \le r$ of the matrix holds the coordinates of $T u_j$, and the bottom block of this column is zero exactly when $T u_j \in U$.
In the same way, the top right block is zero exactly when $T$ maps each $w_j$ into $W$.
If only $U$ is invariant, you get a block upper triangular matrix instead.
So making a matrix block diagonal means finding [[invariant subspaces|0ACX5QT8TE]] that together span $V$ as a direct sum.

### L3 human

A square matrix $A$ over a field $F$ makes $F^n$ a module over $F[x]$, with $x$ acting as $A$.
A block diagonal form $A_1 \oplus A_2$ is a splitting of this module as a direct sum of two submodules.
Over an algebraically closed field, the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]] writes each square matrix, up to similarity, as a block diagonal matrix of [[Jordan blocks|07PH4RX0X5]], and these blocks do not split further.
The same idea appears in representation theory, where a representation is block diagonal in a suitable basis when it is a direct sum of smaller representations.
