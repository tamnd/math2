## Summary

A generic proof is an argument on one particular example, chosen and presented so that each step uses only the properties that all cases share.
The reader sees in the one case the reason why the claim holds for every case.
A pre-formal proof is a correct argument that is not written in formal symbols: it uses actions with objects, pictures, or everyday language.
An action proof is a pre-formal proof in which the argument is carried out by actions on concrete objects, such as counters.
These forms matter in teaching because they let young students give real proofs, and because they form a bridge from examples to general symbolic [[proofs|0B94TKFQJX]].

## Motivation

A child of eight cannot write $(2a + 1) + (2b + 1) = 2(a + b + 1)$.
The same child can arrange counters in pairs and see that two odd numbers of counters leave two single counters, which make one more pair.
The argument with counters is general: it works for each pair of odd numbers, and the child can say why.
Research needed a name for such arguments, which are neither a check of examples nor a formal proof.
The names "generic proof" and "pre-formal proof" fill this gap.

## Notation

John Mason and David Pimm (1984) used "generic example" for an example that is seen as a representative of a class.
Werner Blum and Arnold Kirsch (1991) used "preformal proof" (also written "pre-formal") for a chain of correct conclusions from valid premises that is not represented formally.
Zbigniew Semadeni proposed "action proofs" for proofs by acts on concrete objects.
Some authors write "generic proof" or "proof by generic example" for an argument based on a generic example.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[A generic proof that the sum of three consecutive integers is divisible by 3|QFMBKN41PW]], on the numbers $7$, $8$ and $9$.
2. [[An action proof with counters that the sum of two odd numbers is even|ECTGXEPC65]].
3. A [[proof without words|V6ESFEFKJC]] that $1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 4^2$: an L-shaped layer of $2k - 1$ dots enlarges a square of side $k - 1$ to a square of side $k$. The picture for $k = 4$ shows the general step for the [[sum of the first n odd numbers|9PESCNHE7Q]].

### Non-examples

1. [[Checking many cases is not a generic proof|BH6XNGKBJY]]: a table of sums of three consecutive integers shows the pattern, but no step shows the reason.
2. [[A special figure is not a generic example|4KAG9W328V]]: an equilateral triangle has more symmetry than a general triangle, so an argument on it may use special features.
3. A formal proof in symbols is not pre-formal. It is the target of the bridge, not the bridge.

### Basic properties

1. A generic proof is a proof only if each step holds for every case. The test: replace the particular number by a letter, and check that each step remains true.
2. Whether an argument is generic depends partly on the reader. The same computation $7 + 8 + 9 = 24$ can be a mere check for one reader and a generic proof for another, who sees $24 = 3 \cdot 8$ as "three times the middle number".
3. A generic proof makes the general structure visible by the way it writes the example: $7 + 8 + 9$ written as $(8 - 1) + 8 + (8 + 1)$.
4. Each correct generic proof can be turned into a symbolic proof by replacing the example with variables. The page [[three kinds of reasoning on the sum of the first odd numbers|5W8G4689R2]] shows this step.

### History

Mason and Pimm (1984) introduced the generic example into the research literature, with the aim of "seeing the general in the particular".
Nicolas Balacheff, in the 1980s, put the generic example as the third of four levels of proof: naive empiricism, the test of one critical case, the generic example and the thought experiment.
Blum and Kirsch (1991) argued for pre-formal proofs as a form of proof suited to school, with examples and reflections.
The ICMI Study 19 (2012) has a chapter on the cognitive development of proof, from early childhood to research mathematics.

### Common errors

1. A teacher accepts any example as generic. An example is generic only if the presentation shows the general reason; $7 + 8 + 9 = 24$ alone is a check.
2. A student chooses a special example, such as $0$, $1$ or an equilateral triangle, as the generic case. Its special features can hide the general structure.
3. A teacher treats a pre-formal proof as a step that must be replaced. For the class, a correct pre-formal proof is a full proof, and it can stay as an explanation after the formal proof.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Sometimes one example can show why something is always true.
Take $7 + 8 + 9$.
Write it as $(8 - 1) + 8 + (8 + 1)$: the $-1$ and the $+1$ cancel, so the sum is $3 \times 8$.
The same thing happens for any three numbers in a row, so the sum is always $3$ times the middle number.
An example used in this way is a generic example, and the argument is a generic proof.
Young students can also prove things with objects, such as counters, without any letters.

### L2 human

We test whether an argument on one example is a generic proof.
The claim: for each integer $n$, the number $n^2 - n$ is even.
The argument: "$7^2 - 7 = 7 \cdot 6$, and one of two numbers next to each other is even, so the product is even."

1. Replace $7$ by $n$: $n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$. This step holds for each $n$.
2. Check the second step: one of $n$ and $n - 1$ is even, since they are consecutive integers. This holds for each $n$.
3. Check the conclusion: a product with an even factor is even. This holds for each $n$.
4. No step uses a special feature of $7$, such as being prime or odd.
5. So the argument is a generic proof.

The typical use is in a class that has not yet learned algebra, or as a first draft before a symbolic proof.
A trap is step 4: an argument "$7^2 - 7 = 42$, which is even" uses only the value $42$, and it gives no reason for other $n$.

### L3 human

Generic and pre-formal proofs belong to the research on the development of proof from childhood to university, surveyed in the ICMI Study 19.
In the language of [[proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]], a student who accepts a generic proof because of the general reason holds a transformational scheme, while a student who accepts it because of the example holds an empirical scheme.
The page on [[examples in mathematical practice|F3NNR77WKP]] places generic examples among other kinds of examples.
In the classroom, generic proofs often appear in [[argumentation in class|E77B813MT4]], and curricula use them as a stage in the path toward symbolic proof, as on the page [[proof in school curricula|7ZSHDS63QY]].
