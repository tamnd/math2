## Summary

A twelve-year-old is asked why odd plus odd is always even, and has not yet learned algebra.
She writes one example, but in a special way:
$$7 + 9 = (2 \cdot 3 + 1) + (2 \cdot 4 + 1) = 2 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 4 + 2 = 2 \cdot (3 + 4 + 1).$$
Then she explains that every odd number is two times something, plus one.
The two ones make another two, she says, so the whole sum is two times something.
Nothing in her argument depends on $3$ or $4$, and the reader sees in this one case the reason for every case.

An argument of this kind is a generic proof: an argument on one particular example, written so that each step uses only properties that all cases share.
A pre-formal proof is a correct argument that is not written in formal symbols; it uses actions on objects, pictures or everyday language.
An action proof is a pre-formal proof carried out by actions on concrete objects, such as counters.
These forms let young students give real proofs, and they form a bridge from examples to general symbolic [[proofs|0B94TKFQJX]].

## Motivation

A child of eight cannot write $(2a + 1) + (2b + 1) = 2(a + b + 1)$.
The same child can lay out counters in pairs and see that two odd piles leave two single counters, which make one more pair.
The argument with counters is general: it works for any two odd piles, and the child can say why.
Research needed a name for arguments that are neither a check of examples nor a formal proof, and "generic proof" and "pre-formal proof" fill that gap.
For a teacher, the names matter because they say which arguments from young students to accept as proofs.

## Notation

John Mason and David Pimm (1984) used "generic example" for an example that is seen as standing for a whole class.
Werner Blum and Arnold Kirsch (1991) used "preformal proof", also written "pre-formal", for a chain of correct conclusions from true premises that is not written formally.
Zbigniew Semadeni proposed "action proof" for a proof by acts on concrete objects.
"Generic proof" and "proof by generic example" both name an argument built on a generic example.

## Remarks

### Three generic and pre-formal proofs

[[A generic proof that the sum of three consecutive integers is divisible by 3|QFMBKN41PW]] works on $7 + 8 + 9$, written as $(8 - 1) + 8 + (8 + 1) = 3 \cdot 8$.
[[An action proof with counters|ECTGXEPC65]] shows that the sum of two odd numbers is even by pushing two piles of paired counters together.
A [[proof without words|V6ESFEFKJC]] shows $1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 4^2$ by adding L-shaped layers of dots: a layer of $2k - 1$ dots turns a square of side $k - 1$ into a square of side $k$.
The picture for $k = 4$ carries the general step for the [[sum of the first n odd numbers|9PESCNHE7Q]].

### What is not generic

[[Checking many cases is not a generic proof|BH6XNGKBJY]]: a table of sums of three consecutive integers shows the pattern, but no step shows the reason.
[[A special figure is not a generic example|4KAG9W328V]]: an equilateral triangle has more symmetry than a general triangle, so an argument on it may use features that other triangles lack.
A formal proof in symbols is not pre-formal either; it is the far end of the bridge.

### The test for a generic proof

A generic proof is a proof only if each step holds for every case.
The test is to replace the particular number by a letter and check that each step stays true.
For the opening example, replace $3$ and $4$ by $a$ and $b$:
$$(2a + 1) + (2b + 1) = 2a + 2b + 2 = 2(a + b + 1),$$
and each step still holds, so the argument passes.
Every correct generic proof turns into a symbolic proof in this way, as [[three kinds of reasoning on the sum of the first odd numbers|5W8G4689R2]] shows.

Whether an argument is generic depends partly on the reader.
The line $7 + 8 + 9 = 24$ is a mere check for one reader, and a proof for another who reads $24$ as "three times the middle number".
That is why the way the example is written matters: $(8 - 1) + 8 + (8 + 1)$ shows the structure that $7 + 8 + 9$ hides.

### History

Mason and Pimm (1984) brought the generic example into the research literature, under the slogan of seeing the general in the particular.
Nicolas Balacheff (1988) described four types of justification in pupils' work: naive empiricism, the crucial experiment, the generic example and the thought experiment.
In the first two, checked cases carry the argument, and in the generic example a case is used to show a reason.
Blum and Kirsch (1991) argued for preformal proofs as a form of proof suited to school.
The ICMI Study 19, edited by Gila Hanna and Michael de Villiers (2012), has a chapter on the cognitive development of proof from early childhood to research mathematics.

### Watch out

Not every example is generic.
The bare line $7 + 8 + 9 = 24$ is a check of one case, and only a presentation that shows the reason makes it generic.
A special example, such as $0$, $1$ or an equilateral triangle, is a poor choice for the generic case, because its special features can hide the structure or prove too much.
And a correct pre-formal proof is a full proof for the class, not a draft that must be thrown away; it can stay as the explanation next to the formal proof.

### Try it

Is "$5^2 - 5 = 20$, which is even" a generic proof that $n^2 - n$ is always even?
No, because the step uses only the value $20$, and nothing in it explains other values of $n$.

Turn it into a generic proof.
Write $5^2 - 5 = 5 \cdot 4$ and say that one of two neighboring whole numbers is always even, so their product is even; every step survives replacing $5$ by $n$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Sometimes one example can show why something is always true.
Take $7 + 8 + 9$, and write it as $(8 - 1) + 8 + (8 + 1)$.
The $-1$ and the $+1$ cancel, so the sum is $3 \times 8$.
The same thing happens for any three whole numbers in a row, so the sum is always $3$ times the middle number.
An example used in this way is a generic example, and the argument is a generic proof.
Young students can also prove things with objects, such as counters, without any letters.

### L2 human

We test whether an argument on one example is a generic proof.
The claim is that $n^2 - n$ is even for every integer $n$.
The argument reads: "$7^2 - 7 = 7 \cdot 6$, and of two numbers next to each other one is even, so the product is even."

Replace $7$ by $n$ in the first step:
$$n^2 - n = n(n - 1),$$
which holds for every $n$.
The second step says one of $n$ and $n - 1$ is even, which holds because they are consecutive integers.
The last step says a product with an even factor is even, which holds for every $n$.
No step uses a special feature of $7$, such as being prime or odd, so the argument is a generic proof.

This kind of check is how a teacher decides whether to accept an example-based argument, and it is also a student's first draft of a symbolic proof.
Watch out for the version "$7^2 - 7 = 42$, which is even", which passes no step of the check.

### L3 human

Generic and pre-formal proofs belong to the research on how proof develops from childhood to university, which the ICMI Study 19 surveys.
In the language of [[proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]], a student who accepts a generic proof because of its general reason holds a transformational scheme, while one who accepts it because of the example holds an empirical scheme.
[[Examples in mathematical practice|F3NNR77WKP]] places generic examples among other uses of examples.
Generic proofs often appear in [[argumentation in class|E77B813MT4]], and curricula use them as a stage on the path to symbolic proof, as [[proof in school curricula|7ZSHDS63QY]] describes.
