## Summary

Let $G$ be a [[group|G5S1M9]] and let $S$ be a subset of $G$.
The subgroup generated by $S$ is the smallest [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] of $G$ that contains $S$.
It is the intersection of all subgroups of $G$ that contain $S$.
Its elements are the finite products of elements of $S$ and their [[inverses|0ZNZH0EXFX]].
The two descriptions give the same subgroup.

## Motivation

A subset $S$ of a group is usually not a subgroup.
We often want the least we must add to $S$ to get a subgroup.
The subgroup must contain $S$, the identity, all products and all inverses, so it must contain every product of elements of $S$ and their inverses.
The definition from above, by intersection, shows that a smallest subgroup exists.
The description from below, by products, shows what its elements are.
Generation is the main way to name subgroups: the subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ generated by $4$ and $6$, the subgroup of $S_3$ generated by a transposition and a $3$-cycle.

## Notation

The subgroup generated by $S$ is written $\langle S \rangle$.
For a finite set we drop the braces: $\langle a, b \rangle = \langle \{a, b\} \rangle$.
For one element, $\langle g \rangle$ is the [[cyclic subgroup|08ABT85339]] generated by $g$.
A word in $S$ is a product $s_1^{\varepsilon_1} s_2^{\varepsilon_2} \cdots s_k^{\varepsilon_k}$ with $k \geq 0$, each $s_i \in S$ and each $\varepsilon_i \in \{1, -1\}$.
The word with $k = 0$ is the empty product, and its value is the identity $e$.
In an additive group, the words are the sums $\pm s_1 \pm s_2 \pm \cdots \pm s_k$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The empty set generates the trivial subgroup: $\langle \emptyset \rangle = \{e\}$, because $\{e\}$ is a subgroup and every subgroup contains it.
2. In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the subgroup $\langle n \rangle$ is $n\mathbb{Z}$, and $\langle 1 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}$. Also [[the subgroup generated by 4 and 6 is 2Z|~58909262]]. In general $\langle a, b \rangle = \gcd(a, b)\mathbb{Z}$, by the theorem on the [[subgroups of the integers|0SRATCFF1W]].
3. In the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$, [[the transposition (1 2) and the 3-cycle (1 2 3) generate S3|~4a65c4a8]].
4. In $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$, $\langle \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3} \rangle = \tfrac{1}{6}\mathbb{Z}$. The computation is in the L2 explanation below.
5. If $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, then $\langle H \rangle = H$.

### Non-examples

1. [[The set of e, (1 2) and (1 3) is not the subgroup that they generate|~f8d73bc5]]. A subset that contains $S$ and $e$ is not $\langle S \rangle$ unless it is closed under products and inverses.
2. In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the set $\{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$ of sums of copies of $1$ is not $\langle 1 \rangle$. It contains only the words without inverses, and it does not contain $0$ or $-1$.
3. In $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$, the subgroup $\langle \tfrac{1}{2} \rangle = \{\tfrac{k}{2} : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ does not contain $\tfrac{1}{4}$. The subring generated by $\tfrac{1}{2}$ does contain $\tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}$. Generation depends on the operations that we allow.

### Basic properties

1. $\langle S \rangle$ is a subgroup of $G$ and $S \subseteq \langle S \rangle$. If $H$ is a subgroup of $G$ with $S \subseteq H$, then $\langle S \rangle \subseteq H$.
2. If $S \subseteq T$, then $\langle S \rangle \subseteq \langle T \rangle$.
3. $\langle S \rangle = S$ if and only if $S$ is a subgroup. In particular, $\langle \langle S \rangle \rangle = \langle S \rangle$.
4. $\langle S \rangle$ is the set of values of all words in $S$. The proof is below.
5. If $G$ is abelian and $S = \{a_1, \ldots, a_n\}$, then $\langle S \rangle = \{a_1^{k_1} a_2^{k_2} \cdots a_n^{k_n} : k_1, \ldots, k_n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. In additive notation, $\langle S \rangle = \{k_1 a_1 + \cdots + k_n a_n\}$. We can reorder the factors of a word because the group is abelian.
6. The subgroup $\langle S \rangle$ does not depend on the group around it. If $S \subseteq H \leq G$, then the subgroup of $H$ generated by $S$ equals the subgroup of $G$ generated by $S$, because both are the set of values of the words in $S$.
7. If $f \colon G \to G'$ is a [[homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]], then $f(\langle S \rangle) = \langle f(S) \rangle$. The map $f$ sends the value of a word in $S$ to the value of the same word in $f(S)$.

Proof of property 4.
Let $W$ be the set of values of the words in $S$.
The family of subgroups of $G$ that contain $S$ is not empty, because $G$ is in it.
So $\langle S \rangle$, the intersection of this family, is a subgroup by the lemma on the [[intersection of subgroups|0ZTGMDNVTB]].

1. $e \in W$, because $e$ is the value of the empty word.
2. $W$ is closed under products. Put two words side by side. The result is a word, and by associativity its value is the product of the two values.
3. $W$ is closed under inverses. Let $w = s_1^{\varepsilon_1} \cdots s_k^{\varepsilon_k}$. Put $w' = s_k^{-\varepsilon_k} \cdots s_1^{-\varepsilon_1}$, which is a word. In the product $w w'$, the middle factors $s_k^{\varepsilon_k} s_k^{-\varepsilon_k}$ cancel to $e$, then the next pair cancels, and so on. So $w w' = e$, and $w^{-1} = w'$ by the uniqueness of inverses.
4. By steps 1 to 3, $W$ is a subgroup of $G$. Each $s \in S$ is the value of the word $s^1$, so $S \subseteq W$.
5. So $W$ is one of the subgroups in the intersection that defines $\langle S \rangle$. Hence $\langle S \rangle \subseteq W$.
6. Let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$ with $S \subseteq H$. For each $s \in S$, both $s$ and $s^{-1}$ lie in $H$. By induction on the length $k$ and closure under products, the value of each word of length $k$ lies in $H$. So $W \subseteq H$.
7. Step 6 holds for each subgroup in the intersection, so $W \subseteq \langle S \rangle$.
8. By steps 5 and 7, $\langle S \rangle = W$. $\blacksquare$

### History

Arthur Cayley described a group by a set of generators in his 1878 papers on the graphical representation of groups.
Walther von Dyck studied groups given by generators and relations in 1882.

### Common errors

1. Forgetting the inverses. In a finite group and for $S \neq \emptyset$, the products of one or more elements of $S$ already give $\langle S \rangle$, because each inverse is a positive power. In an infinite group this fails, as non-example 2 shows.
2. Writing $\langle a, b \rangle = \{a^i b^j : i, j \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ in a group that is not abelian. In $S_3$, take $a = (1\,2)$ and $b = (1\,3)$. Then $a^2 = b^2 = e$, so this set is $\{e, a, b, ab\}$, with $4$ elements. But $\langle a, b \rangle = S_3$ has $6$ elements. For example, $ba = (1\,2\,3)$ is not in the set.
3. Confusing $\langle S \rangle$ with $S \cup \{e\} \cup S^{-1}$, where $S^{-1}$ is the set of inverses of elements of $S$. This set is usually not closed under products.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Start with some elements of a group.
They usually do not form a subgroup by themselves.
Add every element that we can get from them: products, inverses, products of those, and so on.
The result is the subgroup generated by the starting elements.
For example, start with the number $3$ in the integers under addition.
We get $3 + 3 = 6$, then $9$, then $-3$, $0$ and all the other multiples of $3$.
So $3$ generates the multiples of $3$.

### L2 human

We compute $\langle \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3} \rangle$ in $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$.

1. $\tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{3}$ is a word in the two generators. So $\tfrac{1}{6} \in \langle \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3} \rangle$, and all integer multiples of $\tfrac{1}{6}$ are in it too. So $\tfrac{1}{6}\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \langle \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3} \rangle$.
2. The set $\tfrac{1}{6}\mathbb{Z} = \{\tfrac{k}{6} : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup, because $\tfrac{k}{6} - \tfrac{l}{6} = \tfrac{k - l}{6}$.
3. It contains $\tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{3}{6}$ and $\tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{2}{6}$. By basic property 1, $\langle \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3} \rangle \subseteq \tfrac{1}{6}\mathbb{Z}$.
4. By steps 1 and 3, $\langle \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3} \rangle = \tfrac{1}{6}\mathbb{Z}$.

This is the typical method: find enough words to get a candidate subgroup, then check that the candidate is a subgroup that contains the generators.

A trap: in a group that is not abelian, the order of the factors in a word matters.
Common error 2 shows that $\{a^i b^j\}$ can miss elements of $\langle a, b \rangle$.

### L3 human

Let $F(S)$ be the [[free group|0KTW26C80J]] on the set $S$.
The inclusion $S \to G$ extends to a unique homomorphism $F(S) \to G$, and its image is $\langle S \rangle$.
This is property 4 in the language of universal properties: an element of $F(S)$ is a reduced word, and the homomorphism takes its value in $G$.
The map $S \mapsto \langle S \rangle$ is a closure operator on the subsets of $G$, and the closed sets are the subgroups.
The subgroups of $G$ form a [[complete lattice|D7A0ZGZT5S]], and the join of a family of subgroups is the subgroup generated by their union.
The words also give a metric.
The length of $g \in \langle S \rangle$ is the least $k$ such that $g$ is the value of a word of length $k$, and the distance from $g$ to $h$ is the length of $g^{-1}h$.
This word metric is the start of geometric group theory, through the Cayley graph of $G$ with respect to $S$.
