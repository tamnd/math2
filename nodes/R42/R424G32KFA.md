## Statement

An invertible matrix has at most one LU decomposition, and pulling the pivots out of $U$ gives a unique balanced form $LDV$, which is $LDL^{\mathsf{T}}$ for a symmetric matrix.
Let $F$ be a field and let $A$ be an [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] $n \times n$ matrix over $F$.

1. If $A = L_1 U_1 = L_2 U_2$ with $L_1, L_2$ unit [[lower triangular|00J3KZP8Y0]] and $U_1, U_2$ [[upper triangular|0N4XY7Z3NN]], then $L_1 = L_2$ and $U_1 = U_2$.
2. If $A$ has an [[LU decomposition|0RKG5YW66Y]] $A = LU$, then $A = L D V$ with $D = \operatorname{diag}(u_{11}, \dots, u_{nn})$ invertible and $V = D^{-1} U$ unit upper triangular.
3. The factorization $A = LDV$ with $L$ unit lower triangular, $D$ [[diagonal|0Q9PGXD9E4]] and $V$ unit upper triangular is unique.
4. If $A$ is also symmetric, then $V = L^{\mathsf{T}}$, so $A = L D L^{\mathsf{T}}$.

## Remarks

### The theorem at work

Take the symmetric matrix $S = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 4 & 11 \end{pmatrix}$.
The step $R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1$ gives
$$L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D = \operatorname{diag}(2, 3), \qquad V = D^{-1}U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
So $V = L^{\mathsf{T}}$, as part 4 predicts.
Check: $L D = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 4 & 11 \end{pmatrix} = S$.

### Why the hypotheses are needed

Invertibility is needed for uniqueness.
For each $t \in F$,
$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ t & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
so the zero matrix has one LU decomposition for each $t$.
The normalization "unit" is needed too.
Without it, $A = (LC)(C^{-1}U)$ for each invertible diagonal $C$, so $L$ and $U$ would be fixed only up to such a $C$.

### What it gives you

The multipliers of elimination without row exchanges depend only on $A$, not on the order in which the entries below the pivots are cleared.
The pivots $u_{kk}$ are invariants of $A$, and by the theorem on [[existence of the LU decomposition|3PPBWG0B96]] they equal $\Delta_k/\Delta_{k-1}$.
For a symmetric [[positive definite|0KX66CZMBB]] matrix, the entries of $D$ are positive, and $L D^{1/2}$ is the factor of the [[Cholesky decomposition|0WKG8CYY6A]].
In the example above, $L D^{1/2} = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 2\sqrt{2} & \sqrt{3} \end{pmatrix}$.

### Converse and generalizations

Each product $LDV$ of the given form with an invertible $D$ is invertible, and it has the LU decomposition $L \cdot (DV)$.
With row exchanges, $PA = LU$ is in general not unique, because different choices of the pivot rows give different $P$.
For a fixed [[permutation matrix|08SP3DF8NB]] $P$, the factorization $PA = LU$ is unique when it exists, by part 1 applied to $PA$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An invertible matrix can be split in only one way into "unit lower triangular times upper triangular".
So the result of elimination does not depend on how you do it, as long as you do not swap rows.
Pulling the pivots out of $U$ gives the more balanced form $LDV$.

### L2 human

To get $LDV$ from $LU$, divide each row of $U$ by its pivot.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 6 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the pivots are $2$ and $1$, so $D = \operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$ and $V = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Check: $DV = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = U$, so $LDV = LU = A$.
A trap: $V$ is not $L^{\mathsf{T}}$ here, because $A$ is not symmetric.

### L3 human

The proof rests on one fact: the unit lower triangular group meets the upper triangular group only in $\{I_n\}$.
This is the uniqueness part of the decomposition $B^- B^+$ in group terms.
Over $\mathbb{R}$, the signs of the entries of $D$ in $A = LDL^{\mathsf{T}}$ give the signature of the symmetric matrix $A$, by Sylvester's law of inertia.
