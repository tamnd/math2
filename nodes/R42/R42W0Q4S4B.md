## Example

The [[strong Goldbach conjecture|063JNBSMJ0]] says: every even integer greater than $2$ is a sum of two [[primes|01826KMG2H]].
The weak Goldbach conjecture says: every odd integer greater than $5$ is a sum of three primes.
In both statements the primes need not be distinct.
We check that the strong conjecture is [[stronger|KCK4AQ469Q]] than the weak one.

1. Assume the strong conjecture, and let $n$ be an odd integer with $n > 5$.
2. The number $n - 3$ is even, because $n$ and $3$ are both odd.
3. Since $n \ge 7$, we have $n - 3 \ge 4 > 2$.
4. By the strong conjecture, $n - 3 = p + q$ for some primes $p$ and $q$.
5. Then $n = 3 + p + q$ is a sum of three primes, so the weak conjecture holds.
6. A numerical case: for $n = 15$ we get $n - 3 = 12 = 5 + 7$, so $15 = 3 + 5 + 7$.

## Remarks

The argument runs in one direction only.
A sum of three primes for $n$ gives no sum of two primes for the even number $n - 3$, so no short argument of this kind leads from the weak conjecture to the strong one.
That is why the names are "strong" and "weak": here the word matches the implication.

The weak conjecture has a long history of partial results.
[[Vinogradov's theorem|C01Z9QRGZY]] proves it for all sufficiently large odd numbers.
Harald Helfgott announced a proof of the full weak conjecture in 2013, while the strong conjecture is still open.
So the weaker statement was proved first, which is the usual order.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The strong conjecture is a claim about even numbers and pairs of primes, such as $12 = 5 + 7$.
If it is true, you get a claim about odd numbers for free.
Take away $3$, split the even rest into two primes, and put the $3$ back.
So the strong claim carries the weak claim inside it.

### L2 human

Write the odd numbers from $7$ to $13$ as sums of three primes by the method of the example:
$$7 = 3 + (2 + 2), \quad 9 = 3 + (3 + 3), \quad 11 = 3 + (3 + 5), \quad 13 = 3 + (5 + 5).$$
In each case the bracket is a sum of two primes for the even number $n - 3$, which is $4$, $6$, $8$ and $10$.

The typical use of this argument is to see that a proof of the strong conjecture would settle the weak one at once.
Watch out: Helfgott's proof of the weak conjecture does not give the strong one, because the implication goes in one direction only.
