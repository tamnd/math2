## Summary

Lifting-line theory is Prandtl's model of a straight wing of finite span and large aspect ratio in an ideal fluid.
The wing is replaced by a single vortex line along the span whose circulation varies, together with a sheet of trailing vortices shed behind it.
The trailing vortices induce a downwash that lowers the effective angle of attack, and this leads to [[Prandtl's lifting-line equation|6GZ8DXT7TN]] for the circulation.
The theory explains induced drag and shows that, among planar wings of given span and lift, an elliptic lift distribution gives the least induced drag.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A wing of finite length cannot keep the high pressure below it separate from the low pressure above it at the tips.
Air curls around the tips and leaves two long swirls behind the plane.
These swirls push the air near the wing downwards, so the wing meets the air at a smaller angle than its geometric tilt.
This costs some lift and tilts the force backwards, which is felt as an extra drag.
For example, a glider has long thin wings because this induced drag shrinks as the wings get longer compared with their width.
