## Definition

The direct product of a family of vector spaces is the space of all families that pick one vector from each space.
Let $F$ be a field and let $(V_i)_{i \in I}$ be a family of [[vector spaces|05EGHEQZAW]] over $F$.
The *direct product* $\prod_{i \in I} V_i$ is the set of all families $(v_i)_{i \in I}$ with $v_i \in V_i$ for each $i \in I$, with the operations
$$(v_i)_i + (w_i)_i = (v_i + w_i)_i, \qquad a (v_i)_i = (a v_i)_i \quad (a \in F).$$
It is a vector space over $F$ whose zero vector is the family $(0)_i$.
For $j \in I$, the *projection* $\pi_j \colon \prod_{i \in I} V_i \to V_j$ is the map $(v_i)_i \mapsto v_j$.

In particular, for two spaces $V$ and $W$, the product $V \times W$ is the set of pairs $(v, w)$ with
$$(v, w) + (v', w') = (v + v', w + w'), \qquad a(v, w) = (av, aw).$$

## Notation

We write $V_1 \times \cdots \times V_k$ for a finite family and $V^I$ for the product of copies of one space $V$, which is the space of all functions $I \to V$.

## Remarks

The definition puts no condition on the families beyond $v_i \in V_i$, and this is what separates it from the [[external direct sum|0S7W6EG32V]], which asks that only finitely many entries be nonzero.
The operations act in each place separately.
So each vector space axiom holds in the product because it holds in each factor, entry by entry, and the negative of $(v_i)_i$ is $(-v_i)_i$.
The projections $\pi_j$ are linear, since $\pi_j$ of a sum is the entry in place $j$ of the sum, which is the sum of the entries.

For example, in $\R^2 \times \R$ we have
$$((1, 2), 3) + 2\,((0, 1), -1) = ((1, 4), 1),$$
and $\pi_1$ of the result is $(1, 4)$.
The definition needs one field $F$ for all the factors.
A real space and a complex space, each with its own scalars, have no product under this definition until both are viewed over $\R$.

For a finite family, the product is also the external direct sum, often written $V \oplus W$.
The [[dimension of a direct product|06HN47VPTH]] is $\dim V + \dim W$, and for example $F^m \times F^n$ is isomorphic to $F^{m + n}$.
The concept page on the [[direct product|0EX2HPXPJ6]] gives more examples and the universal property.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The product of vector spaces has vectors that are lists, with one vector from each space.
We add lists entry by entry and scale every entry by the same number.
The plane $\R^2$ is the product of two copies of the line $\R$.

### L2 human

The space $\R^{\N}$ of all real sequences is the product of countably many copies of $\R$.
The sequences $(1, 1, 1, \dots)$ and $(1, 2, 3, \dots)$ are vectors of it, and their sum is computed entry by entry:
$$(1, 1, 1, \dots) + (1, 2, 3, \dots) = (2, 3, 4, \dots).$$
Both terms have infinitely many nonzero entries, which the product allows.
Watch out: neither sequence lies in the direct sum $\R^{(\N)}$, which allows only finitely many nonzero entries.
