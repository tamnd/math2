## Statement

The Laplace expansion computes a determinant of size $n$ from $n$ determinants of size $n - 1$, one for each entry of a chosen row.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 2$, and let $A = (a_{ij})$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$.
Let $M_{ij}$ be the first [[minors|0SD528P0EK]] of $A$, and let $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}$ be its [[cofactors|0W2QAVJQ1W]].
Then, for each row index $i$,
$$\det A = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} C_{ij} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (-1)^{i+j} a_{ij} M_{ij},$$
where $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].
The same holds for each column index $j$, in the form $\det A = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{ij} C_{ij}$, and this is the [[expansion along a column|06AMCJMMVM]].

## Remarks

### A first expansion

Take the matrix
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 4 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Column $2$ has only one nonzero entry, $a_{22} = 4$, and its sign is $(-1)^{2+2} = +1$.
So the expansion along column $2$ has a single term:
$$\det A = 4 \det \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = 4 (15 - 4) = 44.$$
Check: the expansion along row $1$ uses all three entries of that row, with the signs $+, -, +$:
$$\det A = 3 (4 \cdot 5 - 0 \cdot 0) - 0 \cdot (1 \cdot 5 - 0 \cdot 2) + 2 (1 \cdot 0 - 4 \cdot 2) = 60 - 16 = 44.$$
Both lines give the same number, as the theorem promises, but the column with zeros needed one small determinant instead of two.

### Choosing the line with the most zeros

The work drops with every zero in the chosen row or column, because a zero entry kills its whole term.
Take the $4 \times 4$ matrix
$$B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Row $3$ has the single entry $b_{32} = 5$, with sign $(-1)^{3+2} = -1$.
Deleting row $3$ and column $2$ leaves a $3 \times 3$ matrix, which we expand along its first row:
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 2 (0 - 6) - 1 (1 - 8) + 3 (3 - 0) = -12 + 7 + 9 = 4.$$
So $\det B = -5 \cdot 4 = -20$.
In general, if row $i$ has only one nonzero entry $a_{ij}$, then $\det A = (-1)^{i+j} a_{ij} M_{ij}$.

### Entries of one row, cofactors of another

What happens if we take the entries of one row and the cofactors of a different row?
For the matrix $A$ above, the cofactors of row $1$ are $C_{11} = 20$, $C_{12} = -5$ and $C_{13} = -8$.
Pair them with the entries $2, 0, 5$ of row $3$:
$$2 \cdot 20 + 0 \cdot (-5) + 5 \cdot (-8) = 40 - 40 = 0.$$
This is no accident.
The sum is the expansion of the matrix that has row $3$ copied into row $1$, and a matrix with two equal rows has determinant $0$.
This "expansion with alien cofactors" always gives $0$, and together with the theorem it yields the [[adjugate identity|0528HKWZ1Z]] $A \operatorname{adj} A = (\det A) I_n$.

### Why the signs are needed

The sign $(-1)^{i+j}$ cannot be dropped.
For $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, the unsigned sum along row $1$ is $1 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 3 = 10$, but the determinant is $4 - 6 = -2$.
The [[non-example on unsigned minors|4HC79H7TJR]] shows the same failure for a $3 \times 3$ matrix.
The signs form a checkerboard that starts with $+$ in the top left corner, so the expansion along row $2$ of a $3 \times 3$ matrix starts with a minus sign.

### What it gives you

With $\det(a) = a$ for $1 \times 1$ matrices, the expansion along row $1$ is a recursive definition of the determinant.
This is the Laplace recursion, one of the three definitions that the [[equivalence theorem|W3JN0WPZ10]] shows to agree.
The expansion also shows that $\det A$ is a linear function of row $i$, with the cofactors $C_{i1}, \dots, C_{in}$ as coefficients.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ this means $\partial \det A / \partial a_{ij} = C_{ij}$, the starting point of [[Jacobi's formula|357FEAZ0Q4]].

The cofactors are the only coefficients with this property.
Suppose that $\det A = \sum_j a_{ij} D_j$ for every choice of row $i$, where the $D_j$ do not depend on row $i$.
Put row $i$ equal to the standard row $e_j$: the left side is $C_{ij}$ by the theorem, and the right side is $D_j$.

The [[general Laplace expansion|541X51GQTV]] expands along $k$ rows at once, with products of complementary minors in place of entries and cofactors.

### Watch out

The full recursion on an $n \times n$ matrix with no zeros produces $n!$ products, the same as the [[Leibniz formula|016PE4VZ8Q]].
For $n = 10$ that is $3\,628\,800$ products, while row reduction needs fewer than a thousand operations.
So use the expansion for small matrices, for rows with many zeros and for matrices with symbols, and use row reduction for large numerical matrices.

### Try it

Expand $\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ along row $1$.
The answer is $1 (1 - 2) - 2 (3 - 0) + 0 = -1 - 6 = -7$.

Which row or column of $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 & 7 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ is best, and what is the determinant?
Column $2$ has one nonzero entry, $a_{12} = 1$ with sign $-1$, so $\det = -1 \cdot (0 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 3) = 6$.

## History

The expansion is named after Pierre-Simon Laplace.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To expand a determinant along a row, go through the entries of that row one at a time.
Cross out the row and the column of the entry, and take the determinant of the smaller square that is left.
Multiply the entry by that smaller determinant, with a plus or a minus sign from a checkerboard pattern.
Add the results.
For $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ along the top row, this gives $1 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 3 = -2$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: for which numbers $x$ is the matrix
$$T = \begin{pmatrix} x & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & x & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & x \end{pmatrix}$$
singular?
Row reduction is awkward here, because we would have to divide by expressions in $x$.
The expansion along row $1$ needs no division:
$$\det T = x \det \begin{pmatrix} x & 1 \\ 1 & x \end{pmatrix} - 1 \cdot \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & x \end{pmatrix} + 0 = x (x^2 - 1) - x = x^3 - 2x.$$
So $\det T = x (x^2 - 2)$, and $T$ is singular exactly for $x = 0$ and $x = \pm \sqrt{2}$.
Check at $x = 0$: rows $1$ and $3$ are then $(0, 1, 0)$, so they are equal and the determinant is $0$.
The problem shows the main strength of the expansion: it works with symbols, and it is the standard tool for determinants that depend on a parameter, such as characteristic polynomials.

### L3 human

Expansion along row $i$ says that $x \mapsto \det(\dots, x, \dots)$, with $x$ in row $i$, is the linear form whose coefficients are row $i$ of the cofactor matrix.
In the language of the [[exterior algebra|0MDMZ965VE]], it is the pairing $\Lambda^1 K^n \otimes \Lambda^{n-1} K^n \to \Lambda^n K^n$, and the general Laplace expansion is the pairing of $\Lambda^k$ with $\Lambda^{n-k}$.
For tridiagonal matrices the expansion gives a three-term recursion between the determinants of the leading blocks, and this recursion links determinants with continued fractions and orthogonal polynomials.
The [[adjugate|07W9H8QF38]], [[Cramer's rule|0977KZ6PQB]] and Jacobi's formula all rest on this theorem.
