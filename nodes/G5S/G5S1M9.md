## Summary

A group is a set $G$ with a [[binary operation|0M418FE7BB]] $G \times G \to G$, $(a, b) \mapsto ab$, that satisfies three axioms.
The operation is [[associative|0K8F7PVDMX]]: $(ab)c = a(bc)$ for all $a, b, c \in G$.
There is an [[identity element|07WYQPB8E3]] $e \in G$ with $ea = a = ae$ for all $a \in G$.
Each $a \in G$ has an [[inverse|0ZNZH0EXFX]] $a^{-1} \in G$ with $a a^{-1} = e = a^{-1} a$.
Groups matter because they describe symmetry: the symmetries of any object form a group, and many structures of algebra, geometry and physics contain groups.

## Motivation

Several familiar systems share one pattern.
The integers with addition, the nonzero rational numbers with multiplication, and the rotations of a square with composition each have an operation that combines two elements into a third.
In each system, the grouping of three elements does not change the result, one element does nothing, and each element can be undone.
These three facts are exactly what we use when we solve an equation such as $x + 3 = 7$ or $3x = 7$: we undo the $3$ on both sides.
A group is the abstract form of this pattern.
A theorem that we prove from the three axioms then holds in each of these systems at the same time.

## Notation

A group is written $(G, \cdot)$, or $G$ alone when the operation is understood.
In multiplicative notation the product is $ab$ or $a \cdot b$, the identity is $e$ or $1$, and the inverse of $a$ is $a^{-1}$.
In additive notation the operation is $a + b$, the identity is $0$, and the inverse of $a$ is $-a$.
By convention, additive notation is used only for [[abelian groups|082GHNPJ53]], that is, groups in which $ab = ba$ for all $a$ and $b$.
Some books write the identity as $1_G$ or $e_G$ when several groups occur at the same time.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The [[additive group of integers|0BSCJA5G53]] $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$: addition is associative, $0$ is the identity, and $-n$ is the inverse of $n$.
2. The [[nonzero rational numbers under multiplication|3GJ6J4P05Z]] $(\mathbb{Q}^\times, \cdot)$: the identity is $1$, and the inverse of $a/b$ is $b/a$.
3. The [[integers modulo $n$|03VFZ2WETT]] $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$ with addition modulo $n$: the identity is $0$, and the inverse of $k \neq 0$ is $n - k$. This is the [[cyclic group of order $n$|0G0B3DRG65]].
4. The symmetries of a square, with composition: the identity is the motion that moves nothing, and each rotation or reflection is undone by a rotation or reflection. This group has $8$ elements, and it is a [[dihedral group|0JZ27DMJ5D]].
5. The [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ of invertible real $n \times n$ matrices with matrix multiplication: the identity is the identity matrix $I_n$, and the inverse of $A$ is the inverse matrix $A^{-1}$.
6. The [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$ of all permutations of $\{1, 2, 3\}$ with composition: it has $6$ elements, and [[it is not abelian|QSMVQ4EJ3D]].

### Non-examples

1. [[The natural numbers under addition are not a group|R68D2P175Z]]: the inverse axiom fails, because $1 + m = 0$ has no solution $m \in \mathbb{N}$.
2. [[The integers under multiplication are not a group|NH7XE98CMF]]: the inverse axiom fails, because $2m = 1$ has no solution $m \in \mathbb{Z}$.
3. The integers with subtraction are not a group: associativity fails, because $(1 - 1) - 1 = -1$ but $1 - (1 - 1) = 1$.
4. The real $2 \times 2$ matrices with matrix multiplication are not a group: the inverse axiom fails, because the zero matrix $Z$ satisfies $ZA = Z \neq I_2$ for each matrix $A$.

### Basic properties

1. The identity is unique, each element has exactly one inverse, $(a^{-1})^{-1} = a$, and $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1} a^{-1}$. See [[uniqueness of identity and inverses|0D9NDGE9KP]].
2. Both [[cancellation laws|0VSNX4P2Q0]] hold: $ab = ac$ implies $b = c$, and $ba = ca$ implies $b = c$. Each equation $ax = b$ has exactly one solution $x = a^{-1} b$.
3. Integer powers obey the [[laws of exponents|00DRK90Z6K]] $a^m a^n = a^{m+n}$ and $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$.
4. A product of any number of elements does not depend on the position of the parentheses, by [[general associativity|8HEYE9EZMW]].
5. It is enough to ask for a left identity and left inverses: by the result on [[one-sided group axioms|FF2JMV4F9G]], such a structure is a group.
6. In the [[Cayley table|0YB2NRP0T5]] of a finite group, each element occurs exactly one time in each row and in each column.

### History

Évariste Galois used the word "group" around 1830 for sets of permutations of the roots of an equation that are closed under composition.
In 1854 Arthur Cayley gave the first definition of an abstract finite group, by a set of symbols and their multiplication table.
The axioms in the present form, which also cover infinite groups, became standard in the late nineteenth and the early twentieth century.

### Common errors

1. Closure is not checked. The operation must be a binary operation on $G$: for example, the odd integers with addition are not a group, because $1 + 1 = 2$ is even.
2. A subset has its own identity. The matrices $\begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ with $a \neq 0$ real form a group under matrix multiplication, with the identity $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, not $I_2$. None of these matrices is invertible, so this group is not a [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] of $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{R})$.
3. Groups are taken to be commutative. The axioms do not ask for $ab = ba$, and in $S_3$ this law fails, so $(ab)^{-1} = a^{-1} b^{-1}$ is false in general.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A group is a collection of actions together with a rule to do one action after another.
Three rules must hold.
First, when we do three actions in a row, it does not matter which two we combine first.
Second, there is an action that does nothing.
Third, each action can be undone by another action of the collection.
The turns of a square by $0$, $90$, $180$ and $270$ degrees are an example.
Two turns give a turn, the turn by $0$ degrees does nothing, and a turn by $90$ degrees is undone by a turn by $270$ degrees.
The whole numbers with addition are another example: adding $0$ does nothing, and adding $-5$ undoes adding $5$.

### L2 human

We check the axioms for the set $G = \{1, -1, i, -i\}$ of complex numbers with complex multiplication.

1. Closure. The products of two elements are $\pm 1$ or $\pm i$, because $i \cdot i = -1$, $i \cdot (-i) = 1$, $(-i)(-i) = -1$, and a product with $\pm 1$ only changes the sign. So multiplication is a binary operation on $G$.
2. Associativity. Multiplication of complex numbers is associative, so it is associative on the subset $G$.
3. Identity. The number $1$ is in $G$, and $1 \cdot z = z = z \cdot 1$ for each $z$.
4. Inverses. We have $1 \cdot 1 = 1$, $(-1)(-1) = 1$ and $i \cdot (-i) = 1$. So the inverses are $1^{-1} = 1$, $(-1)^{-1} = -1$, $i^{-1} = -i$ and $(-i)^{-1} = i$, and each of them is in $G$.

Thus $G$ is a group with $4$ elements.
The typical use of the definition is this check of four conditions, in this order, for a candidate set and operation.
A trap is to check that inverses exist in a larger set only: the inverse of $2$ exists in $\mathbb{Q}$, but it is not in $\mathbb{Z}$, so it does not help $(\mathbb{Z}, \cdot)$.
A second trap is the identity: it must be the same element $e$ for every $a$, and the inverses must be taken with respect to this $e$.

### L3 human

A group is a [[monoid|0N6R902FG1]] in which each element is invertible, and a monoid is a [[semigroup|0XMEM406JM]] with an identity.
In the language of universal algebra, a group is a set with a binary operation, a unary operation $a \mapsto a^{-1}$ and a constant $e$ that satisfy equations, so groups form a variety of algebras.
In category theory, a group is a category with one object in which each morphism is invertible, and a group object in a category with finite products is the same axioms written with morphisms.
By [[Cayley's theorem|0151VYVX27]], each group is [[isomorphic|08MG7H8JW5]] to a group of permutations of a set, so the abstract axioms describe exactly the groups of symmetries.
The later sub-areas study subgroups, cosets, homomorphisms, quotients, actions and the structure of finite groups; the area of magmas, [[quasigroups|ZJD31SPPFP]] and loops studies the structures that satisfy only some of the three axioms.
