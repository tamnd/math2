## Statement

1. Let two homogeneous isotropic elastic half-spaces be welded along a plane interface.
Let the upper medium have P-speed $\alpha_1$ and S-speed $\beta_1$, and the lower medium $\alpha_2$ and $\beta_2$.
Let a [[plane|KBJTFMZWSF]] P- or S-wave of speed $v_0$ hit the interface at the angle $i_0$ from the normal.
Let a reflected or transmitted plane wave have speed $v_k \in \set{\alpha_1, \beta_1, \alpha_2, \beta_2}$.
Then it leaves at an angle $i_k$ from the normal with
$$\frac{\sin i_0}{v_0} = \frac{\sin i_k}{v_k},$$
whenever this equation has a solution $i_k \in [0, \pi/2]$.
2. Let the speed $v(z)$ depend only on the depth $z$, and let $i$ be the angle of a ray from the vertical.
Then $\sin i / v(z)$ is constant along each [[seismic ray|AZFGJKMKTP]].
3. Let the speed $v(r)$ depend only on the distance $r$ from a centre.
Let $i$ be the angle of a ray from the radial direction.
Then $r \sin i / v(r)$ is constant along each ray.

## Remarks

The common value in parts 2 and 3 is the [[ray parameter|JAFNCJNF6E]] of the ray.
Part 1 follows from the boundary conditions: displacement and traction must be continuous at every point of the interface and at every time.
This forces all the waves to share the same frequency and the same apparent speed $v_k / \sin i_k$ along the interface.
Parts 2 and 3 follow from part 1 applied to thin layers or shells.
They also follow from the ray equation, since the travel time is invariant under horizontal translations, respectively rotations.
Example: a [[P-wave|EEPJJHC5JZ]] in a crust with $\alpha_1 = 6$ km/s hits a mantle with $\alpha_2 = 8$ km/s.
The transmitted P-wave grazes the interface when $\sin i_0 = 6/8$, that is $i_0 \approx 48.6°$; beyond this critical angle there is no transmitted P-ray, only a head wave along the interface.
An incident P-wave also produces converted [[S-waves|GZ7E9GHQYB]], which leave at a smaller angle because $\beta < \alpha$.
Isotropy is needed: in an anisotropic medium the law holds for the slowness vectors, but the rays need not be parallel to them.
It is the elastic form of the optical [[Snell's law|QDV9SBK6W0]], with $1/v$ in place of the refractive index.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A seismic wave bends when it enters faster or slower rock, like light entering water.
The rule is that $\sin(\text{angle}) / \text{speed}$ stays the same on both sides.
Waves going into faster rock bend away from the vertical; past a critical angle they cannot enter at all.
Unlike light, one seismic wave can turn into two: a compressional and a shear wave, each obeying the same rule with its own speed.

### L2 human

In a layered or spherical Earth the conserved number $p$ labels each ray from source to receiver.
The ray turns where $\sin i = 1$, that is where $v(z) = 1/p$, or $v(r) = r/p$ in a sphere.
This gives the distance and travel time of each ray as integrals over depth, which is the basis of travel-time curves.
The amplitudes of the reflected and converted waves are given by the Zoeppritz equations, which use the angles found here.
