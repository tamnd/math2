## Statement

Jacobi's formula gives the rate of change of a determinant when the matrix depends on a parameter, and it shows that the derivative of the determinant at the identity is the trace.

Let $\mathbb{K}$ be $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, let $n \ge 1$, let $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be an open interval, and let $A \colon I \to \mathbb{K}^{n \times n}$ be a [[differentiable|0K57YG3BB8]] map, which means that every entry $a_{ij}(t)$ is differentiable.
Then $t \mapsto \det A(t)$ is differentiable, and for all $t \in I$
$$\frac{d}{dt} \det A(t) = \operatorname{tr}\big(\operatorname{adj}(A(t))\, A'(t)\big),$$
where $\det$ is the [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]], $\operatorname{adj}$ is the [[adjugate|07W9H8QF38]] and $\operatorname{tr}$ is the [[trace|0XC1RDBY7A]].
If $A(t)$ is invertible, the right side equals $\det A(t) \cdot \operatorname{tr}\big(A(t)^{-1} A'(t)\big)$.

## Remarks

### A check with two by two matrices

Take $A(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ t & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Directly, $\det A(t) = 1 - t^2$, so the derivative is $-2t$.
For the formula we need the adjugate, which swaps the diagonal entries and negates the others, and the derivative of $A$:
$$\operatorname{adj} A(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -t \\ -t & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A'(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Multiply them:
$$\operatorname{adj}(A(t))\, A'(t) = \begin{pmatrix} -t & 1 \\ 1 & -t \end{pmatrix}.$$
The trace is $-2t$, as it should be.
The [[proof by the product rule and Laplace expansion|T4BJJ2BA2M]] shows why this works for every size.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The entries of $A$ must be differentiable, since the formula uses $A'(t)$.
Without it the determinant can fail to be differentiable: for $A(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \lvert t \rvert & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ we get $\det A(t) = \lvert t \rvert$, which has no derivative at $t = 0$.

The form with $A(t)^{-1}$ needs $A(t)$ to be invertible, but the form with the adjugate does not.
For $A(t) = \begin{pmatrix} t & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ the matrix is singular for every $t$, and $\det A(t) = 0$.
The adjugate form still agrees, since $\operatorname{adj} A(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & t \end{pmatrix}$ and $A'(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ have product $0$.

### The derivative at the identity

Put $A(t) = I + tX$ for a fixed matrix $X$.
Then $A(0) = I$, $\operatorname{adj} I = I$ and $A'(0) = X$, so the formula gives
$$\frac{d}{dt} \det(I + tX) \Big|_{t=0} = \operatorname{tr} X.$$
For $X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ we can check this by hand:
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 + t & 2t \\ 3t & 1 + 4t \end{pmatrix} = (1 + t)(1 + 4t) - 6t^2 = 1 + 5t - 2t^2,$$
whose derivative at $0$ is $5 = 1 + 4$.

### What it gives you

For $A(t) = e^{tX}$, the [[matrix exponential|020M04FN1H]], we have $A'(t) = X e^{tX}$, so the formula gives $\frac{d}{dt} \det e^{tX} = \operatorname{tr}(X) \det e^{tX}$.
This differential equation with $\det e^{0} = 1$ has the solution $\det e^{tX} = e^{t \operatorname{tr} X}$, the [[determinant of a matrix exponential|0A1QQN333A]].
In the same way, a fundamental matrix $\Phi$ of the system $x' = M(t) x$ satisfies $\frac{d}{dt} \det \Phi = \operatorname{tr}(M) \det \Phi$, which is [[Liouville's formula|09PS3S0EF6]] for the Wronskian.

Taking $A(t)$ to vary in one entry only shows that $\partial \det A / \partial a_{ij}$ is the cofactor $C_{ij}$.
So the gradient of $\det$ is the matrix of cofactors.
For an invertible $A$ with $\det A > 0$, the formula gives $\frac{d}{dt} \log \det A(t) = \operatorname{tr}\big(A^{-1} A'\big)$, which is used often in statistics and in physics.

### Watch out

The derivative of $\det A$ is not $\det A'$.
In the first check above, $\det A'(t) = 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1 = -1$ for every $t$, while the derivative of $\det A$ is $-2t$.

### Generalizations

The same proof works for any derivation of a commutative ring, for example the formal derivative of polynomials or of power series.
For a matrix of functions of several variables, it gives the differential $d(\det A) = \operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}(A)\, dA)$.

## History

The formula is named after Carl Gustav Jacobi.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Jacobi's formula tells how fast the determinant changes when the matrix changes a little.
Near the identity matrix the answer is simple: the rate of change is the sum of the diagonal changes.
For example, if the diagonal entries are $1 + t$ and $1 + 2t$ and the others are $0$, the determinant is $(1 + t)(1 + 2t) = 1 + 3t + 2t^2$.
For small $t$ it grows at the rate $3$, and $3 = 1 + 2$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: how fast does the area of a parallelogram change under a moving linear map?
Let the columns of $A(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & t \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ span the parallelogram, so its signed area is $\det A(t) = 6 - t$.
At $t = 0$ the matrix is invertible with $\det A(0) = 6$ and
$$A(0)^{-1} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A'(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Their product is $\frac{1}{6} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, with trace $-\frac{1}{6}$.
So the formula gives $6 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{6}\right) = -1$, which matches the derivative of $6 - t$.
The relative rate $\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1} A')$ is the useful number: the area shrinks by about one sixth of itself per unit of $t$.

### L3 human

The formula says that the differential of $\det$ at $A$ is the linear map $X \mapsto \operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}(A)\, X)$.
In Lie theory, the determinant is a homomorphism $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{K}) \to \mathbb{K}^\times$, and its derivative at the identity is the trace $\mathfrak{gl}_n(\mathbb{K}) \to \mathbb{K}$.
This gives $\det \circ \exp = \exp \circ \operatorname{tr}$, and it shows that the Lie algebra of the [[special linear group|0V9PMN0RBH]] consists of the matrices of trace $0$.
In the theory of flows, it says that a vector field with divergence $0$ preserves volume.
