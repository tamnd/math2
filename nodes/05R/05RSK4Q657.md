## Summary

A truth table lists every case of a compound statement and computes its truth value in each case, so a finite check replaces a guess.
Is "it is not true that I passed both tests" the same claim as "I failed at least one test"?
A table with four rows, one for each combination of results, answers yes.
For a formula $\varphi$ with variables $p_1, \dots, p_n$, the truth table has one row for each of the $2^n$ assignments of truth values, and its last column gives the value of $\varphi$ in each row.
Each row is computed from the inside out, one [[logical connective|QS0XVAN9BG]] at a time, with the tables of [[negation|0XR7A7RSXB]], [[conjunction|0M28ASWG8T]], [[disjunction|0H5GKDTB01]], [[material implication|0ZNX6DQ3EJ]] and the [[biconditional|0V6GAWTBYW]].
The method decides whether a formula is a [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]], whether two formulas are [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]], and whether an argument is valid.

## Motivation

The truth value of a compound [[proposition|0ZGC7ZESYN]] depends only on the truth values of its parts.
So a formula with $n$ variables can be true or false in only finitely many ways, one for each assignment of values to the variables.
The question whether $\varphi$ is true for all values of $p$, $q$ and $r$ can therefore be answered by listing all cases.
A truth table is this list, written in a fixed order so that no case is lost or counted twice.
It is the first algorithm of logic, and it proves most laws of propositional logic.

## Notation

The rows follow a standard order.
For two variables the order is TT, TF, FT, FF.
For three variables it is TTT, TTF, TFT, TFF, FTT, FTF, FFT, FFF.
In general the first variable changes every $2^{n-1}$ rows, the second every $2^{n-2}$ rows, and the last in every row.
Many books, and most books of computer science, write $1$ and $0$ for T and F and start with the row $0 \cdots 0$.
The metalanguage writes $\models \varphi$ for "$\varphi$ is a tautology", $\varphi \equiv \psi$ for "$\varphi$ and $\psi$ are logically equivalent", and $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_k \models \psi$ for "the argument is valid".

## Remarks

### Building a table step by step

Let $p$ be "I passed the math test" and $q$ be "I passed the physics test".
We compare $\neg(p \land q)$, "it is not true that I passed both", with $\neg p \lor \neg q$, "I failed math or I failed physics".

1. List the variables and write the $2^2 = 4$ rows in the standard order.
2. List the subformulas from the smallest to the largest, and give each one a column.
3. Fill each column row by row from the columns of its parts and the table of its main connective.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \land q$ | $\neg(p \land q)$ | $\neg p$ | $\neg q$ | $\neg p \lor \neg q$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | F | F | F | F |
| T | F | F | T | F | T | T |
| F | T | F | T | T | F | T |
| F | F | F | T | T | T | T |

The columns of $\neg(p \land q)$ and $\neg p \lor \neg q$ agree in all four rows, so the two sentences make the same claim.
This is one of [[De Morgan's laws|68VBVMFJSW]].

### What a table decides

A formula is a tautology exactly when its column has T in every row.
It is a contradiction when the column has F in every row, and a contingency otherwise.
Two formulas are logically equivalent exactly when their columns agree in every row of a common table, that is, when $\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi$ is a tautology.
An argument with premises $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_k$ and conclusion $\psi$ is valid exactly when no row makes all premises T and $\psi$ F, that is, when $(\varphi_1 \land \dots \land \varphi_k) \to \psi$ is a tautology.
The method always terminates, so the set of tautologies of propositional logic is decidable.

### Short cuts

A full table has $2^n$ rows, which is $1024$ rows for $n = 10$.
The assume-false method supposes that a row makes $\varphi$ F and works out the values this forces on the parts.
If the forced values contradict each other, no such row exists and $\varphi$ is a tautology, and if they are consistent, they give a row where $\varphi$ is F.
To test an argument, it is enough to examine the rows where the conclusion is F and check that a premise is F in each.
Many cells also need no work: an implication with a false antecedent is T, a conjunction with a false part is F, and a disjunction with a true part is T.

### Worked examples

[[The table of $((p \to q) \land (q \to r)) \to (p \to r)$|VP6RN56Z5A]] has $8$ rows and a last column of T only, so the formula is a tautology.
[[Checking the validity of an argument|3FPFGDVQCV]] shows that "$p \lor q$, $p \to r$, $q \to r$, therefore $r$" is valid and that "$p \to r$, $r$, therefore $p$" is not.
[[Peirce's law by the assume-false method|FPDDSFR941]] proves $((p \to q) \to p) \to p$ in a few lines and finds a falsifying row for $((p \to q) \to q) \to q$.
The [[law of excluded middle|ZNQN1JW1V3]] and [[the contrapositive|SWH5EJP8PS]] are proved the same way.

### What a table cannot decide

[[A table of cases does not decide a quantified statement|ZVR0ZW6DF3]]: "$n^2 + n + 41$ is prime" holds for $n = 0, \dots, 39$ and fails at $n = 40$.
A truth table works only for finitely many propositional variables, and a statement about all natural numbers has infinitely many instances.
A table of values of a modal formula $\Box p$ is not a truth table either, because $\Box$ has no truth function.

### Watch out

A table with fewer than $2^n$ rows is not a truth table.
A table of $(p \lor q) \to p$ with only the rows TT and TF suggests a tautology, but the row FT gives F.
One row with F refutes a tautology, so every row must be checked, or the assume-false method must be used.
Also fill each column only from columns to its left, so that no compound is computed before its parts.

### History

Ludwig Wittgenstein, in the Tractatus of 1921, and Emil Post, in his paper of 1921, used truth tables to describe all truth functions.
Post used them to prove that the tautologies are exactly the theorems of the propositional calculus of Principia Mathematica.

### Try it

How many rows does the table of $(p \to q) \lor (r \land \neg s)$ have?
It has $4$ variables, so $2^4 = 16$ rows.

Is $(p \to q) \lor (q \to p)$ a tautology?
Assume a row makes it F.
Then $p \to q$ is F, so $p$ is T and $q$ is F, and $q \to p$ is F, so $q$ is T, which is a contradiction.
So it is a tautology.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A truth table lists every possible case of a statement built from simple parts.
With two parts $p$ and $q$, there are $4$ cases: both true, only $p$ true, only $q$ true, both false.
For "$p$ or $q$" the table gives true, true, true and false.
A statement that comes out true in every case is a law of logic.
"$p$ or not $p$" is such a law: when $p$ is true, the first part is true, and when $p$ is false, the second part is true.

### L2 human

Test whether $p \to (q \to r)$ and $(p \land q) \to r$ are logically equivalent.

1. There are $3$ variables, so there are $2^3 = 8$ rows.
2. The formula $(p \land q) \to r$ is F only when $p \land q$ is T and $r$ is F, that is, in the row TTF.
3. The formula $p \to (q \to r)$ is F only when $p$ is T and $q \to r$ is F, that is, again only in the row TTF.
4. Both formulas are F in the same single row and T in the other $7$, so they are logically equivalent.

Steps 2 and 3 use the short cut of looking only for the rows where an implication is F.
In proofs this equivalence lets us prove "if $P$ and $Q$, then $R$" by assuming $P$, then assuming $Q$, and deriving $R$.
Watch out: $(p \to q) \to r$ is not equivalent to these two, since it is F in the row FTF, where both are T.

### L3 human

A truth table computes the function $\{\mathrm{T}, \mathrm{F}\}^n \to \{\mathrm{T}, \mathrm{F}\}$ that a formula defines, so it is the semantics of propositional logic in its most concrete form.
The number of rows grows as $2^n$, and no method is known that decides tautologies in time polynomial in the size of the formula.
Deciding tautologies is complete for the class [[co-NP|NBSD5FG7NY]], and deciding [[satisfiability|QN7M1JCGT9]] is the complementary problem.
The assume-false method grows into semantic tableaux and resolution, which search for a falsifying row in a structured way.
Truth tables do not extend to [[first-order logic|086QKPF0A6]], where a universal statement over an infinite domain has infinitely many instances.
In [[many-valued logic|DWYCKAKXZD]] the tables have $k^n$ rows for $k$ truth values.
In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]] no finite many-valued table gives exactly the intuitionistic tautologies.
