## Statement

Let $L$ be a [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]], and let $\mathcal{K}$ be a class of [[$L$-structures|983GZBQYT4]] with nonempty domains.

1. $\mathcal{K}$ is a [[universal class|ZSTEZ3PRT9]] if and only if it is closed under isomorphisms, [[substructures|676H7EGTKJ]] and [[ultraproducts|AX9Q86GB8M]].
2. For every class $\mathcal{K}$, a structure $A$ satisfies every universal sentence true in all members of $\mathcal{K}$ if and only if $A$ embeds in an ultraproduct of members of $\mathcal{K}$.
3. If $\mathcal{K}$ is universal, a structure $A$ is in $\mathcal{K}$ if and only if every substructure of $A$ generated by a nonempty finite set is in $\mathcal{K}$.
4. If $L$ is finite and relational, $\mathcal{K}$ is universal if and only if there is a set $\mathcal{F}$ of finite $L$-structures such that $A \in \mathcal{K}$ exactly when no member of $\mathcal{F}$ embeds in $A$.

A proof is in [[the proof of the characterization of universal classes|8Q69SQYY9D]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Ultraproducts. The finite graphs are closed under isomorphisms and substructures, but an ultraproduct of larger and larger finite graphs is infinite. They are not a universal class.
- Substructures. The fields are closed under isomorphisms and ultraproducts, by [[Łoś's theorem|AGYBSTD7BG]], but not under substructures. They are elementary and not universal.
- Finite relational signature in part 4. In the signature $\set{+, -, \cdot, 0, 1}$ the class of integral domains is universal, but a finitely generated subring of an integral domain, such as $\mathbb{Z}$, can be infinite.

### Consequences

- The universal class generated by $\mathcal{K}$ is $\mathbf{I}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{P}_U(\mathcal{K})$, by part 2.
- The $k$-colorable graphs form a universal class: they are closed under substructures, and a coloring of each factor gives a coloring of an ultraproduct. See [[this example|JKQMSK6RMJ]].
- A finite structure $M$ generates the universal class of structures that embed in an ultrapower of $M$; since an ultrapower of a finite $M$ is isomorphic to $M$, this is the class of substructures of copies of $M$.

### Converse

Each part is an equivalence.
The direction from universal axioms to closure is the easy one, by [[this statement|C9494XYEMN]] and Łoś's theorem.

### Generalizations

- Replacing ultraproducts by reduced products gives the [[universal Horn classes|VCJX55N45K]].
- Dropping substructures and adding closure under elementary equivalence gives the [[characterization of elementary classes|4HNAJQQ8Z0]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some families of structures can be described by rules that forbid patterns.
The theorem says when this is possible: the family must keep every part of a member, and it must keep a certain kind of limit of its members, the ultraproduct.
For graphs with at most $3$ colors, both checks pass, so they are described by forbidden patterns.

### L2 human

We use part 4 for graphs without an induced path on three vertices.

1. Let $P_3$ be the graph on $\set{1, 2, 3}$ with edges $12$ and $23$ only.
2. Let $\mathcal{F}$ consist of $P_3$, the one-point structure with a loop, and the two-point structure with one edge in one direction only.
3. An $\set{E}$-structure into which no member of $\mathcal{F}$ embeds is a graph with no induced copy of $P_3$.
4. By part 4, these graphs form a universal class.
5. A graph is in this class exactly when "adjacent or equal" is an equivalence relation, that is, when it is a disjoint union of complete graphs.

The typical use is to read off axioms from forbidden pieces.
A trap is to forbid $P_3$ only as a subgraph, not as an induced one: then the triangle is excluded too.
