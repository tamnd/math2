## Proof

We show that $P = (A^* A)^{1/2}$ and $A$ change lengths in the same way, so that $Px \mapsto Ax$ is an isometry, and then we extend this isometry to a unitary matrix.
Here $^*$ is the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]], $(XY)^* = Y^* X^*$ by the [[transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]], and $\ip{x}{y} = y^* x$ on $\C^n$.

*Existence.*

1. By [[the Gram matrix theorem|0AM050KCAW]], $A^* A$ is positive semidefinite. Let $P = (A^* A)^{1/2}$ be its [[positive semidefinite square root|EQ98KW1ZN4]], so $P^* = P$ and $P^2 = A^* A$.
2. For $x, y \in \C^n$ we get $\ip{Px}{Py} = y^* P^* P x = y^* P^2 x = y^* A^* A x = \ip{Ax}{Ay}$. In particular $\norm{Px} = \norm{Ax}$.
3. By step 2, $Px = 0$ if and only if $Ax = 0$, so $\ker P = \ker A$.
4. Define $U_0 \colon \im P \to \im A$ by $U_0(Px) = Ax$. It is well defined, because $Px = Px'$ gives $P(x - x') = 0$ and then $A(x - x') = 0$ by step 3. It is linear, and its image is all of $\im A$.
5. By step 2, $\ip{U_0 Px}{U_0 Py} = \ip{Ax}{Ay} = \ip{Px}{Py}$, so $U_0$ preserves inner products.
6. Let $r = \dim \im P$. By step 3 and the [[rank-nullity theorem|0W4FSW1TVB]], $\dim \im A = n - \dim \ker A = n - \dim \ker P = r$.
7. By the [[existence of an orthonormal basis|0Y85GYEGSF]], choose an orthonormal basis $e_1, \dots, e_r$ of $\im P$. By step 5, the vectors $U_0 e_1, \dots, U_0 e_r$ are orthonormal, and they lie in $\im A$.
8. By the [[dimension of the orthogonal complement|0E2NYPVSP9]], $(\im P)^\perp$ and $(\im A)^\perp$ both have dimension $n - r$. Choose orthonormal bases $f_1, \dots, f_{n-r}$ and $g_1, \dots, g_{n-r}$ of them.
9. The lists $(e_1, \dots, e_r, f_1, \dots, f_{n-r})$ and $(U_0 e_1, \dots, U_0 e_r, g_1, \dots, g_{n-r})$ are orthonormal, because each part is orthogonal to the other part. Each has $n$ vectors, so each is an orthonormal basis of $\C^n$.
10. Let $E$ and $G$ be the $n \times n$ matrices with these columns. By the [[orthonormal columns criterion|83QE4F4E4V]], $E^* E = G^* G = I$, and by the [[one-sided inverse theorem|0753Y2X5K6]], also $E E^* = I$.
11. Let $U = G E^*$. Then $U^* U = E G^* G E^* = E E^* = I$, so $U$ is [[unitary|0D6RMJ10M9]]. Also $E^* e_i = \epsilon_i$, the $i$-th standard basis vector, so $U e_i = G \epsilon_i = U_0 e_i$ for $i \le r$.
12. By step 11 and linearity, $U = U_0$ on $\im P$. So $U P x = U_0 P x = A x$ for every $x$, that is, $A = UP$. This proves part 1.

*Uniqueness.*

13. Let $A = U' P'$ with $U'$ unitary and $P'$ positive semidefinite. Then $A^* A = P'^* U'^* U' P' = P'^2$, and the uniqueness in [[the square root theorem|EQ98KW1ZN4]] gives $P' = (A^* A)^{1/2} = P$. This proves part 2.
14. Let $A$ be invertible, and let $A = U' P$ as in step 13. By the [[multiplicativity of the determinant|04CPJ6AF00]], $\det U' \det P = \det A \ne 0$, so $\det P \ne 0$ and $P$ is [[invertible|FBSAPTP1PR]].
15. Then $U' = A P^{-1}$ is determined by $A$. Since $0$ is not an eigenvalue of $P$, all eigenvalues of $P$ are positive, and $P$ is positive definite by the [[eigenvalue test|0HYC2RHD3M]]. This proves part 3.

*The real case and the left form.*

16. Let $A$ be real. Then $A^* A = A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ is real, and $P$ is real by the real case of the square root theorem. Steps 4 to 12 work in $\R^n$ with real orthonormal bases, so $U$ is real with $U^{\mathsf{T}} U = I$. This proves part 4.
17. Let $P' = U P U^*$. It is [[Hermitian|0EKZCX4K3P]], and $x^* P' x = (U^* x)^* P (U^* x) \ge 0$, so it is [[positive semidefinite|0880F9EQCP]]. Also $P' U = U P U^* U = UP = A$.
18. Finally $P'^2 = U P^2 U^* = U P P^* U^* = (UP)(UP)^* = A A^*$. By the uniqueness of square roots, $P' = (A A^*)^{1/2}$. This proves part 5.

## Idea

The matrix $P = (A^* A)^{1/2}$ is built so that $\norm{Px} = \norm{Ax}$ for every $x$.
So the rule "send $Px$ to $Ax$" is a well defined map from the range of $P$ onto the range of $A$, and it keeps lengths and angles.
The two ranges have the same dimension, so their orthogonal complements do too.
Any isometry between the two complements completes the map to a unitary $U$ with $UP = A$, and the freedom in that choice is why $U$ is not unique when $A$ is singular.

## Variants

For invertible $A$ the extension in steps 7 to 12 is not needed.
Set $U = A P^{-1}$ and compute directly:
$$U^* U = P^{-1} A^* A P^{-1} = P^{-1} P^2 P^{-1} = I.$$

The [[singular value decomposition|000NPTMX6J]] gives a second proof.
If $A = W \Sigma V^*$ with $W$ and $V$ unitary and $\Sigma$ square and diagonal with nonnegative entries, then
$$A = (W V^*)(V \Sigma V^*),$$
where $W V^*$ is unitary and $V \Sigma V^*$ is positive semidefinite.
That route needs the SVD first, which is proved from the spectral theorem for $A^* A$.
