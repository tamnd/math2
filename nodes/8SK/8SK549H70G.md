## Proof

Let $G$ be a finite [[group|G5S1M9]] with identity $e$, and let $g \in G$.

1. The element $g$ has finite [[order|0WE2NHZJ7B]]. Suppose not. Then $\langle g \rangle$ is infinite, by [[the theorem on the order of a cyclic subgroup|031M8HX7DA]]. This is impossible, because $\langle g \rangle \subseteq G$ and $G$ is finite.
2. Let $m$ be the order of $g$. By [[the theorem on element orders|08V7JHS3QZ]], $m$ divides $|G|$.
3. By [[the divisibility criterion for powers|0F5QC101Q7]], $g^n = e$ for each integer $n$ that is a multiple of $m$.
4. By steps 2 and 3, with $n = |G|$, we get $g^{|G|} = e$.

## Idea

The order of $g$ divides the order of the group, by Lagrange's theorem.
A power of $g$ is the identity whenever its exponent is a multiple of the order of $g$.

## Variants

For an abelian group $G = \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ there is a proof without cosets.
The map $x \mapsto gx$ is a bijection of $G$, so $gx_1, \ldots, gx_n$ is again a list of all elements.
Since $G$ is abelian, the products of the two lists are equal: $x_1 \cdots x_n = (gx_1) \cdots (gx_n) = g^n x_1 \cdots x_n$.
We cancel $x_1 \cdots x_n$ and get $g^n = e$.
This is the classical proof of Euler's theorem with residues modulo $n$.
