## Proof

We expand $\norm{x + y}^2$ once for all $x$ and $y$, read off parts 1, 3 and 4, prove part 2 by induction, and check the example of part 5.
Let $V$ be an inner product space over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, with [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}$ linear in the first slot.

*The expansion.*

1. The second slot is additive: for $u, z, w \in V$, conjugate symmetry and linearity in the first slot give
$$\ip{u}{z + w} = \overline{\ip{z}{u} + \ip{w}{u}} = \ip{u}{z} + \ip{u}{w}.$$
2. By the definition of the [[induced norm|0KG4D8AP6G]], linearity in the first slot and step 1,
$$\norm{x + y}^2 = \ip{x}{x} + \ip{x}{y} + \ip{y}{x} + \ip{y}{y}.$$
3. Conjugate symmetry gives $\ip{x}{y} + \ip{y}{x} = \ip{x}{y} + \overline{\ip{x}{y}} = 2\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y}$, so for all $x, y \in V$
$$\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} + \norm{y}^2.$$

*Parts 3, 1 and 4.*

4. By step 3, $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + \norm{y}^2$ holds if and only if $\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} = 0$, which is part 3.
5. If $x$ and $y$ are [[orthogonal|0TS7VZJBWD]], then $\ip{x}{y} = 0$, so its real part is $0$, and step 4 gives part 1.
6. If $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$, then $\ip{x}{y}$ is real, so $\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} = \ip{x}{y}$.
   Then the identity forces $\ip{x}{y} = 0$ by step 4, which is part 4.

*Part 2, by induction on $k$.*

7. For $k = 1$ both sides are $\norm{x_1}^2$.
8. Assume part 2 for $k$ vectors, let $x_1, \dots, x_{k+1}$ be pairwise orthogonal, and put $s = x_1 + \dots + x_k$.
9. Step 1, applied $k - 1$ times, gives $\ip{x_{k+1}}{s} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \ip{x_{k+1}}{x_j} = 0$, so $x_{k+1} \perp s$.
10. Part 1 gives $\norm{s + x_{k+1}}^2 = \norm{s}^2 + \norm{x_{k+1}}^2$, and the induction hypothesis gives $\norm{s}^2 = \norm{x_1}^2 + \dots + \norm{x_k}^2$.
    This proves part 2 for $k + 1$ vectors.

*Part 5.*

11. Let $V = \mathbb{C}$ with $\ip{z}{w} = z\overline{w}$, and let $x = 1$ and $y = i$.
    Then $\norm{x}^2 = 1$ and $\norm{y}^2 = i \cdot \overline{i} = 1$.
12. Also $\norm{x + y}^2 = (1 + i)\overline{(1 + i)} = (1 + i)(1 - i) = 2 = 1 + 1$, so the identity holds.
13. But $\ip{x}{y} = 1 \cdot \overline{i} = -i \neq 0$, so $x$ and $y$ are not orthogonal, and the converse fails over $\mathbb{C}$.

## Idea

The squared norm of a sum expands like the square of a sum of numbers, with the cross term $2\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y}$.
Orthogonality makes the cross term zero, and induction adds one orthogonal vector at a time.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ the cross term is $2\ip{x}{y}$ itself, so the identity forces orthogonality.
Over $\mathbb{C}$ only the real part appears, and the imaginary part can be anything, which is why the converse fails.

## Variants

Part 2 also has a direct proof without induction.
Expand $\norm{\sum_j x_j}^2 = \sum_{j, l} \ip{x_j}{x_l}$, and note that every term with $j \neq l$ is $0$.
The [[counterexample over $\mathbb{C}$|VT90B84J5V]] works out steps 11 to 13 in more detail, together with the second test with $x + iy$ that repairs the converse.
