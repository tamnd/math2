## Proof

Let $V$ be an inner product space over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, with [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}$.

1. Additivity in the second argument. For $u, z, w \in V$, conjugate symmetry and linearity give $\ip{u}{z + w} = \overline{\ip{z}{u} + \ip{w}{u}} = \ip{u}{z} + \ip{u}{w}$.
2. Expansion. By the definition of the [[induced norm|0KG4D8AP6G]] and step 1, $\norm{x + y}^2 = \ip{x}{x} + \ip{x}{y} + \ip{y}{x} + \ip{y}{y}$.
3. By conjugate symmetry, $\ip{x}{y} + \ip{y}{x} = \ip{x}{y} + \overline{\ip{x}{y}} = 2\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y}$.
4. So $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} + \norm{y}^2$ for all $x, y \in V$.
5. Part 3. By step 4, $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + \norm{y}^2$ holds if and only if $2\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} = 0$.
6. Part 1. If $x$ and $y$ are [[orthogonal|0TS7VZJBWD]], then $\ip{x}{y} = 0$, so $\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} = 0$. Step 5 gives the identity.
7. Part 2, base case. For $k = 1$ both sides are $\norm{x_1}^2$.
8. Part 2, inductive step. Assume part 2 for $k$ vectors. Let $x_1, \dots, x_{k+1}$ be pairwise orthogonal, and put $s = x_1 + \dots + x_k$.
9. By step 1 extended to $k$ terms, $\ip{x_{k+1}}{s} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \ip{x_{k+1}}{x_j} = 0$. So $x_{k+1} \perp s$.
10. Part 1 gives $\norm{s + x_{k+1}}^2 = \norm{s}^2 + \norm{x_{k+1}}^2$.
11. The induction hypothesis gives $\norm{s}^2 = \norm{x_1}^2 + \dots + \norm{x_k}^2$. This proves part 2 for $k + 1$ vectors.
12. Part 4. Let $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$. Then $\ip{x}{y}$ is real, so $\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} = \ip{x}{y}$.
13. So if $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + \norm{y}^2$, then step 5 gives $\ip{x}{y} = 0$, that is, $x \perp y$.
14. Part 5. Let $V = \mathbb{C}$ with $\ip{z}{w} = z\overline{w}$, $x = 1$ and $y = i$. Then $\norm{x}^2 = \norm{y}^2 = 1$.
15. Also $\norm{x + y}^2 = (1 + i)(1 - i) = 2 = 1 + 1$. So the identity holds.
16. But $\ip{x}{y} = 1 \cdot \overline{i} = -i \neq 0$. So $x$ and $y$ are not orthogonal, and the converse fails.

## Idea

The square of the norm of a sum expands like the square of a sum of numbers, with the cross term $2\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y}$.
Orthogonality makes the cross term zero, and induction adds one orthogonal vector at a time.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ the cross term is $2\ip{x}{y}$ itself, but over $\mathbb{C}$ the imaginary part is lost.

## Variants

- One can prove part 2 directly: expand $\norm{\sum_j x_j}^2 = \sum_{j, l} \ip{x_j}{x_l}$, and all terms with $j \neq l$ are $0$.
- The [[counterexample over $\mathbb{C}$|~9026ead5]] gives step 14 to step 16 with more detail, and the test with $x + iy$ that repairs the converse.
