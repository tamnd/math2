## Summary

A formal system is a game with strings of symbols: it fixes where you may start and which moves you may make, and every string you can reach is a theorem.
It turns proofs into finite objects that a machine can check, so that "what can be proved" becomes a mathematical question.

Here is a small one, the [[MIU system|PMN32E37BW]].
The only starting string is $MI$, and four rules rewrite strings: add $U$ after a final $I$, double everything after the $M$, replace $III$ by $U$, and delete $UU$.
So $MI$ gives $MII$, then $MIIII$, then $MUI$.
Can you reach $MU$?
No: the number of letters $I$ starts at $1$, and the rules keep it, double it or lower it by $3$, so it is never a multiple of $3$, while $MU$ has none.

In general, a formal system $S$ consists of four parts, each of them [[decidable|T62EJ749R5]]:

1. an [[alphabet|RABRP2KG81]], a set of symbols;
2. formation rules, which pick out some finite [[strings|1P6EP01HZF]] over the alphabet as the formulas of $S$;
3. a set of formulas, the axioms of $S$;
4. a finite set of rules of inference, each of which has a fixed number of premises and says which formula may follow given premises.

A derivation in $S$ is a finite sequence of formulas in which each formula is an axiom or follows from earlier formulas by a rule.
A theorem of $S$ is the last formula of some derivation, and we write $\vdash_S \varphi$.

## Motivation

A proof in a textbook leaves many small steps to the reader, and two readers can disagree about whether it is complete.
Around 1900 the paradoxes of set theory, such as [[Russell's paradox|0HESMMSYEG]], made this disagreement a serious problem.
A formal system removes it.
The allowed steps are listed in advance, and each step looks only at the shape of strings, so a computer can check a derivation line by line.

The second gain is larger.
Once derivations are finite objects, we can prove facts about all derivations at once, as we did for $MU$.
[[Hilbert's program|P1RW6JWX73]] asked for exactly such a fact: a proof that no derivation in a system for mathematics ends in a contradiction.
Formal systems return in every part of mathematical logic: [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]] compares their theorems with truth, the [[incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]] show their limits for arithmetic, and proof theory studies the shape of their derivations.

## Notation

We write $\vdash_S \varphi$, or $\vdash \varphi$ when $S$ is fixed, for "$\varphi$ is a theorem of $S$".
For a set $\Gamma$ of formulas, $\Gamma \vdash_S \varphi$ means that some derivation ends with $\varphi$ when the members of $\Gamma$ may be used as extra axioms, called hypotheses.
Books also say formal theory, deductive system, logistic system or calculus.
Some books allow a countably infinite alphabet, such as the propositional variables $p_0, p_1, p_2, \dots$, and then the alphabet must be decidable too.
Others drop decidability from the definition and call a system effective when it holds.

Two words need a fixed meaning across the sub-area.
A theory is any set of sentences, and its members are its axioms; the set of all sentences derivable from a theory $T$ is its deductive closure, and $T$ is deductively closed when it equals its closure.
"Complete" has three meanings, and each page says which one it uses.
A proof system is complete for a semantics when it derives every consequence in that semantics.
A theory is negation complete when it proves $\varphi$ or $\neg \varphi$ for each sentence $\varphi$.
A system is Post complete when no new axiom schema can be added without inconsistency, as in [[Post consistency and Post completeness|WK2P0E82B4]].

## Remarks

### Systems you can play with

The MIU system has no meaning at all, and that is its point: you can derive strings with no idea of truth.
The [[pq-system|96BDP1YQMT]] is different.
Its alphabet is $\{ p, q, - \}$, and its theorems are exactly the strings $-^a p -^b q -^{a+b}$ with $a, b \geq 1$, such as $--p-q---$.
If you read $p$ as "plus" and $q$ as "equals", every theorem is a true sum in unary notation.

A [[Hilbert system|1MGMQX43B8]] for propositional logic uses the variables, $\neg$, $\to$ and parentheses, three axiom schemas and the single rule [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]].
Its theorems are exactly the [[tautologies|05006AJ7DZ]], by soundness and completeness.
First-order arithmetic with the [[Peano axioms|0QFT7Z76N5]] and [[Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory|0HE5WDQ9FJ]] are formal systems as well.
Each has infinitely many axioms, given by schemas, but a program can still decide whether a formula is one of them.
A [[formal grammar|DP1HXZGPCM]] also fits the definition: the start symbol is the axiom, and each production is a rule with one premise.

### What is not a formal system

[[True arithmetic taken as a set of axioms|9BW1F5B5GA]] takes every sentence true in the natural numbers as an axiom.
It fails the condition that the axioms form a decidable set, so nobody can check its derivations.
A system with the $\omega$-rule, "from $\varphi(0), \varphi(1), \varphi(2), \dots$ infer $\forall x\, \varphi(x)$", fails the condition that each rule has finitely many premises; it is not a [[finitary formal system|AP5F7FXTVM]].
An ordinary [[mathematical proof|0B94TKFQJX]] in a paper is not a derivation either, because its steps do not come from a fixed list of rules on strings.
The page on the [[axiomatic method|GD2JBXJV5Z]] discusses how the two relate.

### What every formal system has

It is decidable whether a finite list of formulas is a derivation.
Each line needs finitely many checks: is it an axiom, or does one of the finitely many rules produce it from earlier lines?
So we can list all derivations, one after another, and collect their last lines.
This shows that the set of theorems is [[computably enumerable|TN9G24VABN]].

The set of theorems need not be decidable.
For first-order logic it is not, by [[Church's theorem|VA6BNNY2YC]], although its axioms form a decidable set.
The theorems are closed under the rules: write down the derivations of the premises one after the other, and add the conclusion.
The relation $\Gamma \vdash_S \varphi$ is a [[consequence relation|J78Y1M4GND]], the abstract notion of the next pages.

### Induction on derivations

How did we rule out $MU$?
We found a property that the axiom has and that every rule keeps, and we concluded that every theorem has it.
This is induction on the length of derivations, and it is the main tool for showing that a formula is not a theorem.
The proof of [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]] uses the same pattern with "true in every interpretation" as the property.
Failed searches prove nothing: a thousand attempts to derive $MU$ do not show that it is underivable, but the invariant does.

### Syntax and semantics

The rules of a formal system act on strings and use no meaning of the symbols; this is syntax.
A semantics gives formulas a meaning, usually by saying under which valuations or in which structures a formula is true.
The pq-system has the intended reading as addition, and it has other readings too.
A proof system is sound for a semantics when every theorem is true in every interpretation, and complete for it when every formula true in every interpretation is a theorem.
For first-order logic both hold, by soundness and by Gödel's completeness theorem.
For arithmetic, the incompleteness theorems show that no consistent formal system with decidable axioms derives every sentence that is true in $\mathbb{N}$.
The difference between the formulas of $S$ and our statements about $S$ is the subject of [[object language and metalanguage|D9G8M42GKC]].

### Watch out

A theorem of $S$ is a formula with a derivation, not a true statement.
Truth needs a semantics, and an unsound system can derive false conclusions.
Add the rule "from $q$ and $p \to q$ infer $p$" to a Hilbert system.
Then $p$ follows from the hypotheses $q$ and $p \to q$, although the valuation with $p$ false and $q$ true makes both hypotheses true and $p$ false.
A decidable set of axioms does not make the set of theorems decidable, as first-order logic shows.
"$S$ is complete" says nothing until you say which of the three senses you mean.

### History

Gottlob Frege's *Begriffsschrift* of 1879 gave the first formal system for predicate logic, with explicit axioms and rules of inference.
In the 1920s David Hilbert and his school made formal systems themselves the object of mathematical study.
In 1931 Kurt Gödel proved his incompleteness theorems for formal systems of arithmetic.
In 1936 Alonzo Church and Alan Turing showed that the set of theorems of first-order logic is undecidable.
Douglas Hofstadter introduced the MIU system in his book *Gödel, Escher, Bach* of 1979.

### Try it

Derive $MIIIIIIII$ (eight letters $I$) in the MIU system.
Apply the doubling rule three times: $MI$, $MII$, $MIIII$, $MIIIIIIII$.

Is $MIIIIII$ (six letters $I$) a theorem?
No, because $6$ is a multiple of $3$, and the invariant rules out every such string.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a formal system as a board game for strings of letters.
The rules say which string you start with and how you may change one string into another.
In the MIU game you start with MI.
One rule lets you write the part after M twice, so MI gives MII, and MII gives MIIII.
Another rule turns three I's in a row into one U, so MIIII gives MUI.
Every string you can reach is a theorem of the game.
The string MU can never be reached, because the number of I's is never a multiple of three.

### L2 human

Here is a derivation of $p \to p$ in the Hilbert system $H$ for propositional logic.
The axioms of $H$ are all instances of three schemas, where $\varphi, \psi, \chi$ stand for any formulas:
$$\begin{aligned} &\text{A1: } \varphi \to (\psi \to \varphi), \\ &\text{A2: } (\varphi \to (\psi \to \chi)) \to ((\varphi \to \psi) \to (\varphi \to \chi)), \\ &\text{A3: } (\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi) \to ((\neg \psi \to \varphi) \to \psi). \end{aligned}$$
The only rule is modus ponens: from $\varphi$ and $\varphi \to \psi$ infer $\psi$.

1. $(p \to ((p \to p) \to p)) \to ((p \to (p \to p)) \to (p \to p))$ is A2 with $\varphi = p$, $\psi = p \to p$, $\chi = p$.
2. $p \to ((p \to p) \to p)$ is A1 with $\varphi = p$ and $\psi = p \to p$.
3. $(p \to (p \to p)) \to (p \to p)$ follows from lines 2 and 1 by modus ponens.
4. $p \to (p \to p)$ is A1 with $\varphi = p$ and $\psi = p$.
5. $p \to p$ follows from lines 4 and 3 by modus ponens.

You check each line by matching shapes, with no idea of truth.
This shows the two typical jobs: we find derivations to show that formulas are theorems, and we find invariants to show that they are not.
Watch out for one confusion: "$p \to p$ is a theorem of $H$" is a statement about $H$, made in the metalanguage, and only $p \to p$ is a formula of $H$.

### L3 human

The theorems of a formal system form a computably enumerable set of strings, and the converse holds as well.
Given a computably enumerable set $X$, add a new symbol $\#$, take as axioms the strings $w \#^n$ such that a fixed enumeration of $X$ lists $w$ at step $n$, and add the rule "from $w \#^n$ infer $w$".
The axioms form a decidable set, and the theorems without $\#$ are exactly the members of $X$.
So formal systems and computably enumerable sets are two views of one notion, as Post's canonical systems and [[rewriting systems|Y83TQF6V6A]] also show.

Natural deduction and sequent calculi are formal systems whose derivations are trees and whose rules act on sequents; [[proof systems for first-order logic|TTCRX3NFNY]] compares them.
The relation $\Gamma \vdash_S \varphi$ of every formal system is a finitary consequence relation, and the study of [[abstract logical systems|RQXV7PM2WT]] starts from this fact.
The three senses of "complete" lead to three main results of the area: Gödel's completeness theorem for proof systems, the incompleteness theorems for theories, and the Post completeness of classical propositional logic.
