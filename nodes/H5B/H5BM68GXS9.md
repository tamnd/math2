## Summary

The teaching of the foundations of mathematics is the part of mathematics education that studies how learners meet logic, sets, mathematical language, the [[axiomatic method|GD2JBXJV5Z]] and [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]].
The Mathematics Subject Classification collects this part in the class 97E, and the code 97E10 is for comprehensive works on the whole field.
A central question is the place of logic and sets in a curriculum: as a separate topic, as a language for other topics, or only through use.
The field matters because all later mathematics is written in this language, and the choice of a curriculum shapes how students learn to read and write proofs.

## Motivation

School mathematics is mostly calculation, and university mathematics is mostly definition, theorem and proof.
Many students meet the change in the first or second year of university, and many find it hard.
Robert Moore (1994) observed students in a first proof course and listed their difficulties: they did not know the definitions, did not know how to begin, and could not use the notation.
These are difficulties with the foundations, not with any special content.
So the teaching of foundations asks three questions.
Which parts of logic and set language must a student know before proof, and which parts are learned by proving?
At which age, and in which course, should each part come?
Should a teacher treat these parts as objects of study or only as tools?

## Remarks

### Examples

1. A [[transition-to-proof course|HQDCSJG379]] teaches logic, sets, proof methods and induction explicitly, in one course of the first or second university year. It treats the foundations as a separate topic.
2. The New Math of the 1960s put sets and Venn diagrams into primary school, as the page [[teaching sets and relations|12ZNG0NRY7]] describes. It treated sets as the first language of all school mathematics.
3. School geometry has long been the place where students meet axioms and proofs, as the page [[proof in school curricula|7ZSHDS63QY]] describes. It teaches the axiomatic method inside one content area.
4. The NCTM "Principles and Standards for School Mathematics" (2000) made reasoning and proof a process standard for all grades. It treats the foundations as a strand of each topic, not as a separate topic.
5. The 19th study of the International Commission on Mathematical Instruction (ICMI), edited by Gila Hanna and Michael de Villiers (2012), is a comprehensive work. It collects research on proof and proving from primary school to university.

### Non-examples

1. A research course on [[mathematical logic|DYJ0KGTPMR]], with the completeness theorem and the incompleteness theorems, is logic itself. Its teaching belongs here only when the subject of study is how students learn it.
2. A general theory of learning, for example constructivism, applies to all of mathematics. It belongs to the [[general theories of learning|RQQYQVZBYT]] in mathematics education.
3. The teaching of a content field, such as [[calculus|MFGBMK523E]], belongs to the topic of that field. The limit concept is a foundation of calculus, but its teaching is in calculus education.

### Research findings

1. Students who start a first proof course often lack the tools of the foundations: definitions, the use of notation, and a way to begin a proof. Moore (1994) documented these difficulties in a transition course.
2. Susanna Epp (2003) argued that logic should be taught explicitly in connection with proof, and she gave examples of the errors of students with conditionals and quantifiers. This is a position supported by examples, not the result of a controlled comparison.
3. The ICMI Study 19 covers proof at all levels of education, from the early grades to university. So the research treats proof as a school topic, not only as a university topic.

### History

In France and in other countries, the reform movement of the late 1950s and the 1960s brought sets and logic into school curricula.
In the United States, the launch of Sputnik in 1957 led to large reforms of school mathematics, the New Math.
By the end of the 1970s most countries had abandoned the New Math, and later curricula treated sets and logic with less weight.
The NCTM standards of 2000 and the Common Core State Standards of 2010 placed reasoning and argument as practices across all topics.
The ICMI Study 19 on proof and proving held its conference in Taipei in 2009 and published its volume in 2012.

### Common errors

1. A teacher assumes that a student who can calculate can also read a definition and a proof. The two skills differ, and the second one must be taught.
2. A curriculum designer assumes that formal set notation makes mathematics clear to young learners. The experience of the New Math shows that the notation alone does not give understanding.
3. A reader takes a teaching opinion, for example "logic must be taught before proof", as a research result. Many such statements are positions, and the pages of this topic mark them as positions.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The foundations of mathematics are the parts that all other parts use: the words "and", "or", "if" and "for all", sets, definitions and proofs.
In school, students mostly calculate, so they meet these foundations late.
For example, a student can calculate $3 + 5 = 8$ and $7 + 9 = 16$ for years without ever asking why the sum of two odd numbers is always even.
The teaching of foundations studies how and when students learn to ask and answer such questions.
Some curricula teach logic and sets as a separate topic.
Others teach them inside other topics, for example inside geometry.

### L2 human

We compare two ways to teach the meaning of "if $P$, then $Q$" in a first university course.

1. In the explicit way, the course starts with a unit on logic. Students learn the truth table of the conditional and the [[contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]].
2. In the implicit way, the course starts with proofs about integers. Students meet conditionals as statements to prove, for example "if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even".
3. In the explicit way, a student can state that a conditional with a false hypothesis is true. The student may still not see why this matters in a proof.
4. In the implicit way, a student sees why the contrapositive helps: "if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd" is easier to prove. The student may not see the general rule behind it.
5. Most transition courses combine the two ways: a short logic unit, then much practice with proofs that use it.

The typical use of this page is to place a teaching question inside the class 97E and to find the page that treats it.
A trap is to treat the choice between explicit and implicit teaching as settled.
Research shows typical errors in both ways, but it gives no single answer for all students.

### L3 human

The foundations of mathematics as a research field (set theory, model theory, proof theory) and the foundations as a school subject are different things.
The New Math tried to bring the first into the second, following the structural view of mathematics of the Bourbaki group.
Its failure did not show that structure is useless in teaching, but that a formal language without the experience that it organizes does not help learners.
Current research looks at proof as a practice of a classroom community, as the pages on [[reasoning and proof in the classroom|E77B813MT4]] and on [[proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]] describe.
A newer question is the role of [[proof assistants|YM6PRDE88V]], which make the logical rules explicit again, now through software.
The [[mathematics education|9622VKAWMZ]] topic holds the general theories that this research uses.
