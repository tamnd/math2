## Summary

A first-year student who can differentiate any polynomial is asked to prove that if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even, and the student writes nothing at all.
The calculation skills are there, but the student cannot say what "even" means in symbols, does not know whether to start from $n$ or from $n^2$, and has never had to read a conditional statement as something to prove.
The teaching of the foundations of mathematics studies exactly this gap: how learners meet logic, sets, the language of definitions, the [[axiomatic method|GD2JBXJV5Z]] and [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]], and how a curriculum should bring them in.

The Mathematics Subject Classification (MSC 2020) collects this field in class 97E, and the code 97E10 is for comprehensive works on the whole of it.
The central design question is the place of logic and sets in a curriculum: a separate topic of study, a language for other topics, or a skill that is learned only through use.

## Motivation

School mathematics is mostly calculation, and university mathematics is mostly definition, theorem and proof.
Many students meet that change in the first or second year of university, and many of them find it hard.
Robert C. Moore observed students in a university course that introduced proof, and in 1994 he reported their main difficulties in Educational Studies in Mathematics.
They did not know the definitions or could not use them, they did not know how to begin a proof, and they could not handle the notation.
None of these difficulties concerns special content, so all of them are difficulties with the foundations.

Three questions follow for anyone who plans a course.
Which parts of logic and set language must a student know before proving, and which parts are learned by proving?
At which age and in which course should each part come?
Should the teacher make these parts objects of study, or use them only as tools?
The rest of this topic treats these questions one strand at a time.

## Remarks

### Three ways to place the foundations

Curricula answer the design question in three typical ways.
A [[transition-to-proof course|HQDCSJG379]] teaches logic, sets, proof methods and induction explicitly, in one course of the first or second university year.
It treats the foundations as a topic of their own.
The New Math reforms of the 1960s put sets and Venn diagrams into primary school and made sets the first language of school mathematics, as [[teaching sets and relations|12ZNG0NRY7]] describes.
School geometry took a third route: for a long time it was where students met axioms and proofs, inside one content area, as [[proof in school curricula|7ZSHDS63QY]] describes.

The NCTM document *Principles and Standards for School Mathematics* (2000) chose a fourth option.
It made reasoning and proof a process standard for every grade, so the foundations run as a strand through all topics instead of standing alone.

### Comprehensive works

The main comprehensive work on proof is ICMI Study 19, *Proof and Proving in Mathematics Education*, edited by Gila Hanna and Michael de Villiers and published in 2012.
The International Commission on Mathematical Instruction (ICMI) ran the study, and its volume collects research on proof from primary school to university.
Its scope makes a point of its own: research treats proof as a topic for every school level, not only for university.

A shorter and widely read paper is Susanna Epp's "The role of logic in teaching proof" in the American Mathematical Monthly (2003).
Epp argued that logic should be taught explicitly and in connection with proof, and she gave many examples of student errors with conditionals and quantifiers.
Her paper is a reasoned position supported by examples, not the result of a controlled comparison of two courses.

### What belongs elsewhere

A course on [[mathematical logic|DYJ0KGTPMR]] with the completeness and incompleteness theorems is logic itself.
It belongs in 97E only when the question is how students learn it.
A general theory of learning, such as constructivism, applies to all of mathematics, so it belongs to the [[general theories of learning|RQQYQVZBYT]] in mathematics education.
The teaching of a content field, such as [[calculus|MFGBMK523E]], also belongs to that field, even though the limit concept is a foundation of calculus.

### History

After the launch of Sputnik in 1957, the United States funded large reforms of school mathematics, which became known as the New Math.
Similar reforms of the 1960s, often called "modern mathematics", brought sets and logic into school curricula in France and in many other countries.
Strong criticism followed in the 1970s, and later curricula gave sets and formal logic much less weight.
The NCTM standards of 2000 and the Common Core State Standards of 2010 then placed reasoning and argument as practices across all topics.
ICMI Study 19 held its conference in Taipei in 2009 and published its volume in 2012.

### Watch out

A teacher may assume that a student who calculates well can also read a definition and a proof.
The opening example shows the failure: the student knows that $16$ is even but cannot write "$n = 2k$ for some integer $k$".
The two skills differ, and the second must be taught.

A curriculum designer may assume that formal set notation makes mathematics clear to young learners.
The New Math showed that notation without the experience that it organizes does not give understanding.

A reader may take a teaching opinion, such as "logic must come before proof", as a research result.
Many such claims are positions, and the pages of this topic mark them as positions.

### Try it

First, classify a study of how ten-year-olds use the word "or" in a sorting game.
It studies language and logic of young learners, so it fits 97E30 or 97E40, not a content field.

Second, a colleague says that research has shown that logic units must precede proof.
Ask for the study, because Epp's paper argues for explicit logic but is not a controlled comparison.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The foundations of mathematics are the parts that all other parts use: the words "and", "or", "if" and "for all", sets, definitions and proofs.
In school, students mostly calculate, so they meet these foundations late.
A student can add $3 + 5 = 8$ and $7 + 9 = 16$ for years without asking why the sum of two odd numbers is always even.
Research on teaching the foundations asks how and when students learn to ask and answer such questions.
Some courses teach logic and sets as a separate topic, and others teach them inside geometry or algebra.

### L2 human

Take one goal of a first university course: students should understand "if $P$, then $Q$".
In an explicit course, the first unit is logic.
Students learn the truth table of the conditional and the [[contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]], and they can state that a conditional with a false hypothesis is true.
They may still not see why that fact matters when they write a proof.

In an implicit course, students start with statements about integers, such as "if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even".
They find that the contrapositive "if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd" is easier, because they can compute
$$(2k + 1)^2 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1.$$
They see why the contrapositive helps, but they may not see the general rule behind it.

Most transition courses mix the two: a short logic unit, then much practice with proofs that use it.
Watch out: research documents typical errors in both designs, but it does not show that one design is best for all students.

### L3 human

The foundations as a research field (set theory, model theory, proof theory) and the foundations as a school subject are different things.
The New Math tried to bring the first into the second, following the structural view of mathematics of the Bourbaki group.
Its failure did not show that structure is useless in teaching.
It showed that a formal language without the experience that it organizes does not help learners.
Current research looks at proof as a practice of a classroom community, as [[reasoning and proof in the classroom|E77B813MT4]] and [[proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]] describe.
[[Proof assistants|YM6PRDE88V]] raise a newer question, since they make the rules of logic explicit again, now through software.
The general theories that this research uses are in [[mathematics education|9622VKAWMZ]].
