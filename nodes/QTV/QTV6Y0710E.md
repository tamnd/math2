## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
We check that $A$ is positive definite, and then we find its [[positive semidefinite square root|EQ98KW1ZN4]] in two ways.

1. The matrix is symmetric. Its characteristic polynomial is $(5 - t)^2 - 16 = t^2 - 10t + 9 = (t - 9)(t - 1)$, so the eigenvalues are $9$ and $1$. Both are positive, so $A$ is [[positive semidefinite|0880F9EQCP]], and in fact positive definite.
2. Unit eigenvectors are $u_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ for $9$ and $u_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)$ for $1$. Check: $A(1, 1) = (9, 9)$ and $A(1, -1) = (1, -1)$.
3. The projections onto the two eigenlines are
$$P_1 = u_1 u_1^{\mathsf{T}} = \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad P_2 = u_2 u_2^{\mathsf{T}} = \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
and the [[spectral theorem|0DGZBQWZWN]] gives $A = 9 P_1 + 1 P_2$.
4. The square root replaces $9$ by $3$ and keeps $1$:
$$A^{1/2} = 3 P_1 + 1 P_2 = \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 3 + 1 & 3 - 1 \\ 3 - 1 & 3 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
5. Square it back:
$$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 + 1 & 2 + 2 \\ 2 + 2 & 1 + 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = A.$$
6. The matrix $A^{1/2}$ is symmetric with the eigenvalues $3$ and $1$, so it is positive semidefinite, as the theorem requires.
7. The polynomial recipe gives the same matrix. The interpolation polynomial with $p(9) = 3$ and $p(1) = 1$ is
$$p(t) = 3 \cdot \frac{t - 1}{9 - 1} + 1 \cdot \frac{t - 9}{1 - 9} = \frac{t + 3}{4},$$
and $p(A) = \tfrac14 (A + 3I) = \tfrac14 \begin{pmatrix} 8 & 4 \\ 4 & 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

## Remarks

The four sign choices $\pm 3 P_1 \pm 1 P_2$ are all symmetric, and all of them square to $A$, because $P_1 P_2 = 0$ and $P_i^2 = P_i$.
Besides $\pm A^{1/2}$, they give
$$3 P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 3 - 1 & 3 + 1 \\ 3 + 1 & 3 - 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
and its negative.
Check: $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 4 & 2 + 2 \\ 2 + 2 & 4 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = A$.
Only $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is positive semidefinite.
The root $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalues $3$ and $-1$, and it is [[the matrix with positive entries|N9TX4J2W4R]] that fails to be positive definite.

If we change $A$ to $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 5 \\ 5 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, the eigenvalues become $10$ and $0$, and the root becomes $\sqrt{10} \, P_1 = \tfrac{\sqrt{10}}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
That root is semidefinite but not definite, and it has the same null space $\R(1, -1)$ as the new matrix.

The same matrix $A$ equals $M^{\mathsf{T}} M$ for $M = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, since the columns of $M$ have squared lengths $5$ and $5$ and dot product $4$.
So $A^{1/2}$ is the factor $P$ in [[the polar decomposition of M|3PBTVVRPD1]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

We want a matrix $B$ with $B^2 = A$ whose stretching factors are not negative.
The matrix $A$ stretches the direction $(1, 1)$ by $9$ and the direction $(1, -1)$ by $1$.
So $B$ must stretch $(1, 1)$ by $3$ and $(1, -1)$ by $1$.
That gives $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, and multiplying $B$ by itself gives back $A$.

### L2 human

The eigenvector recipe and the polynomial recipe give the same matrix, as the theorem promises.
The polynomial $p(t) = (t + 3)/4$ has degree $1$ because $A$ has only two distinct eigenvalues.
For a matrix with $m$ distinct eigenvalues, the polynomial has degree at most $m - 1$.
Watch out: the entrywise square root $\begin{pmatrix} \sqrt5 & 2 \\ 2 & \sqrt5 \end{pmatrix}$ squares to $\begin{pmatrix} 9 & 4\sqrt5 \\ 4\sqrt5 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$, not to $A$.
