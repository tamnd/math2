## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $A$ is symmetric. Its characteristic polynomial is $(5 - t)^2 - 16 = (t - 9)(t - 1)$, so the eigenvalues are $9$ and $1$. Both are positive, so $A$ is positive definite.
2. Unit eigenvectors are $u_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ for $9$ and $u_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)$ for $1$. Indeed $A(1, 1) = (9, 9)$ and $A(1, -1) = (1, -1)$.
3. The projections onto the two eigenlines are $P_1 = u_1 u_1^T = \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $P_2 = u_2 u_2^T = \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Then $A = 9 P_1 + 1 P_2$.
4. The square root replaces $9$ by $3$ and $1$ by $1$:
$$A^{1/2} = 3 P_1 + 1 P_2 = \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 3 + 1 & 3 - 1 \\ 3 - 1 & 3 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
5. Square back: $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 + 1 & 2 + 2 \\ 2 + 2 & 1 + 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = A$.
6. $A^{1/2}$ is symmetric with eigenvalues $3$ and $1$, so it is positive semidefinite, and in fact positive definite.
7. The polynomial recipe: $p(t) = 3 \cdot \frac{t - 1}{9 - 1} + 1 \cdot \frac{t - 9}{1 - 9} = \frac{t + 3}{4}$ has $p(9) = 3$ and $p(1) = 1$. Then $p(A) = \tfrac14 (A + 3I) = \tfrac14 \begin{pmatrix} 8 & 4 \\ 4 & 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, the same matrix.

## Remarks

- The symmetric matrices $\pm 3 P_1 \pm 1 P_2$ all square to $A$. Besides $\pm A^{1/2}$, they are $\pm \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, since $3P_1 - P_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Check: $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
- Only $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is positive semidefinite. The root $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalue $-1$; it is [[the matrix with positive entries|N9TX4J2W4R]] that is not positive definite.
- The same matrix $A$ is $M^T M$ for $M = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $A^{1/2}$ is the factor $P$ of [[its polar decomposition|3PBTVVRPD1]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

We want a matrix $B$ with $B^2 = A$ and with nonnegative eigenvalues.
$A$ stretches the direction $(1, 1)$ by $9$ and the direction $(1, -1)$ by $1$.
So $B$ must stretch $(1, 1)$ by $3$ and $(1, -1)$ by $1$.
That gives $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, and $B^2 = A$.

### L2 human

The eigenvector recipe and the polynomial recipe give the same matrix, as the theorem says.
The polynomial $p(t) = (t + 3)/4$ is linear here, because $A$ has only two distinct eigenvalues.
A trap: the entrywise square root $\begin{pmatrix} \sqrt5 & 2 \\ 2 & \sqrt5 \end{pmatrix}$ squares to $\begin{pmatrix} 9 & 4\sqrt5 \\ 4\sqrt5 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$, not to $A$.
