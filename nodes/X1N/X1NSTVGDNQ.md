## Summary

A finished proof shows the steps in order, but it does not show how anyone found them, and this part of [[reasoning and proof|SGSVYFDSYK]] is about that search.
It starts with a student who wants the sum $\frac{1}{1 \cdot 2} + \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3} + \dots + \frac{1}{n(n + 1)}$, tries the first few cases, sees $\frac{1}{2}$, $\frac{2}{3}$ and $\frac{3}{4}$, and guesses $\frac{n}{n + 1}$ before proving it.
That move, "try special cases", is a heuristic: a piece of advice that often leads to an idea and never guarantees one, since heuristics are advice, not algorithms.
George Pólya gave such advice names and an order in his four steps, and the pages that follow treat his main heuristics one at a time, each with a real problem that it solves and a case where it misleads.
Imre Lakatos then showed how counterexamples refine a conjecture and its concepts, in his story of Euler's formula for polyhedra.
The last pages turn to the mind of the solver: intuition as a guide and a risk, the reports of Poincaré and Hadamard on sudden ideas, and the posing of new problems once a problem is solved.
The training of problem solving for contests is a subject of its own, in [[problem solving and competitions|2HNEG88WT8]].

## Remarks

### Reading order

1. [[Heuristics in mathematics|6KCEWEP4WA]]: learn what a heuristic is and how it differs from an algorithm and from a proof.
2. [[Pólya's four steps|DYD57G2Y5Z]]: get a frame for any problem, from understanding it to looking back at the solution.
3. [[Analogy|7KDT0R1HF2]]: learn to borrow a method from a similar problem, and to test whether the similarity is real.
4. [[Specialization|FTBJX4G51S]]: use small cases, extreme cases and special values to find a pattern or to refute a claim.
5. [[Generalization as a heuristic|8NJ0VN9Z97]]: see how a more general problem, often with a new parameter, can be easier to solve.
6. [[Inventor's paradox|FVRKDXBJF6]]: understand why a stronger statement can be easier to prove than a weaker one.
7. [[Working backwards|3QTB9S1THS]]: start from the goal, as in the analysis and synthesis of Pappus.
8. [[Auxiliary problem|DYSJB93V11]]: find an easier or equivalent problem that opens the way to the original one.
9. [[Auxiliary elements|8TKKRAXKFN]]: add a line, a variable or a function that makes the problem accessible.
10. [[Decomposing and recombining|KEZPDNM2NH]]: split a problem into parts and put the partial results together.
11. [[Variation of the problem|KT5SASGTQ5]]: change the data, the condition or the unknown until a solvable problem appears.
12. [[Draw a figure and introduce notation|6CVYB413ER]]: choose a figure, names and coordinates that make the structure visible.
13. [[Proofs and refutations|79P9CTRN0V]]: follow Lakatos as counterexamples to Euler's formula sharpen both the theorem and its concepts.
14. [[Mathematical intuition|4BT08RE4CR]]: learn when intuition guides a search well and when it fails.
15. [[Psychology of mathematical invention|XMGGTNSTYC]]: read what Poincaré, Wallas and Hadamard said about preparation, incubation, illumination and verification.
16. [[Problem posing|Y896GWYF8A]]: make new problems from a solved one with the question "what if not?".
