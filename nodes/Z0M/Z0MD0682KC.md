## Example

The starting problem asks how many squares of all sizes an $8 \times 8$ chessboard holds, and [[counting the squares on a chessboard by the four steps|H817ES7NHH]] solves it.
A square of side $k$ fits in $(9 - k)^2$ positions, so the count is $8^2 + 7^2 + \dots + 1^2 = 204$.
We now pose new problems from it, as in [[problem posing|Y896GWYF8A]].

The attributes of the problem are that the board is $8 \times 8$, that the board is square, that the shapes counted are squares, and that the shapes follow the grid lines.
"What if the shapes are not squares?" poses a new problem: how many rectangles of all sizes does the board hold?

A first attempt copies the old method and counts by size.
A rectangle of width $a$ and height $b$ fits in $(9 - a)(9 - b)$ positions, and there are $64$ pairs $(a, b)$ to add.
The work is long, and it gives a number but no view of why the number is what it is.

The helping move is to look at what fixes a rectangle.
A rectangle on the board is fixed by its left and right sides and by its top and bottom sides.
The board has $9$ vertical grid lines and $9$ horizontal grid lines, so a rectangle is a choice of $2$ of the $9$ vertical lines and $2$ of the $9$ horizontal lines.
With the [[binomial coefficient|0GBZM0MT0F]] $\binom{9}{2} = 36$, the number of rectangles is
$$\binom{9}{2} \cdot \binom{9}{2} = 36 \cdot 36 = 1296.$$
The first attempt agrees, since the double sum splits as a product:
$$\sum_{a=1}^{8} \sum_{b=1}^{8} (9 - a)(9 - b) = \Big( \sum_{a=1}^{8} (9 - a) \Big)^2 = 36^2 = 1296.$$
Looking back, the new method is shorter, and it gives $\binom{n + 1}{2}^2$ rectangles on an $n \times n$ board at once.
The variant was easy to pose, it needed a new idea, and its answer posed further questions.

## Remarks

The two counts give a surprise: $1296 = 36^2 = 1^3 + 2^3 + \dots + 8^3$.
In general $\binom{n + 1}{2}^2 = \left( \frac{n(n + 1)}{2} \right)^2$, the [[squared triangular number|23WBA59VWP]], equals $1^3 + 2^3 + \dots + n^3$.
So counting rectangles in two ways poses a further problem: find a way to split the rectangles into groups of sizes $1^3, 2^3, \dots, n^3$.

The other attributes give other variants.
An $m \times n$ board holds $\binom{m + 1}{2} \binom{n + 1}{2}$ rectangles, and a cube of side $n$ holds $\binom{n + 1}{2}^3$ boxes.
The fraction of rectangles on the chessboard that are squares is $\frac{204}{1296} = \frac{17}{108}$, about $16\%$.
Many related counting questions are collected under [[chessboard problems|WPGRNSZ1ZN]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A chessboard has $8$ rows and $8$ columns of small squares.
If we also count the bigger squares made of several small ones, there are $204$ squares in all.
Now change the question and ask how many rectangles there are, of any shape.
Each rectangle is fixed by two of the $9$ lines going across and two of the $9$ lines going down.
There are $36$ ways to choose two lines from nine, so there are $36 \times 36 = 1296$ rectangles.
Changing "square" to "rectangle" gave a new problem and a new way of counting.

### L2 human

We check the formula $\binom{n + 1}{2}^2$ on a $3 \times 3$ board, counting by size.
A $1 \times 1$ rectangle fits in $9$ positions, a $1 \times 2$ or a $2 \times 1$ in $6$ each, and a $1 \times 3$ or a $3 \times 1$ in $3$ each.
A $2 \times 2$ fits in $4$ positions, a $2 \times 3$ or a $3 \times 2$ in $2$ each, and a $3 \times 3$ in $1$.
The total is $9 + 6 + 6 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 36$.
The formula gives $\binom{4}{2}^2 = 6^2 = 36$, which is also $1^3 + 2^3 + 3^3$.
The squares among them number $9 + 4 + 1 = 14$.
Pose the variant, solve it by a new method, and check the new method against the old one on a small case.
Watch out: the variant "what if the board is not $8 \times 8$?" changes only a number and is routine, while the change from squares to rectangles is the one that needs a new idea.
