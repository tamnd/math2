## Statement

Let $X$ be a finite set of outcomes and let $\mathcal{L}(X)$ be the set of lotteries on $X$, the probability vectors $(L(x))_{x \in X}$.
For $L, M \in \mathcal{L}(X)$ and $p \in [0, 1]$, the mixture $pL + (1 - p)M$ is the lottery $x \mapsto pL(x) + (1 - p)M(x)$.
Let $\succsim$ be a binary relation on $\mathcal{L}(X)$.
Consider the following axioms.

1. Completeness and transitivity: $\succsim$ is a [[preference relation|JGP0Q56FRY]].
2. Continuity: if $L \succsim M \succsim N$, then there is $p \in [0, 1]$ with $pL + (1 - p)N \sim M$.
3. Independence: for all $L, M, N \in \mathcal{L}(X)$ and $p \in (0, 1]$, $L \succsim M$ if and only if $pL + (1 - p)N \succsim pM + (1 - p)N$.

Then $\succsim$ satisfies axioms 1 to 3 if and only if there is a function $u \colon X \to \R$ such that
$$L \succsim M \iff \sum_{x \in X} L(x) u(x) \ge \sum_{x \in X} M(x) u(x) \quad \text{for all } L, M \in \mathcal{L}(X).$$
Assume that such a $u$ exists and that some two lotteries are not indifferent.
Then the functions $v$ with the same property are exactly the functions $v = a u + b$ with $a > 0$ and $b \in \R$.

## Remarks

For the main direction, pick a best outcome $\bar{x}$ and a worst outcome $\underline{x}$ among the sure lotteries; by independence they are also best and worst among all lotteries.
By continuity each outcome $x$ is indifferent to a mixture $u(x)\,\bar{x} + (1 - u(x))\,\underline{x}$, and independence makes this $u(x)$ unique.
Repeated use of independence shows that every lottery $L$ is indifferent to the mixture with weight $\sum_x L(x) u(x)$ on $\bar{x}$, and mixtures of $\bar{x}$ and $\underline{x}$ are ranked by that weight.
The converse direction is a direct check, since the expected utility is affine under mixtures.
The Allais paradox shows that many people's choices violate the independence axiom.
Without continuity, lexicographic preferences such as "first maximize the chance of outcome $a$, then minimize the chance of $c$" satisfy the other axioms but have no representation.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The theorem says when a person's choices among gambles can be explained by an [[expected utility|8C2N37XPJ8]].
It is enough that the choices are consistent and that no gamble is infinitely better than another.
Also, mixing two options with the same third option must not change their order.
Then there is a score for each outcome, and the person always picks the gamble with the higher average score.

### L2 human

The theorem is the foundation of expected utility theory in economics, finance and game theory, where mixed strategies are evaluated by expected payoffs.
The utility it produces is cardinal: it is unique up to a positive affine change, so differences of utilities can be compared, unlike for an ordinal [[utility function|GZZS4MTDG9]].
Savage's theory of subjective expected utility extends it to the case where the probabilities are not given.
