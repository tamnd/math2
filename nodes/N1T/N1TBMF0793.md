## Proof

We write each projection in an orthonormal eigenbasis and then check every identity on the basis vectors.

1. The eigenbasis. Let $e_1, \dots, e_n$ be an orthonormal basis with $T e_k = \mu_k e_k$, which exists by hypothesis. For normal $T$ over $\mathbb{C}$ it exists by the [[complex spectral theorem|04CW1P44VD]], and for self-adjoint $T$ over $\mathbb{R}$ by the [[real spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]].
2. Sorting the basis. For $i = 1, \dots, m$, let $K_i$ be the set of indices $k$ with $\mu_k = \lambda_i$. Each index lies in exactly one $K_i$, because each $\mu_k$ is one of the distinct eigenvalues.
3. The action of $T - \lambda_i I$. By the [[expansion in an orthonormal basis|09DZ8CD1AK]], every $v \in V$ is $v = \sum_k \ip{v}{e_k} e_k$, so
$$(T - \lambda_i I) v = \sum_k (\mu_k - \lambda_i) \ip{v}{e_k} e_k.$$
4. A basis of $E_i$. Let $v$ lie in the [[eigenspace|0AYEPBQMAW]] $E_i$. Then the sum in step 3 is $0$, and the $e_k$ are [[linearly independent|0JWM6GJNQW]], so $\ip{v}{e_k} = 0$ whenever $k \notin K_i$. Each $e_k$ with $k \in K_i$ lies in $E_i$, so the vectors $e_k$ with $k \in K_i$ form an orthonormal basis of $E_i$.
5. The projection. Define $Q_i v = \sum_{k \in K_i} \ip{v}{e_k} e_k$. Then $Q_i v \in E_i$, and $v - Q_i v = \sum_{k \notin K_i} \ip{v}{e_k} e_k$ is orthogonal to every $e_k$ with $k \in K_i$, so $v - Q_i v \in E_i^\perp$.
6. The splitting $v = w + w'$ with $w \in E_i$ and $w' \in E_i^\perp$ is unique, by the [[orthogonal decomposition|0SP7P0G7KQ]]. So $Q_i v$ is the [[orthogonal projection|0Q8HZTJC7G]] $P_i v$. In particular $P_i e_k = e_k$ for $k \in K_i$, and $P_i e_k = 0$ otherwise.
7. Checking on a basis. Two linear maps that agree on a basis are equal, so from now on we check each identity on a basis vector $e_k$, with $k \in K_l$.
8. Part 1. The vector $P_i P_j e_k$ is $e_k$ when $i = j = l$, and $0$ otherwise. So $P_i^2 = P_i$, and $P_i P_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$. Also $\sum_i P_i e_k = P_l e_k = e_k$, so $\sum_i P_i = I$.
9. Each $P_i$ is self-adjoint. For $v, w \in V$, step 5 gives
$$\ip{P_i v}{w} = \sum_{k \in K_i} \ip{v}{e_k} \ip{e_k}{w}, \qquad \ip{v}{P_i w} = \sum_{k \in K_i} \overline{\ip{w}{e_k}} \ip{v}{e_k},$$
and the two sums agree, because $\overline{\ip{w}{e_k}} = \ip{e_k}{w}$.
10. Part 2. We have $\sum_i \lambda_i P_i e_k = \lambda_l e_k = \mu_k e_k = T e_k$, so $T = \sum_i \lambda_i P_i$.
11. Part 3. For $r \geq 0$, $T^r e_k = \mu_k^r e_k = \lambda_l^r e_k$, and $\sum_i \lambda_i^r P_i e_k = \lambda_l^r e_k$, so $T^r = \sum_i \lambda_i^r P_i$. A polynomial $p$ is a linear combination of powers, so the same linear combination gives $p(T) = \sum_i p(\lambda_i) P_i$.
12. Part 4. Let $p_i(t) = \prod_{j \neq i} (t - \lambda_j) / (\lambda_i - \lambda_j)$, the [[Lagrange interpolation|QGQTYT6YZR]] polynomial. The denominators are not $0$ because the $\lambda_j$ are distinct, and $p_i(\lambda_i) = 1$ while $p_i(\lambda_l) = 0$ for $l \neq i$. By part 3, $p_i(T) = \sum_l p_i(\lambda_l) P_l = P_i$.
13. Part 5. By the [[algebraic properties of the adjoint|TPP76FP659]] and step 9, $T^* = \sum_i \overline{\lambda_i} P_i^* = \sum_i \overline{\lambda_i} P_i$. The polynomial $q = \sum_i \overline{\lambda_i} p_i$ then gives $q(T) = \sum_l q(\lambda_l) P_l = \sum_l \overline{\lambda_l} P_l = T^*$, by part 3 and the values of $p_i$ in step 12.

## Idea

In an orthonormal eigenbasis, the projection $P_i$ keeps the basis vectors that belong to $\lambda_i$ and sends the others to $0$.
So in that basis every operator in the proof is a diagonal matrix, and $P_i$ is diagonal with entries $0$ and $1$.
Every identity of the statement becomes an identity between diagonal entries, which we read off one basis vector at a time.
Lagrange interpolation then picks out each projection, and so also $T^*$, as a polynomial in $T$.

## Variants

Part 4 can be proved without an orthonormal eigenbasis.
If $T$ is diagonalizable, then $p_i(T)$ is the projection onto $E_i$ along the sum of the other eigenspaces, by the same interpolation argument.
When the eigenspaces are pairwise orthogonal, as [[orthogonality of eigenspaces|0JHGAV9H25]] gives for normal operators, this projection is the orthogonal one.

In matrix form, write $A = U D U^*$ with the orthonormal eigenvector columns $u_1, \dots, u_n$ of $U$.
Then $P_i = \sum_{k \in K_i} u_k u_k^*$, and the identities of part 1 follow from $u_j^* u_k = 0$ for $j \neq k$ and $u_k^* u_k = 1$.
