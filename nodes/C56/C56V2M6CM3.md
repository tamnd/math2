## Statement

Let $\beta \in (0, 1)$ and let $K \ge 1$.
For each $i = 1, \dots, K$, let arm $i$ be a [[Markov chain|0G48PYM96Q]] with countable state space $E_i$, transition matrix $P_i$ and bounded reward function $r_i \colon E_i \to \R$.
At each time $t = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ the controller sees the states of all arms and plays one arm $i$.
It receives $\beta^t r_i(x_i)$, where $x_i$ is the current state of arm $i$.
Then arm $i$ moves according to $P_i$, and all other arms stay frozen.
The arms move independently of each other.
For $x \in E_i$, the *Gittins index* is
$$\nu_i(x) = \sup_{\tau \ge 1} \frac{\mathbb{E}_x\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\tau - 1} \beta^t r_i(Y_t)\big]}{\mathbb{E}_x\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\tau - 1} \beta^t\big]}.$$
Here $(Y_t)$ is the chain of arm $i$ started at $x$ and the supremum runs over stopping times $\tau \ge 1$ of $(Y_t)$.
Let $R_t$ be the reward at time $t$.
Then the policy that always plays an arm whose current state has the largest Gittins index maximizes $\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t \ge 0} \beta^t R_t\big]$ among all policies.

## Remarks

The index $\nu_i(x)$ depends on arm $i$ alone, so a problem in the joint state space of all arms splits into $K$ one-arm problems.
One standard proof, due to Weber, imagines a fair charge for each arm.
The index $\nu_i(x)$ is the largest constant charge per play at which a gambler who may stop at any time still breaks even on arm $i$.
The charge is lowered whenever the gambler would want to stop.
Then no policy can earn more than the expected discounted sum of the charges, and the index policy earns exactly that.
Example: if arm $i$ pays a fixed amount $c_i$ each time, with one state, then $\nu_i = c_i$ and the theorem says to play the arm with the largest $c_i$ forever.
The hypotheses matter: if arms that are not played also change state (restless bandits), or if rewards are not geometrically discounted, the index policy is in general not optimal.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Each slot machine gets a single number, its index, that measures how attractive it is right now, taking into account what one may still learn from it.
The theorem says that the best plan is simply to always play the machine with the highest current index.
This turns a huge planning problem over all machines together into separate small problems, one per machine.

### L2 human

The theorem was published by Gittins and Jones in 1974 and is a central result on the [[multi-armed bandit|8J77QFAEQT]] in its Bayesian, discounted form.
As a [[Markov decision process|WCNHWTJXYD]], the whole bandit has the product of the $E_i$ as state space, and direct dynamic programming over it is costly.
The index reduces the work to one optimal stopping problem per arm.
In the Bayesian bandit, the state of an arm is the posterior law of its unknown parameter, which evolves as a Markov chain when the arm is played.
The frequentist regret setting is different and is handled by methods such as upper confidence bounds.
