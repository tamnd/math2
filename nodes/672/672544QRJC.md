## Example

We want to show that $a^4 + b^4 = c^4$ has no solution in positive integers.
The [[inventor's paradox|FVRKDXBJF6]] suggests trying the more ambitious problem, and we show that here it is a bad guide.

1. We name the ambitious problem.
   [[Fermat's Last Theorem|0Y5X3SDBCZ]] states that $a^n + b^n = c^n$ has no solution in positive integers for any $n \ge 3$, and the given problem is its case $n = 4$.
2. We weigh its cost.
   Fermat stated the theorem around 1637, and Andrew Wiles proved it in 1994, with one step completed jointly with Richard Taylor.
   The proof uses elliptic curves and modular forms, and it is long and difficult.
3. We weigh the cost of the special problem.
   The case $n = 4$ has a short proof by [[infinite descent|0EFYCFHYFG]], which goes back to Fermat; see [[Fermat's Last Theorem for n = 4|0E741HSMJ1]].
4. We recall how that proof works.
   It shows that a solution of $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$ in positive integers gives a smaller one, through the classification of Pythagorean triples.
   Positive integers cannot decrease forever, so there is no solution.
5. We deduce the given problem.
   A solution of $a^4 + b^4 = c^4$ would give the solution $x = a$, $y = b$, $z = c^2$ of $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$, which step 4 rules out.

So the general problem was not the easier one.
A solver who had read the paradox as "prove it for every $n$" would have been stuck for more than three centuries.

## Remarks

The general problem was harder because the method of step 4 depends on the exponent $4$.
It uses that $a^4$ is a square, and no single descent works for every exponent.
This is the standard warning: the paradox is a suggestion, not a law.

The example still contains the paradox in a smaller form.
Step 4 proves the stronger statement about $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$, because the descent produces equations of that form and not of the form $a^4 + b^4 = c^4$.
So the right strengthening was a small one, chosen to make the descent close, and not the most ambitious one.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Fermat claimed that $a^n + b^n = c^n$ has no solutions in whole numbers for any power $n$ above $2$.
For the power $4$ there is a short proof: from one solution you could always build a smaller one, which is impossible.
For all powers at once, the proof took more than $350$ years and needed whole new areas of mathematics.
So here the small question was far easier than the big one.

### L2 human

We check how far the case $n = 4$ reaches.
A solution of $a^8 + b^8 = c^8$ would give
$$(a^2)^4 + (b^2)^4 = (c^2)^4,$$
a solution for $n = 4$, so $n = 8$ is settled.
In the same way, a solution for $n = 12$ gives one for $n = 4$ with $a^3$, $b^3$ and $c^3$, and every multiple of $4$ is settled by the case $n = 4$.

The lesson is typical: before you choose the general problem, ask whether the method for the special case has any chance of working in general.
Watch out for the direction of the reduction.
It goes from an exponent to its divisors, so the case $n = 4$ settles the multiples of $4$, but it says nothing about $n = 3$ or $n = 5$.
