## Example

Let $X$ be the set of triangles in the plane, and let $\sim$ be the relation "is [[congruent|093EDENVA1]] to".
For a triangle $T$, let $f(T)$ be the multiset of its three side lengths.
We show that $f$ is a complete invariant, and we describe all its values.

1. The relation $\sim$ is an [[equivalence relation|0G8VEZ3WTK]]. Each triangle is congruent to itself, the inverse of a rigid motion is a rigid motion, and the composite of two rigid motions is a rigid motion.
2. The map $f$ is an invariant. A rigid motion keeps all distances, so congruent triangles have the same side lengths.
3. The map $f$ is complete. If two triangles have the same three side lengths, they are congruent, by [[side-side-side congruence|0BRBYRK6PA]].
4. Each value $\{a, b, c\}$ of $f$ satisfies $a < b + c$, $b < a + c$ and $c < a + b$, by the triangle inequality for the plane. Equality would put the three vertices on one line.
5. Conversely, each multiset $\{a, b, c\}$ of positive numbers with these three strict inequalities is a value of $f$. Put $A = (0, 0)$ and $B = (c, 0)$; the circles of radius $b$ around $A$ and of radius $a$ around $B$ meet at a point $C$ off the line $AB$.
6. So the congruence classes of triangles correspond exactly to the multisets $\{a, b, c\}$ of positive numbers in which each number is less than the sum of the other two.

We test step 5 on the multiset $\{3, 4, 5\}$.

7. The inequalities hold: $3 < 4 + 5$, $4 < 3 + 5$ and $5 < 3 + 4$.
8. With $c = 5$, $b = 4$, $a = 3$, the point $C = (x, y)$ satisfies $x^2 + y^2 = 16$ and $(x - 5)^2 + y^2 = 9$.
9. Subtracting gives $10x - 25 = 7$, so $x = 3.2$, and then $y^2 = 16 - 10.24 = 5.76$, so $y = 2.4$.
10. The triangle with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(5, 0)$ and $(3.2, 2.4)$ has the side lengths $5$, $4$ and $3$.

## Remarks

The example has all three parts of a classification: an equivalence relation, a complete invariant, and the exact set of its values.
The multiset $\{1, 2, 4\}$ is not a value, because $4 > 1 + 2$, so no triangle has these sides.

If we change the equivalence relation to similarity, the side lengths are no longer an invariant, because a similarity can double them.
The new complete invariant is the multiset of the three angles, with the condition that they are positive and add to $180^\circ$.
Another choice is the multiset of side lengths up to a common positive factor.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Give three sticks of lengths $3$, $4$ and $5$ to two people, and ask each of them to make a triangle.
The two triangles will have the same shape and size, even if one person turns or flips the triangle.
So the three lengths tell everything about a triangle up to moving it.
Not every three lengths work: with sticks $1$, $2$ and $4$, the two short sticks cannot reach across the long one.

### L2 human

We decide which of three triangles are congruent: $T_1$ with sides $5, 6, 7$, $T_2$ with sides $7, 5, 6$, and $T_3$ with sides $5, 6, 8$.

1. Sort the side lengths: $f(T_1) = \{5, 6, 7\}$, $f(T_2) = \{5, 6, 7\}$ and $f(T_3) = \{5, 6, 8\}$.
2. The invariants of $T_1$ and $T_2$ agree, and the invariant is complete, so $T_1 \sim T_2$.
3. The invariants of $T_1$ and $T_3$ differ, so $T_1 \not\sim T_3$.

The typical use is a quick test of congruence.
A trap is to compare the side lengths as ordered lists: $(5, 6, 7)$ and $(7, 5, 6)$ differ as lists, but the triangles are congruent.
The invariant is the multiset, not the list.
