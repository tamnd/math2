## Example

Let $X$ be the set of triangles in the plane, and let $\sim$ be the relation "is [[congruent|093EDENVA1]] to", so that two triangles are equivalent when a rigid motion carries one onto the other.
For a triangle $T$, let $f(T)$ be the multiset of its three side lengths.
We show that $f$ is a complete invariant, and we find all its values.

1. The relation $\sim$ is an [[equivalence relation|0G8VEZ3WTK]].
   Every triangle is congruent to itself, the inverse of a rigid motion is a rigid motion, and the composite of two rigid motions is a rigid motion.
2. The map $f$ is an invariant, because a rigid motion keeps all distances, so congruent triangles have the same side lengths.
3. The map $f$ is complete, because two triangles with the same three side lengths are congruent, by [[side-side-side congruence|0BRBYRK6PA]].
4. Every value $\{a, b, c\}$ of $f$ satisfies $a < b + c$, $b < a + c$ and $c < a + b$, by the [[triangle inequality|03HF5PMY5T]].
   The inequalities are strict, because equality would put the three vertices on one line.
5. Conversely, every multiset $\{a, b, c\}$ of positive numbers with these three strict inequalities is a value of $f$.
   Put $A = (0, 0)$ and $B = (c, 0)$; then the circle of radius $b$ around $A$ and the circle of radius $a$ around $B$ meet at a point $C$ off the line $AB$.
6. So the congruence classes of triangles correspond exactly to the multisets $\{a, b, c\}$ of positive numbers in which each number is less than the sum of the other two.

We carry out step 5 for the multiset $\{3, 4, 5\}$.
The inequalities hold, since $3 < 9$, $4 < 8$ and $5 < 7$.
Take $c = 5$, $b = 4$ and $a = 3$, so the point $C = (x, y)$ must satisfy
$$x^2 + y^2 = 16, \qquad (x - 5)^2 + y^2 = 9.$$
Subtracting the second equation from the first gives $10x - 25 = 7$, so $x = 3.2$, and then
$$y^2 = 16 - 3.2^2 = 16 - 10.24 = 5.76, \qquad y = 2.4.$$
Check: $(3.2 - 5)^2 + 2.4^2 = 3.24 + 5.76 = 9$, so $C = (3.2, 2.4)$ is at distance $3$ from $B$ and at distance $4$ from $A$.

## Remarks

The example has all three parts of a classification: an equivalence relation, a complete invariant, and the exact set of its values.
The multiset $\{1, 2, 4\}$ is not a value, because $4 > 1 + 2$, so the two circles of step 5 do not meet and no triangle has these sides.

What changes when the equivalence relation changes?
Up to similarity, the side lengths are no longer an invariant, because a similarity can double them.
The new complete invariant is the multiset of the three angles, whose values are the triples of positive angles that add to $180^\circ$.
Another choice is the multiset of side lengths up to a common positive factor, so that $\{3, 4, 5\}$ and $\{6, 8, 10\}$ count as one value.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Give three sticks of lengths $3$, $4$ and $5$ to two people, and ask each of them to make a triangle.
The two triangles will have the same shape and size, even if one person turns or flips the triangle.
So the three lengths tell everything about a triangle up to moving it.
Not every three lengths work: with sticks $1$, $2$ and $4$, the two short sticks cannot reach across the long one.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: which of the triangles $T_1$ with sides $5, 6, 7$, $T_2$ with sides $7, 5, 6$ and $T_3$ with sides $5, 6, 8$ are congruent?
Sort the side lengths to read off the invariant:
$$f(T_1) = \{5, 6, 7\}, \qquad f(T_2) = \{5, 6, 7\}, \qquad f(T_3) = \{5, 6, 8\}.$$
The invariants of $T_1$ and $T_2$ agree and the invariant is complete, so $T_1 \sim T_2$.
The invariants of $T_1$ and $T_3$ differ, so $T_1 \not\sim T_3$.
Watch out: compare multisets, not ordered lists.
The lists $(5, 6, 7)$ and $(7, 5, 6)$ differ, but the triangles are congruent.
