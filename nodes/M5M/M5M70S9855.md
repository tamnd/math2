## Example

Let $F$ be the CNF with the four clauses $\{p, q\}$, $\{p, \neg q\}$, $\{\neg p, r\}$ and $\{\neg p, \neg r\}$.
It is unsatisfiable, because the first two clauses force $p = 1$ and the last two force $p = 0$.
Here is a [[resolution refutation|6JSH907W5R]] $\pi$ of $F$, one clause on each line.

1. $\{p, q\}$, an axiom.
2. $\{p, \neg q\}$, an axiom.
3. $\{p\}$, the resolvent of 1 and 2 on $q$.
4. $\{\neg p, r\}$, an axiom.
5. $\{\neg p, \neg r\}$, an axiom.
6. $\{\neg p\}$, the resolvent of 4 and 5 on $r$.
7. $\square$, the empty clause, the resolvent of 3 and 6 on $p$.

We read off four measures of $\pi$, as [[proof complexity|1Q046J0AW2]] defines them.

1. The size in clauses is $7$, one for each line.
2. The size in literals adds the clause lengths:
   $$2 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 0 = 10.$$
3. The width is the size of the widest clause, so $w(\pi) = 2$.
   The axioms already have width $2$, so $w(F) = 2$ and no refutation of $F$ has width below $2$.
4. The refutation is tree-like, because each derived clause, $\{p\}$ and $\{\neg p\}$, is a premise of exactly one later step.
   It is also regular, because each path from $\square$ to an axiom resolves on $p$ and then on $q$ or on $r$, and never repeats a variable.

Now we show that no refutation of $F$ has fewer than $7$ clauses.

1. Each three of the four clauses can be satisfied, as the L2 explanation checks.
   So a refutation must use all four axioms, since the derived clauses follow from the axioms that it uses.
2. Draw the refutation as a graph, with an edge from each premise to its resolvent.
   The part that the empty clause depends on is connected and contains all $4$ axioms.
3. Each of the $k$ resolution steps in that part adds one clause and two edges.
   A connected graph on $4 + k$ vertices needs at least $3 + k$ edges, so $2k \ge 3 + k$ and $k \ge 3$.
4. So every refutation has at least $4 + 3 = 7$ clauses, and $S(F \vdash \square) = 7$.

## Remarks

The example shows three measures on one proof: size in clauses, size in literals and width.
On a formula this small they move together, but on large formulas they come apart.
The [[size-width relation|GWDGRPAQSM]] says that a refutation of small size must have small width, so a lower bound on width forces a lower bound on size.

Change the fourth clause from $\{\neg p, \neg r\}$ to $\{\neg p, \neg s\}$, and the formula becomes satisfiable, with $p = 1$, $r = 1$, $s = 0$.
Then step 6 fails, since $\{\neg p, r\}$ and $\{\neg p, \neg s\}$ have no complementary pair.
If instead you add a fifth clause $\{q, r\}$, the same $\pi$ still refutes the formula, so the shortest size stays $7$, but the new clause is never used.
Watch out: "size" can mean clauses or literals, and a result must say which one it counts.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The four clauses say "$p$ or $q$", "$p$ or not $q$", "not $p$ or $r$" and "not $p$ or not $r$".
The first two together say "$p$", whatever $q$ is.
The last two together say "not $p$", whatever $r$ is.
So the proof writes $7$ short lines, and none of them has more than $2$ letters.

### L2 human

We check that each three clauses of $F$ can be satisfied.

1. Without $\{p, q\}$, take $p = 0$ and $q = 0$, and the other three clauses hold.
2. Without $\{p, \neg q\}$, take $p = 0$ and $q = 1$.
3. Without $\{\neg p, r\}$, take $p = 1$ and $r = 0$.
4. Without $\{\neg p, \neg r\}$, take $p = 1$ and $r = 1$.

So every refutation of $F$ uses all four axioms.
A count of this kind is the typical way to prove that a small refutation is as short as possible.
