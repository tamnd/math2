## Statement

Let $X$ be a nonempty set, and let $\Psi \colon X \times X \to \R$ be a function with $\Psi(x, x) = 0$ and $\Psi(x, y) = \Psi(y, x)$ for all $x, y \in X$.
Then the following are equivalent.

1. $\Psi$ is a [[conditionally negative definite kernel|4ZZX6Q8AGZ]]: $\sum_{i,j} c_i c_j \Psi(x_i, x_j) \le 0$ whenever $x_1, \dots, x_n \in X$ and the real numbers $c_1, \dots, c_n$ satisfy $\sum_i c_i = 0$.
2. For every $t > 0$, the kernel $e^{-t\Psi}$ is [[positive-definite|SCMS389SBG]].

## Remarks

Proof idea: from 2 to 1, if $\sum_i c_i = 0$, then $\sum_{i,j} c_i c_j \frac{1 - e^{-t\Psi(x_i, x_j)}}{t} = -\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i,j} c_i c_j e^{-t\Psi(x_i, x_j)} \le 0$, and the left side tends to $\sum_{i,j} c_i c_j \Psi(x_i, x_j)$ as $t \to 0$.
From 1 to 2, fix $x_0 \in X$; then $\Phi(x, y) = \Psi(x, x_0) + \Psi(y, x_0) - \Psi(x, y)$ is a positive-definite kernel.
By the Schur product theorem, $e^{t\Phi}$ is positive-definite, and $e^{-t\Psi(x, y)} = e^{t\Phi(x, y)}\, e^{-t\Psi(x, x_0)}\, e^{-t\Psi(y, x_0)}$ is again positive-definite.

Example: $\Psi(x, y) = \abs{x - y}^2$ on $\R^n$ is conditionally negative definite, so every Gaussian kernel $e^{-t\abs{x - y}^2}$ is positive-definite.

For a group $G$, applied to $\Psi(g, h) = \psi(h^{-1}g)$, the theorem says that $\psi$ is a conditionally negative definite function exactly when $e^{-t\psi}$ is a positive-definite function for every $t > 0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Squared distances and similarities are linked by the exponential.
The theorem says when a table of numbers behaves like squared Euclidean distances, in the sense of the inequality in 1.
This happens exactly when the table of numbers $e^{-t\Psi}$ behaves like a table of inner products, for every scale $t > 0$.

### L2 human

Schoenberg's theorem is the standard way to produce positive-definite kernels, such as the Gaussian kernel, from conditionally negative definite ones.
For groups, it links affine isometric actions on Hilbert space, which give conditionally negative definite functions, with unitary representations, which give positive-definite functions.
This is the mechanism behind the Delorme–Guichardet theorem on property (T) and behind the Haagerup property.
In probability, a complex-valued version of the theorem underlies the Lévy–Khintchine description of the characteristic exponents of Lévy processes.
