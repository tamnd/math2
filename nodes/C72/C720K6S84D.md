## Example

Let $F = \mathbb{F}_2(s)$ be the field of rational functions in one variable $s$ with coefficients in the field $\mathbb{F}_2$ of two elements.
Let
$$C = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & s \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
the [[companion matrix|0ZCWPNZTQ3]] of $x^2 + s$ over $F$.
We show that $C$ is [[semisimple|08VRX3Q4PB]] over $F$ but not [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] over an algebraic closure of $F$.

1. The columns give $C e_1 = e_2$ and $C e_2 = s e_1$, so $C^2 e_1 = s e_1$ and $C^2 e_2 = s e_2$.
   So $C^2 = s I$, and since $C$ is not a scalar matrix, the minimal polynomial of $C$ is $x^2 - s$, which equals $x^2 + s$ because $-1 = 1$ in $F$.
2. The polynomial $x^2 + s$ has no root in $F$, because $s$ is not the square of a rational function: the degree of numerator minus denominator of a square is even.
   A polynomial of degree $2$ with no root is [[irreducible|03FKX7Z5DC]].
3. So $C$ has no eigenvalue in $F$, hence no invariant line, and the only $C$-invariant subspaces of $F^2$ are $0$ and $F^2$.
4. Each of these two subspaces is an invariant complement of the other, so $C$ is semisimple over $F$.
5. Let $\bar F$ be an [[algebraic closure|0X6AB5P3G1]] of $F$, and let $a \in \bar F$ with $a^2 = s$.
   In characteristic $2$ we have $(x + a)^2 = x^2 + 2ax + a^2 = x^2 + s$.
6. So $a$ is the only eigenvalue of $C$ over $\bar F$.
   A diagonalizable matrix with the single eigenvalue $a$ equals $a I$, and $C \ne a I$, so $C$ is not diagonalizable over $\bar F$.
7. For the nilpotent part, $C - aI = \begin{pmatrix} a & s \\ 1 & a \end{pmatrix}$, since $-a = a$.
   Then
$$(C - aI)^2 = \begin{pmatrix} a^2 + s & 2as \\ 2a & s + a^2 \end{pmatrix} = 0,$$
   because $a^2 + s = 2s = 0$ and $2 = 0$.
   So over $\bar F$ the decomposition $C = aI + (C - aI)$ has a nonzero nilpotent part.

## Remarks

The example shows why "semisimple" and "diagonalizable over the algebraic closure" are different words.
Over a [[perfect field|VWBVKT794W]], for example in characteristic $0$ or over a finite field, every irreducible polynomial has distinct roots in $\bar F$, and the two notions agree.
The field $F = \mathbb{F}_2(s)$ is not perfect, since $s$ has no square root in it, and the irreducible polynomial $x^2 + s$ becomes the square $(x + a)^2$ over $\bar F$.
The same construction works for every prime $p$, with $F = \mathbb{F}_p(s)$ and the companion matrix of $x^p - s = (x - b)^p$, where $b^p = s$.

Over $F$, the semisimple form of the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]] can fail to exist.
Let $T$ be multiplication by $x$ on $F[x]/(f^2)$ with $f = x^2 + s$, so $f^2 = x^4 + s^2$ is the minimal polynomial of $T$.
Suppose $T = S + N$ with $N$ nilpotent and $S N = N S$.
Then $N$ commutes with $T$, and $T$ is cyclic, so $N = h(T)$ for a polynomial $h$, and $f$ divides $h$ because $N$ is nilpotent and $f$ is irreducible.
So $N^2 = h(T)^2 = 0$, and in characteristic $2$ we get $S^2 = (T + N)^2 = T^2 + N^2 = T^2$.
Then $S^2 + s = f(T) \ne 0$ while $(S^2 + s)^2 = f(T)^2 = 0$, so the minimal polynomial of $S$ is $f^2$, and $S$ is not semisimple.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Over the rational, real or complex numbers, a semisimple matrix becomes diagonal once we allow complex numbers.
This example uses an unusual field where that fails.
The matrix keeps no line in place, so it looks as simple as a matrix can be.
In a bigger field, though, its only eigenvalue is a repeated one, and it is not diagonal in any basis there.

### L2 human

The proof of step 2 is the part that uses the field.
Suppose $(g/h)^2 = s$ with polynomials $g, h \in \mathbb{F}_2[s]$ and $h \ne 0$.
Then $g^2 = s h^2$, and comparing degrees gives $2 \deg g = 1 + 2 \deg h$.
The left side is even and the right side is odd, which is impossible.
So $s$ is not a square in $F$, and $x^2 + s$ has no root in $F$.
The typical use of the example is to show that the perfect field hypothesis in the Jordan-Chevalley decomposition cannot be dropped.
The trap is to test semisimplicity in characteristic $p$ by passing to $\bar F$ and looking for distinct eigenvalues, which works only over a perfect field.

### L3 human

The map $C$ makes $F^2$ into the field $F(a) = F[x]/(x^2 + s)$, a purely inseparable extension of $F$ of degree $2$.
After extending scalars, $F(a) \otimes_F \bar F \cong \bar F[x]/((x + a)^2)$ is not reduced, and its nonzero nilpotent element is the nilpotent part of $C$ over $\bar F$.
Over a perfect field every finite extension is separable, the tensor product stays reduced, and semisimplicity survives the change of field.
