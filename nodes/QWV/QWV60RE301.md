## Example

Let $U = \operatorname{diag}(i, i) = iI_2$.
We show that $U$ lies in the [[unitary group|VW5H9XFNJB]] $U(2)$ but not in the [[special unitary group|ZRYYPHWESP]] $SU(2)$.

1. The conjugate transpose of a diagonal matrix conjugates the diagonal entries, so $U^* = \operatorname{diag}(-i, -i)$.
2. We multiply:
$$U^*U = \operatorname{diag}\big((-i) \cdot i, \ (-i) \cdot i\big) = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1) = I_2.$$
So $U$ is unitary.
3. The determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal entries:
$$\det U = i \cdot i = -1.$$
4. Since $\det U \ne 1$, the matrix $U$ is not in $SU(2)$.

Check: every element of $SU(2)$ has the form $\begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ -\overline{\beta} & \overline{\alpha} \end{pmatrix}$, by [[the quaternion theorem|A763JSEVW8]].
The diagonal entries of $U$ are $i$ and $i$, and $i \ne \overline{i} = -i$, so $U$ does not have this form.

## Remarks

The example separates two conditions that are easy to confuse.
Every unitary matrix has $\abs{\det U} = 1$, and here $\abs{-1} = 1$.
The special unitary group asks for more, $\det U = 1$ exactly, and that condition fixes the argument of the determinant as well as its size.

The same test works for every scalar matrix.
Since $\det(zI_n) = z^n$, the scalar $zI_n$ with $\abs{z} = 1$ lies in $SU(n)$ exactly when $z$ is an $n$-th root of unity.
For $n = 2$ the scalars in $SU(2)$ are only $\pm I_2$, so $e^{i\pi/4} I_2$, with determinant $e^{i\pi/2} = i$, fails too.
For $n = 3$ the scalar $iI_3$ has determinant $i^3 = -i$ and fails, while $\omega I_3$ with $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$ has determinant $\omega^3 = 1$ and lies in $SU(3)$.

Since $U$ is a scalar matrix, it commutes with every matrix, so it lies in the center of $U(2)$.
It does not lie in the center of $SU(2)$, because it is not in $SU(2)$ at all.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix multiplies each of the two coordinates by the complex number $i$, which is a quarter turn of the complex plane.
A quarter turn keeps lengths, so the matrix is unitary.
The determinant multiplies the two quarter turns together, and two quarter turns make a half turn, which is $-1$.
So the determinant is not $1$, and the matrix is not special unitary.

### L2 human

Here is the typical use: to test a scalar matrix $zI_n$ for $SU(n)$, compute $z^n$.
For $z = i$ and $n = 2$ this gives $-1$, and for $z = i$ and $n = 4$ it gives $i^4 = 1$, so $iI_4$ lies in $SU(4)$.
Watch out: a turn by the same angle in every complex coordinate is not automatically in $SU(n)$, because the angles add up in the determinant.
