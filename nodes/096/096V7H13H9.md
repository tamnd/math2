## Statement

When a list has exactly as many vectors as the dimension, one of the two conditions for a basis gives the other for free.
Let $F$ be a field and let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite [[dimension|05G2G0P224]] $n$.
Let $v_1, \dots, v_n \in V$ be $n$ vectors.

1. If $v_1, \dots, v_n$ are [[linearly independent|0EVHJFTG9Z]], then they form a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] of $V$.
2. If $v_1, \dots, v_n$ span $V$, then they form a basis of $V$.

## Remarks

### Half the work

Is $(1, 2), (3, 4)$ a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$?
There are two vectors and $\dim \mathbb{R}^2 = 2$, so it is enough to check independence.
From $a(1, 2) + b(3, 4) = 0$ we get the two equations
$$a + 3b = 0, \qquad 2a + 4b = 0.$$
The first gives $a = -3b$, and then the second gives $-6b + 4b = -2b = 0$, so $b = 0$ and $a = 0$.
The vectors are independent, so part 1 says that they span $\mathbb{R}^2$ as well, and we never had to solve $a(1, 2) + b(3, 4) = (x, y)$.

The same shortcut works in other spaces.
In the space $P_2$ of real polynomials of degree at most $2$, which has dimension $3$, the polynomials $1$, $1 + x$ and $(1 + x)^2 = 1 + 2x + x^2$ span $P_2$.
Indeed, $1$ is in the list, $x = (1 + x) - 1$, and $x^2 = (1 + x)^2 - 2(1 + x) + 1$.
So part 2 says that they are also independent, and they form a basis.
Check: $(1 + 2x + x^2) - 2(1 + x) + 1 = x^2$, as claimed.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The number of vectors must be exactly $n$.
With fewer, $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$ are independent in $\mathbb{R}^3$, but they do not span, since every combination has third entry $0$.
With more, $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$ span $\mathbb{R}^2$, but they are dependent, since the third is the sum of the first two.

Finite dimension is needed.
In $F[x]$, the family $x, x^2, x^3, \dots$ is independent and has as many vectors as the basis $1, x, x^2, \dots$, namely $\aleph_0$.
But it does not span, because every combination of it has constant term $0$, so $1$ is missing.

The scalars must form a field.
In $\mathbb{Z}$, which is free of rank $1$, the single element $2$ is independent, but it does not generate $\mathbb{Z}$.

### What it gives you

For a list of exactly $\dim V$ vectors, independent, spanning and basis all mean the same thing.
For $n$ vectors in $F^n$, put them as the columns of a square matrix $A$; they form a basis exactly when $A$ is invertible, by the [[square matrix criterion|WFM1CX5PZT]].
A list $v_1, \dots, v_k$ is independent exactly when its span has dimension $k$.
An independent list is a basis of its span, and if the span has dimension $k$, part 2 applied inside the span makes the list a basis of it.
For linear maps, the criterion becomes the fact that a map between spaces of the same finite dimension is injective exactly when it is surjective; the page on [[injective and surjective endomorphisms|0912EEEVCT]] develops this.

The converse holds as well.
Every basis of $V$ has exactly $n$ vectors, by the [[dimension theorem|0WK0Y8RH1M]], so a list that is a basis always has the right size.

### Watch out

The count must use the dimension of the space you are in, not of a larger space.
The vectors $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$ are independent and form a basis of the plane $z = 0$, which has dimension $2$, but not of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Also, a repeated vector counts twice: the list $(1, 0), (1, 0)$ has two entries, but it is dependent and spans only a line.

### Try it

Do $(1, 1, 1)$, $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(1, 0, 0)$ form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$?
Yes: they span, because $e_1 = (1, 0, 0)$, $e_2 = (1, 1, 0) - (1, 0, 0)$ and $e_3 = (1, 1, 1) - (1, 1, 0)$, and three spanning vectors in dimension $3$ form a basis.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A basis must do two jobs: reach every vector, and have no spare vectors.
If you have exactly as many vectors as the dimension, the two jobs come together.
Three independent arrows in space always reach every point of space.
And three arrows that reach every point of space are never spare.
So you only need to check one of the two jobs.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: for which numbers $t$ do $(1, t)$ and $(t, 4)$ form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$?
By part 1 we only need independence, and two vectors of $\mathbb{R}^2$ are independent exactly when the $2 \times 2$ determinant is nonzero:
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ t & 4 \end{pmatrix} = 4 - t^2.$$
So the vectors form a basis for every $t$ except $t = 2$ and $t = -2$.
Check at $t = 2$: the vectors $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 4)$ are dependent, since $(2, 4) = 2(1, 2)$.
The trap is to apply the criterion with the wrong count, for example to two vectors of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

### L3 human

The criterion is the linear form of a counting principle: for a map between finite sets of the same size, injective and surjective are the same.
Here the map is $F^n \to V$, $(a_1, \dots, a_n) \mapsto a_1 v_1 + \dots + a_n v_n$, and independence and spanning are its injectivity and surjectivity.
Over a commutative ring the spanning half survives: $n$ elements that generate $R^n$ form a basis.
The independent half fails, as $2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ shows.
In infinite dimension both halves fail, and the theorem on the [[dimension of a subspace|0QW084460E]] shows the same gap for subspaces.
