## Statement

Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a complex [[semisimple Lie algebra|EY9DN7QAX1]] with [[Cartan subalgebra|BB098JPWGA]] $\mathfrak{h}$.
Choose positive roots, with [[simple roots|CSBM217V1N]] $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r$ and [[coroots|XV6Q01FVAS]] $h_1, \dots, h_r \in \mathfrak{h}$.
Call $\lambda \in \mathfrak{h}^*$ *dominant integral* if $\lambda(h_i) \in \Z_{\ge 0}$ for $i = 1, \dots, r$.
Then:

1. Every finite-dimensional [[irreducible|HFW8642GBK]] $\mathfrak{g}$-module has a [[highest weight|C5W8BE1DRY]], and this weight is dominant integral.
2. Two finite-dimensional irreducible $\mathfrak{g}$-modules with the same highest weight are isomorphic.
3. Every dominant integral $\lambda$ is the highest weight of a finite-dimensional irreducible $\mathfrak{g}$-module $L(\lambda)$.

So $\lambda \mapsto L(\lambda)$ is a bijection from the dominant integral weights onto the isomorphism classes of finite-dimensional irreducible $\mathfrak{g}$-modules.

## Remarks

For part 1, a weight vector of maximal weight is killed by all positive root vectors, and it generates the irreducible module.
Integrality and dominance follow from the representation theory of the copy of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ attached to each simple root.
Part 2 holds because an irreducible module generated by a highest weight vector of weight $\lambda$ is a quotient of the [[Verma module|809XR5G0DG]] $M(\lambda)$, which has a unique irreducible quotient.
For part 3, one shows that this quotient $L(\lambda)$ is finite-dimensional when $\lambda$ is dominant integral, since its set of weights is then stable under the Weyl group.
Example: for $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\C)$ the dominant integral weights are $\lambda(h) = n \in \Z_{\ge 0}$, and $L(n)$ is the irreducible module of dimension $n + 1$.
Dominance is necessary: for $\lambda(h) = -1$, the irreducible quotient of $M(\lambda)$ is $M(\lambda)$ itself, which is infinite-dimensional.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The finite-dimensional irreducible representations of a complex semisimple Lie algebra can be listed completely.
Each one is labelled by its highest weight, a point of a lattice that lies in a certain cone.
Each such point labels exactly one representation.
For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ the labels are $0, 1, 2, \dots$, and the representation with label $n$ has dimension $n + 1$.

### L2 human

With [[Weyl's theorem on complete reducibility|92EXQ237Q6]], the theorem describes every finite-dimensional module as a sum of modules $L(\lambda)$.
The [[Weyl character formula|G2SA2YNA33]] then gives the weights and multiplicities of $L(\lambda)$.
A similar classification holds for compact connected Lie groups, with highest weights that are integral for the group.
For a simply connected group, all dominant integral weights occur.
In the language of groups, the Borel–Weil theorem realises $L(\lambda)$ as sections of a line bundle on the flag variety.
