## Summary

Once you know what a statement says, the next question is how to prove it, and the answer depends mostly on the shape of the statement.
This part of [[reasoning and proof|SGSVYFDSYK]] collects the standard answers.
It starts with the plainest method, the direct proof that assumes the hypothesis and computes forward, and with the "let $x$ be arbitrary" step that proves a claim about every object at once.
It then turns to the methods that work through negation, the contrapositive and proof by contradiction, and explains why they are different and when each one needs the law of excluded middle.
Proof by cases, two-way proofs of equivalences and the symmetry argument "without loss of generality" break a goal into pieces.
Existence and uniqueness come next: you can prove that an object exists by building it, or by showing that its absence is absurd, as in the famous proof that some irrational number to an irrational power is rational.
The last pages go the other way, with counterexamples that refute false claims and proofs of impossibility such as the mutilated chessboard, and with the forward-backward method for finding a proof in the first place.
Induction, which proves claims about all natural numbers, follows in the topic [[induction and well-ordering as proof methods|EEH2F76CYQ]].

## Remarks

### Reading order

1. [[Proof methods|G9JFJGY4TQ]]: a map of the methods, and how to read the form of a goal to pick one.
2. [[Direct proof|0NTF6DMVAP]]: the unfold, compute and fold pattern that most proofs reduce to.
3. [[Proving universal statements|CX38X989TB]]: why an arbitrary element proves "for every", and why examples never do.
4. [[Proof by contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]]: how to swap a hard implication for an equivalent easy one, and how not to confuse it with the converse.
5. [[Proof by contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]]: the two forms of "suppose not", with the irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ and the infinitude of primes.
6. [[Proof by cases|0WCP0PVET6]]: how to split a problem into cases that cover everything, with the triangle inequality as the model.
7. [[Proving biconditionals and equivalences|TFQ1845T84]]: the two directions of "if and only if", and a cycle of implications for a longer list.
8. [[Constructive proof|01XM7JEKHY]]: existence by exhibiting the object and checking it.
9. [[Non-constructive existence proof|JZQVJ9XT63]]: existence without a witness, and what such a proof does not tell you.
10. [[Proof of uniqueness|GQHHNV79PA]]: how to show that two objects with a property are equal, as for the identity of a group.
11. [[Counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]]: how one instance refutes a universal claim, and what the instance must satisfy.
12. [[Without loss of generality|Y7NE0E6PCV]]: how a named symmetry reduces many cases to one, and when the reduction is not allowed.
13. [[Forward-backward method|V8ZB838JY5]]: how to find a proof by working from both ends until the chains meet.
14. [[Proof of impossibility|6F55216R53]]: invariants, remainders and colorings that show that no object or method of a given kind exists.

### Prerequisites

1. The topic [[the language of mathematical statements|WMY13MXY3W]], for conditionals, quantifiers, negation and "exactly one".
2. The page [[mathematical proof|0B94TKFQJX]] and the topic [[proof, theorems and mathematical method|N4P3E53HM0]], for what a proof is.
3. School algebra with inequalities, and the integers with even and odd numbers and divisibility; a few examples use functions, sets, limits or groups, and they say so at the start.
4. The formal rules of inference behind the methods are in the topic [[propositions, connectives and quantifiers|PM1AYWABA7]], but you do not need them to read the pages.
