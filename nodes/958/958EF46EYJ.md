## Summary

This topic is a part of [[reasoning and proof|SGSVYFDSYK]].
It covers the standard methods to prove conditional, universal, existential and uniqueness statements, and to disprove false statements.
The methods are grouped by the logical form of the goal, and each page gives the method, its logical basis, worked examples and the typical errors.

## Remarks

### Reading order

1. [[Proof methods|G9JFJGY4TQ]]: a map of the methods, and how the form of the goal suggests one.
2. [[Direct proof|0NTF6DMVAP]]: assume the hypotheses and reason forward to the conclusion.
3. [[Proving universal statements|CX38X989TB]]: let $x$ be arbitrary, and why examples do not prove a universal claim.
4. [[Proof by contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]]: prove that not $Q$ implies not $P$.
5. [[Proof by contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]]: assume the negation and derive a contradiction.
6. [[Proof by cases|0WCP0PVET6]]: split into cases that cover everything, and prove each one.
7. [[Proving biconditionals and equivalences|TFQ1845T84]]: two directions, or a cycle of implications.
8. [[Constructive proof|01XM7JEKHY]]: prove existence by exhibiting the object.
9. [[Non-constructive existence proof|JZQVJ9XT63]]: existence without a witness, by contradiction or by cases.
10. [[Proof of uniqueness|GQHHNV79PA]]: two such objects are equal.
11. [[Counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]]: one instance refutes a universal claim.
12. [[Without loss of generality|Y7NE0E6PCV]]: reduce to one case by a symmetry.
13. [[Forward-backward method|V8ZB838JY5]]: plan a proof from both ends.
14. [[Proof of impossibility|6F55216R53]]: show that no object or method of a given kind exists.

### Prerequisites

1. The topic [[the language of mathematical statements|WMY13MXY3W]]: conditionals, quantifiers, negation and "exactly one".
2. The page [[mathematical proof|0B94TKFQJX]] and the topic [[proof, theorems and mathematical method|N4P3E53HM0]], for what a proof is.
3. School algebra with inequalities, and the integers with even and odd numbers and divisibility. A few examples use functions, sets, limits or groups, and they say so at the start.
4. The formal rules of inference behind the methods are in the topic [[propositions, connectives and quantifiers|PM1AYWABA7]]; they are not needed to read the pages.
