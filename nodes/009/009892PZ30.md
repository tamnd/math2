## Definition

The linear map of a matrix is the map that sends each basis vector of the domain to the vector whose coordinates stand in the matching column.
Let $F$ be a field, and let $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces over $F$ with ordered bases $B = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ of $V$ and $C = (c_1, \dots, c_m)$ of $W$.
Let $A = (a_{ij})$ be an $m \times n$ matrix over $F$.
The *linear map of $A$ in the bases $B$ and $C$* is the unique linear map $T_A \colon V \to W$ with
$$T_A(b_j) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{ij} c_i \qquad (1 \le j \le n).$$
For $v = x_1 b_1 + \dots + x_n b_n$ this means $T_A(v) = \sum_{i=1}^m \bigl(\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} x_j\bigr) c_i$.

## Remarks

The definition makes sense because a linear map can send the vectors of a basis anywhere, and it is then fixed by these values, by the [[theorem on bases and linear maps|0R8X9XA83A]].
The size of $A$ must match the bases: $A$ needs one column for each vector of $B$ and one row for each vector of $C$.
The bases must be ordered, because column $j$ is attached to $b_j$ and row $i$ to $c_i$.

For example, take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and the basis $(1, x)$ of the linear polynomials on both sides.
Then $T_A(1) = 0$ and $T_A(x) = 1$, so $T_A(a + bx) = b$, the derivative.
A $2 \times 3$ matrix with the bases of $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$ fails the size condition for a map $\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$, since it has three columns and $\mathbb{R}^2$ has only two basis vectors.

The map $A \mapsto T_A$ is the inverse of $T \mapsto [T]_{C,B}$, the [[matrix of a linear map|0YYMY95FWS]], by the [[theorem on linear maps and matrices|Q3EDKTZ6TA]].
For $V = F^n$ and $W = F^m$ with the standard bases, $T_A$ is the map $x \mapsto Ax$, see [[multiplication by a matrix as a linear map|094V2VBJXJ]].
In Mathlib this map is `Matrix.toLin`, with the basis of the domain first.
The [[concept page|06HMCTAQH3]] has more examples.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A matrix and two bases together define a linear map.
Column $j$ tells you where the map sends basis vector number $j$.
For the matrix with columns $(2, 1)$ and $(0, 3)$ and the standard basis, $(1, 0)$ goes to $(2, 1)$ and $(0, 1)$ goes to $(0, 3)$.
This undoes the step from a linear map to its matrix.
