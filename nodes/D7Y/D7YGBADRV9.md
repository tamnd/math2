## Summary

Fraunhofer diffraction, or far-field diffraction, is the diffraction pattern of an aperture observed very far away, or in the focal plane of a lens.
In this regime the field is, up to a phase factor and a scaling, the [[Fourier transform|DPVM34ADZV]] of the field in the aperture.
It applies when the [[Fresnel number|AK3TF7B3ZW]] is much smaller than $1$, and it is the limiting case of [[Fresnel diffraction|M8669NXXJS]].
Its patterns include the single-slit fringes, the [[Airy disk|DB435TBQJT]] of a circular aperture and the orders of a diffraction grating.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Far from a narrow slit, light spreads into a central bright band with weaker bands on each side.
The narrower the slit, the wider the pattern: shape in the aperture and spread on the screen are inverse to each other.
This is the same trade-off as between a signal and its frequency content, because the far-field pattern is the Fourier transform of the aperture.
A lens brings the far field to its focal plane, so these patterns can be seen on a screen at a short distance.
