## Example

Consider a fluid of density $\rho > 0$ and kinematic viscosity $\nu > 0$ in the gap $R_1 < r < R_2$ between two coaxial cylinders, in cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$.
The inner cylinder rotates with angular velocity $\Omega_1$ and the outer one with $\Omega_2$.
*Circular Couette flow* is the azimuthal flow $u = v(r) \, e_\theta$ with
$$v(r) = A r + \frac{B}{r}, \qquad A = \frac{\Omega_2 R_2^2 - \Omega_1 R_1^2}{R_2^2 - R_1^2}, \qquad B = \frac{(\Omega_1 - \Omega_2) R_1^2 R_2^2}{R_2^2 - R_1^2},$$
and a pressure $p(r)$ with $p'(r) = \rho \, v(r)^2 / r$.

Check: the flow has no radial or axial component and does not depend on $\theta$ or $z$, so it is divergence-free.
The azimuthal [[Navier–Stokes equation|18KXZHVF4M]] reduces to $v'' + v'/r - v/r^2 = 0$, and both $r$ and $1/r$ solve it.
The radial equation reduces to $-\rho v^2 / r = -p'(r)$, which is the choice of $p$.
At $r = R_1$ one gets $v(R_1) = \Omega_1 R_1$ after simplifying, and similarly $v(R_2) = \Omega_2 R_2$, so the [[no-slip condition|DF0M8YNQ3D]] holds.

## Remarks

When the gap is narrow, the flow is close to plane [[Couette flow|G4JB1YWEJZ]].
If only the inner cylinder rotates and fast enough, this flow becomes unstable and is replaced by stacked toroidal vortices, the Taylor vortices.
G. I. Taylor's 1923 analysis of this [[instability|2A3F7MSEMH]] was an early triumph of linear stability theory, with close agreement between theory and experiment.
For an ideal fluid, Rayleigh's criterion says that the flow is stable to axisymmetric disturbances when $(r v)^2$ increases with $r$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Fill the gap between two nested cylinders with liquid and turn one or both.
At slow speeds the liquid just goes round in circles, with a smooth speed profile that matches each wall.
When the inner cylinder spins fast, the circles break up into a stack of doughnut-shaped rolls.
This setup is a standard laboratory for studying how smooth flow turns into turbulence.
