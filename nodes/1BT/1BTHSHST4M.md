## Summary

In 1821 Cauchy published the claim that a convergent series of continuous functions has a continuous sum, and the partial sums $x^n$ on $[0, 1]$ show that the claim needs a hypothesis he did not state.
This part of [[reasoning and proof|SGSVYFDSYK]] asks what makes a proof rigorous, so that such gaps cannot hide, and how a proof comes to be accepted by mathematicians.
It starts with rigor as a standard that depends on the reader and the period, and with the difference between the informal proofs of daily practice and the formal derivations that a machine can check.
A history follows the standard from Euclid through the reform of analysis to set theory and Bourbaki.
Then come the hard cases for the standard, starting with pictures that make an identity visible.
Some proofs need a computer, such as those of the four color theorem and the 200-terabyte proof about Pythagorean triples, and some are too long for one person to read.
The last pages turn to the community: how refereeing and time produce consensus, and what happens when experts disagree.
They end with new proofs of known theorems, the debate of 1993 and 1994 on speculation and rigor, and Erdős's idea of a proof from "the Book".

## Remarks

### Reading order

1. [[Mathematical rigor|E1PE9R8DBB]]: learn what it means for every step to have a checkable reason, and why the standard depends on the reader.
2. [[Formal and informal proofs|8VZ77R4R8S]]: see how a two-line textbook proof expands into a derivation that a program can check.
3. [[History of proof and rigor|20F7K9RVT6]]: follow how each change in the standard answered a concrete failure, from Euclid to proof assistants.
4. [[Proof without words|V6ESFEFKJC]]: read identities from pictures, and learn what a picture leaves out and how to fill the gap.
5. [[Computer-assisted proof|MC18C7BMFT]]: understand how a written reduction and a computation combine into a proof, and where trust in the program comes from.
6. [[Long proofs and surveyability|RM0S65BHWH]]: meet proofs too long for one reader and the question of whether a proof must be surveyable.
7. [[Acceptance of proofs|MJ3EHQJJHX]]: see how refereeing, consensus and time turn a claimed proof into an accepted theorem, and how disputes are reported.
8. [[Many proofs of one theorem|KNQDDN6PT2]]: find out why mathematicians prove known theorems again and what each new proof adds.
9. [[The debate of Jaffe and Quinn with Thurston|K3M3TEX7SJ]]: follow the positions of Jaffe and Quinn in 1993 and of Thurston in 1994 on speculation, rigor and understanding.
10. [[Proofs from the Book|A4RJYP79A7]]: learn what makes a proof feel inevitable, through examples from the collection of Aigner and Ziegler.

[[Proof, theorems and mathematical method|N4P3E53HM0]] explains what a proof is and what it is for.
[[Basic proof methods|958EF46EYJ]] supplies the direct proofs, contrapositives, contradictions and inductions that the examples use.
