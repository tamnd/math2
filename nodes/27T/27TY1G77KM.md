## Example

Let $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the map $(x, y) \mapsto (y, x)$.
We show that $S$ is orthogonal but is not a [[rotation matrix|HNY4CPEEJA]].

1. The columns $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$ are orthonormal, so $S$ is [[orthogonal|0QRBVFJ50X]].
2. The determinant is $\det S = 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1 = -1$, while every rotation matrix has determinant $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$. So $S$ is not a rotation.
3. A direct check gives the same answer. If $S = R(\theta)$, the entry $(1, 1)$ gives $\cos\theta = 0$ and the entry $(2, 1)$ gives $\sin\theta = 1$.
4. Then the entry $(1, 2)$ of $R(\theta)$ is $-\sin\theta = -1$, but the entry $(1, 2)$ of $S$ is $1$, a contradiction.
5. The map fixes each point $(t, t)$ of the line $y = x$, and it sends $(1, -1)$ to $(-1, 1)$. So $S$ is the reflection in the line $y = x$.

## Remarks

A rotation of the plane other than $I_2$ fixes only the origin, while $S$ fixes a whole line.
That is a second way to see that $S$ is not a rotation.

The swap is a rotation and a reflection put together: $S = R(\pi/2)\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$.
Check: $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
So $S$ first reflects in the $x$-axis and then makes a quarter turn.

The swap also satisfies $S^2 = I_2$.
The only rotations with square $I_2$ are $I_2$ and $-I_2$, because $R(\theta)^2 = R(2\theta) = I_2$ forces $\theta = 0$ or $\theta = \pi$ up to full turns.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Swapping the two coordinates is a mirror image in the diagonal line $y = x$.
A mirror image turns a left hand into a right hand, and a rotation never does that.
So the swap keeps lengths, but it is not a rotation.

### L2 human

An orthogonal $2 \times 2$ matrix is a rotation exactly when its determinant is $1$, so the determinant settles the question in one line.
For $S$ it is
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1 = -1.$$
Compare the quarter turn $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which has the same entries up to one sign and determinant $0 - (-1) = 1$.
Watch out: a matrix with entries in $\{0, 1, -1\}$ and orthonormal columns can be a rotation or a reflection, and only the determinant tells which.
