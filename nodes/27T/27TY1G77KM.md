## Example

Let $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the map $(x, y) \mapsto (y, x)$.
We show that $S$ is orthogonal, but it is not a [[rotation matrix|HNY4CPEEJA]].

1. The columns $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$ are orthonormal, so $S$ is [[orthogonal|0QRBVFJ50X]].
2. $\det S = 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1 = -1$, so $S$ is not a rotation.
3. A second check: if $S = R(\theta)$, then the $(1, 1)$ entry gives $\cos\theta = 0$, and the $(2, 1)$ entry gives $\sin\theta = 1$.
4. Then the $(1, 2)$ entry of $R(\theta)$ is $-\sin\theta = -1$, but the $(1, 2)$ entry of $S$ is $1$. This is a contradiction.
5. $S$ fixes each point of the line $y = x$, and it sends $(1, -1)$ to $(-1, 1)$. So $S$ is the reflection in the line $y = x$.

## Remarks

- A rotation of the plane other than $I_2$ fixes only the origin, but $S$ fixes a whole line.
- $S = R(\pi/2)\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$: reflect in the $x$-axis, then turn by a quarter turn.
- $S^2 = I_2$. The only rotations with square $I_2$ are $I_2$ and $-I_2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Swapping the two coordinates is a mirror image in the diagonal line $y = x$.
A mirror image turns a left hand into a right hand.
A rotation never does this.
So the swap keeps lengths, but it is not a rotation.

### L2 human

The worked check is in the Example.
The typical use: an orthogonal $2 \times 2$ matrix is a rotation exactly when its determinant is $1$.
A trap: a matrix with entries in $\set{0, 1, -1}$ and orthonormal columns can be a rotation or a reflection, and only the determinant tells which.
