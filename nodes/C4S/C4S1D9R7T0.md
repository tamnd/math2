## Example

Fix a real number $\varepsilon > 0$.
We show that "$1/n < \varepsilon$" holds [[for all sufficiently large|FEQ7WATMHF]] positive integers $n$, and we find the least threshold $N$.

1. Put
   $$N = \left\lfloor \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \right\rfloor + 1,$$
   where $\lfloor x \rfloor$ is the largest integer at most $x$.
   Then $N > 1/\varepsilon$ and $N \ge 1$.
2. Let $n \ge N$.
   Then $n > 1/\varepsilon > 0$, so $1/n < \varepsilon$.
3. So "$1/n < \varepsilon$" holds for every $n \ge N$, that is, it holds eventually.
4. No smaller threshold works.
   If $N \ge 2$, then $n = N - 1 = \lfloor 1/\varepsilon \rfloor$ is a positive integer with $n \le 1/\varepsilon$, so $1/n \ge \varepsilon$.
5. A numerical case: for $\varepsilon = 0.03$ we get $1/\varepsilon = 33.33\ldots$ and $N = 34$.
   Check: $1/34 = 0.0294\ldots < 0.03$, while $1/33 = 0.0303\ldots > 0.03$.
6. A case with equality: for $\varepsilon = 0.25$ we get $1/\varepsilon = 4$ and $N = 5$.
   At $n = 4$ we have $1/4 = 0.25$, which is not less than $0.25$, so the $+1$ in the formula for $N$ is needed.

## Remarks

The threshold $N$ depends on $\varepsilon$, and a smaller $\varepsilon$ needs a larger $N$.
Step 1 uses the [[Archimedean property|0A74SX55QP]] of the real numbers, which says that some integer is larger than $1/\varepsilon$.

Since the statement holds eventually for every $\varepsilon > 0$, the sequence $1/n$ converges to $0$, by the [[definition of the limit of a sequence|05M5SWSZZD]].
The [[order of quantifiers|0FYQMKFE77]] matters here.
For every $\varepsilon$ there is an $N$, but no single $N$ works for every $\varepsilon$, because $\varepsilon = 1/(2N)$ fails at $n = N$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The fractions $1, \tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac14, \ldots$ get as small as you want.
Pick a small target, say $0.03$.
The fractions are above the target at first, but from $1/34$ on they are all below it.
For a smaller target you must go further along the list, but such a point always exists.

### L2 human

Take $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$.
Then $1/\varepsilon = 1000$, so
$$N = \lfloor 1000 \rfloor + 1 = 1001.$$
For $n = 1001$ we get $1/1001 < 1/1000$, so the statement is true there, and step 2 covers every larger $n$.
For $n = 1000$ we get $1/1000 = \varepsilon$, so the strict inequality fails, and $1000$ is not a threshold.

The typical use is a proof that a sequence converges: for every $\varepsilon$ we produce a threshold that works.
Watch out: "eventually, $1/n < \varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$" is false, because it would force $1/n \le 0$, and $1/n$ is never $0$.
