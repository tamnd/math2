## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $\mathcal{F}$ be a set of endomorphisms of $V$.
Assume that $S T = T S$ for all $S, T \in \mathcal{F}$, and that the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] of each $T \in \mathcal{F}$ splits over $F$.

1. The members of $\mathcal{F}$ have a common [[eigenvector|0N6960W0BV]]: there is $v \ne 0$ with $T v \in F v$ for every $T \in \mathcal{F}$.
2. There is a flag $0 = V_0 \subset V_1 \subset \cdots \subset V_n = V$ with $\dim V_i = i$, in which each $V_i$ is $T$-[[invariant|0ACX5QT8TE]] for every $T \in \mathcal{F}$.
3. There is a basis of $V$ in which the matrix of every $T \in \mathcal{F}$ is [[upper triangular|0N4XY7Z3NN]].

In particular, every set of pairwise [[commuting matrices|8G3X8MPG7E]] in $M_n(\C)$ has one invertible $P$ such that $P^{-1} A P$ is upper triangular for every $A$ in the set.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The members must commute, or satisfy a weaker condition such as McCoy's. The matrices $E_{12} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $E_{21} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ have the eigenvectors $F e_1$ and $F e_2$ only, so they have no common eigenvector.
- Each characteristic polynomial must split. Over $\R$, the rotation by a right angle commutes with $I$, but it has no invariant line.

### Consequences

- For commuting $A$ and $B$ over $\C$, the eigenvalues of $A + B$ and $A B$ are $\alpha_i + \beta_i$ and $\alpha_i \beta_i$, where $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ are the diagonal entries of the common triangular forms.
- So $\operatorname{tr}(A B) = \sum_i \alpha_i \beta_i$, and $A + B$ is [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]] when $A$ and $B$ are commuting nilpotent matrices.
- Commuting diagonalizable matrices are even [[simultaneously diagonalizable|0FK17N4DKA]].

### Converse

The converse is false.
The matrices $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ are both upper triangular, but they do not commute.
The exact condition over an algebraically closed field is McCoy's theorem: $A$ and $B$ are simultaneously triangularizable if and only if $f(A, B)(A B - B A)$ is nilpotent for every polynomial $f$ in two noncommuting variables.

### Generalizations

For a solvable Lie algebra of matrices over $\C$, a common invariant flag exists by [[Lie's theorem|11K6SVNXMH]].
For a Lie algebra of nilpotent matrices, Engel's theorem gives a common flag with zero diagonal.
These belong to Lie theory, and this page only names them.

## History

The statement for commuting matrices is a textbook theorem, for example in Hoffman and Kunze, Section 6.5.
McCoy proved the criterion for simultaneous triangularization by a polynomial condition on the commutator.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two matrices that commute can be made triangular with the same change of basis, over the complex numbers.
The key is a direction that both matrices only stretch.
Once we have it, we put it first and repeat the argument with one dimension less.

### L2 human

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 4 \\ -4 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$, which commute.

1. $A$ has the single eigenvalue $2$, with eigenvector $(1, 1)$.
2. $B(1, 1) = (4, 4)$, so $(1, 1)$ is a common eigenvector.
3. With $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $Q^{-1} A Q = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. $B Q = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 4 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$ and $Q^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so $Q^{-1} B Q = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.
5. Both are upper triangular, and $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ checks step 4.

The typical use is to prove facts about commuting matrices, such as the sum of commuting nilpotent matrices being nilpotent.
The trap is to apply the result to matrices that do not commute, such as $E_{12}$ and $E_{21}$.

### L3 human

In the language of algebras, a commutative subalgebra of $M_n(\C)$ is conjugate to a subalgebra of the upper triangular matrices.
The common eigenvector of part 1 is a common weight: a linear form $\alpha$ on the span of $\mathcal{F}$ with $T v = \alpha(T) v$.
Lie's theorem replaces commutativity by solvability, and the proof of the weight lemma becomes harder.
