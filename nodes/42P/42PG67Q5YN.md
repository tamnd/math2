## Statement

Commuting maps whose characteristic polynomials split can be made upper triangular by one common basis.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $\mathcal{F}$ be a set of endomorphisms of $V$.
Assume that $S T = T S$ for all $S, T \in \mathcal{F}$, and that the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] of each $T \in \mathcal{F}$ splits over $F$.
Then the following hold.

1. The members of $\mathcal{F}$ have a common [[eigenvector|0N6960W0BV]]: there is $v \ne 0$ with $T v \in F v$ for every $T \in \mathcal{F}$.
2. There is a flag $0 = V_0 \subset V_1 \subset \dots \subset V_n = V$ with $\dim V_i = i$, in which each $V_i$ is $T$-[[invariant|0ACX5QT8TE]] for every $T \in \mathcal{F}$.
3. There is a basis of $V$ in which the matrix of every $T \in \mathcal{F}$ is [[upper triangular|0N4XY7Z3NN]].

In particular, for every set of pairwise [[commuting matrices|8G3X8MPG7E]] in $M_n(\C)$ there is one invertible $P$ such that $P^{-1} A P$ is upper triangular for every $A$ in the set.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The members must commute, or at least satisfy a weaker condition such as the one in McCoy's theorem below.
Take $E_{12} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $E_{21} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
The eigenvectors of $E_{12}$ lie on the line $F e_1$ and those of $E_{21}$ on the line $F e_2$, so the two matrices have no common eigenvector, and part 1 fails.
They do not commute: $E_{12} E_{21} = \operatorname{diag}(1, 0)$ while $E_{21} E_{12} = \operatorname{diag}(0, 1)$.

Each characteristic polynomial must split.
Over $\R$, the rotation by a right angle commutes with $I$, but its characteristic polynomial $t^2 + 1$ has no real root, so it has no invariant line and no triangular form, by the [[triangularization theorem|0363JMQ42Y]].

### What it gives you

Let $A$ and $B$ be commuting matrices over $\C$, and let $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ and $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n$ be the diagonals of their common triangular forms.
Sums and products of upper triangular matrices are upper triangular, with the sums and products of the diagonal entries.
So $A + B$ has the eigenvalues $\alpha_i + \beta_i$, and $A B$ has the eigenvalues $\alpha_i \beta_i$, paired in this order.
For example, if $A$ and $B$ are commuting [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]] matrices, then all $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ are $0$, so $A + B$ is strictly upper triangular in the common basis and therefore nilpotent.
Commuting diagonalizable matrices give even more, since they are [[simultaneously diagonalizable|0FK17N4DKA]].

### The converse

The converse is false.
The matrices $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ are already upper triangular in the standard basis, but they do not commute, as the products $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ show.
The exact condition is McCoy's theorem.
Over an algebraically closed field, matrices $A$ and $B$ are simultaneously triangularizable if and only if $f(A, B) (A B - B A)$ is nilpotent for every polynomial $f$ in two noncommuting variables.

### Generalizations

For a solvable Lie algebra of matrices over $\C$, a common invariant flag exists by [[Lie's theorem|11K6SVNXMH]].
For a Lie algebra of nilpotent matrices, [[Engel's theorem|V0ZWMA3FTH]] gives a common flag in which every member is strictly upper triangular.
Both belong to Lie theory and are only named here.

## History

The theorem for commuting matrices is standard in textbooks, for example in Section 6.5 of Hoffman and Kunze.
The criterion for pairs of matrices that need not commute carries the name of McCoy.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two matrices that commute can be made triangular by the same change of basis, as long as you work over the complex numbers.
The heart of the matter is a direction that both matrices only stretch.
Once you have it, you put it first in the basis and repeat the argument in one dimension less.
For example, any matrix and its square commute, and both only stretch the eigenvectors of the matrix.

### L2 human

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = A^2$.
Every matrix commutes with its own square, and direct multiplication gives
$$B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - 1 & 1 + 3 \\ -1 - 3 & -1 + 9 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 4 \\ -4 & 8 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The matrix $A$ has $p_A(t) = (t - 2)^2$, and its eigenvectors are the multiples of $(1, 1)$.
Since $B(1, 1) = (4, 4)$, the vector $(1, 1)$ is a common eigenvector.
Extend it to the basis $(1, 1), (0, 1)$, so $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $Q^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Multiply out:
$$Q^{-1} A Q = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q^{-1} B Q = Q^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 4 & 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, as it must be since $B = A^2$.
The typical use is to prove facts about commuting matrices, such as the nilpotence of a sum of commuting nilpotent matrices.
The trap is to apply the result to matrices that do not commute, such as $E_{12}$ and $E_{21}$.

### L3 human

In the language of algebras, the theorem says that a commutative subalgebra of $M_n(\C)$ is conjugate to a subalgebra of the upper triangular matrices.
The common eigenvector of part 1 is a common weight: a linear form $\alpha$ on the span of $\mathcal{F}$ with $T v = \alpha(T) v$ for all $T$.
Lie's theorem replaces commutativity by solvability, and the proof of the existence of a weight becomes harder.
The sub-area on canonical forms studies the centralizer of one matrix, the set of all matrices that commute with it.
