## Statement

Let a system in internal [[equilibrium|5EKTEE00FZ]] undergo an [[isothermal process|C9MM7GFRWN]] at [[thermodynamic temperature|AJ8M9MGZGF]] $T$ between two equilibrium states.
Let $\Delta S(T)$ be the change of its [[entropy|SPY89W0RJ7]] in this process.
Then $\Delta S(T) \to 0$ as $T \to 0^+$.

## Remarks

Walther Nernst first stated it for chemical reactions between condensed phases.
In that form it says that the changes of [[Gibbs free energy|RBG5ZBM35Y]] and [[enthalpy|722RZK72P5]] of the reaction satisfy $\Delta G - \Delta H = -T \, \Delta S \to 0$ faster than $T$.
So the curves of $\Delta G$ and $\Delta H$ against $T$ meet at $T = 0$ with a common horizontal tangent.
The theorem is a postulate supported by experiment, and quantum statistical mechanics explains it when the ground state is not degenerate.
It fails for systems that are not in internal equilibrium, such as glasses, which keep a residual entropy.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Near absolute zero, changing a system at constant temperature changes its entropy less and less.
At absolute zero all states of the system have the same entropy.

### L2 human

The Nernst heat theorem is one form of the third law of thermodynamics.
It allows absolute values of entropy to be assigned, so that equilibrium constants of reactions can be computed from heat measurements alone.
It also implies that heat capacities tend to $0$ as $T \to 0^+$.
