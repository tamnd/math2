## Example

Let a point mass $M$, the lens, lie at distance $D_L$ from an observer.
Let a point source lie at distance $D_S > D_L$ exactly behind it on the same line.
Let $D_{LS} = D_S - D_L$ be the distance from lens to source, and work with small angles.
By symmetry, the image of the source is a circle around the lens, the *Einstein ring*, of angular radius
$$\theta_E = \sqrt{\frac{4 G M}{c^2} \, \frac{D_{LS}}{D_L D_S}}.$$
Check: a ray seen at angle $\theta$ from the lens passes it at impact parameter $b = D_L \theta$.
By the [[gravitational deflection of light|JJCKCYTBXD]], it is bent by the angle $\alpha = 4GM/(c^2 b)$.
For the ray to reach the source, which sits at angle $0$, the geometry of small angles requires $\theta D_S = \alpha D_{LS}$.
So $\theta D_S = 4GM D_{LS}/(c^2 D_L \theta)$, which gives $\theta^2 = \theta_E^2$.

## Remarks

If the source is at a small angle $\beta \ne 0$ from the lens, the same geometry gives the lens equation $\beta = \theta - \theta_E^2/\theta$, which has two solutions: the ring breaks into two images.
For cosmological distances, $D_L$, $D_S$ and $D_{LS}$ must be taken as angular diameter distances, and then $D_{LS} \ne D_S - D_L$ in general.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When a distant galaxy, a closer galaxy and the Earth are perfectly lined up, light from the far galaxy bends around the near one on all sides at once.
We then see the far galaxy smeared into a ring of light around the near one.
The size of the ring tells us the mass of the lensing galaxy.
Many nearly complete Einstein rings have been photographed with large telescopes.
