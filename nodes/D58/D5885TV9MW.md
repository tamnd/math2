## Example

Let $A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $A_2 = (3)$ and
$$M = \operatorname{diag}(A_1, A_2) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. By the [[block multiplication rule|WR3RCF5PSQ]], $M^2 = \operatorname{diag}(A_1^2, A_2^2)$, because the off-diagonal blocks of the product are $A_1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot A_2 = 0$.
2. $A_1^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $A_2^2 = (9)$, so $M^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. By induction, $A_1^m = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & m \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so $M^m = \operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & m \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, (3^m)\right)$.
4. $A_1^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $A_2^{-1} = (1/3)$. Put $N = \operatorname{diag}(A_1^{-1}, A_2^{-1})$.
5. $MN = \operatorname{diag}(A_1 A_1^{-1}, A_2 A_2^{-1}) = \operatorname{diag}(I_2, 1) = I_3$, and in the same way $NM = I_3$.
6. So $M$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] and $M^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1/3 \end{pmatrix}$.

## Remarks

The matrix $M$ is not diagonal, and the block $A_1$ cannot be diagonalized.
Even so, the block structure reduces each computation to a $2 \times 2$ and a $1 \times 1$ computation.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To square a block diagonal matrix, we square each block.
To invert it, we invert each block.

### L2 human

The two blocks act on the span of $e_1, e_2$ and on the span of $e_3$, and they do not mix these.
So each power or inverse of $M$ also keeps the two parts apart.
