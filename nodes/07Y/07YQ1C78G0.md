## Summary

Let $F$ be a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]] and let $V$ be a [[vector space|000AYPA9AJ]] over $F$.
A linear form on $V$ is a [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] $f \colon V \to F$, where $F$ is a vector space over itself.
So $f(u + v) = f(u) + f(v)$ and $f(av) = a f(v)$ for all $u, v \in V$ and all $a \in F$.
Linear forms are the simplest linear maps, and every linear equation, every coordinate and every linear measurement is one of them.

## Motivation

The left side of a linear equation $2x - 3y + z = 5$ is a rule that takes a vector $(x, y, z)$ and returns one number.
This rule respects sums and scalar multiples.
Many other rules have the same shape: the $i$-th coordinate in a basis, the trace of a matrix, the value of a polynomial at a point, and the integral of a function.
The idea of a linear form collects all of them into one notion.
The set of all linear forms on $V$ is again a vector space, the [[dual space|07JBSZE0V4]], and its study is the theory of duality.

## Notation

Linear forms are also called linear functionals, covectors or one-forms.
"Functional" is common in analysis, and "covector" is common in physics and differential geometry.
Letters such as $f$, $g$, $\varphi$, $\psi$ and $\lambda$ are usual.
The value $f(v)$ is often written $\langle f, v \rangle$, to stress that it pairs a form and a vector.
On $F^n$, a linear form is often written as a row vector $(c_1, \dots, c_n)$, which acts on a column vector $x$ by the matrix product.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[Row vectors as linear forms on column vectors|~455e6319]]: each row vector $c$ gives the form $x \mapsto cx$ on $F^n$, and every linear form on $F^n$ arises in this way.
- [[Evaluation and integration on polynomials|~6fee40fb]]: the maps $p \mapsto p(2)$ and $p \mapsto \int_0^1 p(t)\,dt$ are linear forms on polynomials.
- The coordinate forms: for a [[basis|0KCQ9Y3QKA]] $b_1, \dots, b_n$ of $V$, the map that sends $v$ to its $i$-th [[coordinate|04CYQHN6MC]] is linear. These forms make up the [[dual basis|0ZD2CF6ZS0]].
- The [[trace|0XC1RDBY7A]] $\tr \colon M_n(F) \to F$ is a linear form, because $\tr(A + B) = \tr A + \tr B$ and $\tr(cA) = c \tr A$.
- On the space of convergent real sequences, the limit $(x_k) \mapsto \lim_{k \to \infty} x_k$ is a linear form, by the sum and product rules for limits.
- The zero map $V \to F$ is a linear form. It is the only linear form whose [[kernel|0S23E6YE83]] is all of $V$.

### Non-examples

- [[The determinant is not a linear form|~15c53a14]] on $2 \times 2$ matrices: homogeneity fails, since $\det(2I) = 4 \ne 2 = 2 \det I$.
- The map $(x, y) \mapsto x + y + 1$ on $\R^2$ is not a linear form: it sends $(0, 0)$ to $1$, and a linear map sends $0$ to $0$.
- Complex conjugation $z \mapsto \bar z$ on $\C$, as a vector space over $\C$, is additive but not homogeneous: $\overline{i \cdot 1} = -i \ne i = i \cdot \bar 1$.
- The map $(x, y) \mapsto xy$ on $\R^2$ is not additive: it sends $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ to $0$, but their sum $(1, 1)$ to $1$.

### Basic properties

- Every linear form satisfies $f(0) = 0$ and $f(-v) = -f(v)$, as every linear map does.
- A linear form is determined by its values on a basis, and these values can be chosen freely, by the [[theorem on bases|0R8X9XA83A]].
- On $F^n$, every linear form is $f(x) = c_1 x_1 + \dots + c_n x_n$ with $c_i = f(e_i)$. The proof is in the [[row vector example|~455e6319]].
- A nonzero linear form is surjective onto $F$: if $f(v) = a \ne 0$, then $f(a^{-1} b v) = b$ for every $b \in F$.
- The kernel of a nonzero linear form is a hyperplane, and two forms with the same kernel are proportional, by the [[theorem on hyperplanes|~577d8228]].
- The linear forms on $V$ form the vector space $V^* = \Hom(V, F)$, the [[dual space|07JBSZE0V4]].

### History

Linear forms in finitely many variables, $a_1 x_1 + \dots + a_n x_n$, appear in the study of linear equations long before abstract vector spaces.
Peano gave an axiomatic definition of vector spaces and linear maps in 1888.
The name "functional" comes from analysis, where the vectors are functions, and a functional takes a function to a number.

### Common errors

- Thinking that an affine function such as $x \mapsto 3x + 1$ is a linear form. In linear algebra, "linear" requires $f(0) = 0$.
- Mixing up a linear form with a vector. On $\R^n$, both are lists of $n$ numbers, but a form acts on vectors, and its list depends on the basis in a different way (see the [[change of dual basis|~6d879f22]]).
- Forgetting that a linear form on an infinite-dimensional space need not be a finite combination of coordinate forms. The [[sum of coordinates on F^(N)|~d17873c6]] is an example.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear form is a rule that takes a vector and returns one number, in a way that respects sums and scaling.
For example, the rule "take $2$ times the first coordinate minus $3$ times the second" is a linear form on the plane.
It sends $(1, 1)$ to $2 - 3 = -1$ and $(3, 2)$ to $6 - 6 = 0$.
If we double the vector, the number doubles.
If we add two vectors, the numbers add.
A price list is a good picture: a shopping basket is a vector of quantities, and the total cost is a linear form of the basket.

### L2 human

We check that $f(p) = p(1) + p'(0)$ is a linear form on the space $P_2(\R)$ of real polynomials of degree at most $2$, and we write it in coordinates.

1. Additivity: $(p + q)(1) + (p + q)'(0) = p(1) + q(1) + p'(0) + q'(0) = f(p) + f(q)$, because evaluation and the derivative respect sums.
2. Homogeneity: $(cp)(1) + (cp)'(0) = c\,p(1) + c\,p'(0) = c f(p)$ for every real $c$.
3. On the basis $1, x, x^2$, we get $f(1) = 1 + 0 = 1$, $f(x) = 1 + 1 = 2$ and $f(x^2) = 1 + 0 = 1$.
4. So $f(a + bx + cx^2) = a + 2b + c$. In coordinates, $f$ is the row vector $(1, 2, 1)$.
5. Check with $p = 3 - x + 4x^2$: directly, $p(1) = 6$ and $p'(0) = -1$, so $f(p) = 5$. In coordinates, $3 - 2 + 4 = 5$.

The typical use is to describe a subspace as the solution set of linear equations $f_1(v) = \dots = f_k(v) = 0$.
A trap: the row vector of a form depends on the basis, and it does not change in the same way as the coordinates of a vector.

### L3 human

A linear form is a morphism $V \to F$ in the category of vector spaces over $F$, and $F$ is the unit object for the tensor product.
So linear forms are the "co" side of vectors: vectors are maps $F \to V$, and forms are maps $V \to F$.
In differential geometry, a one-form assigns a linear form on each tangent space, and in physics the components of covectors carry lower indices.
In functional analysis, the linear forms that are continuous for a norm form the [[continuous dual space|4A82S5FVRT]], which is in general smaller than the algebraic dual studied here.
Linear forms reappear in this area as rows of matrices, as the bilinear pairing $V^* \times V \to F$, and through the [[Riesz representation theorem|04GYPXDXNH]] as inner products with a fixed vector.
