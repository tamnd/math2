## Summary

A linear form is a rule that turns each vector into one number and respects sums and scalar multiples.
The left side of a linear equation, a coordinate in a basis and the value of a polynomial at a point are all linear forms, so the idea is everywhere in linear algebra.
Let $F$ be a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]] and let $V$ be a [[vector space|000AYPA9AJ]] over $F$.
A *linear form* on $V$ is a [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] $f \colon V \to F$, where $F$ counts as a vector space over itself.
So $f$ satisfies
$$f(u + v) = f(u) + f(v), \qquad f(av) = a f(v)$$
for all $u, v \in V$ and all $a \in F$.
The [[formal definition|0RT96J4D3H]] states this in one place.

## Motivation

Look at the equation $2x - 3y + z = 5$.
Its left side is a machine that takes a vector $(x, y, z)$ and returns one number, and doubling the vector doubles the number.
Many other machines have the same shape: the $i$-th coordinate in a basis, the trace of a matrix, the value of a polynomial at a point, and the integral of a function over an interval.
The notion of a linear form puts all of them under one name, so that one theory covers them all.

The linear forms on $V$ can be added and scaled, and they form a new vector space, the [[dual space|07JBSZE0V4]].
The rest of the sub-area on duality studies that space: its bases, its dimension, the forms that vanish on a subspace, and the way a linear map acts on forms.
In physics a covector such as a gradient is a linear form, and in analysis a measurement such as "the average value on $[0, 1]$" is one.

## Notation

Books also say *linear functional*, *covector* or *one-form*.
"Functional" is common in analysis, and "covector" is common in physics and differential geometry.
Letters such as $f$, $g$, $\varphi$, $\psi$ and $\lambda$ are usual.
The value $f(v)$ is often written $\langle f, v \rangle$, to stress that a form and a vector are paired to give a number.
On $F^n$ a linear form is usually written as a row vector $(c_1, \dots, c_n)$ that acts on a column vector $x$ by the matrix product.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take the form $f(x, y, z) = 2x - 3y + z$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$, and the vectors $u = (1, 4, 2)$ and $v = (0, 1, 3)$.
Evaluate each one:
$$f(u) = 2 - 12 + 2 = -8, \qquad f(v) = 0 - 3 + 3 = 0.$$
Now evaluate the sum $u + v = (1, 5, 5)$:
$$f(u + v) = 2 - 15 + 5 = -8 = f(u) + f(v).$$
Check: $f(3u) = f(3, 12, 6) = 6 - 36 + 6 = -24 = 3 f(u)$.
The numbers $2, -3, 1$ are the values of $f$ on the standard basis vectors, and the [[row vector example|EGJXHX96R9]] shows that every linear form on $F^n$ is a row like this, for exactly one row.

### Forms you already know

On the space $P_2(\mathbb{R})$ of polynomials of degree at most $2$, the [[evaluation at 2 and the integral over [0, 1]|CMAHB6NY50]] are linear forms.
On the basis $1, x, x^2$ the first takes the values $1, 2, 4$, and the second takes the values
$$\int_0^1 1 \, dt = 1, \qquad \int_0^1 t \, dt = \tfrac12, \qquad \int_0^1 t^2 \, dt = \tfrac13.$$
So for $p = 1 - 3x + 2x^2$ we get $p(2) = 1 - 6 + 8 = 3$ and $\int_0^1 p = 1 - \tfrac32 + \tfrac23 = \tfrac16$.

The [[trace|0XC1RDBY7A]] is a linear form on $n \times n$ matrices, since $\tr(A + B) = \tr A + \tr B$ and $\tr(cA) = c \tr A$.
For example, $\tr \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = 1 + 4 = 5$.
For a [[basis|0KCQ9Y3QKA]] $b_1, \dots, b_n$ of $V$, the map that sends $v$ to its $i$-th [[coordinate|04CYQHN6MC]] is a linear form, and these $n$ forms make up the [[dual basis|0ZD2CF6ZS0]].
On the space of convergent real sequences, the limit $(x_k) \mapsto \lim_{k \to \infty} x_k$ is a linear form, by the sum and product rules for limits.
The zero map $V \to F$ is a linear form too, and it is the only one whose [[kernel|0S23E6YE83]] is all of $V$.

### What is not a linear form

The [[determinant is not a linear form|AA0NDX7JXV]] on $2 \times 2$ matrices, although it returns one number.
Scaling fails, since $\det(2I) = 4$ while $2 \det I = 2$.

The map $(x, y) \mapsto x + y + 1$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ fails because it sends $(0, 0)$ to $1$, and every linear map sends $0$ to $0$.
Such affine maps are close relatives, but the [[affine functions on the plane|FWNY6EYBC3]] form a bigger space than the linear forms.
The map $(x, y) \mapsto xy$ fails additivity: it sends $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ to $0$, but their sum $(1, 1)$ to $1$.

Complex conjugation $z \mapsto \bar z$ on $\mathbb{C}$, as a space over $\mathbb{C}$, is additive but not homogeneous.
With $a = i$ and $z = 1$ we get $\overline{i \cdot 1} = -i$, while $i \cdot \bar 1 = i$.

### The rules you can use

Every linear form has $f(0) = 0$ and $f(-v) = -f(v)$, as every linear map does.
A linear form is fixed by its values on a basis, and these values can be chosen freely, by the [[theorem on bases|0R8X9XA83A]].
On $F^n$ this means $f(x) = c_1 x_1 + \dots + c_n x_n$ with $c_i = f(e_i)$.

A nonzero form takes every value in $F$.
Indeed, if $f(v) = a \ne 0$, then for any $b \in F$ linearity gives $f(a^{-1} b v) = a^{-1} b \, a = b$.
So the image is $F$, and in finite dimension $n$ the kernel has dimension $n - 1$.
That kernel is a hyperplane, and two forms with the same kernel are proportional, by the [[theorem on hyperplanes|XR0EQN0H8T]].

### Watch out

An affine function such as $x \mapsto 3x + 1$ is called "linear" in school, but it is not a linear form, because it sends $0$ to $1$.
In linear algebra, "linear" always includes $f(0) = 0$.

A form on $\mathbb{R}^n$ and a vector of $\mathbb{R}^n$ are both lists of $n$ numbers, but they change in different ways when the basis changes.
For example, with the basis $b_1 = (2, 0)$, $b_2 = (0, 1)$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$, the vector $(2, 0)$ gets coordinates $(1, 0)$, while the form $x \mapsto x_1$ gets the row $(f(b_1), f(b_2)) = (2, 0)$.
The rule for forms is the [[change of dual basis|SXWX10HSB6]].

In infinite dimension, a linear form need not be a finite combination of coordinate forms.
The [[sum of all coordinates on finitely supported sequences|JCMJDY59CH]] is a form that is not such a combination.

### History

Linear expressions $a_1 x_1 + \dots + a_n x_n$ were studied with systems of linear equations long before abstract vector spaces existed.
Peano gave an axiomatic definition of a vector space, together with linear maps, in 1888.
The word "functional" comes from analysis, where the vectors are functions and a functional sends a function to a number.

### Try it

First, decide whether $g(x, y, z) = 2x - z + 3$ is a linear form on $\mathbb{R}^3$.
It is not, since $g(0, 0, 0) = 3 \ne 0$.

Second, find the row of the form $f(p) = p(1) - p(-1)$ on $P_2(\mathbb{R})$ in the basis $1, x, x^2$.
The values are $f(1) = 0$, $f(x) = 2$ and $f(x^2) = 0$, so the row is $(0, 2, 0)$, and $f(a + bx + cx^2) = 2b$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear form is a rule that takes a vector and gives back one number, in a way that respects adding and scaling.
For example, "take $2$ times the first entry minus $3$ times the second" is a linear form on the plane.
It sends $(1, 1)$ to $2 - 3 = -1$ and $(3, 2)$ to $6 - 6 = 0$.
If you double the vector, the number doubles, and if you add two vectors, the numbers add.
A price list is a good picture.
A shopping basket is a vector of quantities, and the total cost of the basket is a linear form of it.

### L2 human

Here is a typical task: show that $f(p) = p(1) + p'(0)$ is a linear form on $P_2(\mathbb{R})$, and write it as a row.
Additivity holds because evaluation and differentiation both respect sums:
$$f(p + q) = p(1) + q(1) + p'(0) + q'(0) = f(p) + f(q).$$
Scaling works the same way, since $(cp)(1) = c \, p(1)$ and $(cp)'(0) = c \, p'(0)$.

Next we evaluate $f$ on the basis $1, x, x^2$.
The derivatives are $0$, $1$ and $2x$, so
$$f(1) = 1 + 0 = 1, \qquad f(x) = 1 + 1 = 2, \qquad f(x^2) = 1 + 0 = 1.$$
So $f(a + bx + cx^2) = a + 2b + c$, and the row of $f$ is $(1, 2, 1)$.
Check with $p = 3 - x + 4x^2$: directly $p(1) = 6$ and $p'(0) = -1$, so $f(p) = 5$, and the row gives $3 - 2 + 4 = 5$.
This is the typical use of linear forms: once a form is a row, every value is a short multiply-and-add.
Watch out: the row belongs to the basis, so a different basis gives a different row for the same form.

### L3 human

A linear form is a morphism $V \to F$ in the category of vector spaces over $F$, where $F$ is the unit for the tensor product.
Vectors of $V$ are the same as linear maps $F \to V$, so forms are the arrows that go the other way.
In differential geometry a one-form chooses a linear form on each tangent space, and in physics the components of a covector carry lower indices.
In functional analysis one keeps only the forms that are continuous for a norm, and these make up the [[continuous dual space|4A82S5FVRT]], which is in general smaller than the algebraic dual.
Later in the area, linear forms return as the rows of matrices, as the pairing $V^* \times V \to F$, and, through the [[Riesz representation theorem|04GYPXDXNH]], as inner products with one fixed vector.
