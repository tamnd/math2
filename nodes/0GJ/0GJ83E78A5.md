## Statement

Let $G$ and $H$ be [[groups|G5S1M9]], and let $N$ be a [[normal subgroup|0CB0DX5BVV]] of $G$.
Let $G/N$ be the [[quotient group|0KYX2N69J0]], and let $\pi \colon G \to G/N$ be the projection $\pi(g) = gN$.
Let $\varphi \colon G \to H$ be a [[group homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]] with $N \subseteq \ker \varphi$, where $\ker \varphi$ is the [[kernel|0W88CEGQP1]] of $\varphi$.
Then the following hold.

1. There is exactly one group homomorphism $\bar{\varphi} \colon G/N \to H$ with $\bar{\varphi} \circ \pi = \varphi$. It is given by $\bar{\varphi}(gN) = \varphi(g)$.
2. The [[image|0S21GNW9ZB]] of $\bar{\varphi}$ is the image of $\varphi$.
3. The kernel of $\bar{\varphi}$ is $(\ker \varphi)/N = \{kN : k \in \ker \varphi\}$.

Also, the map $\psi \mapsto \psi \circ \pi$ is a bijection from the set of homomorphisms $G/N \to H$ to the set of homomorphisms $\varphi \colon G \to H$ with $N \subseteq \ker \varphi$.

The proof is in [[the proof of the universal property of the quotient group|CXPNXCC5Y8]].

## Remarks

We say that $\varphi$ factors through $\pi$, and that $\bar{\varphi}$ is the map induced by $\varphi$.
The result is also called the factor theorem.
In a picture, the triangle with the maps $\pi \colon G \to G/N$, $\bar{\varphi} \colon G/N \to H$ and $\varphi \colon G \to H$ commutes.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The condition $N \subseteq \ker \varphi$ is needed. Take $G = H = \mathbb{Z}$, $N = 2\mathbb{Z}$ and $\varphi$ the identity map. Then $\pi(0) = \pi(2)$ but $\varphi(0) \ne \varphi(2)$, so no map $\bar{\varphi}$ with $\bar{\varphi} \circ \pi = \varphi$ exists.
- The condition that $\varphi$ is a homomorphism is needed. Define $\varphi \colon \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ by $\varphi(g) = 0$ for even $g$ and $\varphi(g) = 1$ for odd $g$. This map is constant on the cosets of $2\mathbb{Z}$, so it gives a map $\bar{\varphi}$ on $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, but $\bar{\varphi}(1 + 2\mathbb{Z}) + \bar{\varphi}(1 + 2\mathbb{Z}) = 2 \ne 0 = \bar{\varphi}(2 + 2\mathbb{Z})$.
- The condition that $N$ is normal is needed for $G/N$ to be a group. For a subgroup that is not normal, the product of cosets is not defined, as [[this example|N5NTPAR2KT]] shows.

### Consequences

- With $N = \ker \varphi$, part 3 gives a trivial kernel, and the [[first isomorphism theorem|0CXXJNG5B6]] follows.
- The homomorphisms from $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ to a group $H$ correspond to the elements $h \in H$ with $h^n = e$. Each homomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to H$ has the form $k \mapsto h^k$, and its kernel contains $n\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $h^n = e$.
- For positive integers $m$ and $n$, the formula $a + n\mathbb{Z} \mapsto a + m\mathbb{Z}$ defines a homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $m$ divides $n$, because the kernel of reduction modulo $m$ is $m\mathbb{Z}$, and $n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq m\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $m$ divides $n$.
- The [[universal property of the abelianisation|0EJ4PC8NJ6]] is the special case where $N$ is the commutator subgroup and $H$ is abelian.
- The proof of the [[third isomorphism theorem|0TJ1V9DDPC]] uses parts 1 to 3.

### Converse

The condition $N \subseteq \ker \varphi$ is also necessary.
If $\varphi = \psi \circ \pi$ for a homomorphism $\psi \colon G/N \to H$, then for each $n \in N$ we get $\varphi(n) = \psi(nN) = \psi(N) = e_H$, because $nN = N$ is the identity of $G/N$.
Thus a homomorphism factors through $\pi$ if and only if its kernel contains $N$.

### Generalizations

The same statement holds for rings and ideals, for modules and submodules, and for vector spaces and subspaces, with the same proof.
In [[category|A2DE66937D]] language, $\pi$ is the [[cokernel|H8W38933NC]] of the inclusion $N \to G$ in the [[category of groups|2CX7RPPXA9]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A quotient group $G/N$ is made by gluing together the elements of $G$ that differ by an element of $N$.
Suppose that a homomorphism sends every element of $N$ to the identity.
Then it cannot see the difference between two glued elements, so it already makes sense on the glued group.
For example, the map that sends an integer $k$ to the remainder of $2k$ on division by $4$ sends all multiples of $2$ to $0$.
So it gives a well defined map on the two classes "even" and "odd": even goes to $0$ and odd goes to $2$.

### L2 human

We count the homomorphisms from $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.

1. By the theorem, these homomorphisms correspond to the homomorphisms $\varphi \colon \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ with $6\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \ker \varphi$.
2. A homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$ is fixed by the value $h = \varphi(1)$, because $\varphi(k) = kh$ for each integer $k$.
3. The kernel contains $6\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $\varphi(6) = 6h = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.
4. We test the four values: $6 \cdot 0 = 0$, $6 \cdot 1 = 6 \equiv 2$, $6 \cdot 2 = 12 \equiv 0$ and $6 \cdot 3 = 18 \equiv 2$ modulo $4$.
5. Thus $h = 0$ and $h = 2$ work, and there are exactly two homomorphisms: the trivial one and $a + 6\mathbb{Z} \mapsto 2a + 4\mathbb{Z}$.

The typical use is to define a homomorphism on a quotient group.
We write a formula on representatives, and we check only that the formula sends $N$ to the identity.
The theorem then gives the well definedness and the homomorphism property together.

A trap is to define a map on $G/N$ by a formula on representatives without this check.
The formula $a + 6\mathbb{Z} \mapsto a + 4\mathbb{Z}$ is not well defined, because $0 + 6\mathbb{Z} = 6 + 6\mathbb{Z}$, but $0 + 4\mathbb{Z} \ne 6 + 4\mathbb{Z}$.

### L3 human

The theorem says that $\pi \colon G \to G/N$ is the universal homomorphism out of $G$ that kills $N$.
In the [[category of groups|2CX7RPPXA9]], this makes $\pi$ the coequaliser of the inclusion $N \to G$ and the trivial map $N \to G$.
Thus the quotient group is fixed up to a unique isomorphism by this property alone, without the construction by cosets.
Every algebraic theory has the same pattern: quotients of rings by ideals, of modules by submodules, and of general algebras by congruences.
For a subgroup $S$ of $G$ that is not normal, the universal homomorphism that kills $S$ still exists: it is the projection to $G/\langle\langle S \rangle\rangle$, where $\langle\langle S \rangle\rangle$ is the [[normal closure|~99dbea13]] of $S$.
This is the way that presentations of groups define a group by generators and relations.
