## Proof

We prove the [[theorem|473MBZST4D]] for $2n = 2$ and $2n = 4$ by computing both sides, and we outline the general case under Variants.

*Size 2.*

1. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & a \\ -a & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
   The only perfect matching of $\{1, 2\}$ is $\{\{1, 2\}\}$, and its permutation $\pi_M$ is the identity.
   So the [[definition|C9JJD2AVJR]] gives $\operatorname{pf}(A) = a$.
2. The formula for a $2 \times 2$ determinant gives
$$\det A = 0 \cdot 0 - a \cdot (-a) = a^2 = \operatorname{pf}(A)^2.$$

*Size 4.*

3. Write $a = a_{12}$, $b = a_{13}$, $c = a_{14}$, $d = a_{23}$, $e = a_{24}$ and $f = a_{34}$, so that
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & a & b & c \\ -a & 0 & d & e \\ -b & -d & 0 & f \\ -c & -e & -f & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
4. The three matchings $\{12, 34\}$, $\{13, 24\}$ and $\{14, 23\}$ have signs $+1$, $-1$ and $+1$ by the definition.
   So $\operatorname{pf}(A) = af - be + cd$, and we call this number $P$.
5. By the [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]] along row 1, whose first entry is $0$,
$$\det A = a\, C_{12} + b\, C_{13} + c\, C_{14},$$
   where $C_{1j} = (-1)^{1+j} M_{1j}$ are the [[cofactors|0W2QAVJQ1W]] and $M_{1j}$ is the determinant of $A$ without row 1 and column $j$.
6. Deleting column 2 leaves the rows $(-a, d, e)$, $(-b, 0, f)$ and $(-c, -f, 0)$.
   Expanding along the first row gives
$$M_{12} = -a(0 + f^2) - d(0 + cf) + e(bf - 0) = -f(af - be + cd) = -fP.$$
   So $C_{12} = -M_{12} = fP$.
7. Deleting column 3 leaves the rows $(-a, 0, e)$, $(-b, -d, f)$ and $(-c, -e, 0)$.
   Expanding along the first row gives
$$M_{13} = -a(0 + ef) - 0 + e(be - cd) = -e(af - be + cd) = -eP.$$
   So $C_{13} = M_{13} = -eP$.
8. Deleting column 4 leaves the rows $(-a, 0, d)$, $(-b, -d, 0)$ and $(-c, -e, -f)$.
   Expanding along the first row gives
$$M_{14} = -a(df - 0) - 0 + d(be - cd) = -d(af - be + cd) = -dP.$$
   So $C_{14} = -M_{14} = dP$.
9. Putting steps 5 to 8 together,
$$\det A = afP - beP + cdP = (af - be + cd) P = P^2.$$
   This proves the theorem for size 4.

## Idea

For size $4$, each cofactor in the first row turns out to be the Pfaffian times the entry of the complementary pair.
For example, the cofactor of $a = a_{12}$ is $f P$, and $f = a_{34}$ pairs the indices $3$ and $4$ that $a_{12}$ leaves out.
So the Laplace expansion along row 1 rebuilds the Pfaffian a second time, and the determinant becomes its square.

## Variants

The general case has a short proof once two facts are known.
The first is the rule $\operatorname{pf}(B A B^{\mathsf{T}}) = \det(B) \operatorname{pf}(A)$, which follows from the exterior algebra description $\omega^n = n! \operatorname{pf}(A)\, e_1 \wedge \dots \wedge e_{2n}$.
The second is the normal form over a field: every alternating matrix equals $B D B^{\mathsf{T}}$ for an invertible $B$, where $D$ has $r$ blocks $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ on its diagonal and zeros elsewhere.
If $r = n$, both sides of the theorem equal $(\det B)^2$, since $\operatorname{pf}(D) = \det D = 1$.
If $r < n$, then $D$ has a zero row, so $\operatorname{pf}(D) = \det D = 0$ and both sides are $0$.
Applied to the matrix of indeterminates over the field of fractions of $\mathbb{Z}[x_{ij}]$, this proves an identity of polynomials, which then holds over every commutative ring.

A second route expands the Pfaffian along its first row, $\operatorname{pf}(A) = \sum_{j=2}^{2n} (-1)^{j} a_{1j} \operatorname{pf}(A_{\hat 1 \hat j})$, where $A_{\hat 1 \hat j}$ deletes rows and columns $1$ and $j$.
For $n = 2$ this expansion is exactly the formula of step 4, and steps 5 to 9 compare it with the Laplace expansion of the determinant.
