## Example

Let $W, X, Y, Z$ be sets, and let $h \colon W \to X$, $g \colon X \to Y$ and $f \colon Y \to Z$ be [[functions|0EPAT51DXQ]].
The [[composition|0THBK9V825]] $f \circ g$ is the function $X \to Z$ with $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$.
We check that $(f \circ g) \circ h = f \circ (g \circ h)$.

1. Both sides are functions from $W$ to $Z$. The function $f \circ g$ goes from $X$ to $Z$, so $(f \circ g) \circ h$ goes from $W$ to $Z$. The function $g \circ h$ goes from $W$ to $Y$, so $f \circ (g \circ h)$ goes from $W$ to $Z$.
2. Let $w \in W$. By the definition of composition, $((f \circ g) \circ h)(w) = (f \circ g)(h(w)) = f(g(h(w)))$.
3. By the definition of composition, $(f \circ (g \circ h))(w) = f((g \circ h)(w)) = f(g(h(w)))$.
4. By steps 2 and 3, the two functions have the same value at each $w \in W$. Two functions with the same domain, the same codomain and the same values are equal. So $(f \circ g) \circ h = f \circ (g \circ h)$.

For a concrete case, take $W = X = Y = Z = \mathbb{R}$, the [[real numbers|08J2M1JYAG]], and $f(x) = x + 1$, $g(x) = 2x$, $h(x) = x^2$.

5. $(f \circ g)(x) = 2x + 1$, so $((f \circ g) \circ h)(x) = 2x^2 + 1$.
6. $(g \circ h)(x) = 2x^2$, so $(f \circ (g \circ h))(x) = 2x^2 + 1$.

The two results agree, as step 4 says.
In particular, for a set $X$, composition is an [[associative|0K8F7PVDMX]] operation on the set of all functions $X \to X$.

## Remarks

The proof does not use any property of $f$, $g$ and $h$.
Thus composition is associative for all functions, also for functions that are not injective or not surjective.
The same proof shows that composition of permutations, of linear maps and of the symmetries of a figure is associative.
This is the main reason that the associative law holds in so many groups: most groups act on a set, and their operation is composition.
Matrix multiplication is associative for the same reason, because the product of two matrices corresponds to the composition of two linear maps.
Composition is not [[commutative|099E72KTF8]]: for $f$ and $g$ above, $(f \circ g)(x) = 2x + 1$, but $(g \circ f)(x) = 2x + 2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of three machines in a row: $h$ first, then $g$, then $f$.
We can join the last two machines into one, or join the first two machines into one.
In both cases, the input goes through $h$, then through $g$, then through $f$.
So the result is the same.

### L2 human

To show that two functions are equal, show that they have the same value at each point of the domain.
Here both values are $f(g(h(w)))$, by the definition of composition used twice.
The typical use is a short proof of associativity for a group whose elements are maps, such as a group of permutations or of rotations.
A trap is to try to check associativity with formulas for each case, when the composition argument proves it for all cases at once.
