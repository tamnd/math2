## Statement

Let $A$ be an [[abelian group|082GHNPJ53]], written additively, and let $p$ be a [[prime number|01826KMG2H]].
Let $A[p] = \{a \in A : pa = 0\}$.
Each abelian group $H$ with $pH = 0$ is a [[vector space|000AYPA9AJ]] over the field $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.
We count each dimension in $\mathbb{N} \cup \{\infty\}$, and we write $\infty$ for every infinite dimension.
For $n \in \mathbb{N}$, the Szmielew invariants of $A$ are:

1. $U(p, n; A) = \dim_{\mathbb{F}_p} \big( (p^n A)[p] / (p^{n+1} A)[p] \big)$;
2. $D(p; A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \dim_{\mathbb{F}_p} (p^n A)[p]$;
3. $\mathrm{Tf}(p; A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \dim_{\mathbb{F}_p} \big( p^n A / p^{n+1} A \big)$;
4. $\mathrm{Exp}(A)$, the least integer $m \ge 1$ with $mA = 0$, or $\infty$ when there is no such $m$.

The two limits exist, because the two sequences are non-increasing.
For the first sequence, $(p^{n+1} A)[p] \subseteq (p^n A)[p]$.
For the second sequence, the map $x + p^{n+1}A \mapsto px + p^{n+2}A$ is a surjective linear map from $p^n A / p^{n+1} A$ onto $p^{n+1} A / p^{n+2} A$.

**Theorem (Szmielew).**

1. Two abelian groups $A$ and $B$ are [[elementarily equivalent|P3B1V182N4]] if and only if $\mathrm{Exp}(A) = \mathrm{Exp}(B)$ and, for every prime $p$ and every $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $U(p, n; A) = U(p, n; B)$, $D(p; A) = D(p; B)$ and $\mathrm{Tf}(p; A) = \mathrm{Tf}(p; B)$.
2. The [[elementary theory|RX9MB386C5]] of the class of abelian groups is [[decidable|HWC687ES0Q]].


## Remarks

### The invariants of some groups

1. For $k \ge 1$, the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}$ has $U(p, k-1) = 1$, all other invariants at $p$ equal to $0$, and $\mathrm{Exp} = p^k$. At a prime $q \ne p$, multiplication by $q$ is a bijection of this group, so all invariants at $q$ are $0$.
2. The [[Prüfer group|F0XAMM2PPS]] $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ has $D(p) = 1$, all other invariants at all primes equal to $0$, and $\mathrm{Exp} = \infty$.
3. The group $\mathbb{Z}$ has $\mathrm{Tf}(p) = 1$ for every prime $p$, because $p^n \mathbb{Z} / p^{n+1} \mathbb{Z}$ has $p$ elements. All $U$ and $D$ invariants are $0$, because $\mathbb{Z}[p] = 0$, and $\mathrm{Exp} = \infty$.
4. The group $\mathbb{Q}$ has all $U$, $D$ and $\mathrm{Tf}$ invariants equal to $0$, because $p\mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Q}[p] = 0$, and $\mathrm{Exp} = \infty$.
5. The trivial group has all invariants $0$ and $\mathrm{Exp} = 1$.
6. The invariants of a finite direct sum are the sums of the invariants of the summands, except $\mathrm{Exp}$, which is the least common multiple, with $\infty$ when one summand has $\mathrm{Exp} = \infty$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The hypothesis that the groups are abelian is needed for part 2: the elementary theory of all groups is undecidable, by a theorem of Tarski.
Each family of invariants is needed in part 1.

1. Without $\mathrm{Exp}$: the trivial group and $\mathbb{Q}$ have all $U$, $D$ and $\mathrm{Tf}$ invariants $0$. They are not elementarily equivalent, because $\exists x \, (x \ne 0)$ separates them.
2. Without $D$: the groups $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ differ only in $D(p)$. The sentence $\exists x \, (x \ne 0 \wedge px = 0)$ separates them.
3. Without $\mathrm{Tf}$: the groups $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ differ only in the $\mathrm{Tf}$ invariants. The sentence $\forall x \exists y \, (x = py)$ separates them.
4. Without $U$: the groups $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/p^2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Q}$ differ only in $U(p, 0)$ and $U(p, 1)$. The sentence $\exists x \, (x \ne 0 \wedge px = 0 \wedge \forall y \, (py \ne x))$ is true in the first group and false in the second.

The convention that every infinite dimension counts as $\infty$ is also needed.
The direct sums of countably many and of uncountably many copies of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ are elementarily equivalent, but their dimensions over $\mathbb{F}_p$ are different infinite cardinals.

### Consequences

1. Two nontrivial [[torsion-free|PEMNN32A43]] abelian groups $A$ and $B$ are elementarily equivalent if and only if $\dim_{\mathbb{F}_p} A/pA = \dim_{\mathbb{F}_p} B/pB$ for every prime $p$. For such groups $A[p] = 0$, $\mathrm{Exp} = \infty$, and $a \mapsto p^n a$ induces an isomorphism $A/pA \cong p^n A / p^{n+1} A$.
2. So $(\mathbb{Q}, +) \equiv (\mathbb{R}, +)$, and $\mathbb{Z} \equiv \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Q}$, but $\mathbb{Z} \not\equiv \mathbb{Z}^2$. In none of these pairs are the two groups isomorphic.
3. There are $2^{\aleph_0}$ complete theories of abelian groups. For a set $S$ of primes, let $A_S \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$ be the fractions whose denominators have no prime factor in $S$. Then $A_S / pA_S$ has $p$ elements for $p \in S$ and one element for $p \notin S$.
4. The theory of a single abelian group $A$ is determined by countably many values in $\mathbb{N} \cup \{\infty\}$.

### Converse

Part 1 is an equivalence.
The direction "elementarily equivalent groups have the same invariants" is the easy one: for each value $k$, the statement "the invariant is at least $k$" is expressed by first-order sentences.
The invariants do not classify abelian groups up to isomorphism, as consequence 2 shows.
For countable abelian $p$-groups without nonzero divisible subgroups, [[Ulm's theorem|D8A4VEQR98]] gives a classification up to isomorphism by other invariants.

### Generalizations

Eklof and Fisher gave a second proof in 1972 with saturated models.
For [[modules over a ring|0NZ8JA25WK]], the theorem of Baur and Monk reduces each formula to a Boolean combination of positive primitive formulas and of sentences that bound the index of one definable subgroup in another.
Martin Ziegler developed from this a general model theory of modules.
For abelian groups, which are the modules over $\mathbb{Z}$, these results give back Szmielew's theorem.

## History

Wanda Szmielew wrote her doctoral thesis on the elementary theory of abelian groups at Berkeley in 1950, under Alfred Tarski.
She published the result in 1955 in the paper "Elementary properties of Abelian groups", Fundamenta Mathematicae 41, pages 203 to 271.
She was the first to prove that this theory is decidable, and her decision procedure uses quantifier elimination.
The notation $U$, $D$, $\mathrm{Tf}$ and $\mathrm{Exp}$ for the invariants is the notation of later accounts.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two groups can satisfy exactly the same sentences of the language of groups, even when they are different groups.
For example, the fractions and the real numbers, both with addition, cannot be told apart by any such sentence.
Szmielew found, for each abelian group, a list of numbers that decides exactly which sentences are true in it.
The numbers count, for each prime $p$, the pieces of different kinds that the group contains.
For example, the clock group with $4$ hours and the group of pairs of bits with addition both have $4$ elements.
The sentence "some element added to itself is not $0$" is true for the clock group with $4$ hours, since $1 + 1 = 2$.
It is false for the pairs of bits, and the list of numbers of Szmielew shows the same difference.

### L2 human

We compute the invariants of $A = \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and $B = \mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Q}$, and we conclude that $A \equiv B$.

1. Take $p = 2$. The elements $a$ of $A$ with $2a = 0$ are $(0, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, so $\dim A[2] = 1$.
2. We have $2A = 2\mathbb{Z} \oplus 0$, so $(2^n A)[2] = 0$ for $n \ge 1$. Thus $U(2, 0; A) = 1$, $U(2, n; A) = 0$ for $n \ge 1$, and $D(2; A) = 0$.
3. For $n \ge 1$, $2^n A / 2^{n+1} A \cong 2^n \mathbb{Z} / 2^{n+1} \mathbb{Z}$ has $2$ elements. So $\mathrm{Tf}(2; A) = 1$.
4. Take an odd prime $p$. Then $A[p] = 0$, and $p^n A / p^{n+1} A \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for $n \ge 0$, since multiplication by $p$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. So $U(p, n; A) = D(p; A) = 0$ and $\mathrm{Tf}(p; A) = 1$.
5. The element $(1, 0)$ has infinite order, so $\mathrm{Exp}(A) = \infty$.
6. By items 4 and 6 of the list of invariants above, $\mathbb{Q}$ adds $0$ to each $U$, $D$ and $\mathrm{Tf}$ invariant, and $\mathrm{Exp}(B) = \infty$. So $A$ and $B$ have the same invariants, and $A \equiv B$ by the theorem.
7. The groups are not isomorphic: $B$ has the nonzero element $(0, 0, 1)$ that is divisible by every $2^k$, and in $A$ only $(0, 0)$ is divisible by every $2^k$.

The typical use is the one above: compute the invariants, compare them, and conclude elementary equivalence without a check of infinitely many sentences.
Step 7 also shows that "some nonzero element is divisible by every power of $2$" is not a first-order property.
A trap is to compute $\mathrm{Tf}(2; A)$ as $\dim A/2A$.
Here $A/2A$ has dimension $2$, because the torsion part also contributes at $n = 0$, and only the limit gives $\mathrm{Tf}(2; A) = 1$.

### L3 human

Abelian groups are the modules over $\mathbb{Z}$, and the model theory of modules grows out of Szmielew's theorem.
For any ring, the definable subgroups given by positive primitive formulas and the indices between them control the complete theories of modules, and every complete theory of modules is [[stable|T7JW5QY6KR]].
The theorem also shows a contrast between abelian and general groups: one extra axiom, commutativity, changes the undecidable theory of groups into a decidable theory.
Other decidable theories of algebraic structures, such as [[Presburger arithmetic|~14d8268c]] and the theories of [[algebraically closed fields|0PF6TM8TWT]] and [[real closed fields|DDY3Q3PJ6W]], are also proved decidable by elimination of quantifiers.
In the later sub-areas, the structure theory of abelian groups gives the isomorphism classification, with [[divisible groups|ZXVBEPMTCN]] and Ulm invariants, while Szmielew's theorem gives the coarser classification up to elementary equivalence.
