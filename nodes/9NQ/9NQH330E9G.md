## Example

One inverse solves two $3 \times 3$ systems, each with a single matrix-vector product.
Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} -24 & 18 & 5 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
where the inverse comes from the [[worked inversion|743HGXHH7Q]].
We solve $Ax = b_1$ and $Ax = b_2$ for $b_1 = (6, 5, 11)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $b_2 = (1, 0, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$.

For $b_1$, each entry of $A^{-1}b_1$ is a row of $A^{-1}$ times $b_1$:
$$\begin{aligned} -24 \cdot 6 + 18 \cdot 5 + 5 \cdot 11 &= -144 + 90 + 55 = 1, \\ 20 \cdot 6 - 15 \cdot 5 - 4 \cdot 11 &= 120 - 75 - 44 = 1, \\ -5 \cdot 6 + 4 \cdot 5 + 1 \cdot 11 &= -30 + 20 + 11 = 1. \end{aligned}$$
So $x = (1, 1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
Check: $A(1, 1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the vector of row sums of $A$, which is $(6, 5, 11)^{\mathsf{T}}$.

For $b_2 = e_1$, the product $A^{-1}e_1$ is column 1 of $A^{-1}$, so $x = (-24, 20, -5)^{\mathsf{T}}$ with no arithmetic at all.
Check:
$$A \begin{pmatrix} -24 \\ 20 \\ -5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -24 + 40 - 15 \\ 0 + 20 - 20 \\ -120 + 120 + 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
By the [[solution theorem|3RQX3T70NX]], these are the only solutions.

## Remarks

The two systems together are the matrix equation $AX = B$ with $B = [b_1 \; b_2]$, and
$$X = A^{-1}B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -24 \\ 1 & 20 \\ 1 & -5 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Once $A^{-1}$ is known, each new right side costs $9$ multiplications here, and no new row reduction.

The entries of $A^{-1}$ are large compared with those of $A$, so a small change in $b$ can move $x$ a lot.
For example, adding $0.01$ to the first entry of $b_1$ changes $x$ by $0.01 \cdot (-24, 20, -5)^{\mathsf{T}}$, which moves the first entry by $0.24$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

We computed the inverse once, and then each system needs only one matrix times vector.
The second system was even easier: its answer is a column of the inverse.

### L2 human

The point is reuse: the second system costs no new row reduction.
A trap: large entries in $A^{-1}$ mean that small errors in $b$ grow in $x$.
This is a first sign of the sensitivity that numerical analysis measures with the condition number.
