## Statement

Let $\mathfrak{M} = (W, R, V)$ be a [[Kripke model|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]], and let $\Sigma$ be a set of [[modal formulas|G8VGTW9P4F]] closed under subformulas.
We use the notation $W_\Sigma$, $[w]$, $V^f$, $R^s$, $R^l$ and $R^t$ of [[filtration|6ZZSHAKG5F]].

1. Filtration lemma. Let $\mathfrak{M}^f = (W_\Sigma, R^f, V^f)$ be any filtration of $\mathfrak{M}$ through $\Sigma$. For every $\varphi \in \Sigma$ and every $w \in W$, $\mathfrak{M}, w \models \varphi$ holds if and only if $\mathfrak{M}^f, [w] \models \varphi$.
2. Range of filtrations. $R^s$ and $R^l$ are filtrations, and a relation $R'$ on $W_\Sigma$ is a filtration if and only if $R^s \subseteq R' \subseteq R^l$.
3. Size. If $\Sigma$ is finite with $n$ elements, then $W_\Sigma$ has at most $2^n$ elements.
4. Transitive filtration. If $R$ is transitive, then $R^t$ is a filtration and $R^t$ is transitive. If $R$ is also reflexive, then $R^t$ is reflexive.
5. Symmetry. If $R$ is symmetric, then $R^s$ is symmetric.
6. Equivalence. If $R$ is an equivalence relation, then the relation $R^e$, with $[w] R^e [v]$ when $w$ and $v$ agree on every $\square\varphi \in \Sigma$, is a filtration and an [[equivalence relation|0G8VEZ3WTK]].

The proof is in [[the proof of the filtration theorem|CXHS8K1MB0]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Closure under subformulas is needed in part 1.
Take $W = \{ 0, 1 \}$, $R = \{ (0, 1) \}$, $V(p) = \{ 1 \}$ and $\Sigma = \{ \square p \}$, which lacks the subformula $p$.
In $\mathfrak{M}$, $\square p$ is true at $0$ because $1$ has $p$, and true at $1$ because $1$ has no successors.
So there is one class, (min) gives it a loop, and $V^f(p) = \emptyset$ because $p \notin \Sigma$.
In the quotient $\square p$ is false, although it is true at both worlds of $\mathfrak{M}$.

The quotient must be by agreement on all of $\Sigma$, and agreement on the variables alone does not work, as [[the collapsing example|CYP4SYP20S]] shows.

Transitivity of $R$ is needed in part 4.
Take the successor model on $\mathbb{N}$ with $V(p) = \{ 1 \}$ and $\Sigma = \{ p, \square p \}$.
There $0 R 1$, but $\square p$ holds at $0$ and fails at $1$, so $([0], [1]) \notin R^t$ and $R^t$ fails (min).

Part 4 needs $R^t$ and not $R^s$, because the smallest filtration of a transitive model can fail to be transitive, as [[the transitive example|V0J3A9Q4SY]] shows.

### What it gives you

Each of $\mathsf{K}$, $\mathsf{T} = \mathsf{KT}$, $\mathsf{D} = \mathsf{KD}$, $\mathsf{K4}$, $\mathsf{S4} = \mathsf{KT4}$, $\mathsf{B} = \mathsf{KTB}$ and $\mathsf{S5} = \mathsf{KT5}$ has the finite model property.
A non-theorem $\varphi$ fails in a model with at most $2^{n}$ worlds, where $n$ is the number of subformulas of $\varphi$, as in [[the finite model property theorem|XNPJK6B921]].
Each of these logics is therefore decidable, by [[Harrop's theorem|VSYQ1AZ9G7]].
[[The successor model|42QZ2YBM2R]] and [[the countermodel for axiom 4|VK1RP97KKY]] work the theorem through on concrete models.

### The converse

Part 1 has no converse in the obvious sense: a finite model that agrees with $\mathfrak{M}$ on $\Sigma$ need not be a filtration of it.
Part 2 is itself an equivalence, and it says that (min) and (max) describe all filtrations.

### Generalizations

The same lemma holds for languages with several boxes, with one relation $R_i^f$ for each box, as in [[multimodal logic|ERG3KAQWYF]].
Selective filtrations pick a subset of worlds instead of a quotient, and they give the finite model property of $\mathsf{GL}$ and of other logics of conversely well-founded frames.
Filtration through the Fischer-Ladner closure gives the finite model property of [[propositional dynamic logic|VJ78A0Q22R]].

## History

The algebraic form of the lemma is in McKinsey's paper of 1941 on the decidability of $\mathsf{S2}$ and $\mathsf{S4}$.
Segerberg's An Essay in Classical Modal Logic of 1971 gave the relational form a systematic treatment.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A filtration glues together the situations that look the same for a fixed list of statements.
The theorem says that the glued model gives the same answer as the original model for every statement on the list.
It also says that the glued model is small: it has at most two to the power of the length of the list situations.

### L2 human

We check part 1 on a small model.
Take $W = \{ 0, 1, 2 \}$, $R = \{ (0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 2) \}$, $V(p) = \{ 2 \}$ and $\Sigma = \{ p, \square p \}$.
$\square p$ is true at $1$ and $2$ and false at $0$, and $p$ is true only at $2$.
The three patterns differ, so the classes are $\{ 0 \}$, $\{ 1 \}$ and $\{ 2 \}$, and the smallest filtration is a copy of $\mathfrak{M}$.
The largest filtration lets $[0]$ see every class, since $\square p$ is false at $0$, so it adds $(0, 0)$ and $(0, 2)$.
In $R^l$, $\square p$ is still false at $[0]$, since $[0] R^l [0]$ and $p$ is false at $0$.
The typical use is to check that a quotient keeps the formulas we care about.
Watch out: $R^l$ is defined from truth values in $\mathfrak{M}$, so it is not the image of $R$.

### L3 human

The quotient map $w \mapsto [w]$ behaves like a bounded morphism, but only for the formulas of $\Sigma$, and the filtration lemma is the matching restricted form of preservation of truth under bounded morphisms.
The bound $2^n$ gives a nondeterministic exponential time algorithm for satisfiability.
The sharper bounds of [[the complexity theorem|1P2B0C7CQ4]] use tableaux instead.
