## Statement

Let $\mathfrak{M} = (W, R, V)$ be a [[Kripke model|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]], and let $\Sigma$ be a set of [[modal formulas|G8VGTW9P4F]] closed under subformulas.
Use the notation $W_\Sigma$, $[w]$, $V^f$, $R^s$, $R^l$ and $R^t$ of [[filtration|6ZZSHAKG5F]].

1. Filtration lemma. Let $\mathfrak{M}^f = (W_\Sigma, R^f, V^f)$ be any filtration of $\mathfrak{M}$ through $\Sigma$. For every $\varphi \in \Sigma$ and every $w \in W$, $\mathfrak{M}, w \models \varphi$ if and only if $\mathfrak{M}^f, [w] \models \varphi$.
2. Range of filtrations. $R^s$ and $R^l$ are filtrations, and a relation $R'$ on $W_\Sigma$ is a filtration if and only if $R^s \subseteq R' \subseteq R^l$.
3. Size. If $\Sigma$ is finite, then $|W_\Sigma| \le 2^{|\Sigma|}$.
4. Transitive filtration. If $R$ is transitive, then $R^t$ is a filtration and $R^t$ is transitive. If $R$ is also reflexive, then $R^t$ is reflexive.
5. Symmetry. If $R$ is symmetric, then $R^s$ is symmetric.
6. Equivalence. If $R$ is an equivalence relation, then $[w] R^e [v]$, defined by "$w$ and $v$ agree on every $\square\varphi \in \Sigma$", is a filtration and an [[equivalence relation|0G8VEZ3WTK]].

The proof is in [[the proof of the filtration theorem|CXHS8K1MB0]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. Closure under subformulas is needed in part 1. With $\Sigma = \set{\square p}$ only, the quotient forgets $p$, and $V^f(p)$ is not defined from $\Sigma$, so $\square p$ can change its value.
2. The quotient must be by agreement on all of $\Sigma$. Agreement on the variables only does not work, see [[the collapsing example|CYP4SYP20S]].
3. Transitivity of $R$ is needed in part 4. If $R$ is not transitive, $R^t$ can fail (min). An example is the successor model on $\N$ with $V(p) = \set{1}$ and $\Sigma = \set{p, \square p}$. There the pair $(0, 1)$ is in $R$, but $\square p$ holds at $0$ and fails at $1$.
4. Part 4 needs $R^t$, not $R^s$: the smallest filtration of a transitive model can fail to be transitive, see [[the transitive example|V0J3A9Q4SY]].

### Consequences

1. Each of $\mathsf{K}$, $\mathsf{T}$, $\mathsf{D}$, $\mathsf{K4}$, $\mathsf{S4}$, $\mathsf{B}$ and $\mathsf{S5}$ has the finite model property. The countermodels have size at most $2^{|\operatorname{Sub}(\varphi)|}$, see [[the finite model property theorem|XNPJK6B921]].
2. Each of these logics is decidable, see [[Harrop's theorem|VSYQ1AZ9G7]].
3. Examples: [[the successor model|42QZ2YBM2R]] and [[the countermodel for axiom 4|VK1RP97KKY]].

### Generalizations

1. The same lemma holds for multimodal languages, with one relation $R_i^f$ for each box, see [[multimodal logic|ERG3KAQWYF]].
2. Selective filtrations pick a subset of worlds instead of a quotient. They give the finite model property of $\mathsf{GL}$ and of other logics of conversely well-founded frames.
3. Filtration through the Fischer-Ladner closure gives the finite model property of propositional dynamic logic, see [[dynamic modal logic|VJ78A0Q22R]].

## History

The algebraic form of the lemma is in McKinsey's paper of 1941 on the decidability of $\mathsf{S2}$ and $\mathsf{S4}$.
Lemmon in 1966 and Segerberg in 1968 and 1971 gave the relational form.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A filtration glues together the situations that look the same for a fixed list of statements.
The theorem says that the glued model gives the same answer as the original model for every statement in the list.
It also says that the glued model is small: at most two to the power of the length of the list.

### L2 human

We check part 1 at one world of a filtration.

1. Take $W = \set{0, 1, 2}$, $R = \set{(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 2)}$, $V(p) = \set{2}$ and $\Sigma = \set{p, \square p}$.
2. $\square p$ is true at $1$ and $2$, and false at $0$. $p$ is true only at $2$.
3. The classes are $\set{0}$, $\set{1}$ and $\set{2}$, since the three patterns differ.
4. So the smallest filtration is a copy of $\mathfrak{M}$, and part 1 holds at once.
5. The largest filtration adds every pair $([w], [v])$ with $v \models p$ or $w \not\models \square p$; it adds $(0, 0)$ and $(0, 2)$, and $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 2)$ are already there.
6. In $R^l$, $\square p$ at $[0]$ is false, since $[0] R^l [0]$ and $p$ is false at $0$. This matches step 2.

The typical use is to check that a quotient keeps the formulas we care about.
A trap: $R^l$ is defined from the truth values in $\mathfrak{M}$, so it is not the image of $R$.

### L3 human

The filtration lemma is a restricted form of the preservation of truth under bounded morphisms.
The quotient map $w \mapsto [w]$ is a bounded morphism with respect to the formulas of $\Sigma$ only.
The bound $2^{|\Sigma|}$ gives a nondeterministic exponential time algorithm for satisfiability; the sharper bounds of [[the complexity theorem|1P2B0C7CQ4]] use tableaux instead.
