## Statement

When the characteristic polynomial splits, the space is the direct sum of the generalized eigenspaces, and the map is a diagonalizable part plus a nilpotent part.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear.
Assume that the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] of $T$ splits over $F$ as
$$p_T(t) = (t - \lambda_1)^{m_1} \cdots (t - \lambda_k)^{m_k}$$
with pairwise distinct $\lambda_i$ and all $m_i \ge 1$.
Let $V_{\lambda_i}$ be the [[generalized eigenspace|0AJ035ZTZE]] of $\lambda_i$.

1. The space is the [[direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] $V = V_{\lambda_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus V_{\lambda_k}$.
2. Each $V_{\lambda_i}$ is $T$-[[invariant|0ACX5QT8TE]], $\dim V_{\lambda_i} = m_i$, and $T - \lambda_i \id_V$ is [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]] on $V_{\lambda_i}$.
3. Let $\pi_i$ be the projection onto $V_{\lambda_i}$ along the other summands.
   Each $\pi_i$ is a polynomial in $T$.
4. Let $D = \lambda_1 \pi_1 + \cdots + \lambda_k \pi_k$ and $N = T - D$.
   Then $T = D + N$, where $D$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]], $N$ is nilpotent, $D N = N D$, and $D$ and $N$ are polynomials in $T$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The characteristic polynomial must split.
The rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\R$ has $p = t^2 + 1$ and no real eigenvalue, so every real generalized eigenspace is $0$ and their sum is not $\R^2$.
Over $\C$ the same matrix splits as $(t - i)(t + i)$, and then the theorem applies.

Finite dimension is needed, both for $p_T$ to exist and for the conclusion.
The shift $(x_1, x_2, \dots) \mapsto (0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ on complex sequences has no eigenvalue, so all its generalized eigenspaces are $0$.

### What it gives you

In a basis adapted to the decomposition, the matrix of $T$ is [[block diagonal|001T5FJDC1]], and the block for $\lambda_i$ is $\lambda_i I + N_i$ with $N_i$ nilpotent.
Each $N_i$ is strictly upper triangular in a suitable basis, by the [[characterizations of nilpotent maps|FSKECQK1GW]], so each block is [[triangular|0363JMQ42Y]] with $\lambda_i$ on the diagonal.
Over an [[algebraically closed field|0PF6TM8TWT]] such as $\C$ the hypothesis holds for every $T$, so the theorem applies to every square complex matrix.

Part 4 is the existence half of the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]] in the split case.
It also gives a test: $T$ is diagonalizable exactly when $N = 0$, that is, when each $V_{\lambda_i}$ equals the eigenspace.

### The converse

The decomposition determines its pieces.
If $V = U_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_k$ with $T$-invariant $U_i$ and $T - \lambda_i$ nilpotent on $U_i$, then $U_i = V_{\lambda_i}$ for each $i$.
The reason is that $T - \lambda_i$ is invertible on each $U_j$ with $j \ne i$, so it sends no nonzero vector of $\bigoplus_{j \ne i} U_j$ to $0$ in any number of steps.

### Generalizations

Without the splitting hypothesis, the [[primary decomposition theorem|49ZHBMYKVE]] splits $V$ along the irreducible factors of the minimal polynomial.
For a finite commuting family of maps whose characteristic polynomials split, $V$ is the direct sum of the common generalized eigenspaces.
In Mathlib, `Module.End.iSup_maxGenEigenspace_eq_top` says that over an algebraically closed field the generalized eigenspaces span $V$, which is weaker than part 1.

## History

The decomposition is the first step of the usual proof of the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]], which Camille Jordan published in his treatise on substitutions of 1870.
Axler's textbook *Linear Algebra Done Right* proves the decomposition without determinants.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Over the complex numbers, the space splits into pieces, one for each eigenvalue.
On each piece, the map is the eigenvalue times the identity plus a part that dies after a few steps.
For example, a $3 \times 3$ matrix with eigenvalues $1, 2, 2$ splits the space into a line for $1$ and a plane for $2$.
So one hard problem breaks into smaller problems, one for each eigenvalue.

### L2 human

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ with $p_A = (t - 1)(t - 2)^2$, from the [[worked 3 × 3 example|18WSA33PKA]].
There, $V_1$ is the line spanned by $(1, 1, -1)$ and $V_2 = \ker(A - 2I)^2$ is the plane $z = 0$, so $\R^3 = V_1 \oplus V_2$.

The identity $(t - 2)^2 - (t - 3)(t - 1) = 1$ gives the projections as polynomials in $A$:
$$\pi_1 = (A - 2I)^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \pi_2 = I - \pi_1.$$
Check: $\pi_1$ sends $(1, 1, -1)$ to itself and every vector with $z = 0$ to $0$.
Now form the two parts:
$$D = \pi_1 + 2 \pi_2 = 2I - \pi_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad N = A - D = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $N^2 = 0$, and $D N$ and $N D$ both have the rows $(-2, 2, 0)$, $(-2, 2, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 0)$.

The typical use is to reduce questions about $T$, such as powers or exponentials, to the blocks $\lambda_i I + N_i$.
Watch out: $D$ is not the diagonal part of the matrix $A$.
Here that diagonal part is $\operatorname{diag}(1, 3, 1)$, which differs from $D$.

### L3 human

Make $V$ a module over $F[t]$, with $t$ acting as $T$.
The decomposition is the primary decomposition of this module when all its primes are linear.
The projections $\pi_i$ are the idempotents that the Chinese remainder theorem gives in $F[t] / (p_T)$.
The splitting $T = D + N$ is the additive Jordan-Chevalley decomposition, and the sub-area on canonical forms refines each nilpotent block $N_i$ into Jordan blocks.
