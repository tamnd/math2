## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear.
Assume that the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] of $T$ splits over $F$ as
$$p_T(t) = (t - \lambda_1)^{m_1} \cdots (t - \lambda_k)^{m_k}$$
with pairwise distinct $\lambda_i$ and $m_i \ge 1$.

1. $V = V_{\lambda_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus V_{\lambda_k}$, a [[direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]], where $V_{\lambda_i}$ is the [[generalized eigenspace|0AJ035ZTZE]] of $\lambda_i$.
2. Each $V_{\lambda_i}$ is $T$-[[invariant|0ACX5QT8TE]], $\dim V_{\lambda_i} = m_i$, and $T - \lambda_i \id$ is [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]] on $V_{\lambda_i}$.
3. Let $\pi_i$ be the projection onto $V_{\lambda_i}$ along the other summands. Each $\pi_i$ is a polynomial in $T$.
4. Let $D = \lambda_1 \pi_1 + \cdots + \lambda_k \pi_k$ and $N = T - D$. Then $T = D + N$, where $D$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]], $N$ is nilpotent, $D N = N D$, and $D$ and $N$ are polynomials in $T$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The characteristic polynomial must split. For the rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\R$, there is no real eigenvalue, and every real generalized eigenspace is $0$.
- Finite dimension is needed. The shift $(x_1, x_2, \dots) \mapsto (0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ on real sequences has no eigenvalue, so all its generalized eigenspaces are $0$.

### Consequences

- If $F$ is [[algebraically closed|0PF6TM8TWT]], for example $F = \C$, the hypothesis holds for every $T$.
- In a basis adapted to the decomposition, the matrix of $T$ is [[block diagonal|001T5FJDC1]], and the block for $\lambda_i$ is $\lambda_i I + N_i$ with $N_i$ nilpotent.
- Each block can be put in [[triangular form|0363JMQ42Y]] with $\lambda_i$ on the diagonal, by the [[nilpotent characterizations|FSKECQK1GW]].
- Part 4 is the existence half of the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]] in the split case.
- $T$ is diagonalizable exactly when $N = 0$, that is, when each $V_{\lambda_i}$ is the eigenspace.

### Converse

The decomposition characterizes the $V_{\lambda_i}$.
If $V = U_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_k$ with $T$-invariant $U_i$ and $T - \lambda_i$ nilpotent on $U_i$, then $U_i = V_{\lambda_i}$ for each $i$.

### Generalizations

Without the splitting hypothesis, the [[primary decomposition theorem|49ZHBMYKVE]] splits $V$ along the irreducible factors of the minimal polynomial.
For a commuting family of maps whose characteristic polynomials split, $V$ is the direct sum of the common generalized eigenspaces.
For a nilpotent Lie algebra acting on a finite-dimensional module over an algebraically closed field, the same holds with generalized weight spaces.
In Mathlib, `Module.End.iSup_maxGenEigenspace_eq_top` says that the generalized eigenspaces span $V$ over an algebraically closed field; this is weaker than part 1.

## History

The decomposition is the first step of the proof of the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].
Jordan stated the normal form in his 1870 treatise on substitutions.
The proof by generalized eigenspaces without determinants is the approach of Axler, Chapter 8.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Over the complex numbers, the space splits into pieces, one for each eigenvalue.
On each piece, the map is $\lambda$ times the identity plus a part that dies after a few steps.
So a hard problem breaks into simpler problems, one for each eigenvalue.

### L2 human

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, with $p_A = (t - 1)(t - 2)^2$, as in the [[worked 3 × 3 example|18WSA33PKA]].

1. $V_1 = \ker (A - I)$ is the line spanned by $(1, 1, -1)$.
2. $V_2 = \ker (A - 2I)^2$ is the plane $z = 0$.
3. $1 + 2 = 3$, and $(1, 1, -1)$ is not in the plane. So $\R^3 = V_1 \oplus V_2$.
4. The projection onto $V_1$ is $\pi_1 = (A - 2I)^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $\pi_2 = I - \pi_1$.
5. $D = \pi_1 + 2 \pi_2 = 2I - \pi_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $N = A - D = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, with $N^2 = 0$.

The typical use is to reduce questions about $T$, such as powers or exponentials, to the blocks $\lambda_i I + N_i$.
The trap is to take $D$ as the diagonal part of the matrix $A$.
Here that is $\operatorname{diag}(1, 3, 1)$, which is not $D$.

### L3 human

In module language, $V$ is an $F[t]$-module, and the decomposition is its primary decomposition when all primes are linear.
The idempotents $\pi_i$ come from the Chinese remainder theorem in $F[t]/(p_T)$.
The splitting $T = D + N$ is the additive Jordan-Chevalley decomposition, which extends to algebraic groups and Lie algebras.
