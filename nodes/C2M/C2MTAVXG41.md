## Statement

Let $A$, $B$ and $C$ be [[thermodynamic systems|GYNNJBMTCP]], each in an equilibrium state.
If $A$ is in thermal [[equilibrium|5EKTEE00FZ]] with $C$, and $B$ is in thermal equilibrium with $C$, then $A$ is in thermal equilibrium with $B$.

## Remarks

The law is a postulate drawn from experience, not a theorem.
Together with the convention that each system is in thermal equilibrium with itself, it makes thermal equilibrium an [[equivalence relation|0G8VEZ3WTK]] on equilibrium states.
Its classes can then be labelled by real numbers, and such a labelling is an empirical [[temperature|KK46Q2YV1D]].
A thermometer is the system $C$: if it shows the same reading in $A$ and in $B$, then $A$ and $B$ placed in contact exchange no net heat.
Without the law, a thermometer reading would not decide whether two bodies are in balance with each other.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If a cup of tea and a glass of milk each have the same temperature as a thermometer, they have the same temperature as each other.
Put them in contact and no heat will flow between them.
This sounds obvious, but it is what makes thermometers work.
It was named the zeroth law because it was recognised as basic after the first and second laws already had their names.

### L2 human

In the axiomatic treatment of thermodynamics, the zeroth law is the reason that temperature exists as a state function.
Together with reflexivity, it makes the relation "in thermal equilibrium with" symmetric and transitive, so the equilibrium states split into classes of equal temperature.
Choosing a real-valued label for these classes gives an empirical temperature; the second law later selects the thermodynamic temperature among them.
