## Statement

The matrix of a linear map computes the map: multiply it by the coordinates of a vector, and you get the coordinates of the image.
Let $F$ be a field, and let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over $F$ with finite ordered bases $B = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ and $C = (c_1, \dots, c_m)$.
Let $T \colon V \to W$ be a [[linear map|0RJF668P1T]], with [[matrix|0YYMY95FWS]] $[T]_{C,B}$.
Then for each $v \in V$,
$$[T(v)]_C = [T]_{C,B}\, [v]_B,$$
where $[\,\cdot\,]_B$ and $[\,\cdot\,]_C$ are [[coordinate vectors|0BMVBHTWV2]] and the right side is a [[matrix-vector product|0PDVQEP0ZC]].
Conversely, $[T]_{C,B}$ is the only $m \times n$ matrix $M$ such that $[T(v)]_C = M [v]_B$ for each $v \in V$.
The [[proof|NZKVYS61XP]] expands $v$ in the basis $B$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The map $T$ must be linear.
For $f(x) = x + 1$ on $\mathbb{R}$ with $B = C = (1)$, the recipe for the matrix gives the $1 \times 1$ array $(f(1)) = (2)$.
But $f(2) = 3$, while the array sends the coordinate $2$ to $4$, so no matrix computes $f$.

The bases must be ordered and must be bases.
If we reorder $C$, then the rows of $[T]_{C,B}$ and the entries of $[T(v)]_C$ are permuted in the same way, so the formula survives only when both sides use the same order.
If $C$ were a spanning family that is not independent, coordinates in $C$ would not be unique, and the left side would have no single meaning.

### What it gives you

The formula turns every question about $T$ into a question about one matrix $A = [T]_{C,B}$.
A vector $v$ lies in $\ker T$ exactly when $[v]_B$ lies in the [[null space|0PHDYNR24D]] of $A$.
A vector $w$ lies in $\im T$ exactly when $[w]_C$ lies in the [[column space|03AJKXH1AV]] of $A$, so the rank of $T$ is the rank of $A$.
Applied twice, the formula gives the [[matrix of a composition|05Q1B7MH0M]].

For example, take the derivative $D \colon P_2 \to P_1$ with $B = (1, x, x^2)$, $C = (1, x)$ and $[D]_{C,B} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
The null space of this matrix is spanned by $(1, 0, 0)$, which is the coordinate vector of the constant $1$.
So the kernel of $D$ is the space of constants, as calculus says.
The [[derivative example|KWN1RM45D5]] and the [[symmetric map example|7HRGTM3RKJ]] check the formula on numbers.

### The converse

The uniqueness part is a converse.
If some matrix $M$ computes the coordinates of every image, then $M$ is the matrix of $T$.
The reason is that testing on $v = b_j$ gives $[b_j]_B = e_j$, and $M e_j$ is column $j$ of $M$, so column $j$ must be $[T(b_j)]_C$.
Some books use this property as the definition of the matrix of $T$.

### Generalizations

The same formula holds for free modules of finite rank over a commutative ring, with the same proof, since the proof uses only finite sums and unique coordinates.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To find the coordinates of $T(v)$, multiply the matrix of $T$ by the coordinates of $v$.
For the map $(x, y) \mapsto (x + 2y, 3y)$ with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, the vector $(1, 1)$ goes to $(1 + 2, 0 + 3) = (3, 3)$, and the map itself gives $(1 + 2, 3) = (3, 3)$.
So all computation with $T$ becomes computation with one table of numbers.

### L2 human

The proof expands $v = x_1 b_1 + \dots + x_n b_n$ and uses linearity, so $T(v) = x_1 T(b_1) + \dots + x_n T(b_n)$.
Each $T(b_j)$ has coordinates equal to column $j$ of the matrix, so the coordinate vector of $T(v)$ is $x_1$ times column $1$, plus $x_2$ times column $2$, and so on up to $x_n$ times column $n$.
That weighted sum of columns is exactly the matrix-vector product.
Watch out: both sides must use the same bases, so $[v]_B$ must be in the basis $B$ and the answer is in the basis $C$, even when $V = W = \mathbb{R}^n$ and the standard basis is close at hand.

### L3 human

The coordinate maps $\phi_B \colon V \to F^n$ and $\phi_C \colon W \to F^m$ are isomorphisms.
The statement says that the square with $T$ on top, $x \mapsto [T]_{C,B}\, x$ at the bottom and $\phi_B$, $\phi_C$ on the sides commutes, that is, $\phi_C \circ T = [T]_{C,B} \circ \phi_B$.
So $[T]_{C,B}$ is the matrix of $\phi_C \circ T \circ \phi_B^{-1}$ in the standard bases, and the [[theorem on linear maps and matrices|Q3EDKTZ6TA]] turns this into an isomorphism $\Hom(V, W) \cong M_{m \times n}(F)$.
