## Problem

Assume the universe is infinite, static and Euclidean.
Assume it is uniformly filled, on average, with stars of the same mean luminosity that have shone forever.
Then every line of sight from the observer ends on the surface of a star.
So the whole night sky should be about as bright as the surface of a typical star.
Why is the night sky dark?

## Remarks

The argument: let $n$ be the number density of stars and $L$ their mean luminosity.
A thin shell of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ around the observer contains $4 \pi r^2 n \, dr$ stars, each of which sends a flux $L / (4 \pi r^2)$.
So each shell contributes the same flux $n L \, dr$, and the sum over all shells diverges.
Since near stars hide far ones, the sky instead reaches the surface brightness of a star.

Status: resolved within [[physical cosmology|5T9JA1ZAT5]].
The universe has a finite age, so light reaches us only from a finite region, and stars have finite lifetimes.
These facts alone make the sky dark.
The mean distance along a line of sight to a stellar surface is far larger than the distance light has travelled since stars formed.
The expansion of the universe adds a further dimming through the [[cosmological redshift|8K69WPX4GS]] of distant light.

The question is named after Olbers, who wrote about it in 1823; it had been raised earlier, for example by Kepler in 1610 and by de Chéseaux in 1744.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In a forest that goes on forever, every line of sight ends on a tree trunk.
In the same way, in an endless and eternal universe full of stars, every direction would end on a star, and the night would be as bright as the Sun.
The night is dark because the universe is not like that.
It has a finite age, so we see light only from a limited region, and the stars there do not cover the sky.
