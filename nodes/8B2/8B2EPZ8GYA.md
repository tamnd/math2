## Example

Let $K_n(\Z)$ be the [[algebraic K-groups|5GVFZWMWC8]] of the ring of [[integers|0C4JS9RF5M]].
The first groups are
$$K_0(\Z) \cong \Z, \quad K_1(\Z) \cong \Z/2, \quad K_2(\Z) \cong \Z/2, \quad K_3(\Z) \cong \Z/48, \quad K_4(\Z) = 0.$$

Check of $K_0$: $\Z$ is a principal ideal domain, so every finitely generated projective $\Z$-module is free, and its class is determined by its rank.
Check of $K_1$: $\Z$ is a Euclidean domain, so row operations reduce every matrix of determinant $1$ to the identity.
Thus $SL(\Z)$ is generated by elementary matrices.
Hence the determinant gives $K_1(\Z) \cong \Z^\times = \set{1, -1}$.

## Remarks

The group [[$K_2(\Z)$|ZBVSX0FYQ0]] is generated by the Steinberg symbol $\set{-1, -1}$; this is due to Milnor.
The group $K_3(\Z) \cong \Z/48$ was computed by Lee and Szczarba, and $K_4(\Z) = 0$ by Rognes.
By [[Quillen's finite generation theorem|PXYP8675C2]] every $K_n(\Z)$ is finitely generated.
By [[Borel's theorem|AKGV0SAD4V]], for $n \ge 1$ the rank of $K_n(\Z)$ is $1$ when $n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and $n \ge 5$, and $0$ otherwise.
So $K_5(\Z)$, $K_9(\Z)$, $K_{13}(\Z)$, and so on, are infinite, and all other positive-degree groups are finite.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The integers are the simplest ring of arithmetic, but their K-groups are hard to compute.
The first two groups are easy: $K_0$ counts the rank of free modules, and $K_1$ records the sign of a determinant.
After that each group needed new methods, and the torsion in the higher groups is tied to deep questions about Bernoulli numbers and cyclotomic fields.
