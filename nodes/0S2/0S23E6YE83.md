## Summary

The kernel of a linear map is the set of vectors that the map sends to zero, and it measures exactly how much information the map loses.
For a [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] $T \colon V \to W$ it is
$$\ker T = \{v \in V : T(v) = 0\},$$
and the [[formal definition|02YFME3RGW]] states it.
The kernel is a [[subspace|00YC1D652K]] of $V$, and $T$ is injective exactly when its kernel is $\{0\}$.

## Motivation

A homogeneous linear system $Ax = 0$ asks for the vectors that the map $x \mapsto Ax$ sends to $0$, so its solution set is a kernel.
The kernel controls the inhomogeneous equations too.
If $T(v_0) = w$, then the solutions of $T(v) = w$ are exactly the vectors $v_0 + k$ with $k \in \ker T$.
So one solution plus the kernel gives all solutions, and the size of the kernel tells you how many solutions there are when there is one.

The same idea appears in other parts of mathematics.
The solutions of a linear differential equation $y'' + y = 0$ form the kernel of $y \mapsto y'' + y$, and the general solution of $y'' + y = f$ is one particular solution plus that kernel.
Later in the area the kernel returns as the [[nullity|0APCFW7639]], in the [[rank-nullity theorem|0W4FSW1TVB]], and as the eigenspace $\ker(T - \lambda \id)$ of an eigenvalue $\lambda$.

## Notation

The kernel is written $\ker T$, $\ker(T)$ or $N(T)$.
For a matrix $A$, the kernel of $x \mapsto Ax$ is the [[null space|0PHDYNR24D]] of $A$, written $\operatorname{Nul} A$ or $N(A)$, and some books also say "null space of $T$" for $\ker T$.
"Null space" is common in books on matrices, while "kernel" is shared with homomorphisms of groups and rings.
Mathlib writes `LinearMap.ker f`, a submodule of the source.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Let $T \colon \R^2 \to \R$ be $T(x, y) = x - 2y$.
A vector is in the kernel when $x - 2y = 0$, that is $x = 2y$, so
$$\ker T = \{(2t, t) : t \in \R\},$$
the line through the origin in the direction $(2, 1)$.
The kernel is closed under sums: $(2, 1)$ and $(4, 2)$ are in it, and so is their sum $(6, 3)$, since $6 - 2 \cdot 3 = 0$.
Larger examples follow the same pattern: for [[a matrix map of R³|RZNXVEHTCE]], solving $Ax = 0$ gives the line spanned by $(-1, -1, 1)$.

### More examples

The [[derivative on polynomials of degree at most 3|SMEX3CNE69]] has the constant polynomials as kernel, since only constants have derivative $0$.
The projection $(x, y, z) \mapsto (x, y)$ of $\R^3$ onto $\R^2$ has the $z$-axis as kernel.
The [[trace|0XC1RDBY7A]] $M_n(F) \to F$ has as kernel the matrices with trace $0$, a subspace of dimension $n^2 - 1$.
At the two extremes, the identity map has kernel $\{0\}$, and the zero map $V \to W$ has kernel $V$.

### What it is not

The [[solution set of T(v) = w with w ≠ 0|HSXSS57MS3]] is not a kernel: it misses $0$, so it is not a subspace.
It is a shifted copy $v_0 + \ker T$ of the kernel, a line or plane that does not pass through the origin.
The set $\{v : T(v) = v\}$ of fixed points is not the kernel of $T$ either.
It is a subspace, but it is the kernel of $T - \id$, since $T(v) = v$ means $(T - \id)(v) = 0$.

### The rules you can use

The kernel is a subspace of $V$, by the [[lemma on the kernel|REWYZ8N7EN]], and the same lemma gives $T(u) = T(v)$ if and only if $u - v \in \ker T$.
From this, $T$ is injective if and only if $\ker T = \{0\}$, by the [[theorem on injectivity|0BJBYCGMWE]].
If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $\dim \ker T + \dim \operatorname{im} T = \dim V$ by the rank-nullity theorem, so a large kernel means a small image.
For a second linear map $S \colon W \to U$, we have $\ker T \subseteq \ker(S \circ T)$, because $T(v) = 0$ gives $S(T(v)) = S(0) = 0$.
Finally, the quotient $V / \ker T$ is isomorphic to the image of $T$, by the [[first isomorphism theorem|0MTBGVZ4XJ]].

### Watch out

The kernel lives in the source $V$, not in the target $W$.
For $T(x, y, z) = (x, y)$, the kernel is the $z$-axis in $\R^3$, and writing it as a subset of $\R^2$ is a common slip.
The statement $\ker T = 0$ means $\ker T = \{0\}$, not the empty set, because the kernel always contains $0$.

### History

The kernel of $x \mapsto Ax$ is the solution set of the homogeneous system $Ax = 0$, which was studied long before abstract linear maps were defined.

### Try it

First, find the kernel of $T \colon \R^3 \to \R^2$, $T(x, y, z) = (x + y, \; y + z)$.
The equations $x + y = 0$ and $y + z = 0$ give $x = -y$ and $z = -y$, so the kernel is the line spanned by $(-1, 1, -1)$.

Second, is $T(x, y) = (x + y, \; 2x + 2y)$ injective?
No: $(1, -1)$ is a nonzero vector with $T(1, -1) = (0, 0)$, so the kernel is not $\{0\}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The kernel of a linear map is the set of vectors that the map sends to zero.
It always contains the zero vector, and it is closed under sums and scalar multiples.
Think of the shadow of a stick on the floor under a lamp straight overhead.
A vertical stick has a shadow that is a single point, so the vertical line through the origin is the kernel of this projection.

### L2 human

Here is the standard method, for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$.
Subtracting twice the first row from the second gives the single equation $x + 2y + 3z = 0$.
The free variables are $y = s$ and $z = t$, so $x = -2s - 3t$, and
$$\ker A = \{s(-2, 1, 0) + t(-3, 0, 1) : s, t \in \R\}.$$
Check: $A(-2, 1, 0)^{\mathsf T} = (-2 + 2, \; -4 + 4) = (0, 0)$, and in the same way $A(-3, 0, 1)^{\mathsf T} = 0$.
The two vectors in the parametric form are a basis of the kernel, so the kernel is a plane and the map is far from injective.
Watch out: the fixed points of a map $T$ are the kernel of $T - \id$, not of $T$.

### L3 human

The kernel is the equalizer of $T$ and the zero map, so it is the kernel in the categorical sense in the category of vector spaces.
Together with the cokernel $W / \operatorname{im} T$, it measures how far $T$ is from an isomorphism.
In homological algebra, a sequence $U \to V \to W$ is exact at $V$ when the kernel of the second map is the image of the first.
The same definition gives the kernel of a module homomorphism, a submodule, and the kernel of a [[group homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]], a normal subgroup.
