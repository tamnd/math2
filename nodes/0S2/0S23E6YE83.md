## Summary

The kernel of a [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] $T \colon V \to W$ is the set of vectors $v \in V$ with $T(v) = 0$.
It is a [[subspace|00YC1D652K]] of $V$.
It measures how far $T$ is from being injective: $T$ is injective exactly when its kernel is $\{0\}$.

## Motivation

A homogeneous linear system $Ax = 0$ asks for the vectors that the map $x \mapsto Ax$ sends to $0$.
So the solution set of the system is the kernel of this map.
For any $w$, the solutions of $T(v) = w$ form one shifted copy of the kernel, or the empty set.
So the kernel controls all equations $T(v) = w$, and not only the homogeneous one.

## Notation

The kernel is written $\ker T$, $\ker(T)$ or $N(T)$.
For a matrix $A$, the kernel of $x \mapsto Ax$ is the [[null space|0PHDYNR24D]] of $A$, written $\operatorname{Nul} A$ or $N(A)$.
Some books say "null space of $T$" for the kernel of $T$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[A matrix map of $\R^3$|RZNXVEHTCE]]: the kernel of $x \mapsto Ax$ is the line spanned by $(-1, -1, 1)$.
- [[Differentiation on polynomials of degree at most 3|SMEX3CNE69]]: the kernel is the space of constant polynomials.
- The kernel of the projection $(x, y, z) \mapsto (x, y)$ of $\R^3$ onto $\R^2$ is the $z$-axis.
- The kernel of the [[trace|0XC1RDBY7A]] $M_n(F) \to F$ is the space of matrices with trace $0$, of dimension $n^2 - 1$.
- The kernel of the identity map is $\{0\}$, and the kernel of the zero map $V \to W$ is $V$.

### Non-examples

- [[The solution set of $T(v) = w$ with $w \ne 0$|HSXSS57MS3]] is not a kernel, and it is not a subspace.
- The set $\{v : T(v) = v\}$ of fixed points is not the kernel of $T$. It is the kernel of $T - \id$.

### Basic properties

- $\ker T$ is a subspace of $V$, by the [[lemma on the kernel|REWYZ8N7EN]].
- $T(u) = T(v)$ if and only if $u - v \in \ker T$.
- $T$ is injective if and only if $\ker T = \{0\}$, by the [[theorem on injectivity|0BJBYCGMWE]].
- If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $\dim \ker T + \dim \operatorname{im} T = \dim V$, by the [[rank-nullity theorem|0W4FSW1TVB]]. The dimension of the kernel is the [[nullity|0APCFW7639]].
- $\ker T \subseteq \ker(S \circ T)$ for each linear map $S \colon W \to U$.
- The quotient $V / \ker T$ is isomorphic to the image of $T$, by the [[first isomorphism theorem|0MTBGVZ4XJ]].

### History

The kernel of $x \mapsto Ax$ is the solution set of the homogeneous system $Ax = 0$, studied long before abstract linear maps.
Both names survive: "null space" is common in books on matrices, and "kernel" is shared with homomorphisms of groups and rings.

### Common errors

- Confusing the kernel, a subspace of the source $V$, with the image, a subspace of the target $W$.
- Writing $\ker T = 0$ and reading it as the empty set. It means $\ker T = \{0\}$; the kernel always contains $0$.
- Thinking that a large kernel means a large image. By rank-nullity, the opposite holds.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The kernel of a linear map is the set of vectors that the map sends to zero.
It always contains the zero vector, and it is closed under sums and scalar multiples.
For a projection of space onto the floor, the kernel is the vertical line through the origin.

### L2 human

To find the kernel of $x \mapsto Ax$, solve $Ax = 0$ by row reduction and write the solution in parametric form.
The vectors in the parametric form are a basis of the kernel.
The kernel tells two things: whether $T$ is injective, and how many solutions $T(v) = w$ has when it has one.
The trap is the fixed-point set, which is the kernel of $T - \id$ and not of $T$.

### L3 human

The kernel is the equalizer of $T$ and the zero map, so it is the categorical kernel in the category of vector spaces.
Together with the cokernel $W / \operatorname{im} T$, it measures how far $T$ is from an isomorphism.
In homological algebra, the exactness of $U \to V \to W$ at $V$ means that the kernel of the second map is the image of the first.
Mathlib writes `LinearMap.ker f`, a submodule of the source.
