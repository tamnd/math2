## Summary

A proof turns a well-tested guess into a theorem, and this part of [[reasoning and proof|SGSVYFDSYK]] explains how that happens and what it is worth.
It starts from a warning: the polynomial $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for $n = 0$ to $39$ and fails at $n = 40$, so no number of checked cases can replace a proof.
From there it explains what a proof is, how deduction differs from the guessing that comes before it, and what the labels theorem, lemma and corollary say about a result.
It shows how to read the hypotheses of a theorem by dropping them one at a time, and how the axiomatic method builds a whole subject from a few starting rules.
The later pages turn to the life of a result before and after its proof: conjectures that were proved, refuted or left open, the jobs a proof does besides convincing, and the way examples, generalization and classification shape what mathematicians decide to prove.

## Remarks

### Reading order

1. [[Reasoning and proof|SGSVYFDSYK]]: a map of the whole area, so that you know where each later topic sits.
2. [[Mathematical proof|0B94TKFQJX]]: what counts as a proof, why forty good cases are not one, and how a computer checks a formal proof.
3. [[Deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning|5V35SGEN1T]]: how to tell the step that guesses from the step that proves, and why mathematical induction is a deduction.
4. [[Theorems, lemmas, corollaries and propositions|KK5F0F03ZW]]: what the labels on results signal, and why a lemma is as certain as a theorem.
5. [[Hypotheses and conclusion of a theorem|W48XN2MEM9]]: how to read a statement and to test each hypothesis with a counterexample.
6. [[Axiomatic method|GD2JBXJV5Z]]: how primitive terms, axioms and definitions produce theorems, with small finite geometries as test cases.
7. [[Conjecture|WMEMMHZ3BH]]: how a believed statement becomes a theorem, falls to a counterexample, or stays open.
8. [[Functions of proof|ENY0GKRJS8]]: why mathematicians reprove known results, and what an explaining proof gives that a checking proof does not.
9. [[Methodology of mathematics|3FJ3XMV9KD]]: how concepts are formed and problems are chosen, seen through real episodes.
10. [[Examples in mathematical practice|F3NNR77WKP]]: how generic, extreme and non-examples test conjectures and shape definitions.
11. [[Abstraction and generalization|976RZZCT1H]]: how a theorem grows when a hypothesis is weakened, and when the general proof is the simpler one.
12. [[Classification and invariants|9HQ3TADPA8]]: how to list objects up to equivalence, and which numbers tell them apart.
13. [[Problem solvers and theory builders|Z29HM45R29]]: how single hard problems create theories, and how theories solve old problems.

### Prerequisites

No earlier topic is needed, and the examples use school algebra and geometry.
A few pages, such as those on hypotheses, generalization and classification, also use examples from first-year calculus and algebra, such as continuity, groups and vector spaces.
[[The language of mathematical statements|WMY13MXY3W]] is useful background on "if", "for all" and "there exists".
[[Mathematical logic|DYJ0KGTPMR]] gives the formal side of proof for the reader who wants it.
