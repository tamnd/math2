## Example

Let $p$ be a propositional variable.
We show that the following five formulas form a derivation of $p \to p$ from no hypotheses in the [[Hilbert system|1MGMQX43B8]] L.

$$\begin{array}{rl} 1. & p \to ((p \to p) \to p) \\ 2. & (p \to ((p \to p) \to p)) \to ((p \to (p \to p)) \to (p \to p)) \\ 3. & (p \to (p \to p)) \to (p \to p) \\ 4. & p \to (p \to p) \\ 5. & p \to p \end{array}$$

We check each line against the definition of a derivation.

1. Line 1 is the instance of A1, $\varphi \to (\psi \to \varphi)$, with $\varphi := p$ and $\psi := p \to p$.
2. Line 2 is the instance of A2, $(\varphi \to (\psi \to \chi)) \to ((\varphi \to \psi) \to (\varphi \to \chi))$, with $\varphi := p$, $\psi := p \to p$ and $\chi := p$. The first part $\varphi \to (\psi \to \chi)$ becomes $p \to ((p \to p) \to p)$, which is line 1.
3. Line 3 follows by [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] from line 1 and line 2, since line 2 is the formula "line 1 $\to$ line 3".
4. Line 4 is the instance of A1 with $\varphi := p$ and $\psi := p$.
5. Line 5 follows by modus ponens from line 4 and line 3, since line 3 is the formula "line 4 $\to$ line 5".
6. Each line is an axiom or follows by modus ponens from earlier lines, and the last line is $p \to p$. So $\vdash p \to p$.

## Remarks

The example is the case $\varphi = p$ of the theorem T1 in the [[basic theorems of L|Y060MF2NZ7]].
If $p$ is replaced by any formula $\varphi$ at every place, each line stays an instance of the same axiom or a step of modus ponens.
So the same derivation proves $\vdash \varphi \to \varphi$.
The derivation does not use A3, so it works in every Hilbert system with A1, A2 and modus ponens, such as the [[implicational propositional calculus|ZMVBAY7ZZD]].

The choice $\psi := p \to p$ in line 2 is the step that needs an idea.
It makes the antecedent of line 2 equal to line 1, which is an instance of A1.
After one step of modus ponens, the new antecedent $p \to (p \to p)$ is again an instance of A1, with $\psi := p$.
With $\psi := p$ in line 2 instead, the antecedent of line 2 would be $p \to (p \to p)$, and the remaining antecedent $p \to p$ is the formula we want to derive.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"If $p$ then $p$" is the simplest true law of logic, but the system L does not have it as an axiom.
So we must build it from the axiom patterns, with modus ponens as the only tool.
The five lines do this: two lines fill in the axiom patterns, one more line fills in the first pattern again, and two steps of modus ponens finish.
Each line can be checked by matching it against a pattern, with no thought about meaning.

### L2 human

We use the same method to derive $q \to q$ for a second variable $q$.

1. $q \to ((q \to q) \to q)$, by A1 with $\varphi := q$ and $\psi := q \to q$.
2. $(q \to ((q \to q) \to q)) \to ((q \to (q \to q)) \to (q \to q))$, by A2 with $\varphi := q$, $\psi := q \to q$, $\chi := q$.
3. $(q \to (q \to q)) \to (q \to q)$, by modus ponens from lines 1 and 2.
4. $q \to (q \to q)$, by A1 with $\varphi := q$ and $\psi := q$.
5. $q \to q$, by modus ponens from lines 4 and 3.

The typical use of this derivation is inside the proof of the [[deduction theorem|9X08XK5RV2]], in the case where a line of a derivation is the hypothesis itself.
A trap is to apply modus ponens to line 2 and line 4: line 2 has the antecedent $p \to ((p \to p) \to p)$, not $p \to (p \to p)$, so this step is wrong.
