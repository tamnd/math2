## Summary

The group axioms neither prove nor refute "any two elements commute", and yet first-order logic is complete.
There is no conflict, because "complete" means two different things: one is a property of a logic, the other a property of a theory.

A proof system is complete when it derives every logical consequence:
$$\Gamma \models \varphi \implies \Gamma \vdash \varphi.$$
A theory $T$ is complete, or [[negation complete|319P85G2C1]], when it settles every sentence $\sigma$ of its language:
$$T \vdash \sigma \quad \text{or} \quad T \vdash \neg \sigma.$$
[[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]] of 1929 gives the first property for first-order logic.
Gödel's [[first incompleteness theorem|X9WYNYE29C]] of 1931 shows that the second property fails for every consistent, [[recursively axiomatizable|3ANXZC3BEG]] theory that contains enough arithmetic.

## Motivation

Students meet both theorems under Gödel's name and often read them as opposites.
The completeness theorem is about the logic: the rules of proof capture every consequence of any set of axioms.
The incompleteness theorem is about particular theories: one checkable list of axioms cannot decide every sentence of arithmetic.
Put together, they say that a sentence that a theory leaves undecided is true in some models of the theory and false in others.
That is how [[nonstandard models of arithmetic|1QWN5CV4CG]] enter the story, and it is why the two theorems are best learned side by side.

## Notation

$\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ is derivability in a fixed proof system, such as the Hilbert system of the [[soundness theorem|AVD7T7J063]].
$\Gamma \models \varphi$ is logical consequence: every model of $\Gamma$ satisfies $\varphi$.
$\mathrm{PA}$ is first-order [[Peano arithmetic|0QFT7Z76N5]], and $\mathcal{N} = (\mathbb{N}, 0, S, +, \cdot)$ is its standard model.
Some books say "semantically complete" for the first notion and "syntactically complete" or "negation complete" for the second, and a [[complete theory|655K8Y7VS8]] is often defined as a consistent one with the second property.

## Remarks

### Complete and incomplete theories

The theory of dense linear orders without endpoints is complete.
All its countable models are isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$, and the Łoś-Vaught test turns this into completeness.
Presburger arithmetic, the theory of $(\mathbb{N}, 0, S, +)$ with induction for its language, is complete and decidable.

$\mathrm{PA}$ is incomplete if it is consistent: its Gödel sentence $G$ is neither provable nor refutable in $\mathrm{PA}$.
The set $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{N})$ of all sentences true in $\mathcal{N}$ is complete, and by the incompleteness theorem it is [[not recursively axiomatizable|YSC90EM2M7]].

### Incomplete for other reasons

The theory of groups is incomplete, but not for Gödel's reason.
The sentence $\forall x \forall y \, (x y = y x)$ holds in $\mathbb{Z}$ and fails in the symmetric group $S_3$, so neither it nor its negation follows from the axioms, and no arithmetic is involved.

Second-order Peano arithmetic has only one model up to isomorphism, so every sentence is either true in all its models or false in all of them.
This does not give a complete, recursively axiomatized first-order theory, because second-order logic has no complete and effective proof system.

### How the two theorems fit together

By the completeness theorem, $T \nvdash \sigma$ if and only if some model of $T$ satisfies $\neg \sigma$.
So a consistent theory is complete if and only if all its models satisfy the same sentences, that is, they are elementarily equivalent.
A complete, recursively axiomatizable theory is decidable: list all derivations until $\sigma$ or $\neg \sigma$ appears.
The proof of the incompleteness theorem uses only derivations, not models.
The completeness theorem then turns its conclusion into a statement about models: $\mathrm{PA}$ has a model in which $G$ is false, and such a model is not isomorphic to $\mathcal{N}$.

### History

Kurt Gödel proved the completeness theorem in his doctoral dissertation of 1929 and published it in 1930.
He published the incompleteness theorems in 1931.
Mojżesz Presburger proved in 1929 that his arithmetic of addition is complete and decidable.

### Watch out

Do not read the completeness theorem as "every theory is complete".
It says that every consequence is provable, not that every sentence or its negation is a consequence; the group axioms show the difference.

Do not say that $G$ is "true but unprovable" without naming a model.
$G$ is true in $\mathcal{N}$, and false in some nonstandard models of $\mathrm{PA}$.

Do not blame incompleteness on a weak proof system.
A stronger sound proof system for first-order logic proves nothing new, by the completeness theorem, so the gap lies in the axioms.

### Try it

First, is the theory of fields complete?
No: $\exists x \, (x \cdot x = 1 + 1)$ holds in $\mathbb{R}$ and fails in $\mathbb{Q}$, so the field axioms neither prove nor refute it.

Second, by the completeness theorem, what does $\mathrm{PA} \nvdash G$ give?
It gives a model of $\mathrm{PA}$ in which $\neg G$ is true, since $\mathrm{PA} \not\models G$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"Complete" can describe the rules of reasoning or a list of axioms.
The rules of first-order logic are complete: whatever follows from the axioms can be proved.
A list of axioms is complete when it answers every yes-or-no question in its language.
Gödel showed that the rules are complete, and that no reasonable list of axioms for arithmetic is complete.
Both hold because some questions get different answers in different worlds that obey the same axioms.

### L2 human

We show that $\mathrm{PA}$ has a nonstandard model, if $\mathrm{PA}$ is consistent.

1. Let $G$ be the Gödel sentence of $\mathrm{PA}$.
   By the first incompleteness theorem, $\mathrm{PA} \nvdash G$.
2. By the completeness theorem, $\mathrm{PA} \not\models G$, so some model $\mathcal{M}$ of $\mathrm{PA}$ satisfies $\neg G$.
3. Inside $\mathrm{PA}$, $\neg G$ is equivalent to the statement that some number codes a proof of $G$.
4. No standard number $n$ codes such a proof, since $\mathrm{PA} \nvdash G$, and $\mathrm{PA}$ proves this fact about each standard $n$.
   So the witness in $\mathcal{M}$ is a nonstandard number, and $\mathcal{M}$ is a nonstandard model.

The typical use of the two theorems together is this passage from "not provable" to "false in some model".
Watch out at step 4: the witness exists in $\mathcal{M}$, but it does not code a real, finite proof.

### L3 human

The completeness theorem makes the set of logical consequences of a recursively axiomatizable theory a $\Sigma^0_1$ set.
The incompleteness theorem shows that no consistent theory with this property that contains Robinson arithmetic is complete.
Together they say that first-order theories of arithmetic cannot pin down $\mathcal{N}$.
Second-order logic can pin it down, but then the logic loses its complete proof system.
[[Lindström's theorem|FYZ3CJGZYV]] makes this trade-off exact: a logic stronger than first-order logic loses compactness or the downward Löwenheim-Skolem property.
The [[Henkin construction|RY8KRKR097]] and the [[model existence theorem|G0DTR159RN]] are the tools behind the completeness side.
