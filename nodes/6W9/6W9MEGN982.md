## Definition

A general frame is a Kripke frame with a chosen algebra of admissible propositions, and a descriptive frame is one whose admissible sets determine its worlds and its relation.

Let $(W, R)$ be a [[Kripke frame|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]].
For $X \subseteq W$ let $m_R(X) = \{ w \in W : w R v \text{ for some } v \in X \}$.
A *general frame* is a triple $\mathfrak{g} = (W, R, A)$ where $A \subseteq \mathcal{P}(W)$ is closed under finite unions, under complement relative to $W$, and under $m_R$.
The members of $A$ are the *admissible sets*.
So $(A, \cup, W \setminus \cdot, \emptyset, m_R)$ is a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]] of sets with an operator, the *complex algebra* $\mathfrak{g}^*$ of $\mathfrak{g}$.

A valuation $V$ is *admissible* when $V(p) \in A$ for every variable $p$.
A formula $\varphi$ is *valid* on $\mathfrak{g}$, written $\mathfrak{g} \models \varphi$, when $\varphi$ is true at every world under every admissible valuation.

A general frame $\mathfrak{g} = (W, R, A)$ is:

1. *differentiated* if for all $u \neq v$ in $W$ some $a \in A$ has $u \in a$ and $v \notin a$;
2. *tight* if, whenever $u R v$ fails, some $a \in A$ has $v \in a$ and $u \notin m_R(a)$;
3. *compact* if every family of admissible sets with the finite intersection property has nonempty intersection;
4. *refined* if it is differentiated and tight;
5. *descriptive* if it is refined and compact.

## Notation

Many texts write $\langle R \rangle X$ or $\lozenge X$ for $m_R(X)$.
The *full* general frame of $(W, R)$ is $(W, R, \mathcal{P}(W))$, and we identify it with the Kripke frame.
For a modal algebra $\mathfrak{B} = (B, \vee, \neg, 0, \lozenge)$, its *dual general frame* $\mathfrak{B}_*$ has the [[ultrafilters|CQM84EW10G]] of $B$ as worlds.
Its relation is $u Q v$ if and only if $\lozenge b \in u$ for every $b \in v$, and its admissible sets are the sets $\hat{b} = \{ u : b \in u \}$ for $b \in B$.

## Remarks

### Why each condition is there

Closure under union and complement makes the truth sets of $\vee$ and $\neg$ admissible.
Closure under $m_R$ does the same for $\lozenge$, so by induction every formula has an admissible truth set under an admissible valuation.
Without it, substituting a formula for a variable could leave the admissible valuations, and validity would not be closed under substitution.

The three extra conditions say how well the admissible sets see the frame.
Differentiation says that the admissible sets tell worlds apart.
Tightness says that the relation can be read off from them: $u R v$ holds exactly when $u \in m_R(a)$ for every admissible $a$ that contains $v$.
Compactness says that the worlds are all the points that the admissible sets can describe, so no "point at infinity" is missing.
Together they make $\mathfrak{g}$ isomorphic to the dual of its own complex algebra.

### A small case and a failing case

Take $W = \{0, 1\}$ with $0 R 1$ and all subsets admissible.
For tightness at the pair $(1, 0)$, where $1 R 0$ fails, the set $a = \{0\}$ has $m_R(a) = \emptyset$, which misses $1$.
Every other condition holds as well, so this frame is descriptive, as [[a finite full frame is descriptive|1CEKMPZX9P]] proves in general.

Now take two worlds $a, b$ that see each other and themselves, with only $\emptyset$ and $W$ admissible.
This is a general frame, since $m_R(W) = W$, but it is not differentiated: no admissible set contains $a$ and misses $b$.
Its complex algebra is the two-element algebra, whose dual has one reflexive world.
On $(\mathbb{N}, <)$ with the finite and cofinite sets, differentiation and tightness hold and compactness fails; see [[a refined frame that is not descriptive|F5WN5GCAEQ]].

### What the definitions give

Every normal modal logic $\Lambda$ is the logic of its canonical general frame, with the maximal $\Lambda$-consistent sets as worlds and the sets $\{ \Gamma : \varphi \in \Gamma \}$ as admissible sets.
That frame is the dual of the [[Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra|QC9M3Q8B8Q]] of $\Lambda$, so it is descriptive.
By the [[Jónsson-Tarski representation theorem|HC5V2QFFWB]], every modal algebra $\mathfrak{B}$ is isomorphic to $(\mathfrak{B}_*)^*$ by $b \mapsto \hat{b}$.
A general frame is descriptive exactly when it is isomorphic to $(\mathfrak{g}^*)_*$.
The concept page [[general frame|GGG7BB78JB]] tells the story and gives more examples.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A general frame is a picture of points and arrows, together with a list of allowed regions.
A statement may only be true on an allowed region.
A descriptive frame is one where the allowed regions are rich enough to tell every two points apart and to recover every arrow.
On the numbers $0, 1, 2, \dots$ with "less than", allow the finite regions and the regions that miss finitely many numbers.
Then "true only at $5$" is allowed, and "true exactly at the even numbers" is not.

### L2 human

We check that the finite and cofinite subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ are closed under $m_<$.
Let $X$ be finite and nonempty with largest element $m$.
A world $w$ sees a point of $X$ exactly when $w < m$, so
$$m_<(X) = \{0, 1, \dots, m - 1\},$$
which is finite.
Also $m_<(\emptyset) = \emptyset$.
If $X$ is cofinite, every $w$ has some larger $v \in X$, so $m_<(X) = \mathbb{N}$, which is cofinite.
Finite and cofinite sets are closed under unions and complements, so $(\mathbb{N}, <, A)$ is a general frame.
The typical use of such frames is to validate a logic that its underlying Kripke frame refutes, which is how [[Kripke incompleteness|W67YJQTT50]] is proved.

### L3 human

Descriptive frames are the modal spaces of Jónsson-Tarski duality: [[Stone spaces|6KQ6E3YHKR]] with a relation whose point images are closed and whose operator preserves clopen sets.
The duality extends Stone's duality for Boolean algebras, and it is the setting of the Goldblatt-Thomason theorem and of Sahlqvist canonicity.
Its counterpart for Heyting algebras is Esakia duality, which the [[Blok-Esakia theorem|BB9TA5Q9PN]] uses.
