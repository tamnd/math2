## Definition

Let $n \ge 1$, and let $U$ be a complex $n \times n$ matrix.
The matrix $U$ is *unitary* when
$$U^* U = I_n,$$
where $U^* = \overline{U}^T$ is the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]] of $U$ and $I_n$ is the [[identity matrix|06DW9BAWB1]].

## Notation

- The conjugate transpose is also written $U^H$ or $U^\dagger$.
- The set of unitary $n \times n$ matrices is written $U(n)$.
- Mathlib writes `Matrix.unitaryGroup n ℂ` for this set. Its condition is `star A * A = 1`, and the star of a complex matrix is its conjugate transpose.

## Remarks

- The $(j, k)$ entry of $U^*U$ is $\sum_m \overline{u_{mj}}u_{mk}$, the inner product of column $k$ with column $j$. So the definition says that the columns of $U$ are orthonormal in $\C^n$.
- [[The properties of unitary matrices|GZ7NG7J3J8]] show that $U^{-1} = U^*$, that $UU^* = I_n$, and that the eigenvalues lie on the unit circle.
- A real matrix is unitary exactly when it is [[orthogonal|0QRBVFJ50X]].
- Examples and non-examples are in the node on the [[concept|0AR68JTMDT]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Unitary matrices are the complex version of orthogonal matrices.
Their columns are arrows of length $1$ in complex space, and each two of them are perpendicular.
They keep all lengths and inner products in $\C^n$.

### L2 human

Worked check for $U = \operatorname{diag}(i, -1)$:

1. $U^* = \operatorname{diag}(\overline{i}, -1) = \operatorname{diag}(-i, -1)$.
2. $U^*U = \operatorname{diag}(-i \cdot i, (-1)(-1)) = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1) = I_2$.
3. So $U$ is unitary, and $\det U = -i$.

A trap: without the conjugate, $U^TU = \operatorname{diag}(i^2, 1) = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1) \neq I_2$.
