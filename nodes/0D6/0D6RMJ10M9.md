## Definition

A unitary matrix is a complex square matrix whose conjugate transpose is its inverse.
Let $n \ge 1$, and let $U$ be a complex $n \times n$ matrix.
The matrix $U$ is *unitary* when
$$U^* U = I_n,$$
where $U^* = \overline{U}^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]] of $U$ and $I_n$ is the [[identity matrix|06DW9BAWB1]].

## Notation

The conjugate transpose is also written $U^{\mathsf{H}}$ or $U^\dagger$, and the set of unitary $n \times n$ matrices is written $U(n)$.
Mathlib writes `Matrix.unitaryGroup n ℂ` for this set, for a finite index type `n`.
It is the set `unitary (Matrix n n ℂ)` of matrices `A` with `star A * A = 1` and `A * star A = 1`, where `star` is the conjugate transpose.
The Mathlib version asks for both products, and the [[properties theorem|GZ7NG7J3J8]] shows that for square matrices one of them is enough.

## Remarks

The condition says that the columns of $U$ are orthonormal in $\C^n$.
The entry in position $(j, k)$ of $U^* U$ is
$$\sum_m \overline{u_{mj}} \, u_{mk} = \langle c_k, c_j \rangle,$$
the inner product of column $k$ with column $j$ for $\langle x, y \rangle = \sum_m x_m \overline{y_m}$.
So $U^* U = I_n$ means that each column has length $1$ and that two different columns are orthogonal.

The conjugate is there because complex lengths need it: $\lvert z \rvert^2 = \overline{z} z$, not $z^2$.
The matrix must be square for the condition to give an inverse.
The [[properties theorem|GZ7NG7J3J8]] then shows that $U^{-1} = U^*$, that $U U^* = I_n$ as well, and that the eigenvalues lie on the unit circle.
A real matrix is unitary exactly when it is [[orthogonal|0QRBVFJ50X]], because for real entries $U^* = U^{\mathsf{T}}$.
The [[concept page|0AR68JTMDT]] gives examples and non-examples.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Unitary matrices are the complex version of orthogonal matrices.
Their columns are arrows of length $1$ in complex space, and every two of them are perpendicular.
So they keep all lengths and all inner products in $\C^n$.

### L2 human

Here is the check for $U = \operatorname{diag}(i, -1)$.
Conjugate and transpose: $U^* = \operatorname{diag}(\overline{i}, -1) = \operatorname{diag}(-i, -1)$.
Multiply:
$$U^* U = \operatorname{diag}\big((-i) \cdot i, \ (-1)(-1)\big) = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1) = I_2.$$
So $U$ is unitary, and its determinant is $i \cdot (-1) = -i$, a point of the unit circle that is not $\pm 1$.
Watch out: without the conjugate the test fails, since $U^{\mathsf{T}} U = \operatorname{diag}(i^2, 1) = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1) \ne I_2$.
