## Example

The *Burgers material* is a [[Maxwell material|PQ8Z5HS2JR]] with spring modulus $E_1 > 0$ and dashpot viscosity $\eta_1 > 0$.
It is in series with a [[Kelvin–Voigt material|2SP692ZRAH]] with spring modulus $E_2 > 0$ and dashpot viscosity $\eta_2 > 0$.
In series the stress is common and the strains add.
[[Creep|YFTQJ7VY31]]: under a stress step $\sigma_0$ at time $0$, the Maxwell part contributes $\sigma_0/E_1 + \sigma_0 t/\eta_1$ and the Kelvin–Voigt part contributes $(\sigma_0/E_2)(1 - e^{-E_2 t/\eta_2})$, so
$$\varepsilon(t) = \frac{\sigma_0}{E_1} + \frac{\sigma_0 t}{\eta_1} + \frac{\sigma_0}{E_2} \left(1 - e^{-E_2 t / \eta_2}\right).$$
This creep curve has an instantaneous jump, a primary stage with decreasing rate, and a secondary stage with constant rate $\sigma_0/\eta_1$.

## Remarks

The constitutive law is a second-order linear differential equation relating $\sigma$, $\dot\sigma$, $\ddot\sigma$ to $\dot\varepsilon$ and $\ddot\varepsilon$.
The model is used for asphalt, ice and rocks, which show both delayed elasticity and steady flow.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Burgers model joins the two simplest spring–dashpot models end to end.
Under a constant load it stretches at once, then stretches quickly but more and more slowly, and in the end keeps flowing at a steady rate.
This three-part curve is typical of asphalt on a hot day or of a glacier.
