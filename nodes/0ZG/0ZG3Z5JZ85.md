## Summary

Let $F$ be a figure, that is, a subset of the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ or of space $\mathbb{R}^3$.
A symmetry of $F$ is a [[Euclidean isometry|0J1N3WTKM4]] $f$ of the plane or of space with $f(F) = F$.
Thus $f$ keeps all distances, and it maps the figure onto itself.
The symmetry group $\operatorname{Sym}(F)$ is the set of all symmetries of $F$, with [[composition|0THBK9V825]] $f \circ g$ as the operation.
It is a [[group|G5S1M9]], and we prove this in the Remarks.
The symmetry group measures how symmetric a figure is, and it is a main source of groups in geometry, in crystals and in physics.

## Motivation

A square looks the same after a turn by $90^\circ$ about its centre, and after a flip in a diagonal.
A rectangle that is not a square looks the same after a turn by $180^\circ$, but not after a turn by $90^\circ$.
So the square is more symmetric than the rectangle, and we want to make this exact.
We collect all the motions that leave a figure unchanged.
Two such motions, done one after the other, give a third motion of the same kind.
Each motion can be undone, and the motion "do nothing" is in the collection.
These are the group axioms, and so the symmetries of a figure form a group.
The size and the structure of this group describe the symmetry of the figure.

## Notation

We write $\operatorname{Sym}(F)$ for the symmetry group of $F$.
Some books write $G(F)$ or $\operatorname{Isom}(F)$.
The product $fg$ means $f \circ g$: first apply $g$, then apply $f$.
The symmetries that are rotations (or the identity) form the rotation group $\operatorname{Sym}^+(F)$, also called the proper symmetry group.
The symmetry group of a regular $n$-gon is the [[dihedral group|0JZ27DMJ5D]], written $D_n$ in some books and $D_{2n}$ in others.
Many books also write $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ for the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] of all bijections of a set $X$, and the context tells the two uses apart.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The symmetry group of a [[regular polygon|01KJJ9A400]] with $n \ge 3$ vertices is the dihedral group of order $2n$. It has $n$ [[rotations|0HFREYRSZP]] about the centre, by the angles $360^\circ k / n$ for $k = 0, 1, \ldots, n - 1$, and $n$ [[reflections|06BRDF3F0E]] in lines through the centre. The [[dihedral group of order 8|054SBCQ93T]] is the case of the square.
2. [[The symmetry group of an equilateral triangle|~16f3aba4]] has $6$ elements: $3$ rotations and $3$ reflections. As permutations of the vertices, these are all $6$ elements of the [[symmetric group S3|09H9Y0Q11W]].
3. [[The symmetry group of a rectangle that is not a square|~4f79269e]] has $4$ elements: the identity, the turn by $180^\circ$ and the reflections in the two lines through the midpoints of opposite sides. It is the [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]].
4. The symmetry group of a circle with centre $0$ is the [[orthogonal group|0TKYAEJ8N7]] $O(2)$: all rotations about $0$ and all reflections in lines through $0$. Each symmetry fixes $0$, because $0$ is the midpoint of each two points of the circle at the largest distance. The group is infinite.
5. The rotation group of a regular [[tetrahedron|JFX8ASQS3E]] has $12$ elements, and it is [[isomorphic|08MG7H8JW5]] to the [[alternating group|0Z5905Q73D]] $A_4$. The full symmetry group of the tetrahedron has $24$ elements, and it is isomorphic to $S_4$.
6. The rotation group of a cube has $24$ elements, and it is isomorphic to $S_4$. The reasons for examples 5 and 6 are in the section "Solids" below.

### Non-examples

1. [[The reflections of a square do not form a group|~43a8cd0b]]: closure fails, because the composite of two reflections is a rotation. The set also does not contain the identity.
2. The set of all turns of a square by multiples of $60^\circ$ is not a set of symmetries of the square: a turn by $60^\circ$ does not map the square onto itself, because it moves the vertex $(1, 1)$ to a point that is not a vertex.
3. A stretch $(x, y) \mapsto (2x, y)$ is not a symmetry in the sense of this page, even when it maps a figure onto itself: it is not an isometry, because it doubles the distance between $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$.

### Basic properties

1. $\operatorname{Sym}(F)$ is a group. We check the axioms for a figure $F \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$.
   - Each isometry $f$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ has the form $f(x) = Ax + b$ with $A$ an orthogonal matrix, so $f$ is a [[bijection|0GG9TVW90M]].
   - Closure: if $f$ and $g$ keep distances, then $|f(g(x)) - f(g(y))| = |g(x) - g(y)| = |x - y|$. If $f(F) = F$ and $g(F) = F$, then $f(g(F)) = f(F) = F$.
   - Associativity: composition of functions is associative, because $(f \circ g) \circ h$ and $f \circ (g \circ h)$ both send $x$ to $f(g(h(x)))$.
   - Identity: the [[identity map|0PSKT5W817]] keeps all distances, and it maps $F$ onto $F$.
   - Inverses: put $u = f^{-1}(x)$ and $v = f^{-1}(y)$. Then $|u - v| = |f(u) - f(v)| = |x - y|$, so $f^{-1}$ is an isometry. Since $f$ is a bijection with $f(F) = F$, also $f^{-1}(F) = F$.
2. By property 1, $\operatorname{Sym}(F)$ is a [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] of the group of all isometries of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
3. Let $F$ be a plane figure whose symmetries all fix the point $0$, for example a regular polygon with centre $0$. If $\operatorname{Sym}(F)$ is finite and contains a reflection $s$, then exactly half of its elements are rotations. The map $g \mapsto s g$ sends rotations to reflections and reflections to rotations by the rules in the next section, and it is its own inverse because $s s$ is the identity.
4. A plane isometry that fixes three points $A$, $B$, $C$ not on one line is the identity. The reason: a point $P$ is determined by its distances to $A$, $B$ and $C$, and an isometry keeps these distances. So a symmetry of a polygon is known when we know where it sends the vertices, and $\operatorname{Sym}(F)$ is isomorphic to a group of permutations of the vertices.

### Rotations and reflections

In the plane, let $s_\alpha$ be the reflection in the line through $0$ at the angle $\alpha$ to the $x$-axis.
Let $r_\theta$ be the rotation about $0$ by the angle $\theta$.
In matrices,
$$s_\alpha = \begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\alpha & \sin 2\alpha \\ \sin 2\alpha & -\cos 2\alpha \end{pmatrix}, \qquad r_\theta = \begin{pmatrix} \cos \theta & -\sin \theta \\ \sin \theta & \cos \theta \end{pmatrix}.$$
We multiply the matrices and use the addition formulas for $\cos$ and $\sin$.
This gives $s_\alpha s_\beta = r_{2(\alpha - \beta)}$ and $r_\theta s_\alpha = s_{\alpha + \theta / 2}$.
So two reflections in lines through a point compose to a rotation by twice the angle between the lines.
A reflection followed by a rotation is again a reflection.
A rotation has determinant $1$ and a reflection has determinant $-1$, and this gives the same rules in short form.

### Solids

The rotations of a regular tetrahedron move the $4$ vertices among themselves.
Each symmetry also fixes the centre of the tetrahedron, because the centre is the average of the $4$ vertices.
The rotations that fix a vertex $v$ turn the tetrahedron about the line through $v$ and the centre of the opposite face, so there are $3$ of them (by $0^\circ$, $120^\circ$ and $240^\circ$).
Each vertex can be moved to each other vertex $w$ by a rotation $\rho$.
The rotations that move $v$ to $w$ are then the products $\rho \sigma$ with $\sigma$ a rotation that fixes $v$.
So there are $4 \cdot 3 = 12$ rotations.
They are the identity, $8$ turns by $\pm 120^\circ$ about the $4$ lines through a vertex and the centre of the opposite face, and $3$ turns by $180^\circ$ about the $3$ lines through the midpoints of opposite edges.
On the vertices, these give the identity, the $8$ cycles of length $3$ and the $3$ products of two disjoint transpositions, that is, all of $A_4$.
The reflection in the plane through one edge and the midpoint of the opposite edge swaps two vertices, and it adds the transpositions.
A symmetry that fixes the $4$ vertices is the identity, for the reason of property 4 with four points not in one plane.
So the full symmetry group is isomorphic to a group of permutations of the vertices that contains $A_4$ and a transposition, and this group is $S_4$.

Next, take a cube with centre $0$.
Its rotations include the identity and $9$ turns about the $3$ lines through the centres of opposite faces.
They also include $8$ turns about the $4$ diagonals and $6$ turns about the $6$ lines through the midpoints of opposite edges.
This gives $1 + 9 + 8 + 6 = 24$ different rotations.
We show that there are no others, and that the group is $S_4$.
Each rotation permutes the $4$ diagonals of the cube, and this gives a homomorphism to $S_4$.
Let $d_1 = (1, 1, 1)$, $d_2 = (-1, 1, 1)$, $d_3 = (1, -1, 1)$ and $d_4 = (1, 1, -1)$ be vectors along the diagonals, so that $d_1 = d_2 + d_3 + d_4$.
A rotation that maps each diagonal to itself sends each $d_i$ to $\pm d_i$, and the relation forces the same sign for all four, so the rotation is $I$ or $-I$.
The matrix $-I$ of size $3$ has determinant $-1$, so it is not a rotation, and only the identity fixes all diagonals.
Thus the homomorphism is injective, so the cube has at most $24$ rotations.
The list above has $24$ rotations, so the rotation group has exactly $24$ elements, and the injective homomorphism to $S_4$ is an isomorphism.

### Friezes

A frieze is a pattern on an infinite strip that repeats in one direction, such as a row of copies of the letter F at the points $(k, 0)$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.
The translations $(x, y) \mapsto (x + k, y)$ with $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ are symmetries of this frieze, so its symmetry group is infinite.
The letter F has no symmetry other than the identity, and for this frieze the symmetries are exactly these translations.
The map $k \mapsto$ (translation by $k$) is an isomorphism from the [[additive group of integers|0BSCJA5G53]] to this symmetry group.
Other friezes also have reflections, turns by $180^\circ$ or [[glide reflections|0167F9B344]], and there are exactly seven types of frieze groups.
The patterns that repeat in two directions of the plane have the [[wallpaper groups|MY3CBHARYQ]] as their symmetry groups.

### History

In his Erlangen program of 1872, Felix Klein described each geometry by a group of transformations and the properties that the group keeps.
In 1891 Evgraf Fedorov and Arthur Schoenflies independently found the $230$ symmetry groups of crystal patterns in space, the [[crystallographic groups|7KTJKFQT2N]].
In physics, [[Noether's theorem|XHPDM5T2JG]] of 1918 joins each continuous symmetry of a system to a conserved quantity.

### Common errors

1. The identity is forgotten. The motion "do nothing" is a symmetry of every figure, and it must be counted: a rectangle has $4$ symmetries, not $3$.
2. The order of composition is ignored. For the equilateral triangle, a reflection after a second reflection gives a different rotation than the same reflections in the other order.
3. Rotations are confused with all symmetries. The tetrahedron has $12$ rotations, but $24$ symmetries when we also count reflections.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A symmetry of a shape is a motion that puts the shape back on top of itself.
The motion must not stretch or bend the shape.
A square has $8$ symmetries: $4$ turns (by $0$, $90$, $180$ and $270$ degrees) and $4$ flips.
The capital letter A has $2$ symmetries: "do nothing" and the flip in its middle line.
Two symmetries done one after the other give a symmetry again.
Each symmetry can be undone, and "do nothing" counts as a symmetry.
So the symmetries of a shape form a group, and a larger group means a more symmetric shape.

### L2 human

We find the symmetry group of the capital letter H, drawn with two equal vertical bars and a horizontal bar at the middle.
Put the centre of the letter at $(0, 0)$, with the bars parallel to the axes.

1. The identity $e$ is a symmetry.
2. The turn $r$ by $180^\circ$ about $0$, $(x, y) \mapsto (-x, -y)$, swaps the two bars and maps the middle bar onto itself, so it is a symmetry.
3. The reflection $v$ in the $y$-axis, $(x, y) \mapsto (-x, y)$, swaps the two bars, so it is a symmetry.
4. The reflection $h$ in the $x$-axis, $(x, y) \mapsto (x, -y)$, maps each bar onto itself, so it is a symmetry.
5. A turn by $90^\circ$ makes the vertical bars horizontal, so it is not a symmetry.
6. The products are $vh = hv = r$, $rv = vr = h$ and $rh = hr = v$, and each element is its own inverse.

So $\operatorname{Sym}(\mathrm{H}) = \{e, r, v, h\}$, and it is the Klein four-group.
The letter N has only $e$ and $r$, and the letter A has only $e$ and $v$.
The typical use is to compare figures: two figures with isomorphic symmetry groups have the same kind of symmetry.
A trap is to count only the motions that keep a card face up on the table.
A reflection of a plane figure is a symmetry, although we cannot slide the figure onto its mirror image inside the plane.

### L3 human

The same idea works for any structure: a symmetry of an object is a bijection that keeps all of its structure, and the symmetries form the [[automorphism group|0K1SCPBNKJ]] of the object.
For a set with no structure, this gives the symmetric group, and for a vector space it gives the general linear group.
Each group $G$ is the symmetry group of some structure on the set $G$.
A permutation $f$ of $G$ with $f(xg) = f(x)g$ for all $x, g$ is the left multiplication by $f(e)$, so these permutations form a group isomorphic to $G$.
This is a form of [[Cayley's theorem|0151VYVX27]].
The continuous symmetry groups, such as $O(n)$ and $SO(n)$, are [[Lie groups|7JBRGCVJRB]].
Later sub-areas use symmetry groups as the main examples of a [[group action|0WCY42EV9V]]: the group acts on the figure, and the orbits and stabilizers count the parts of the figure.
