## Statement

Let $T$ be a [[theory|92SWXST1ZM]] in the language $\set{0, S, +, \cdot}$ that contains [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] $\mathsf{Q}$.
Fix a [[Gödel numbering|MFJXPAF0R0]], and let $\ulcorner \psi \urcorner$ be the numeral of the code of $\psi$.
For every [[formula|HYJ10AF6RT]] $\varphi(x)$ with exactly one free variable $x$, there is a [[sentence|DKFGQTRYZE]] $\psi$ such that
$$T \vdash \psi \leftrightarrow \varphi(\ulcorner \psi \urcorner).$$
The sentence $\psi$ is called a fixed point of $\varphi$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. The proof uses $T \supseteq \mathsf{Q}$ only to represent the diagonal function $d$ of the [[proof|~1f171cdf]]. Every theory that represents $d$ in the same sense satisfies the lemma.
2. The formula $\varphi$ must have one free variable, so that $\varphi(\ulcorner \psi \urcorner)$ is a sentence. Formulas with more free variables need the [[diagonal lemma with parameters|P3XFQC6ACX]].
3. The conclusion is a provable equivalence, not an identity of sentences. With prime-power codes, $\# \psi$ is larger than the length of $\psi$, and the numeral $\ulcorner \psi \urcorner$ has $\# \psi$ symbols $S$. So $\varphi(\ulcorner \psi \urcorner)$ is longer than $\psi$ and cannot be equal to it.
4. No consistency hypothesis is needed. If $T$ is inconsistent, every sentence is a fixed point.

### Consequences

1. With $\varphi(x) := \neg \mathrm{Prov}_T(x)$, the lemma gives the Gödel sentence of [[Gödel's first incompleteness theorem|X9WYNYE29C]].
2. With $\varphi(x) := \neg \mathrm{Tr}(x)$ for a supposed truth definition $\mathrm{Tr}$, it gives a liar sentence, and so [[Tarski's undefinability theorem|YRV6SYK2BY]].
3. With $\varphi(x) := \mathrm{Prov}_T(x) \to \sigma$, it gives the sentence of the proof of [[Löb's theorem|3HD30YG3DG]].
4. With $\varphi(x) := \mathrm{Prov}_T(x)$, it gives a [[Henkin sentence|SBMQ5QHG9W]].
5. With the Rosser formula, it gives the sentence of [[Rosser's theorem|~71fdf6fd]].

### Converse

The converse question is whether every sentence is a fixed point of some formula.
This is true and easy: $\psi$ is a fixed point of the formula $\varphi(x) := \psi \wedge x = x$, since $T \vdash \psi \leftrightarrow (\psi \wedge \ulcorner \psi \urcorner = \ulcorner \psi \urcorner)$.
Fixed points of a given formula are not unique as sentences.
For $\varphi(x) := \neg \mathrm{Prov}_T(x)$ and $T \supseteq \mathsf{PA}$, all its fixed points are $T$-equivalent to $\mathrm{Con}(T)$, by the [[second incompleteness theorem|~c79a35b5]].

### Generalizations

1. The [[diagonal lemma with parameters|P3XFQC6ACX]] gives a formula $\psi(\vec{y})$ with $T \vdash \psi(\vec{y}) \leftrightarrow \varphi(\ulcorner \psi(\vec{y}) \urcorner, \vec{y})$.
2. The [[uniform diagonal lemma|YEHH5WV8B6]] gives such a $\psi$ that works for every tuple of numerals.
3. The [[simultaneous diagonal lemma|R8TPVT9G5Z]] gives sentences $\psi_1, \psi_2$ that refer to each other's codes.
4. The [[strong diagonal lemma|7PZDPCVK7Z]] gives a closed term $t$ with $T \vdash t = \ulcorner \varphi(t) \urcorner$, in languages with enough function symbols, such as that of $\mathsf{PRA}$.
5. The lemma is an arithmetic form of [[Kleene's recursion theorem|F3Z30M606H]] and of [[Lawvere's fixed point theorem|N84M16W19X]].

## History

Gödel used a diagonal construction for the one sentence he needed in his 1931 paper on incompleteness.
Carnap was the first to publish the lemma in some generality, in 1934, in terms of truth rather than provability.
The lemma is also called the fixed point lemma.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The lemma says that arithmetic can build sentences that talk about themselves.
Take any property of sentences that a formula can express, such as "has an even code".
The lemma gives a sentence that says, in effect, "my own code is even".
The sentence does not contain its own code literally; it describes a calculation whose result is its code.
This is the same trick as the sentence "the result of writing this phrase twice, the second time in quotes".

### L2 human

1. Take $\varphi(x) := \neg \mathrm{Prov}_T(x)$.
2. Build $\theta(x) := \exists y \, (D(x, y) \wedge \neg \mathrm{Prov}_T(y))$, where $D$ represents the diagonal function.
3. Put $\psi := \theta(\ulcorner \theta \urcorner)$; its code is $d(\# \theta)$.
4. Then $T \vdash \psi \leftrightarrow \neg \mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner \psi \urcorner)$: the sentence says "I am not provable".

The typical use is to produce a sentence with a prescribed relation to its own provability or truth.
A trap: the fixed point is only provably equivalent to $\varphi(\ulcorner \psi \urcorner)$; a property that is not invariant under provable equivalence, such as the length of the sentence, behaves differently.

### L3 human

The lemma is a fixed point theorem: the map $\psi \mapsto \varphi(\ulcorner \psi \urcorner)$ on sentences, up to provable equivalence, has a fixed point.
Lawvere's theorem gives the categorical form: in a cartesian closed category, a point-surjective map $A \to B^A$ forces every endomap of $B$ to have a fixed point.
Here $A$ is the set of formulas with one free variable, $B$ is the set of sentences up to $T$-equivalence, and substitution of codes plays the role of evaluation.
The same pattern gives Cantor's theorem, the recursion theorem, and the Y combinator of the lambda calculus.
