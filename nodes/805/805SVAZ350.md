## Example

Let HLJ be the calculus HLC of the [[definition of hypersequent calculus|3PR9PEMXJ4]] without the communication rule $\mathsf{com}$.
Let LJm be the sequent calculus with the same axioms and the same internal, logical and cut rules, applied to single sequents with no side hypersequent.
LJm is a multiset form of [[LJ|PZX9J3VEMT]], so it derives exactly the intuitionistically valid sequents.
We show that HLJ does not derive $\Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$, although it has hypersequents, external weakening and external contraction.

### The components never interact

**Claim.** If a hypersequent $H$ is derivable in HLJ, then some component of $H$ is derivable in LJm.
We prove the claim by induction on the derivation of $H$.

1. Axiom: $H$ is $A \Rightarrow A$ or $\bot \Rightarrow$, with one component, and that component is an axiom of LJm.
2. External weakening: $H$ is $G \mid S$ and $G$ is derivable.
   By induction a component of $G$ is derivable in LJm, and it is also a component of $H$.
3. External contraction: $H$ is $G \mid S$ and $G \mid S \mid S$ is derivable.
   By induction a component of $G$ or the component $S$ is derivable in LJm, and both are components of $H$.
4. One premise $G \mid S$ and conclusion $G \mid S'$: internal weakening and contraction, ${\wedge}$L, ${\vee}$R and ${\to}$R.
   By induction a component of $G$ is derivable, and it is in $H$, or $S$ is derivable, and the same rule of LJm derives $S'$.
5. Two premises $G \mid S_1$ and $G \mid S_2$ and conclusion $G \mid S'$: ${\wedge}$R, ${\vee}$L, ${\to}$L and cut.
   If a component of $G$ is derivable, it is in $H$.
   Otherwise the induction hypothesis gives $S_1$ and $S_2$ in LJm, and the same rule of LJm derives $S'$.
6. These cases cover every rule of HLJ, so the claim holds.

### A Kripke model refutes the formula

7. Suppose that HLJ derives $\Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$.
   It has one component, so by the claim LJm derives $\Rightarrow (p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$.
8. By soundness of LJ, the formula $(p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$ then holds in every intuitionistic Kripke model.
9. Take a root $r$ and two incomparable worlds $a, b$ above $r$, with $p$ true at $a$ only and $q$ true at $b$ only.
10. At $r$, $p \to q$ fails, because $a \ge r$ forces $p$ and not $q$.
    Also $q \to p$ fails, because $b \ge r$ forces $q$ and not $p$.
11. So the disjunction fails at $r$, which contradicts step 8, and HLJ does not derive the hypersequent.

## Remarks

The proof of the claim uses one feature of the rules: every rule except $\mathsf{com}$ has the same side hypersequent $G$ in all premises and in the conclusion.
So the condition of a hypersequent calculus that fails here is the presence of a rule that makes two components interact.
The [[example of the prelinearity axiom|BNCS8H51B2]] shows that adding $\mathsf{com}$ is enough to derive the formula.

The argument works for any extension of HLJ by rules that act on one component.
External structural rules alone never make a calculus stronger than its sequent part.

What changes if the model is a chain?
The [[Kripke semantics|NGHWZ9Y7ZV]] model of step 9 has two incomparable worlds, and that is essential.
On a model whose worlds form a chain, $(p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$ is valid, and that is why $\mathsf{com}$ is sound for LC.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A hypersequent says "one of these claims holds".
If the rules only ever work on one claim at a time, the bars do nothing useful.
Each proof then shows one of the claims on its own.
So the calculus without the communication rule proves only what ordinary intuitionistic logic proves.
Ordinary intuitionistic logic cannot prove "$p$ gives $q$, or $q$ gives $p$", so this calculus cannot prove it either.

### L2 human

The argument has two parts.
An induction on derivations shows that each derivable hypersequent has a component that is derivable alone.
A Kripke model with three worlds then shows that $(p \to q) \vee (q \to p)$ is not intuitionistically valid, so no component of the one-component goal is derivable.
The typical use of the argument is to check that a new structural rule really adds strength to a hypersequent calculus.
Watch out: external contraction does not let components interact, since it only removes a copy of a component that is already there.
