## Statement

Robinson arithmetic proves every true sentence of the form "some number passes a finite check".
Let $\mathfrak{N}$ be the [[standard structure|WP27CABPA4]] $(\mathbb{N}, 0, S, +, \cdot, <)$, and let $\mathsf{Q}$ be [[Robinson arithmetic|TT8MXRTVBM]].

1. For every $\Delta_0$ sentence $\delta$: if $\mathfrak{N} \models \delta$ then $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \delta$, and if $\mathfrak{N} \not\models \delta$ then $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \neg \delta$.
2. For every $\Sigma_1$ sentence $\varphi$: if $\mathfrak{N} \models \varphi$ then $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \varphi$.

The classes $\Delta_0$ and $\Sigma_1$ are those of the [[definition of the formula classes|Y0JTZXBRT5]].
Part 2 applies to a $\Sigma_1$ formula $\varphi(x_1, \ldots, x_k)$ through its instances: if $\mathfrak{N} \models \varphi(n_1, \ldots, n_k)$, then $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \varphi(\overline{n_1}, \ldots, \overline{n_k})$.
Both parts hold for every theory that extends $\mathsf{Q}$.

## Remarks

### A first use

Take the $\Sigma_1$ sentence $\exists x\,(x \cdot x = \overline{9})$, which is true because $3 \cdot 3 = 9$.
The theorem says that $\mathsf{Q}$ proves it, and the proof follows the witness.
The axioms $\mathsf{Q}4$ to $\mathsf{Q}7$ compute the closed term, as in the proof of $2 + 2 = 4$ for [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]]:
$$\mathsf{Q} \vdash \overline{3} \cdot \overline{3} = \overline{9}.$$
Existential generalization on $\overline{3}$ then gives $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \exists x\,(x \cdot x = \overline{9})$.
Every case works like this: find the witness, then check a $\Delta_0$ fact about numerals.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The restriction to $\Sigma_1$ sentences is needed.
The $\Pi_1$ sentence $\forall x\,(0 + x = x)$ is true in $\mathfrak{N}$, but it is false in [[a model of Q with two extra elements|PR8Z1YPJDK]].
So $\mathsf{Q}$ does not prove it, by [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]].

Truth in $\mathfrak{N}$ is needed too.
$\mathfrak{N}$ is a model of $\mathsf{Q}$, so $\mathsf{Q}$ proves no sentence that is false in $\mathfrak{N}$.

Part 1 decides every $\Delta_0$ sentence, but Part 2 does not refute false $\Sigma_1$ sentences.
A false $\Sigma_1$ sentence has a true $\Pi_1$ negation, and $\mathsf{Q}$ may fail to prove it.
For example, $\exists x\,(0 + x \ne x)$ is false in $\mathfrak{N}$, but $\mathsf{Q}$ does not refute it.

### The converse

For a $\Sigma_1$ sentence $\varphi$, $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \varphi$ implies $\mathfrak{N} \models \varphi$, since $\mathfrak{N} \models \mathsf{Q}$.
So for $\Sigma_1$ sentences, $\mathsf{Q} \vdash \varphi$ holds exactly when $\mathfrak{N} \models \varphi$.
For an extension $T$ of $\mathsf{Q}$ the converse can fail, because a [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]] $T$ may still prove a false $\Sigma_1$ sentence.

### What it gives you

A consistent theory $T$ that extends $\mathsf{Q}$ proves no false $\Pi_1$ sentence.
Suppose that $T \vdash \pi$ with $\pi$ a false $\Pi_1$ sentence.
Then $\neg \pi$ is equivalent to a true $\Sigma_1$ sentence, $T$ proves it by Part 2, and $T$ is inconsistent.

The true $\Sigma_1$ sentences are exactly the $\Sigma_1$ theorems of $\mathsf{Q}$, so this set is [[computably enumerable|TN9G24VABN]].
Part 2 is one half of [[representability|BJF29XET83]], which applies it to the graph of a computable function.
By [[Gödel's first incompleteness theorem|X9WYNYE29C]], a consistent recursively axiomatized extension of $\mathsf{Q}$ leaves some true sentence unproved.
Part 2 shows that such a sentence is never a true $\Sigma_1$ sentence, and the Gödel sentence is in fact $\Pi_1$.

### Generalizations

The theorem can be proved inside stronger theories.
In $\mathsf{PA}$, with a [[provability predicate|NB2BCZR40F]] $\mathrm{Prov}$ for $\mathsf{PA}$, every $\Sigma_1$ sentence $\sigma$ satisfies
$$\mathsf{PA} \vdash \sigma \to \mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner \sigma \urcorner).$$
This formalized form is the main step in the proof of the second incompleteness theorem.

## History

Gödel proved an early form in 1931, as Theorem V of his incompleteness paper.
It says that his formal system proves each true instance of a primitive recursive relation and refutes each false one.
Robinson introduced $\mathsf{Q}$ in 1950, and Tarski, Mostowski and Robinson published it in 1953.
Since then the result has usually been stated for $\mathsf{Q}$, the weakest of the common theories to which it applies.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A $\Sigma_1$ sentence says "there is a number with a property that a finite computation can check".
If such a sentence is true, a witness exists, and the finite check for that witness can be written out as a proof.
The theorem says that the very weak axioms of Robinson arithmetic are enough to write out that proof.
So $\mathsf{Q}$ proves every true statement of the form "this search succeeds".
It may fail to prove true statements of the form "this search never succeeds".

### L2 human

We prove the $\Sigma_1$ sentence $\exists x\,(\overline{2} < x \land x \cdot x = x + x + x)$ in $\mathsf{Q}$, read with the left grouping $(x + x) + x$.
The witness is $3$, since $2 < 3$ and $3 \cdot 3 = 9 = 3 + 3 + 3$.
The inner formula at $\overline{3}$ is a $\Delta_0$ sentence, so Part 1 applies.
For the first conjunct, $\mathsf{Q}8$ needs $v + S\overline{2} = \overline{3}$, and $v = 0$ works:
$$0 + \overline{3} = \overline{3}.$$
For the second conjunct, both closed terms compute to $\overline{9}$, so $\mathsf{Q}$ proves they are equal.
Existential generalization on $\overline{3}$ finishes the proof.

The typical use of the theorem is to show that a theory extending $\mathsf{Q}$ proves every true instance of a computable relation.
Watch out: the theorem says nothing about false $\Sigma_1$ sentences, and $\mathsf{Q}$ cannot refute some of them.

### L3 human

The theorem marks the exact strength of $\mathsf{Q}$: it is complete for $\Sigma_1$ sentences and incomplete for $\Pi_1$ sentences.
A theory is $\Sigma_1$-sound when every $\Sigma_1$ sentence it proves is true.
By the theorem, every $\Sigma_1$-sound extension of $\mathsf{Q}$ proves the same $\Sigma_1$ sentences as $\mathsf{Q}$, so extra axioms can only add $\Pi_1$ and more complex truths.
The third Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability condition, $\mathsf{PA} \vdash \mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner \sigma \urcorner) \to \mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner \mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner \sigma \urcorner) \urcorner)$, is the formalized form applied to the $\Sigma_1$ sentence $\mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner \sigma \urcorner)$.
The [[incompleteness theorems|HXHHWY3328]] use it in exactly this way.
