## Statement

The inner product of two vectors is at most the product of their lengths in absolute value, and the two sides are equal only when the vectors lie on one line.
Let $V$ be an [[inner product space|01EK923SKP]] over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and let $\norm{\cdot}$ be the [[induced norm|0KG4D8AP6G]].
Then, for all $x, y \in V$,
$$\abs{\ip{x}{y}} \leq \norm{x} \, \norm{y}.$$
Equality holds if and only if $x$ and $y$ are [[linearly dependent|0EVHJFTG9Z]], that is, one of them is a scalar multiple of the other.

Two proofs are given, the [[proof by orthogonal projection|6NB1CYDTNK]] and the [[proof by the discriminant of a quadratic|7SW8PQYAEH]], and both cover the complex case and the case of equality.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The norm on the right must be the norm induced by the same inner product.
For the dot product on $\mathbb{R}^2$ paired with the maximum norm, the vectors $x = y = (1, 1)$ give $x \cdot y = 2$, while $\max(1, 1) \cdot \max(1, 1) = 1$.

Positivity of the form is needed for the inequality.
Take the [[Minkowski form|TFGECQ5C32]] $\beta(x, y) = x_1 y_1 - x_2 y_2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, with $x = (1, 1)$ and $y = (1, 0)$.
Then $\beta(x, y)^2 = 1$, but $\beta(x, x)\,\beta(y, y) = 0 \cdot 1 = 0$.

Definiteness is needed for the equality case.
The form $\beta(x, y) = x_1 y_1$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is never negative on the diagonal, but it is zero on $(0, 1)$.
For $x = (0, 1)$ and $y = (1, 0)$ both sides are $0$, although $x$ and $y$ are independent.

Over $\mathbb{C}$ the conjugate is needed even to state the result.
For the [[bilinear form on $\mathbb{C}^2$|3W8AFC4A9P]] the value at $(i, 0)$ is $-1$, so there is no length to put on the right side.

### What it gives you

In a real inner product space with $x, y \neq 0$, the inequality says
$$-1 \leq \frac{\ip{x}{y}}{\norm{x}\,\norm{y}} \leq 1,$$
so there is exactly one [[angle|0ZEHZY85E6]] $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ with this cosine.
It also gives the [[triangle inequality|SRN4T2G6AM]], and with it the fact that the induced norm is a norm.

For the dot product on $\mathbb{R}^n$ the inequality reads $(\sum_k a_k b_k)^2 \leq (\sum_k a_k^2)(\sum_k b_k^2)$.
With $a = (1, 1, 1)$ and $b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$ it gives $(b_1 + b_2 + b_3)^2 \leq 3(b_1^2 + b_2^2 + b_3^2)$.
For $b = (1, 2, 3)$ this is $36 \leq 42$.
For continuous real functions on $[a, b]$ and the integral inner product, it reads $(\int_a^b fg)^2 \leq \int_a^b f^2 \int_a^b g^2$.

### The converse

The equality part is already an equivalence, since equality holds exactly for dependent vectors.
The inequality alone does not single out inner products.
The form $h(x, y) = -\ip{x}{y}$ satisfies $\abs{h(x, y)}^2 \leq h(x, x)\, h(y, y)$ for all $x, y$, because both sides are the same numbers as for the inner product, but $h(x, x) < 0$ for $x \neq 0$.

### Generalizations

The same proof by the discriminant shows $\abs{h(x, y)}^2 \leq h(x, x)\, h(y, y)$ for every positive semidefinite [[Hermitian form|48P4A1CX85]] $h$, and only the equality case needs definiteness.
The inequality holds in every [[Hilbert space|HM2HHE3J51]], and for square integrable functions it is the version for integrals.
For two vectors it says that the [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]] has determinant $\norm{x}^2\norm{y}^2 - \abs{\ip{x}{y}}^2 \geq 0$, and the version for $k$ vectors is the fact that [[Gram matrices are positive semidefinite|0AM050KCAW]].
The Hölder inequality $\sum_k \abs{a_k b_k} \leq (\sum_k \abs{a_k}^p)^{1/p} (\sum_k \abs{b_k}^q)^{1/q}$, with $\frac1p + \frac1q = 1$, extends the version for sums to other exponents, and $p = q = 2$ gives Cauchy-Schwarz back.
The topic [[Cauchy-Schwarz, Hölder and Minkowski inequalities|MEY67W00T7]] treats these.

## History

Cauchy published the inequality for finite sums in 1821.
Bunyakovsky published the version for integrals in 1859, and Schwarz found it again in 1888.
The inequality carries the names of Cauchy and Schwarz, and some books add the name of Bunyakovsky.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The inner product of two vectors is never bigger than the product of their lengths, in absolute value.
In the plane, the dot product equals the product of the lengths times the cosine of the angle, and a cosine is between $-1$ and $1$.
For example, $(3, 4)$ and $(4, 3)$ have dot product $24$, and both have length $5$.
Since $24 \leq 25$, the inequality holds.
The two sides are equal only when the vectors lie on one line through the origin.

### L2 human

Here is a check of the inequality and of its equality case in $\mathbb{C}^2$, with $\ip{x}{y} = x_1\overline{y_1} + x_2\overline{y_2}$.
Take $x = (1, i)$ and $y = (2, 1)$.
Then
$$\ip{x}{y} = 1 \cdot 2 + i \cdot 1 = 2 + i, \qquad \abs{\ip{x}{y}}^2 = 5, \qquad \norm{x}^2 \norm{y}^2 = 2 \cdot 5 = 10.$$
Since $5 < 10$, the inequality is strict, as it must be for independent vectors.
Now take $y = ix = (i, -1)$ instead:
$$\ip{x}{y} = 1 \cdot \overline{i} + i \cdot \overline{(-1)} = -i - i = -2i.$$
So $\abs{\ip{x}{y}} = 2$, and $\norm{x}\,\norm{y} = \sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2} = 2$, so equality holds.
The typical use is to bound an inner product, such as an integral $\int fg$, by two norms that are easier to compute.
Watch out: over $\mathbb{C}$ "dependent" allows complex multiples, and $x$ and $ix$ are dependent although neither is a real multiple of the other.

### L3 human

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality says that the Gram determinant of two vectors is nonnegative, and its general form is the positive semidefiniteness of every [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]].
In a real space it is the condition that makes Euclidean angles possible.
In probability, covariance is an inner product on random variables of mean zero, and the inequality says that a correlation coefficient lies in $[-1, 1]$.
In a [[Hilbert space|HM2HHE3J51]] it gives the continuity of the inner product, and in quantum mechanics it is the first step of the proof of the uncertainty relation.
Within the theory of norms its job is to prove the [[triangle inequality|SRN4T2G6AM]].
