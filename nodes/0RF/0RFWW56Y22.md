## Statement

Let $V$ be an [[inner product space|01EK923SKP]] over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and let $\norm{\cdot}$ be the [[induced norm|0KG4D8AP6G]].
Then, for all $x, y \in V$,
$$|\ip{x}{y}| \leq \norm{x} \, \norm{y}.$$
Equality holds if and only if $x$ and $y$ are [[linearly dependent|0EVHJFTG9Z]], that is, one of them is a scalar multiple of the other.

Two proofs are given: the [[proof by orthogonal projection|6NB1CYDTNK]] and the [[proof by the discriminant of a quadratic|~79f198c9]].
Both cover the real and the complex case and the equality case.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The norm must be the induced norm. For the dot product on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the maximum norm, $x = y = (1, 1)$ give $|x \cdot y| = 2$, but $\max(1, 1)^2 = 1$.
- Positivity is needed for the inequality. For the [[Minkowski form|TFGECQ5C32]] $\beta(x, y) = x_1 y_1 - x_2 y_2$, take $x = (1, 1)$ and $y = (1, 0)$. Then $|\beta(x, y)| = 1$, but $\beta(x, x) \beta(y, y) = 0$.
- Definiteness is needed for the equality case. For the semidefinite form $\beta(x, y) = x_1 y_1$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, take $x = (0, 1)$ and $y = (1, 0)$. Both sides are $0$, but $x$ and $y$ are independent.
- Conjugate symmetry is needed over $\mathbb{C}$. Without it, the [[bilinear form on $\mathbb{C}^2$|~09439661]] gives no norm at all, since $\beta((i, 0), (i, 0)) = -1$.

### Consequences

- The [[triangle inequality|~0cdd82af]] $\norm{x + y} \leq \norm{x} + \norm{y}$ follows from it. So the induced norm is a norm.
- In a real inner product space, $-1 \leq \frac{\ip{x}{y}}{\norm{x}\norm{y}} \leq 1$ for $x, y \neq 0$. So the [[angle|0ZEHZY85E6]] $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ with this cosine is defined.
- For real numbers, $(\sum_k a_k b_k)^2 \leq (\sum_k a_k^2)(\sum_k b_k^2)$. This is the case of the dot product on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- For continuous real functions on $[a, b]$, $(\int_a^b fg)^2 \leq \int_a^b f^2 \int_a^b g^2$.
- The [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]] of two vectors has the determinant $\norm{x}^2\norm{y}^2 - |\ip{x}{y}|^2 \geq 0$, with equality exactly for dependent vectors.

### Converse

The equality part is already an equivalence: equality holds exactly for dependent vectors.
The inequality alone does not characterize inner products.
The form $h(x, y) = -\ip{x}{y}$ satisfies $|h(x, y)|^2 \leq h(x, x) \, h(y, y)$ for all $x, y$, because both sides equal those of the inner product, but $h(x, x) < 0$ for $x \neq 0$.

### Generalizations

- The inequality $|h(x, y)|^2 \leq h(x, x) \, h(y, y)$ holds for every positive semidefinite [[Hermitian form|48P4A1CX85]] $h$, with the same proof by the discriminant. The equality case needs definiteness.
- In every [[Hilbert space|HM2HHE3J51]], with the same proof. For square integrable functions this is the version for integrals.
- The Gram determinant of $k$ vectors is $\geq 0$. For $k = 2$ this is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. The general case is the statement that [[Gram matrices are positive semidefinite|0AM050KCAW]].
- The Hölder inequality $\sum_k |a_k b_k| \leq (\sum_k |a_k|^p)^{1/p} (\sum_k |b_k|^q)^{1/q}$, with $\frac1p + \frac1q = 1$, extends the version for sums to other exponents. The case $p = q = 2$ is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the topic [[Cauchy-Schwarz, Hölder and Minkowski inequalities|MEY67W00T7]] treats the rest.

## History

According to the Wikipedia article, Cauchy published the inequality for finite sums in 1821.
Bunyakovsky in 1859 and Schwarz in 1888 published the version for integrals.
The name of the inequality comes from Cauchy and Schwarz, and some books add the name of Bunyakovsky.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The inner product of two vectors is never bigger than the product of their lengths, in absolute value.
In the plane, the dot product equals the product of the lengths times the cosine of the angle.
A cosine is between $-1$ and $1$.
For example, $(3, 4)$ and $(4, 3)$ have dot product $24$, and their lengths are $5$ and $5$.
Since $24 \leq 25$, the inequality holds.
The two sides are equal only when the vectors lie on one line through the origin.

### L2 human

Worked example: we check the inequality and its equality case in $\mathbb{C}^2$ with the standard inner product $\ip{x}{y} = x_1\overline{y_1} + x_2\overline{y_2}$.

1. Let $x = (1, i)$ and $y = (2, 1)$. Then $\ip{x}{y} = 1 \cdot 2 + i \cdot 1 = 2 + i$, so $|\ip{x}{y}|^2 = 5$.
2. The norms are $\norm{x}^2 = 1 + 1 = 2$ and $\norm{y}^2 = 4 + 1 = 5$, so $\norm{x}^2\norm{y}^2 = 10$.
3. Since $5 \leq 10$, the inequality holds, and it is strict, because $x$ and $y$ are independent.
4. Now let $y = i x = (i, -1)$. Then $\ip{x}{y} = 1 \cdot \overline{i} + i \cdot \overline{-1} = -i - i = -2i$, so $|\ip{x}{y}| = 2$.
5. Also $\norm{y} = |i| \, \norm{x} = \sqrt{2}$, so $\norm{x}\norm{y} = 2$. Equality holds, as the theorem predicts for dependent vectors.

The typical use is to bound an inner product, for example an integral $\int fg$, by two norms that are easier to compute.
A trap: over $\mathbb{C}$, "dependent" allows complex multiples, as in step 4.
The vectors $x$ and $ix$ are dependent, but they are not real multiples of each other.

### L3 human

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality is the statement that the Gram determinant of two vectors is nonnegative.
The general form is the positive semidefiniteness of every [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]].
In a real space it says that the cosine of an angle lies in $[-1, 1]$.
So it is the condition that makes Euclidean angles possible in every inner product space.
In probability, the covariance is an inner product on random variables of mean zero, and the inequality says that a correlation coefficient lies in $[-1, 1]$.
In a [[Hilbert space|HM2HHE3J51]] it gives the continuity of the inner product.
In quantum mechanics it is the first step of the proof of the uncertainty relation.
Its role in the theory of norms is to prove the [[triangle inequality|~0cdd82af]], and so to make the induced norm a norm.
