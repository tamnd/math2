## Definition

Let $G = (N, \Sigma, P, S)$ be a [[context-free grammar|CTCY2R8Q77]].
The grammar $G$ is in *Chomsky normal form* if every rule in $P$ has one of the following forms:

1. $A \to BC$, where $A \in N$ and $B, C \in N \setminus \set{S}$;
2. $A \to a$, where $A \in N$ and $a \in \Sigma$;
3. $S \to \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$ is the empty string.

## Remarks

Example: take $N = \set{S, T, A, B, C}$ and the rules $S \to AB \mid AC$, $T \to AB \mid AC$, $C \to TB$, $A \to a$, $B \to b$.
Each rule has two nonterminals other than $S$ on the right, or one terminal, so all clauses hold.
The nonterminal $T$ generates $\set{a^n b^n : n \ge 1}$, so $C$ generates $\set{a^n b^{n+1} : n \ge 1}$, and $S$ generates $\set{a^n b^n : n \ge 1}$.
Non-example: the rule $S \to aSb$ fails clauses 1 and 2, since its right side mixes terminals and nonterminals and has length $3$.
Every [[context-free language|Q56EWEQYE2]] is generated by a grammar in Chomsky normal form, which can be computed from any context-free grammar for it.
In such a grammar, a derivation of a word of length $n \ge 1$ has exactly $2n - 1$ steps.
