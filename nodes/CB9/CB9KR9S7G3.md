## Statement

Let a fixed amount of an [[ideal gas|N7R0H2VNDJ]] be kept at a fixed pressure.
Then its volume $V$ is proportional to its [[thermodynamic temperature|AJ8M9MGZGF]] $T$:
$$\frac{V}{T} = \text{const},$$
so that for any two such states $V_1 / T_1 = V_2 / T_2$.

## Remarks

It is the special case $p$ and $n$ fixed of the [[ideal gas law|VXVGVDSJ0W]] $pV = nRT$, and it describes the states of an [[isobaric process|WYAY33ZVYQ]] of an ideal gas.
Example: a balloon holding $2\,\mathrm{L}$ of air at $300\,\mathrm{K}$, warmed to $330\,\mathrm{K}$ at constant pressure, expands to $2.2\,\mathrm{L}$.
The temperature must be absolute: in degrees Celsius the volume is not proportional to the temperature, but it is an affine function of it.
Extrapolating the straight lines $V(T)$ of different gases to $V = 0$ gives the same point, the absolute zero $T = 0$, which was historically one route to the absolute temperature scale.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Warm a gas without changing its pressure, and it expands in proportion to its absolute temperature.
Going from $300$ to $330$ kelvin, ten percent warmer, makes the gas take ten percent more room.
This is why a hot-air balloon works: the warm air inside is less dense than the cool air outside.

### L2 human

Charles's law, Boyle's law and Avogadro's law each fix two of the four quantities $p$, $V$, $T$, $n$ and relate the other two; together they give $pV = nRT$.
Its form is the reason the [[thermal expansion coefficient|2NRW3XGP3T]] of an ideal gas is $\alpha = 1/T$.
For real gases it holds only approximately, and well only at low pressure.
