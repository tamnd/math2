## Summary

A teacher asks a class of twelve-year-olds whether the sum of three consecutive integers is always divisible by $3$.
One student reports $1 + 2 + 3 = 6$, $4 + 5 + 6 = 15$ and $10 + 11 + 12 = 33$ and says that it always works.
Another says that the middle number times $3$ gives the sum, because the first number is one less and the last is one more.
The teacher does not say who is right, but asks the class which of the two reasons would convince a skeptic, and why.
In that exchange the class is deciding what counts as a proof.

Reasoning and proof in the classroom studies how a class makes, tests and justifies mathematical claims, and how a teacher organizes that work.
It covers argumentation in whole-class talk, proving tasks, the norms that decide which argument counts, and the roles of the teacher.
Students learn what a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] is from the arguments that their class accepts, long before they meet any formal account of proof.

## Motivation

A proof in a research paper is written for experts and meets the standards of the research community.
A class of ten-year-olds cannot use those standards, but it can still ask "why is this true?" and "does it always work?".
Teachers need to know how such questions can grow into real arguments, and which arguments a class can accept as proof.
Researchers need to describe classroom talk, because much of the learning about proof happens there.
So the field asks what a proof is at each level of schooling, and how the standards of a class can grow toward those of mathematics.

## Notation

"Argumentation" in this research means the public activity of giving and examining reasons, and an "argument" is one chain of reasons for one claim.
"Sociomathematical norms" are the shared expectations of a class about what counts as a good mathematical explanation.
"Proving task" is the common name for a task whose goal is a justified claim, not a numerical answer.

## Remarks

### What counts as a proof at school

Andreas Stylianides (2007), in the Journal for Research in Mathematics Education, proposed a definition of proof for the school classroom.
A proof is an argument for a claim that uses true statements that the class accepts without further justification.
It also uses forms of reasoning that are valid and known to the class, or within its reach.
Finally, it uses forms of expression that the class knows or can understand.
The definition makes the materials of a proof depend on the class, but it keeps the demand for valid reasoning, so an argument from five examples does not qualify at any age.

### Norms that a class builds

Erna Yackel and Paul Cobb (1996) studied second-grade classrooms and introduced sociomathematical norms.
These are the norms of a class about what counts as a different solution, an efficient solution or an acceptable explanation.
They showed how such norms form in the interaction of teacher and students, often when the teacher asks "is that a different way, or the same way?".
A norm such as "a claim about all numbers needs a reason that works for all numbers" is a sociomathematical norm, and the class in the opening is building it.

### A teacher's own classroom

Magdalene Lampert (1990) described her own fifth-grade class, in which students made and argued about conjectures on the last digits of powers.
She drew explicitly on the views of Pólya and Lakatos and organized the lessons around conjecture and refutation.
Her report is a reflective study by a teacher-researcher of one classroom, and it shows what such teaching can look like, not how often it works.

### Studying whole-class arguments

Christine Knipping (2008) gave a method to reconstruct the arguments of a whole class during a proving lesson.
It uses the [[Toulmin model of argumentation|RVZCSFDB2Y]] for each local step and then joins the steps into a global structure for the lesson.
ICMI Study 19, *Proof and Proving in Mathematics Education*, edited by Gila Hanna and Michael de Villiers (2012), gathered this line of work.
Its chapters cover the design of tasks for conjecturing and proving, the interaction of teacher and students, and argumentation in class.

### History

Before the 1980s, proof in school meant mostly the written proofs of Euclidean geometry.
Under the influence of Lakatos and Pólya, researchers began to study proof as an activity of a class.
Research on classroom argumentation grew in the 1990s, with the work of Yackel, Cobb, Lampert and Götz Krummheuer.
The NCTM *Principles and Standards for School Mathematics* (2000) asked for reasoning and proof in all grades, which gave the research a practical aim.
ICMI Study 19 held its conference in Taipei in 2009, and its volume appeared in 2012.

### Watch out

A teacher may accept every argument that convinces the class.
In the opening, the first student convinced several classmates with three examples, so the teacher has to lead the class toward a general reason.
A teacher may also reject every argument that is not written in algebra.
A [[pre-formal proof|SG9XA69J64]], such as the "middle number" argument, can be a valid proof for the class.
A researcher, finally, should not judge a classroom proof by the standards of a research paper, since the question is what the class can know.

### Try it

First, which of the two arguments in the opening meets Stylianides's definition?
The "middle number" argument does, since it works for every triple; the list of three sums does not, since its reasoning is not valid for all triples.

Second, extend the task: is the sum of four consecutive integers ever divisible by $4$?
The sum is $(n - 1) + n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) = 4n + 2$, which always leaves remainder $2$, so never.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In a classroom where students reason, they do not only get answers.
They also say why their answers are right, and they listen to the reasons of others.
A student may say that $5 + 7 = 12$ and $9 + 11 = 20$ show that two odd numbers add to an even number.
The teacher asks whether this works for all odd numbers, and how anyone can be sure.
Another student says that each odd number is some pairs plus one left over, so two odd numbers have two left-overs, which make one more pair.
That is a real argument, and from it the class learns what a proof is.

### L2 human

Here is the rest of the lesson from the opening.
The class has the "middle number" idea, and the teacher asks for a way to say it for every triple.
With the middle number $n$, the three numbers are $n - 1$, $n$ and $n + 1$, so
$$(n - 1) + n + (n + 1) = 3n.$$
The teacher then asks how this differs from the three sums.
The class agrees that the letter covers every integer $n$, while each sum covers one case.
A student adds that $10 + 11 + 12 = 3 \cdot 11$ already showed the reason, which is the idea of a [[generic example|QFMBKN41PW]].

At this point the class has a [[conjecture|WMEMMHZ3BH]], a general argument and a norm.
The typical use of such a lesson is to make the norm "a claim about all numbers needs a general reason" explicit.
Watch out: if the teacher only announces that algebra is better, students learn a rule about form, not the reason behind it.

### L3 human

Classroom research on proof combines three traditions.
The first is the French didactics of Guy Brousseau and Nicolas Balacheff, which studies the conditions under which students need proof.
The second is the interactionist research of Cobb, Yackel and Krummheuer, which focuses on norms and argumentation.
The third is the philosophy of mathematical practice after Lakatos, with its proofs and refutations.
The [[functions of proof|ENY0GKRJS8]] of de Villiers list aims that a class can serve, and [[proof in school curricula|7ZSHDS63QY]] shows how curricula state those aims.
Questions about a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]] to one step of an argument, and how a class repairs it, connect the classroom with the work of Lakatos.
