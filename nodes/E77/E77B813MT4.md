## Summary

Reasoning and proof in the classroom is the study of how a class makes, tests and justifies mathematical claims, and of how a teacher organizes this work.
It covers argumentation in whole-class talk, proving tasks, the norms that decide which argument counts, and the roles of the teacher.
Andreas Stylianides (2007) defined a proof in a school class as an argument that uses statements accepted by the class, valid forms of reasoning known to the class, and forms of expression that the class can understand.
The field matters because students learn what a [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] is from the arguments that their class accepts, long before they meet formal definitions of proof.

## Motivation

A proof in a research paper is written for experts, and its standards are those of the research community.
A class of ten-year-olds cannot use these standards, but it can still ask "why is this true?" and "does it always work?".
Teachers need to know how such questions can lead to real arguments, and which arguments a class can accept as proof.
Researchers need to describe what happens in classroom talk, because most of the learning about proof happens there.
The field asks what a proof is at each level of schooling, and how the standards of a class can grow toward the standards of mathematics.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. A class of eight-year-olds conjectures that the sum of two odd numbers is even. The teacher asks for a reason that works for all odd numbers, and a student pairs counters, as in the [[action proof|ECTGXEPC65]] of this topic.
2. A secondary class measures the angles of many triangles and finds sums near $180^\circ$. The teacher asks why the sum cannot be $181^\circ$, which leads to the [[angle sum theorem|05MDKQBEAF]] and its proof.
3. A university class debates whether an argument for a statement about functions is a proof. The class finds a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]] to one step and repairs it.
4. Magdalene Lampert (1990) described her own fifth-grade class, in which students made conjectures about the last digits of powers and argued about them. She modelled the class on the views of Pólya and Lakatos.

### Non-examples

1. A lesson in which the teacher writes a finished proof on the board and the students copy it is a lesson about a proof. The class does not reason, and the research of this page studies other kinds of lessons.
2. A study of one student alone solving a problem in an interview belongs to the research on problem solving or on [[proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]]. It does not study the class.

### Research findings

1. Erna Yackel and Paul Cobb (1996) introduced sociomathematical norms: the norms of a class about what counts as a different, an efficient or an acceptable mathematical explanation. They showed how such norms form in the interaction of teacher and students.
2. Stylianides (2007) proposed the definition of proof in school above. His definition makes proof depend on the knowledge of the class, and it keeps the requirement of valid reasoning.
3. Christine Knipping (2008) gave a method to reconstruct the structure of the arguments of a whole class during a proving lesson. The method uses the [[Toulmin model of argumentation|RVZCSFDB2Y]] for the local steps and joins them into a global structure.
4. The ICMI Study 19, edited by Gila Hanna and Michael de Villiers (2012), collected research on proof from primary school to university. Its chapters include the design of tasks for conjecturing and proving, the interaction of teacher and students, and argumentation in the classroom.

### History

Before the 1980s, proof in school meant mostly the written proofs of Euclidean geometry.
Under the influence of Lakatos and Pólya, researchers began to study proof as an activity of a class.
Research on classroom argumentation grew in the 1990s, with the work of Yackel, Cobb, Lampert and Götz Krummheuer.
The NCTM standards of 2000 asked for reasoning and proof in all grades, which gave the research a practical aim.
The ICMI Study 19 held its conference in Taipei in 2009, and its volume appeared in 2012.

### Common errors

1. A teacher accepts each argument that convinces the class. A class can be convinced by examples, and the teacher must lead it toward general arguments.
2. A teacher rejects each argument that is not in formal language. A [[pre-formal proof|SG9XA69J64]] can be a valid proof for the class.
3. A researcher reads a classroom proof by the standards of a research paper. The research of this page judges it by the knowledge of the class.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In a classroom where students reason, they do not only get answers.
They also say why their answers are right, and they listen to the reasons of others.
For example, a student may say that $5 + 7 = 12$ and $9 + 11 = 20$ show that two odd numbers add to an even number.
The teacher then asks: does this work for all odd numbers, and how can we be sure?
Another student says that each odd number is a pair of pairs plus one, so two odd numbers give two extra ones, which make a pair.
This is a real argument, and the class can learn from it what a proof is.

### L2 human

We follow one proving lesson in a secondary class, with the task "is the sum of three consecutive integers always divisible by $3$?".

1. Exploration: students compute $1 + 2 + 3 = 6$, $4 + 5 + 6 = 15$ and $10 + 11 + 12 = 33$. Each sum is divisible by $3$.
2. Conjecture: the class states "the sum of any three consecutive integers is divisible by $3$". The teacher writes it as a [[conjecture|WMEMMHZ3BH]], not as a fact.
3. A first argument: a student notes that $10 + 11 + 12 = 3 \cdot 11$, the middle number times $3$. This is a [[generic example|QFMBKN41PW]].
4. A general argument: the teacher asks the class to write the three numbers with a letter. They write $(n - 1) + n + (n + 1) = 3n$.
5. Norms: the teacher asks why step 4 is stronger than step 1. The class agrees that step 4 covers all integers $n$, while step 1 shows the reason in one case.
6. Extension: the class asks about four consecutive integers. The sum $(n - 1) + n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) = 4n + 2$ is never divisible by $4$, which gives a new result.

The typical use of such a lesson is to move a class from examples to general arguments, and to make the norm "a claim about all numbers needs a general reason" explicit.
A trap is step 5: if the teacher only announces that algebra is better, students learn a rule, not the reason.

### L3 human

Classroom research on proof combines three traditions.
The first is the French didactics of Guy Brousseau and Nicolas Balacheff, with its study of the conditions under which students need proof.
The second is the interactionist research of Cobb, Yackel and Krummheuer, with its focus on norms and on argumentation.
The third is the philosophy of mathematical practice after Lakatos, with its focus on proofs and refutations.
The [[functions of proof|ENY0GKRJS8]] of de Villiers give a list of aims that a classroom can serve, and the page on [[proof in school curricula|7ZSHDS63QY]] shows how curricula state these aims.
The [[Toulmin model|RVZCSFDB2Y]] is the most used tool for the analysis of the arguments of a class.
