## Summary

A display calculus is a sequent calculus with enough structural moves to bring any part of a sequent out to stand alone on one side of the turnstile.
For example, the sequent $p \circ q \vdash r$, read "$p$ and $q$ give $r$", becomes $p \vdash {*}q \circ r$, read "$p$ gives not $q$ or $r$".
Exactly, a *display calculus* is a [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] whose sequents $X \vdash Y$ relate *structures*, built from formulas with structural connectives such as a unit $\mathrm{I}$, a negation ${*}$, a comma $\circ$ and a modal ${\bullet}$.
Its invertible structural rules, the *display postulates*, give the *display property*: every antecedent part $Z$ of a sequent can be brought to the form $Z \vdash W$, and every succedent part to the form $W \vdash Z$.
Its logical rules introduce a connective only in a formula that is the whole antecedent or the whole succedent.
The [[definition|1X7D5CBXB7]] gives Belnap's calculus for the tense logic $\mathrm{K_t}$, and Belnap's theorem gives cut elimination for every display calculus whose rules pass eight checks on their shape.

## Motivation

Gentzen's [[cut-elimination theorem|AYRZ98Y0NW]] has to be proved again for each new sequent calculus, and each proof checks many cases by hand.
For many modal and substructural logics, ordinary sequent calculi do not have cut elimination at all.
Belnap asked for a format in which cut elimination follows from simple checks on the shape of each rule.

The hard part of a cut-elimination proof is the parametric case, where the cut formula sits deep inside a sequent.
If every part of a sequent can be displayed, the cut formula can always be brought to the surface, and one uniform argument handles that case.
A second aim was modularity.
The structural rules carry the special properties of a logic, and the logical rules stay the same for all logics, so you change the logic by changing a structural rule.

## Notation

Belnap writes $\mathrm{I}$, ${*}$ and $\circ$ for the structural unit, negation and comma.
Later papers often write a comma or a semicolon for $\circ$, and $\sharp$ for structural negation in substructural settings.
Modal structural connectives are written ${\bullet}$ or $\circ$, depending on the author, so check each paper.
Here $\Box$ is the forward box and $\blacklozenge$ the backward diamond.
The structure ${\bullet}X$ reads as $\blacklozenge$ in the antecedent and as $\Box$ in the succedent.

## Remarks

### A first display

Take $p \circ (q \circ {*}r) \vdash s$ in $\mathrm{DK_t}$, and display $r$.
The postulate D1 moves $p$ to the right, and then $q$:
$$q \circ {*}r \vdash {*}p \circ s, \qquad {*}r \vdash {*}q \circ ({*}p \circ s).$$
The postulate D3 then moves the whole right side to the left and cancels the double ${*}$:
$$ {*}({*}q \circ ({*}p \circ s)) \vdash r.$$
Check: the first sequent reads $(p \wedge q \wedge \neg r) \to s$ and the last reads $(p \wedge q \wedge \neg s) \to r$, and both fail exactly when $p, q$ are true and $r, s$ are false.
The [[full example|83HPD7MWGD]] gives each step and also displays $p$.

### A derivation with a box

The modal postulate D4, ${\bullet}X \vdash Y \iff X \vdash {\bullet}Y$, mirrors the adjunction between $\blacklozenge$ and $\Box$.
It lets a derivation move a modal marker out of the way so that a logical rule can act.
The [[derivation of a box over a conjunction|57BVV7TVAW]] proves $\Box p \wedge \Box q \vdash \Box(p \wedge q)$ without cut and moves ${\bullet}$ across the turnstile three times.
For example, $\Box p \vdash {\bullet}p$ comes from $p \vdash p$ by ${\Box}$L, and D4 turns it into ${\bullet}\Box p \vdash p$, which reads $\blacklozenge \Box p \to p$.

### Why display gives cut elimination

Belnap listed eight conditions C1 to C8 on the rules, and each condition looks at one rule at a time.
The two central ones are C5, that a principal formula of a rule is the whole antecedent or the whole succedent, and C8, that a cut between two principal occurrences of $A$ can be replaced by cuts on smaller formulas.
His theorem says that C2 to C8 give cut elimination, and C1 adds the [[subformula property|Z2NXKDK06G]] for cut-free derivations.
The proof traces the cut formula upward to the places where it is principal, which the display property makes possible.
So adding a new structural rule that passes the checks keeps cut elimination without a new proof.

### What it is not

Gentzen's [[LK|PZX9J3VEMT]] has no display property.
From $p, q \Rightarrow r$ no structural rule of LK reaches a sequent whose antecedent is $p$ alone, because LK has no structural negation to move $q$ across; the [[non-example|TWXM3REK0K]] proves this.

The usual sequent rule for the modal logic K infers $\Box\Gamma \Rightarrow \Box A$ from $\Gamma \Rightarrow A$.
It introduces all formulas of $\Box\Gamma$ at once, so they are not the whole antecedent, and C5 fails.
In the same way, ${\wedge}$L of LK acts on a formula inside a larger antecedent and breaks C5.
That is harmless for LK, which has its own cut-elimination proof, but Belnap's general theorem does not apply to it.

### Changing the logic

Because the logic lives in the structural rules, small changes give new logics.
Add the structural rule "from $X \vdash {\bullet}Y$ infer $X \vdash Y$", which is sound on reflexive frames since $\Box y \to y$ holds there.
Then $\Box A \to A$ follows: from $A \vdash A$, ${\Box}$L gives $\Box A \vdash {\bullet}A$, and the new rule gives $\Box A \vdash A$.
In the other direction, drop the rules W and C from the non-modal part and you get a display calculus for a logic without weakening and contraction, with the same logical rules and the same cut-elimination theorem.

### Watch out

The structural negation ${*}$ changes the side.
In ${*}r \vdash Y$ the structure $r$ is a succedent part, so you can display it as $W \vdash r$ but never as $r \vdash W$.

The structure ${\bullet}X$ does not always mean $\Box$.
In the succedent it reads as $\Box$, but in the antecedent it reads as the backward diamond $\blacklozenge$, and reading it as $\Box$ there makes D4 look unsound.

Display postulates do not change the meaning of a sequent.
They are invertible, and the two sides of each postulate have equivalent formula readings.

### History

Nuel Belnap introduced display logic in his 1982 paper "Display logic" in the Journal of Philosophical Logic.
In his framework, structural connectives can be as rich as the logical connectives, and one cut-elimination theorem covers the whole family.
Display calculi were later given for many modal, tense and substructural logics.

### Try it

Display $q$ in $p \circ q \vdash r$.
D1 in its third form gives $q \vdash {*}p \circ r$, which reads $q \to (\neg p \vee r)$.

Is $s$ an antecedent part or a succedent part of ${*}(p \circ {*}s) \vdash r$?
The structure ${*}(p \circ {*}s)$ is an antecedent part, so $p \circ {*}s$ and then ${*}s$ are succedent parts, and $s$ is an antecedent part.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A sequent is a claim that some assumptions give some conclusions.
A display calculus adds moves that shift a piece of a sequent from one side to the other without changing the claim.
For instance, "$x$ and $y$ give $z$" says the same as "$x$ gives: not $y$, or $z$".
With these moves, any piece can be made to stand alone on one side.
Every rule for a logical symbol then works on a formula that stands alone.
Because all rules have this simple shape, one general argument shows that lemmas can be removed from proofs.

### L2 human

Let us derive necessitation in $\mathrm{DK_t}$: if $\mathrm{I} \vdash A$ is derivable, then so is $\mathrm{I} \vdash \Box A$.
Weakening W turns $\mathrm{I} \vdash A$ into $\mathrm{I} \circ {\bullet}\mathrm{I} \vdash A$, and the unit rule IL removes the $\mathrm{I}$:
$$ {\bullet}\mathrm{I} \vdash A.$$
Now D4 moves the marker to the right, and ${\Box}$R turns it into a box:
$$\mathrm{I} \vdash {\bullet}A, \qquad \mathrm{I} \vdash \Box A.$$
The problem shows the typical use of the display postulates: they move a structure so that a logical rule can act.
Watch out: you cannot apply ${\Box}$R to $\mathrm{I} \vdash A$ directly, because the rule needs the succedent ${\bullet}A$.

### L3 human

Display calculi separate structure from logic.
The structural connectives come in residuated families, and the display postulates state the residuation laws, as in residuated lattices and Heyting algebras.
This is why display works well for tense logics and for the [[Lambek calculus|5FAC9KX4H0]], where every connective has an adjoint.
A modal logic with no backward modality is handled through its tense extension; for K this is $\mathrm{K_t}$, which is conservative over K.
Compared with [[hypersequent calculi|73J7F0FN4A]] and [[labelled sequent calculi|F46GV42XCE]], display calculi have a very general cut-elimination theorem, at the price of a richer notion of structure.
