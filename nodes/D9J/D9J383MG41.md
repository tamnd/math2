## Example

A uniform plane [[supersonic flow|G4EN6Y0ZZ2]] of an ideal gas with [[Mach number|GFHJ858AN1]] $M_1 > 1$ runs along a straight wall.
At a convex corner the wall turns away from the flow by an angle $\theta > 0$.
The flow turns around the corner through a *Prandtl–Meyer expansion fan*: a family of straight [[Mach waves|C5QBSMGHCR]] that all start at the corner.
Upstream, the fan is bounded by the Mach line at angle $\mu_1 = \arcsin(1/M_1)$ to the incoming flow.
Downstream, it is bounded by the Mach line at angle $\mu_2 = \arcsin(1/M_2)$ to the outgoing flow.
Inside the fan the flow is [[isentropic|SH8FHGTMSE]] and depends only on the polar angle around the corner.
The downstream Mach number $M_2$ is fixed by the [[Prandtl–Meyer function|88XSF5YV97]] $\nu$:
$$\nu(M_2) = \nu(M_1) + \theta.$$
Check for air, $\gamma = 1.4$: a sonic flow, $M_1 = 1$ and $\nu(M_1) = 0$, turned by $\theta = 26.38^\circ$ gives $\nu(M_2) = 26.38^\circ$, so $M_2 = 2$.
Then the fan spreads from the Mach line at $90^\circ$ to the incoming flow to the Mach line at $30^\circ$ to the outgoing flow.

## Remarks

Since $\nu$ is increasing, $M_2 > M_1$: the flow accelerates, and its pressure and temperature fall by the isentropic relations.
The turning angle is limited by $\theta \le \nu_{\max} - \nu(M_1)$; for a larger corner angle the flow expands to vacuum and leaves a region next to the wall empty.
For a concave corner the flow is compressed instead, and the Mach waves converge into an oblique [[shock wave|JSDMGGTG7G]] rather than a fan.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When supersonic air reaches a corner where the wall bends away, it cannot be warned in advance, so it turns in a spread-out region starting at the corner.
Inside this fan the air speeds up and its pressure drops smoothly, with no shock.
How much it speeds up depends only on how sharp the corner is, through a single function of the Mach number.
Such fans appear at the rear of supersonic wings and at the edges of rocket exhaust jets.
