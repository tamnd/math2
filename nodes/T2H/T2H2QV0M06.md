## Statement

Let $F$ be a [[field|0QA8CHH8MG]] and let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix over $F$ of [[rank|0EWQEYRVA8]] $r$.

1. (Rank normal form.) There are [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] matrices $P$ of size $m \times m$ and $Q$ of size $n \times n$ such that
$$PAQ = N_r = \begin{pmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
where the zero blocks have sizes $r \times (n - r)$, $(m - r) \times r$ and $(m - r) \times (n - r)$.
Also, $P$ and $Q$ can be chosen as products of elementary matrices.
2. (Classification.) Two $m \times n$ matrices over $F$ are [[equivalent|0P21AVFFSP]] if and only if they have the same rank.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The matrices must have the same size; equivalence is only defined for matrices of one size.
- $F$ must be a field. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the matrices $(2)$ and $(1)$ both have rank $1$, but $(2) = p\,(1)\,q$ with units $p, q = \pm 1$ is impossible. Over a principal ideal domain the classification needs the Smith normal form.

### Consequences

- Row rank equals column rank: $A^{\mathsf{T}} = Q^{\mathsf{T}} N_r^{\mathsf{T}} P^{\mathsf{T}}$, and $N_r^{\mathsf{T}}$ has rank $r$, so $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ has rank $r$.
- A matrix of rank $r$ is a sum of $r$ matrices of rank one, because $N_r = e_1 e_1^{\mathsf{T}} + \dots + e_r e_r^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $A = P^{-1} N_r Q^{-1}$.
- Each matrix of rank $r$ factors as $A = BC$ with $B$ of size $m \times r$ and $C$ of size $r \times n$, both of rank $r$. This is a full rank factorization.
- A square matrix is invertible if and only if its normal form is $I_n$.

### Converse

Part 2 is an equivalence.
The normal form $N_r$ of $A$ is unique, because $r$ is determined by $A$.
But $P$ and $Q$ are far from unique.

### Generalizations

- For a linear map $T \colon V \to W$ of rank $r$ between finite-dimensional spaces. There are bases of $V$ and $W$ in which the matrix of $T$ is $N_r$.
- For similarity of square matrices, the classification is much finer. It is given by the rational canonical form, or by the Jordan normal form over an algebraically closed field.

## Explanations

### L1 human

With row operations and column operations together, every matrix can be brought to a very simple form.
It has $1$s on the diagonal in the first $r$ places and $0$s everywhere else.
The number $r$ is the rank, and it cannot change under these operations.

### L2 human

Worked example: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, of rank $1$.

1. $R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1$ and $R_3 \to R_3 - 3R_1$ give $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. $C_2 \to C_2 - 2C_1$ gives $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = N_1$.
3. So $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. Check: $PA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and $PAQ = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 + 2 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = N_1$.

The [[worked $2 \times 3$ example|RX693922PV]] also needs a column exchange.

### L3 human

The theorem says that $\mathrm{GL}_m(F) \times \mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ has exactly $\min(m, n) + 1$ orbits on the $m \times n$ matrices.
This is the simplest case of a classification of matrices up to a group action.
For similarity, congruence and unitary equivalence, the analogous problems lead to the canonical forms of later sub-areas.
