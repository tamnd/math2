## Statement

A finite-dimensional space and its dual have the same dimension, and the dual basis of any basis is a basis of the dual.
Let $F$ be a field and let $V$ be a [[finite-dimensional|018W3V3TCT]] vector space over $F$ with a basis $b_1, \dots, b_n$.

1. The [[dual basis|0W6M3PSD08]] $b_1^*, \dots, b_n^*$ is a basis of the [[dual space|0M856Q1AMZ]] $V^*$.
2. Each $f \in V^*$ equals $f = \sum_{i=1}^n f(b_i)\, b_i^*$.
3. In particular, $V^*$ is finite-dimensional and $\dim V^* = \dim V$, where $\dim$ is the [[dimension|05G2G0P224]].

## Remarks

### A first computation

Part 2 says that the coordinates of a form in the dual basis are its values on the basis.
Take $V = P_1(\mathbb{R})$, the polynomials $a + bx$, with the basis $b_1 = 1$ and $b_2 = 1 + x$.
To find the dual basis, write a polynomial in the basis:
$$a + bx = (a - b) \cdot 1 + b\,(1 + x).$$
So $b_1^*(a + bx) = a - b$ and $b_2^*(a + bx) = b$.
Now take the form $f(p) = p(3)$.
Its values on the basis are $f(b_1) = 1$ and $f(b_2) = 4$, so part 2 gives
$$f = b_1^* + 4\, b_2^*.$$
Check at $p = 2 + 5x$: the left side is $f(p) = 2 + 15 = 17$, and the right side is $(2 - 5) + 4 \cdot 5 = 17$.

### Why finite dimension is needed

The [[proof|N3391DFGPS]] uses a finite sum in part 2, and that is exactly where infinite dimension breaks the result.
In the space $F^{(\mathbb{N})}$ of finitely supported sequences, the coordinate forms are still independent, but [[they do not span the dual|JCMJDY59CH]].
The form that adds up all the terms of a sequence is not a finite combination of them.
In fact the dual is strictly bigger: the [[Erdős-Kaplansky theorem|S5ET1HG2ZX]] gives $\dim V^* > \dim V$ for every infinite-dimensional $V$.

The field matters too.
Over the ring $\mathbb{Z}$, the module $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by one element, but every $\mathbb{Z}$-linear map $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ is zero.
The reason is that $2 f(\bar 1) = f(\bar 0) = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ forces $f(\bar 1) = 0$.
So the dual module is $0$, and "the same size as the space" fails.

### What it gives you

Since $V$ and $V^*$ have the same finite dimension, they are isomorphic, and $b_i \mapsto b_i^*$ is one isomorphism.
That isomorphism depends on the basis, as the [[change of dual basis lemma|SXWX10HSB6]] and the [[example in the plane|A3CP855PNV]] show.
Applying the theorem twice gives $\dim V^{**} = \dim V$, which is the step that makes the [[map to the double dual|0ZJM9D7NPS]] an isomorphism.
The same count gives $\dim U^0 = \dim V - \dim U$ for the annihilator of a subspace, in the theorem on the [[dimension of the annihilator|06Q1EFVH0Q]].

### The converse and a generalization

If $V^*$ is finite-dimensional, then so is $V$.
Indeed, an infinite-dimensional $V$ has an infinite basis, and its coordinate forms are infinitely many independent elements of $V^*$.

Part 3 is the case $W = F$ of the formula $\dim \Hom_F(V, W) = \dim V \cdot \dim W$ from the [[dimension of the space of linear maps|0Z9N1ER0W0]].
Part 1 also holds for a free module of finite rank over a commutative ring: the dual basis is a basis of the dual module.

### Watch out

Equal dimension gives an isomorphism $V \cong V^*$, but not a preferred one.
For example, in $\mathbb{R}^2$ the bases $(1, 0), (0, 1)$ and $(1, 0), (1, 1)$ both contain $(1, 0)$.
The first sends $(1, 0)$ to the form $x$, and the second sends it to the form $x - y$, since the dual basis of the second basis is $x - y, y$.
So the isomorphism $b_i \mapsto b_i^*$ cannot be used as an identification of $V$ with $V^*$.

### Try it

First, find the dual basis of $b_1 = (1, 1)$, $b_2 = (1, -1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
Solving $(x, y) = s(1, 1) + t(1, -1)$ gives $s = \tfrac{x + y}{2}$ and $t = \tfrac{x - y}{2}$, so $b_1^* = \tfrac{1}{2}(x + y)$ and $b_2^* = \tfrac{1}{2}(x - y)$.

Second, write the form $f(x, y) = 3x + y$ in that dual basis.
Part 2 gives $f = f(b_1)\, b_1^* + f(b_2)\, b_2^* = 4\, b_1^* + 2\, b_2^*$, and indeed $2(x + y) + (x - y) = 3x + y$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear form on a space with $n$ basis vectors is fixed by its $n$ values on those vectors.
These $n$ values can be any numbers you like.
So choosing a form is the same as choosing a list of $n$ numbers.
For the plane, a form is $ax + by$, and choosing it means choosing $a$ and $b$.
That is why the forms make up a space with the same dimension $n$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: the space $V$ of real polynomials of degree at most $2$, and the three forms $f_0(p) = p(0)$, $f_1(p) = p(1)$ and $f_2(p) = p(2)$.
Do they form a basis of $V^*$?
Since $\dim V^* = \dim V = 3$, it is enough to show that the three forms are independent.
Suppose $a f_0 + b f_1 + c f_2 = 0$, and test this on $1$, $x$ and $x^2$:
$$a + b + c = 0, \qquad b + 2c = 0, \qquad b + 4c = 0.$$
The last two equations give $c = 0$ and $b = 0$, and then the first gives $a = 0$.
So the forms are independent, and the dimension count makes them a basis without any check of spanning.
This is the typical use of the theorem: it halves the work of finding a basis of a dual space.

### L3 human

The theorem says that $V \mapsto V^*$ preserves dimension on finite-dimensional spaces.
Together with the injectivity of the evaluation map $V \to V^{**}$, this gives the [[canonical isomorphism with the double dual|0ZJM9D7NPS]].
In infinite dimension the restriction $f \mapsto f|_B$ still gives $V^* \cong F^B$ for a basis $B$ of $V$.
But $F^B$ is much larger than the finitely supported functions on $B$, and its dimension is $\abs{F}^{\abs{B}}$ by the [[Erdős-Kaplansky theorem|S5ET1HG2ZX]].
Functional analysis avoids this growth by keeping only the continuous forms, in the [[continuous dual space|4A82S5FVRT]].
