## Statement

Let $F$ be a field and let $V$ be a [[finite-dimensional|018W3V3TCT]] vector space over $F$ with a basis $b_1, \dots, b_n$.

1. The [[dual basis|0W6M3PSD08]] $b_1^*, \dots, b_n^*$ is a basis of the [[dual space|0M856Q1AMZ]] $V^*$.
2. Each $f \in V^*$ is $f = \sum_{i=1}^n f(b_i)\, b_i^*$.
3. In particular, $V^*$ is finite-dimensional and $\dim V^* = \dim V$, where $\dim$ is the [[dimension|05G2G0P224]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Finite dimension is needed. For $V = F^{(\N)}$, the coordinate forms are independent but [[do not span V*|~d17873c6]], and $\dim V^* > \dim V$ by the [[Erdős-Kaplansky theorem|~ecdd288b]].
- $F$ must be a field, or at least the scalars must allow a basis. Over the ring $\Z$, the module $\Z/2\Z$ is finitely generated, but every $\Z$-linear map $\Z/2\Z \to \Z$ is zero. So its dual is $0$.

### Consequences

- $V$ and $V^*$ are isomorphic, by the classification of finite-dimensional spaces by dimension. The isomorphism $b_i \mapsto b_i^*$ depends on the basis; see the [[change of dual basis lemma|~6d879f22]] and the [[basis-dependent isomorphism of R^2 and its dual|A3CP855PNV]].
- $\dim V^{**} = \dim V$, which is a key step in the theorem on the [[double dual|0ZJM9D7NPS]].
- For $U \subseteq V$, the annihilator satisfies $\dim U^0 = \dim V - \dim U$; see the [[dimension of the annihilator|06Q1EFVH0Q]].

### Converse

If $V^*$ is finite-dimensional, then $V$ is finite-dimensional.
Indeed, an infinite-dimensional $V$ has infinitely many independent coordinate forms, as in the counterexample above.

### Generalizations

- $\dim \Hom_F(V, W) = \dim V \cdot \dim W$ for finite-dimensional $V$ and $W$, by the theorem on the [[dimension of the space of linear maps|0Z9N1ER0W0]]. The case $W = F$ is part 3.
- For a free module of finite rank over a commutative ring, the dual basis is again a basis of the dual module.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear form on an $n$-dimensional space is fixed by its $n$ values on a basis.
These $n$ values can be any numbers.
So the linear forms make up an $n$-dimensional space too.

### L2 human

We use part 2 to write a form in a dual basis of $P_1(\R)$, the polynomials $a + bx$.

1. Take the basis $b_1 = 1$, $b_2 = 1 + x$. Then $b_1^*(a + bx) = a - b$ and $b_2^*(a + bx) = b$, since $a + bx = (a - b) \cdot 1 + b(1 + x)$.
2. Take the form $f(p) = p(3)$. Then $f(b_1) = 1$ and $f(b_2) = 4$.
3. Part 2 gives $f = b_1^* + 4 b_2^*$.
4. Check at $p = 2 + 5x$: $f(p) = 2 + 15 = 17$, and $b_1^*(p) + 4 b_2^*(p) = (2 - 5) + 4 \cdot 5 = 17$.

The typical use is to compute in $V^*$ with coordinates, exactly as in $V$.
A trap: equal dimension does not give a preferred isomorphism $V \to V^*$.

### L3 human

The theorem says that the functor $V \mapsto V^*$ preserves dimension on finite-dimensional spaces.
Together with the injectivity of $V \to V^{**}$, it gives the canonical isomorphism with the double dual.
In infinite dimension, $V^* \cong F^B$ for a basis $B$ of $V$, and the dimension jumps to $\abs{F}^{\abs{B}}$.
