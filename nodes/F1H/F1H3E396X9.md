## Definition

Let $\lambda$ be east longitude, $\varphi$ latitude, $z$ height and $t$ universal time.
Let $\Omega_d = 2\pi / T$, where $T$ is the length of the solar day or of the lunar day.
An *atmospheric tide* is a global-scale periodic oscillation of the atmospheric fields (pressure, temperature, wind) of the form
$$\operatorname{Re} \sum_{s} A_{n,s}(\varphi, z) \, e^{i(n \Omega_d t + s \lambda)}.$$
Here $n \ge 1$ is a fixed integer and the zonal wavenumbers $s$ are integers.
The oscillation is excited by the periodic solar heating of the atmosphere or by the gravitational tidal potential of the Sun or the Moon.
The oscillation is called *diurnal* for $n = 1$, *semidiurnal* for $n = 2$ and *terdiurnal* for $n = 3$.
The component with $s = n$ is the *migrating* tide, and the components with $s \ne n$ are *non-migrating* tides.

## Remarks

A migrating tide depends only on local time $t + \lambda / \Omega_d$, so it follows the apparent motion of the Sun or the Moon westward around the globe.
In classical tidal theory, the linearised equations for a stratified isothermal atmosphere separate into a vertical structure equation and a latitudinal equation.
The latitudinal part is given by the [[Laplace tidal equations|9BF0QWFZA9]], whose eigenfunctions are the Hough functions.

Example: the solar semidiurnal migrating tide is the largest tidal signal in surface pressure.
Its amplitude is about $1$ hPa in the tropics, with maxima near $10$ and $22$ local time.
It is forced mainly by absorption of sunlight by water vapour in the troposphere and by ozone in the stratosphere.

Non-example: a pressure oscillation produced by a travelling weather system has no fixed relation to the solar or lunar day and is not a tide.

The energy flux of an upward-propagating wave is roughly conserved while the density decreases exponentially.
So the amplitude of a freely propagating tidal wave grows roughly like $e^{z / (2 H_s)}$, where $H_s$ is the density scale height.
