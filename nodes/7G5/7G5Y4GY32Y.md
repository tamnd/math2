## Definition

Let $E(t)$ and $I(t)$ be the mean activities at time $t$ of an excitatory and an inhibitory population of neurons.
Let $\tau_e, \tau_i > 0$ be time constants, let $c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4 \ge 0$ be coupling strengths, let $r_e, r_i \ge 0$ be refractory constants, and let $P(t), Q(t)$ be external inputs.
Let $S_e, S_i \colon \R \to (0, 1)$ be increasing sigmoid functions.
The *Wilson–Cowan model* is the system of [[ordinary differential equations|0NT64JV96A]]
$$\tau_e \frac{dE}{dt} = -E + (1 - r_e E)\, S_e(c_1 E - c_2 I + P(t)),$$
$$\tau_i \frac{dI}{dt} = -I + (1 - r_i I)\, S_i(c_3 E - c_4 I + Q(t)).$$

## Remarks

A common choice is the logistic sigmoid $S(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-a(x - \theta)})$ with gain $a > 0$ and threshold $\theta$, which is an [[increasing function|0A7KV2WB7A]] with values in $(0, 1)$.
Example: with $r_e = r_i = 0$, $c_1 = c_2 = c_3 = c_4 = 0$ and constant inputs, the equations decouple and $E(t) \to S_e(P)$, $I(t) \to S_i(Q)$ exponentially; this satisfies the definition, though it has no interaction.
Non-example: the system $\tau \, dE/dt = -E + S(c_1 E + P)$ alone is not a Wilson–Cowan model, because it has no inhibitory population.
The factors $1 - r_e E$ and $1 - r_i I$ account for cells that are refractory; many authors set $r_e = r_i = 0$.
The original paper writes these factors as $k_e - r_e E$ and $k_i - r_i I$ with further constants $k_e, k_i$.
