## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A = (a_{ij})$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$.
Let $S_n$ be the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] of permutations of $\set{1, \dots, n}$, and let $\sgn(\sigma) \in \set{1, -1}$ be the [[sign|0SK90238BH]] of $\sigma$.
Then the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]] of $A$ is
$$\det A = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \sgn(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i \sigma(i)}.$$

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The ring $K$ must be commutative, so that the product $\prod_i a_{i \sigma(i)}$ does not depend on the order of its factors.
The matrix must be square, because a permutation matches the $n$ rows with the $n$ columns.

### Consequences

- The sum has $n!$ terms, and each term takes exactly one entry from each row and from each column.
- For $n = 2$ it gives $ad - bc$, by the [[2 × 2 formula|0ZN2J42SK7]]; for $n = 3$ the six terms are the diagonals of the [[rule of Sarrus|7H2P40X0MV]].
- The determinant is a polynomial in the $n^2$ entries with integer coefficients, homogeneous of degree $n$, and of degree $1$ in each entry.
- So $\det$ is a continuous function on $M_n(\mathbb{R})$, and it commutes with ring homomorphisms: $\det(\varphi(A)) = \varphi(\det A)$ when $\varphi$ is applied to each entry.
- The formula gives the [[determinant of a triangular matrix|0K1KKMFHYH]] and the [[determinant of the transpose|0ZZQ2E1MTE]] at once.

### Converse

The formula determines $\det$, and conversely the [[existence and uniqueness theorem|1C8RRWHD45]] shows that the right side is multilinear, alternating and normalized.
So the formula can serve as the definition, as in Mathlib.

### Generalizations

Replacing $\sgn(\sigma)$ by $1$ gives the [[permanent|X9B1XETBPF]].
Replacing $\sgn$ by another character of $S_n$ gives the immanants.
The same sum with $\sigma$ restricted to fixed-point-free involutions, and suitable signs, gives the [[Pfaffian|NH5HWBPWS9]] of a skew-symmetric matrix.

## History

Determinants originated in the work of Seki in Japan in 1683 and of Leibniz in 1693.
Cramer stated his rule, without proof, in 1750.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To compute a determinant by this formula, choose one entry in each row so that no two lie in the same column.
Multiply the chosen entries, and give the product a plus or a minus sign.
The sign is plus when an even number of swaps puts the chosen columns in order.
Add these signed products over all choices.
For a $2 \times 2$ table there are two choices, which give $ad$ and $-bc$.

### L2 human

We compute $\det A$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. A term is nonzero only if it avoids the zero entries $a_{11}$, $a_{22}$ and $a_{33}$.
2. So $\sigma(i) \neq i$ for all $i$. The only such permutations of $S_3$ are the $3$-cycles $(1\,2\,3)$ and $(1\,3\,2)$, which are even.
3. $(1\,2\,3)$ gives $a_{12} a_{23} a_{31} = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 2$, and $(1\,3\,2)$ gives $a_{13} a_{21} a_{32} = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 = 6$.
4. So $\det A = 2 + 6 = 8$.
5. Check by row $1$: $0 - 1 \cdot (0 - 2) + 2 \cdot (3 - 0) = 2 + 6 = 8$.

The typical use is in proofs, and in hand calculations for matrices with many zeros.
A trap: the formula has $n!$ terms, so it is useless for computation with large $n$; for $n = 20$ there are about $2.4 \cdot 10^{18}$ terms.

### L3 human

The formula expresses $\det$ in the basis of monomials of the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[x_{ij}]$; the determinant of the generic matrix $(x_{ij})$ is an irreducible polynomial.
This is the starting point of the generic matrix method: an identity between polynomials in the entries, proved for invertible matrices over a field, holds over every commutative ring.
The formula also shows that $\det \colon \mathrm{GL}_n \to \mathbb{G}_m$ is a morphism of algebraic groups.
