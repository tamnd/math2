## Example

Let $F$ be a field.
Let $F^{(\N)}$ be the space of sequences $(x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ in $F$ with only finitely many nonzero terms, and let $F^{\N}$ be the space of all sequences in $F$.
Both are [[spaces of sequences|0GZB2SN0PH]] with the termwise operations.
We show that the [[dual space|0M856Q1AMZ]] of $F^{(\N)}$ is isomorphic to $F^{\N}$.
The plan is to record the values of a form on a basis, as in finite dimension.

1. Let $e_k$ be the sequence with $1$ at place $k$ and $0$ elsewhere. The vectors $e_0, e_1, e_2, \dots$ form a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] of $F^{(\N)}$, because each $x$ is the finite sum $\sum_k x_k e_k$ in exactly one way.
2. Define $\Phi \colon (F^{(\N)})^* \to F^{\N}$ by $\Phi(f) = (f(e_0), f(e_1), f(e_2), \dots)$.
3. The map $\Phi$ is linear, since $(f + g)(e_k) = f(e_k) + g(e_k)$ and $(af)(e_k) = a f(e_k)$ for each $k$.
4. The map $\Phi$ is injective. If $\Phi(f) = 0$, then $f$ is zero on a basis, so $f = 0$.
5. The map $\Phi$ is surjective. Given $c = (c_0, c_1, \dots)$ in $F^{\N}$, put $f(x) = \sum_k c_k x_k$. The sum has only finitely many nonzero terms, because only finitely many $x_k$ are nonzero.
6. This $f$ is linear, since each term $c_k x_k$ is linear in $x$, and $f(e_k) = c_k$. So $\Phi(f) = c$.
7. By steps 3 to 6, $\Phi$ is a [[linear isomorphism|0B8T10CE7N]]. It is the case $B = \N$ of the [[theorem on maps determined by a basis|0R8X9XA83A]].

Two forms with numbers show how this works.
Take $x = (2, 0, 5, 0, 0, \dots)$.
The form with $c = (1, 1, 1, \dots)$ adds up the terms of a sequence:
$$f(x) = 1 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 5 = 7.$$
The form with $c_k = 2^k$, that is, $c = (1, 2, 4, 8, \dots)$, gives
$$g(x) = 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 5 = 22.$$

## Remarks

The example shows that the dual of an infinite-dimensional space can be much larger than the space.
The space $F^{(\N)}$ has the countable basis $e_0, e_1, \dots$, but its dual is the space of all sequences.
Let $e_k^*$ be the coordinate form $x \mapsto x_k$.
A finite combination of the $e_k^*$ corresponds to a sequence $c$ with finitely many nonzero terms, so the summing form with $c = (1, 1, 1, \dots)$ is not such a combination.
So the coordinate forms [[do not span the dual|JCMJDY59CH]].

For $F = \Q$ the sizes can be compared directly.
The space $\Q^{(\N)}$ is countable, while $\Q^{\N}$ is uncountable.
A space over $\Q$ with a countable basis is countable, so $\Q^{\N}$ has no countable basis, and the two spaces are not isomorphic.
This is a case of the [[Erdős-Kaplansky theorem|S5ET1HG2ZX]].

The map $(x_k) \mapsto \sum_k x_k t^k$ is an isomorphism from $F^{(\N)}$ onto the [[space of polynomials|0JGZCDT5DN]] $F[t]$.
So $F[t]^* \cong F^{\N}$, and a linear form on polynomials is the same as a free choice of its values on $1, t, t^2, \dots$.
For example, evaluation at $1$ is the form with $c = (1, 1, 1, \dots)$, since $p(1)$ is the sum of the coefficients of $p$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A finitely supported sequence is a list of numbers that is zero after some point.
A linear rule on such lists is fixed by its value on each unit list, the list with one $1$ and zeros elsewhere.
These values can be any infinite list of numbers, with no condition, because each input list has only finitely many nonzero terms.
So the dual space is the space of all infinite lists, which is much larger than the space of finitely supported lists.

### L2 human

The method is the same as in finite dimension: evaluate a form on a basis.
For a form $f$ on $F^{(\N)}$, the numbers $c_k = f(e_k)$ determine $f$ through
$$f(x) = \sum_k c_k x_k,$$
and every sequence $c$ gives a form.
For the form with $c_k = k$ and the input $x = (4, 1, 0, 3, 0, \dots)$, we get $f(x) = 0 \cdot 4 + 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 3 = 10$.
The typical use is to see why duals of infinite-dimensional spaces are larger than the spaces themselves.
Watch out: the coordinate forms $e_k^*$ span only the sequences $c$ with finitely many nonzero terms, not all of $F^{\N}$.
