## Proof

We walk through $\pi$ from the first clause to the last and define $D_i$ for each $i$ with $C_i|_\rho \ne 1$, by induction on $i$, with a case split on the pivot.
Each $D_i$ is either a new clause of $\pi'$, an axiom or a resolvent of earlier $D_j$, or it is equal to an earlier $D_j$.

### Part 1: the construction

1. *Axioms.* Let $C_i \in F$ with $C_i|_\rho \ne 1$.
   Then $C_i|_\rho \in F|_\rho$ by the definition of $F|_\rho$, so we put $D_i = C_i|_\rho$, a new axiom of $\pi'$.
2. *Resolution steps.* Let $C_i = A \cup B$ be the resolvent of $C_j = A \cup \{x\}$ and $C_k = B \cup \{\neg x\}$, with $j, k < i$, and let $C_i|_\rho \ne 1$.
   Then no literal of $A$ or of $B$ is true under $\rho$, and $C_i|_\rho = A|_\rho \cup B|_\rho$.
3. *Case $\rho(x) = 1$.* No literal of $C_k = B \cup \{\neg x\}$ is true under $\rho$, so $C_k|_\rho = B|_\rho$ and $D_k$ is defined.
   We put $D_i = D_k$, and then $D_i \subseteq B|_\rho \subseteq C_i|_\rho$.
4. *Case $\rho(x) = 0$.* In the same way $C_j|_\rho = A|_\rho$, and we put $D_i = D_j \subseteq A|_\rho \subseteq C_i|_\rho$.
5. *Case $x$ not set by $\rho$.* Then $C_j|_\rho = A|_\rho \cup \{x\}$ and $C_k|_\rho = B|_\rho \cup \{\neg x\}$, and neither is $1$.
   So $D_j$ and $D_k$ are defined.
6. If $x \notin D_j$, we put $D_i = D_j$.
   Then $D_i \subseteq A|_\rho \subseteq C_i|_\rho$, since $D_j \subseteq A|_\rho \cup \{x\}$.
7. Otherwise, if $\neg x \notin D_k$, we put $D_i = D_k$, and $D_i \subseteq B|_\rho \subseteq C_i|_\rho$ in the same way.
8. Otherwise $x \in D_j$ and $\neg x \in D_k$.
   We let $D_i$ be the resolvent of $D_j$ and $D_k$ on $x$, a new clause of $\pi'$, and it is contained in $A|_\rho \cup B|_\rho = C_i|_\rho$.

### Part 2: the three properties

9. *Property 1* holds by steps 1 to 8, which define $D_i$ in every case.
10. *Property 2.* Each index $i$ adds at most one new clause to $\pi'$, so $\pi'$ has at most $s$ clauses.
    Each $D_i$ satisfies $\lvert D_i \rvert \le \lvert C_i|_\rho \rvert \le \lvert C_i \rvert$, so the width does not grow.
11. *Property 3.* A clause $D_j$ of $\pi'$ is used at index $i$ in two ways: as a premise in step 8, or passed on unchanged in steps 3, 4, 6 and 7.
    In both cases $C_j$ is a premise of the step that gives $C_i$.
    If $\pi$ is tree-like, each $C_j$ is a premise of at most one step, so each clause of $\pi'$ is used at most once, and $\pi'$ is tree-like.
    Its leaves are the new axioms of step 1, at most one for each leaf of $\pi$.
12. *Refutations.* If $C_s = \square$, then $C_s|_\rho = \square \ne 1$, and $D_s \subseteq \square$.
    So $D_s = \square$, and $\pi'$ is a refutation of $F|_\rho$.

$\blacksquare$

## Idea

Follow the old refutation and keep only what survives the restriction.
When $\rho$ sets the pivot, one premise is already as strong as the conclusion, so we keep that premise and skip the step.
When a restricted premise has already lost its pivot literal, it is again as strong as the conclusion, and we keep it.
Only when both premises still carry the pivot do we make a real resolution step.

## Variants

In the [[proof of Haken's theorem|8D74S641EK]], $\rho$ is a partial matching of pigeons to holes, and $F|_\rho$ is the pigeonhole formula of the remaining pigeons and holes.
In the [[proof of the size-width relation|9BVKW1XJZK]], $\rho$ sets one literal at a time, and the lemma is applied once for each value.
A semantic proof is also possible: $F|_\rho$ is unsatisfiable, so it has some refutation by completeness, but that argument gives no bound on size, and the bound is the whole point of the lemma.
