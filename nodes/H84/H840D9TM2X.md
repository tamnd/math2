## Summary

A symmetry argument finds a change of the problem that leaves it the same, and uses that sameness to do half the work or to fix the answer.

Here is the pattern on a real goal: $\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^2 x \, dx = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
The substitution $x \mapsto \frac{\pi}{2} - x$ maps $[0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$ onto itself and turns $\sin x$ into $\cos x$.
So $\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^2 x \, dx = \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^2 x \, dx$.
The two integrals add up to $\int_0^{\pi/2} 1 \, dx = \frac{\pi}{2}$, so each is $\frac{\pi}{4}$, with no antiderivative of $\sin^2$ needed.

In general, a symmetry of a problem is a transformation $T$ that maps the data of the problem to themselves, and so maps each solution to a solution.
You then prove one case and transfer it by $T$, or note that a unique answer must be fixed by $T$, or write a quantity in two ways and add them.

## Motivation

Many problems treat several objects in the same way.
The system $x + y = 10$, $xy = 21$ does not change when $x$ and $y$ are exchanged, and the two base angles of an isosceles triangle play the same role.
In such a problem, two proofs that differ only in the names of the objects are the same proof.
A careful author writes it once and names the symmetry that transfers it.

Symmetry also guides discovery.
If the problem does not change under $T$, a unique answer cannot change under $T$ either, and that often locates the answer before any computation.
It explains the shape of answers too: a formula for a symmetric problem in $x$ and $y$ comes out symmetric in $x$ and $y$.
George Pólya lists symmetry among the heuristics of problem solving, with the advice to use it and to keep it.

## Notation

A symmetry is often written as a map: $x \leftrightarrow y$ (exchange), $x \mapsto -x$ (reflection), $x \mapsto 1/x$ (inversion), $x \mapsto a - x$ (reflection about $a/2$).
A function $f(x, y)$ is symmetric when $f(x, y) = f(y, x)$, and a function of one variable is even or odd as in [[even and odd functions|0FP31SX5EA]].
The phrase "by symmetry" means that the omitted case follows from the written case by the stated symmetry.
The related phrase "without loss of generality" appears in [[a sentence using wlog|K35RJ9YHC0]].
The elementary symmetric polynomials in $x$ and $y$ are $e_1 = x + y$ and $e_2 = xy$.

## Remarks

### Three ways to use a symmetry

The first way transfers a case.
In the [[isosceles triangle theorem|056VS31A0J]], with $AB = AC$, the reflection in the bisector of the angle at $A$ exchanges $B$ and $C$ and fixes the triangle, so the angles at $B$ and $C$ are equal.

The second way fixes a unique answer.
If $T$ is a symmetry and the problem has exactly one solution $s$, then $T(s)$ is also a solution, so $T(s) = s$.
When several solutions exist, they come in orbits: for the system $x + y = 10$, $xy = 21$, [[Vieta's formulas|0M8EWQJ1YV]] make $x$ and $y$ the roots of $z^2 - 10z + 21 = (z - 3)(z - 7)$, and the exchange swaps $(3, 7)$ with $(7, 3)$.

The third way averages.
If a substitution shows $\int_a^b f = \int_a^b g$, then each equals $\tfrac12 \int_a^b (f + g)$, as in the opening goal.
For an odd $f$ on $[-a, a]$, the substitution $x \mapsto -x$ gives $\int_{-a}^{a} f = -\int_{-a}^{a} f$, so $\int_{-1}^{1} x^3 \cos x \, dx = 0$.

### Where the pattern appears

[[The integral of the square of the sine|C9YTHB7TGH]] works the opening goal in full.
[[A palindromic quartic equation|X1DNC12D6Z]], $x^4 + x^3 - 4x^2 + x + 1 = 0$, is unchanged by $x \mapsto 1/x$, and the symmetric variable $t = x + 1/x$ reduces it to a quadratic.
The sum $1 + 2 + \dots + n$ is unchanged when read backward, and adding the two readings gives $n$ copies of $n + 1$, as in [[two proofs of that formula|D6G69GNKPC]].

The symmetries of a problem are closed under composition and inverses, so they form its [[symmetry group|0ZG3Z5JZ85]], and it is enough to check generators.
Every symmetric polynomial is a polynomial in the elementary symmetric ones, by the [[fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials|0GW1ZZH0R4]], so a symmetric problem can be rewritten in $e_1, \ldots, e_n$.

### When symmetry says nothing

[[Symmetry does not place an extremum at the symmetric point|XJYV3VKP6H]]: on the segment $x + y = 2$, $x, y \ge 0$, the symmetric $x^2 + y^2$ has its minimum at $(1, 1)$ but its maximum at $(2, 0)$ and $(0, 2)$.
The function $x + 2y$ on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ is not symmetric under $x \leftrightarrow y$, and its maximum $\sqrt{5}$ sits at $(1/\sqrt{5}, 2/\sqrt{5})$, where $x \neq y$.
The equation $x + y = 2$ is symmetric, yet its solution $(0, 2)$ is not fixed by the exchange, because only a unique solution must be fixed.

### Watch out

The most common slip assumes that a symmetric function has its extremum at the symmetric point.
A second slip writes "by symmetry" when one hypothesis treats $x$ and $y$ differently, so name the symmetry and check every hypothesis.
A third uses a substitution without checking that it maps the domain onto itself: $x \mapsto 1/x$ is not defined at $0$, so that case needs a separate check.

### History

Symmetry arguments are as old as Greek geometry.
Proclus reports a proof of the isosceles triangle theorem by Pappus that compares the triangle $ABC$ with the same triangle read as $ACB$, in place of Euclid's longer construction.
In "How to Solve It" (1945), George Pólya included symmetry in his short dictionary of heuristics.

### Try it

Compute $\int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin x}{\sin x + \cos x} \, dx$.
The substitution $x \mapsto \frac{\pi}{2} - x$ turns the integrand into $\frac{\cos x}{\cos x + \sin x}$, the two integrals add up to $\frac{\pi}{2}$, and each is $\frac{\pi}{4}$.

Which point of the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 2$ in the first quadrant maximizes $xy$, and does symmetry alone prove it?
The answer is $(1, 1)$, by $2xy \le x^2 + y^2 = 2$, and symmetry only suggests it.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A problem is symmetric when you can swap some of its parts and get the same problem.
The question "which two numbers add up to $10$ and multiply to $21$" does not care which number comes first.
So if $3$ and $7$ is an answer, then $7$ and $3$ is the same answer.
Symmetry saves work: if a proof works for the left half of a picture, its mirror image works for the right half.
If a square is cut along its diagonal, the two halves are mirror images, so each has half the area.

### L2 human

We compute $\int_0^{1} \frac{x^4}{x^4 + (1 - x)^4} \, dx$ by symmetry.

1. Let $I$ be the integral.
   The denominator is positive on $[0, 1]$, because $x$ and $1 - x$ are never both $0$.
2. The substitution $x \mapsto 1 - x$ maps $[0, 1]$ onto itself, and by [[integration by substitution|0D37QFPEZE]] it gives $I = \int_0^1 \frac{(1 - x)^4}{(1 - x)^4 + x^4} \, dx$.
3. Adding the two forms gives $2I = \int_0^1 1 \, dx = 1$, so $I = \tfrac12$.

The pattern fits any integral or sum that does not change under a reflection and whose two forms add up to something simple.
Watch out: the substitution must map the interval onto itself, so $x \mapsto 1 - x$ works on $[0, 1]$ but not on $[0, 2]$.

### L3 human

A symmetry argument uses a group action that preserves a problem.
The solutions form a union of orbits, a unique solution is a fixed point, and averaging over the group (the Reynolds operator) projects a quantity onto its invariant part.
This connects the school method to [[Galois theory|WNQDKQYZ2R]], which studies the symmetries of the roots of a polynomial, and to [[Noether's theorem|XHPDM5T2JG]], which turns a continuous symmetry of a physical system into a conservation law.
In analysis, [[Steiner symmetrization|3YF7P33WRM]] makes a set more symmetric while it keeps the area and does not raise the perimeter, a route to the isoperimetric inequality.
In combinatorics, Burnside's lemma reduces a count to a count of orbits.
A symmetry often reduces a problem to one normalized case, which links the method to [[reduction to a special case|DX2H7AD66G]].
