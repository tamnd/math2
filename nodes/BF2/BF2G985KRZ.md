## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ be the [[shear|HTAWRAC3QE]] of $\mathbb{R}^2$, and let $q(x) = x - 1$.
We show that $q$ kills the eigenvector $e_1$ but is not an [[annihilating polynomial|052M4SX57M]] of $A$.

1. By the definition of the [[value q(A)|0BNEXSKNRV]],
$$q(A) = A - I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
2. Apply $q(A)$ to $e_1 = (1, 0)$: the first column of $q(A)$ is $(0, 0)$, so $q(A) e_1 = 0$.
   So $q$ kills $e_1$, which says again that $e_1$ is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $1$.
3. Apply $q(A)$ to $e_2 = (0, 1)$: the second column of $q(A)$ is $(1, 0)$, so $q(A) e_2 = e_1 \ne 0$.
4. A map that is nonzero on one vector is not the zero map, so $q(A) \ne 0$ and $q$ does not annihilate $A$.
5. The square does annihilate $A$:
$$(A - I)^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
   So $(x - 1)^2$ is an annihilating polynomial of $A$.

## Remarks

The condition $q(A) = 0$ is about every vector of the space at once.
The shear has only one line of eigenvectors, the multiples of $e_1$, so a test on eigenvectors misses the vector $e_2$ that the shear moves.
That is why the shear needs the square $(x - 1)^2$, and in fact $(x - 1)^2$ is its [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]].

The polynomials that kill one fixed vector $v$ also form an ideal, and it contains every annihilating polynomial of $A$.
For $v = e_1$ that ideal is generated by $x - 1$, and for $v = e_2$ it is generated by $(x - 1)^2$, by steps 3 and 5.
If you replace the shear by the identity $I$, the off-diagonal $1$ disappears, $q(I) = 0$, and $x - 1$ becomes an annihilating polynomial.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The polynomial $x - 1$ asks a vector whether the shear leaves it in place.
The vector $(1, 0)$ says yes, because the shear does not move it.
The vector $(0, 1)$ says no, because the shear pushes it to $(1, 1)$.
A polynomial annihilates the shear only if every vector says yes, so $x - 1$ fails.

### L2 human

To decide whether $q$ annihilates a matrix, compute the matrix $q(A)$ and check that every entry is zero.
Here the entry in place $(1, 2)$ of $A - I$ is $1$, and that one entry settles the question.
Watch out: checking $q(A) v = 0$ on the eigenvectors alone is the wrong test, because it misses exactly the part of $A$ that is not diagonalizable.
