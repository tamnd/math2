## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the [[shear|HTAWRAC3QE]], and let $q(x) = x - 1$.

1. $q(A) = A - I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, by the definition of the [[value q(A)|0BNEXSKNRV]].
2. $q(A)e_1 = (0, 0)$. So $q$ kills the vector $e_1$.
3. $q(A)e_2 = (1, 0) \ne 0$. So $q(A) \ne 0$.
4. Hence $q$ is not an [[annihilating polynomial|052M4SX57M]] of $A$.
5. The polynomial $(x - 1)^2$ does annihilate $A$, because $(A - I)^2 = 0$.

## Remarks

- An annihilating polynomial must satisfy $q(A)v = 0$ for every vector $v$, not for one eigenvector.
- The polynomials that kill a single vector $v$ form a larger ideal, generated by the local minimal polynomial of $v$. For $v = e_1$ it is $x - 1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The polynomial $x - 1$ sends the eigenvector $e_1$ to zero.
But it does not send $e_2$ to zero.
So it does not annihilate the whole map.

### L2 human

The typical lesson is to test $q(A) = 0$ as a matrix.
A trap is to test $q(A)v = 0$ on eigenvectors only, which misses the defective part of $A$.
