## Statement

The polynomials that kill a linear map form an ideal, in finite dimension this ideal is not zero, and one monic polynomial generates it.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be a linear map.
Let $I_T = \{ p \in F[x] : p(T) = 0 \}$ be the set of [[annihilating polynomials|0PBN8VEKY8]] of $T$.

1. $I_T$ is an [[ideal|096Z9MP2ME]] of $F[x]$.
2. If $\dim V = n$ is finite, then $I_T$ contains a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $n^2$.
3. If $I_T \ne 0$, then there is exactly one monic polynomial $g$ with $I_T = g\,F[x]$, and $g$ is the unique monic polynomial of least degree in $I_T$.
4. If $I_T \ne 0$ and $d = \deg g$, then $I, T, \dots, T^{d-1}$ is a basis of the algebra $F[T] = \{ p(T) : p \in F[x] \}$, so $\dim F[T] = d$.

The same holds for an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over $F$ in place of $T$.

## Remarks

### What it gives you

The polynomial $g$ of part 3 is the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] of $T$, and the theorem is the reason that the minimal polynomial exists and is unique.
Part 3 also says that every annihilating polynomial is a multiple of $g$, which is the theorem that the [[minimal polynomial divides every annihilating polynomial|0VTAD25A3G]].
The [[proof by counting dimensions|47AJE1N8C3]] proves all four parts.

Part 2 is a weak bound, and it is enough for existence.
The [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]] gives a much better one, a nonzero annihilating polynomial of degree $n$.

### A worked case

Take the [[projection|F5TTF31NR5]] $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{R}$.
First, $P^2 = P$, so $x^2 - x$ lies in $I_P$.
No nonzero constant $c$ lies in $I_P$, since $cI \ne 0$.
No monic $x - c$ lies in $I_P$, because
$$P - cI = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - c & 1 \\ 0 & -c \end{pmatrix}$$
has the entry $1$ in the corner and so is never zero.
So $g = x^2 - x$, and $I_P = (x^2 - x)$.
Part 4 then says that $F[P]$ has the basis $I, P$: every polynomial in $P$ is $aI + bP$ for some numbers $a$ and $b$.

### Inverses as polynomials

Suppose that $V$ is finite-dimensional and that $T$ is invertible.
Then $g(0) \ne 0$.
Indeed, if $g(0) = 0$, then $g = x h$ with $\deg h = d - 1$, and $T\, h(T) = 0$ gives $h(T) = 0$ after we apply $T^{-1}$, which contradicts the least degree of $g$.
Now write $g = x h_1 + g(0)$.
Then $0 = g(T) = T\, h_1(T) + g(0) I$, so
$$T^{-1} = -g(0)^{-1} h_1(T).$$
For example, $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ has $g = x^2 - 3x + 2$, and the formula gives $A^{-1} = -\tfrac{1}{2}(A - 3I) = \operatorname{diag}(1, \tfrac{1}{2})$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Part 2 needs finite dimension.
The [[derivative on smooth functions|1SKH5XJ5RB]] has every $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ as an eigenvalue, with the eigenvector $e^{\lambda x}$.
A nonzero $p$ with $p(D) = 0$ would give $p(\lambda) = 0$ for every real $\lambda$, which is impossible for a nonzero polynomial, so $I_D = 0$.

Parts 3 and 4 need $I_T \ne 0$.
The zero ideal is generated by $0$ only, and $0$ is not monic.
In the example of the derivative, the maps $I, D, D^2, \dots$ are linearly independent, so $F[D]$ has infinite dimension.

### Generalizations

The same proof works for any element $a$ of a finite-dimensional algebra $B$ over $F$: the kernel of $p \mapsto p(a)$ is a nonzero ideal of $F[x]$.
For an element of a finite field extension, its monic generator is the [[minimal polynomial of field theory|0H628RSGWX]].
In the language of modules, $V$ is a module over $F[x]$ with $x$ acting as $T$, and $I_T$ is the annihilator of that module.

## History

Hoffman and Kunze introduce the minimal polynomial as the monic generator of this ideal.
Axler, in *Linear Algebra Done Right*, proves that the minimal polynomial exists before he defines determinants, so the existence does not depend on the characteristic polynomial.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The powers $I, T, T^2, T^3, \dots$ of a linear map on a plane are all $2 \times 2$ matrices, and a $2 \times 2$ matrix is just four numbers.
You cannot have five independent lists of four numbers, so some combination of the first five powers is zero.
That combination is a polynomial that kills $T$.
The theorem adds that all such polynomials are multiples of one smallest polynomial with leading coefficient $1$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: describe all polynomials in $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
First, $N^2 = 0$, so $x^2 \in I_N$.
Next, no polynomial of degree at most $1$ other than $0$ lies in $I_N$, because $aI + bN = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & a \end{pmatrix}$ is zero only for $a = b = 0$.
So $g = x^2$, and part 4 says that every polynomial in $N$ is $aI + bN$.
For example, divide $p(x) = 1 + 2x + 5x^2 + 7x^3$ by $x^2$; the remainder is $1 + 2x$, so
$$p(N) = I + 2N = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The theorem turns every calculation in $F[N]$ into a calculation with remainders.
Watch out: the bound $n^2$ in part 2 is far from the truth, since here $n^2 = 4$ but $\deg g = 2$.

### L3 human

Evaluation $F[x] \to \operatorname{End}_F(V)$ is a ring homomorphism with kernel $I_T$ and image $F[T]$, so $F[T] \cong F[x]/I_T$.
In finite dimension the image is finite-dimensional, so the kernel is nonzero, and since $F[x]$ is a principal ideal domain the kernel has one monic generator.
The same argument shows that every element of a finite field extension is [[algebraic|0VX9EP2GS8]].
The theory of [[modules over a principal ideal domain|WKJXXX0DYG]] refines the ideal $I_T$ into the invariant factors of $T$.
