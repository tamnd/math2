## Statement

Let $K$ be a [[commutative ring|0310REJJY8]], and let $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ with $a, b, c, d \in K$.

1. The [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] of $A$ is $\det A = ad - bc$.
2. Let $K$ be a [[field|0QA8CHH8MG]]. Then $A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] if and only if $ad - bc \neq 0$. In this case $A^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$.
3. Let $K$ be a field, let $e, f \in K$ and let $ad - bc \neq 0$. Then the system $ax + by = e$, $cx + dy = f$ has exactly one solution, namely
$$x = \frac{ed - bf}{ad - bc}, \qquad y = \frac{af - ec}{ad - bc}.$$

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Part 1 needs only a commutative ring, because the formula uses only sums and products.
Parts 2 and 3 divide by $ad - bc$, so they need that $ad - bc$ has an inverse.
Over the ring $\mathbb{Z}$, the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $2 \neq 0$, but it has no inverse with integer entries.
Over a commutative ring the correct test is that $ad - bc$ is a [[unit|0XVEZDE9J8]], by the [[theorem for commutative rings|03Q7RE2HXR]].

### Consequences

- The numerators in part 3 are determinants: $ed - bf = \det \begin{pmatrix} e & b \\ f & d \end{pmatrix}$ and $af - ec = \det \begin{pmatrix} a & e \\ c & f \end{pmatrix}$. This is [[Cramer's rule|0977KZ6PQB]] for $n = 2$.
- Over $\mathbb{R}$, the number $\abs{ad - bc}$ is the area of the parallelogram with sides $(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$. This is the case $n = 2$ of the theorem on [[signed volume|RC5BTSG8T2]].
- The sign of $ad - bc$ tells if the pair of columns has the same [[orientation|0QJJAQ6ZYZ]] as the standard basis.

### Converse

Part 2 is an equivalence, so its converse is part of the statement.
For part 3, the converse also holds over a field: if the system has exactly one solution for one right side, then $ad - bc \neq 0$.
The reason is that $ad - bc = 0$ makes the columns dependent, and then a solution is never unique.

### Generalizations

The [[Leibniz formula|016PE4VZ8Q]] gives the determinant of an $n \times n$ matrix.
For $n = 3$ the [[rule of Sarrus|7H2P40X0MV]] arranges its six terms in a picture.
The [[inverse via the adjugate|0ENVM493VK]] generalizes the inverse formula of part 2.

## History

In 1545, Cardano expressed the solutions of linear systems of two equations by a determinant-like entity.
Cramer stated his rule, without proof, in 1750.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take a square table of four numbers.
Multiply the two numbers on the main diagonal, then multiply the two numbers on the other diagonal.
The determinant is the first product minus the second product.
For the table with rows $1, 2$ and $3, 4$, it is $1 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 3 = -2$.
The size of this number is the area of a parallelogram that the columns make.
When the determinant is zero, the parallelogram is flat, and the matrix has no inverse.

### L2 human

We solve $3x + 2y = 7$ and $x + 4y = 9$ with the formula.

1. The matrix is $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, and $\det A = 3 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 1 = 10$.
2. Since $10 \neq 0$, the system has exactly one solution.
3. $x = (7 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 9)/10 = (28 - 18)/10 = 1$.
4. $y = (3 \cdot 9 - 7 \cdot 1)/10 = (27 - 7)/10 = 2$.
5. Check: $3 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 = 7$ and $1 + 4 \cdot 2 = 9$.

The typical use is a fast test of invertibility and a fast inverse for $2 \times 2$ matrices.
A trap: the formula $ad - bc$ subtracts the product of the other diagonal, and the order of the subtraction matters.
The value $bc - ad$ has the wrong sign.

### L3 human

The map $(a, b, c, d) \mapsto ad - bc$ is the only function of the columns that is linear in each column, zero on equal columns, and $1$ on $I_2$.
So it is the first case of the [[alternating multilinear map|0K6CH0MT0F]] that defines the determinant in every dimension.
Geometrically, $ad - bc$ is the area form $e_1^* \wedge e_2^*$ of the plane, and the group $\mathrm{SL}_2$ is the group of matrices that keep it.
