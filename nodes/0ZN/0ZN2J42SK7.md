## Statement

One number, $ad - bc$, decides everything about a $2 \times 2$ matrix: whether it has an inverse, what the inverse is, and how to solve the linear system that it describes.
Let $K$ be a [[commutative ring|0310REJJY8]], and let $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ with $a, b, c, d \in K$.

1. The [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] of $A$ is $\det A = ad - bc$.
2. Let $K$ be a [[field|0QA8CHH8MG]]. Then $A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] if and only if $ad - bc \neq 0$, and in that case
$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}.$$
3. Let $K$ be a field, let $e, f \in K$, and let $ad - bc \neq 0$. Then the system $ax + by = e$, $cx + dy = f$ has exactly one solution, namely
$$x = \frac{ed - bf}{ad - bc}, \qquad y = \frac{af - ec}{ad - bc}.$$

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
Multiply along the main diagonal, multiply along the other diagonal, and subtract:
$$\det A = 3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 = 5.$$
The determinant is not zero, so part 2 gives the inverse.
Swap the diagonal entries, change the sign of the other two, and divide by $5$:
$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{5} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, and dividing by $5$ gives $I_2$.

Now take $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, with $\det B = 4 - 4 = 0$.
Its second column is twice its first column.
The system $2x + 4y = e$, $x + 2y = f$ has a solution only when $e = 2f$, and then it has infinitely many.
So a zero determinant destroys both the inverse and the uniqueness of solutions.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Part 1 needs only a commutative ring, because the formula uses only sums and products.
Parts 2 and 3 divide by $ad - bc$, so they need an inverse of that number.
Over the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ this is a real restriction.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $2 \neq 0$, but its inverse $\begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has an entry outside $\mathbb{Z}$.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $1$, and its inverse $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is an integer matrix.
Over a commutative ring the correct test is that $ad - bc$ is a [[unit|0XVEZDE9J8]], as the theorem on [[determinants over commutative rings|03Q7RE2HXR]] shows.

### What it gives you

The numerators in part 3 are determinants too:
$$ed - bf = \det \begin{pmatrix} e & b \\ f & d \end{pmatrix}, \qquad af - ec = \det \begin{pmatrix} a & e \\ c & f \end{pmatrix}.$$
Each one is $\det A$ with one column replaced by the right side, and this is [[Cramer's rule|0977KZ6PQB]] for $n = 2$.

Over $\mathbb{R}$, the number $\lvert ad - bc \rvert$ is the area of the parallelogram with sides $(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$, the columns of $A$.
For the matrix $A$ above, the columns $(3, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$ span a parallelogram of area $5$.
This is the case $n = 2$ of the theorem on the [[determinant as a volume scaling factor|RC5BTSG8T2]].
The sign of $ad - bc$ says whether the columns have the same [[orientation|0QJJAQ6ZYZ]] as the standard basis, that is, whether the turn from the first column to the second is counterclockwise.

### The converse

Part 2 is an equivalence, so its converse is already part of the statement.
Part 3 also has a converse over a field.
If the system has exactly one solution for one right side, then $ad - bc \neq 0$.
The reason is that $ad - bc = 0$ makes the columns dependent, so some nonzero $(x_0, y_0)$ solves the system with right side $0$, and adding it to a solution gives a second solution.

### Watch out

The determinant is not linear in the matrix.
Doubling every entry multiplies each of the two products by $4$, so $\det(2A) = 4 \det A$.
For $A = I_2$ this gives $\det(2 I_2) = 4$, not $2$.
A second slip is in the inverse: the diagonal entries change places, while the other two entries stay in place and only change sign.

### Generalizations

The [[Leibniz formula|016PE4VZ8Q]] gives the determinant of an $n \times n$ matrix as a sum of $n!$ signed products.
For $n = 3$ the [[rule of Sarrus|7H2P40X0MV]] arranges its six terms in a picture.
The inverse formula of part 2 is the case $n = 2$ of the [[inverse via the adjugate|0ENVM493VK]], because the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$ is the adjugate of $A$.

### Try it

Compute the inverse of $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
The determinant is $5 - 6 = -1$, so the inverse is $-\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ -2 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 3 \\ 2 & -5 \end{pmatrix}$.

For which real $t$ is $\begin{pmatrix} t & 2 \\ 3 & t - 1 \end{pmatrix}$ not invertible?
The determinant is $t(t - 1) - 6 = (t - 3)(t + 2)$, so the answer is $t = 3$ or $t = -2$.

## History

Cramer stated the rule that solves a linear system by quotients of determinants in 1750, without a proof.
The case of two equations in part 3 is the smallest case of his rule.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take a square table of four numbers, say with rows $3, 1$ and $1, 2$.
Multiply the two numbers on the main diagonal, which gives $6$, and the two numbers on the other diagonal, which gives $1$.
The determinant is the difference, $6 - 1 = 5$.
Draw the arrows $(3, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$ from the origin and complete the parallelogram.
It sits in a $4 \times 3$ box of area $12$, and the box minus four corner triangles and two small squares leaves $12 - 3 - 2 - 2 = 5$.
So the determinant is the area of the parallelogram, and it is zero exactly when the parallelogram is flat.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: solve $3x + 2y = 7$ and $x + 4y = 9$.
The matrix of the system has determinant
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = 3 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 1 = 10,$$
which is not zero, so there is exactly one solution.
Replace the first column by the right side for $x$, and the second column for $y$:
$$x = \frac{7 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 9}{10} = \frac{10}{10} = 1, \qquad y = \frac{3 \cdot 9 - 7 \cdot 1}{10} = \frac{20}{10} = 2.$$
Check: $3 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 = 7$ and $1 + 4 \cdot 2 = 9$.
For two equations this is as fast as elimination, and it gives the answer as a formula in the coefficients.
Watch the order of the subtraction.
If you write $bc - ad = -10$ in the denominator but keep the numerators, the answer becomes $x = -1$ and $y = -2$, which fails the check.

### L3 human

The map $(a, b, c, d) \mapsto ad - bc$ is the only function of the two columns that is linear in each column, zero when the columns are equal, and $1$ on $I_2$.
So it is the first case of the [[alternating multilinear map|0K6CH0MT0F]] that defines the determinant in every dimension.
In the language of exterior algebra, $ad - bc$ is the area form $e_1^* \wedge e_2^*$ of the plane evaluated on the columns.
The matrices with determinant $1$ form the group $\mathrm{SL}_2(K)$, the matrices that keep this area form.
The formula for the inverse returns over any commutative ring, where it shows that $A$ is invertible exactly when $ad - bc$ is a unit.
