## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be a linear map.
Let $m_T$ be the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] and $p_T$ the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] of $T$.

1. $m_T$ divides $p_T$. In particular, $\deg m_T \le n$.
2. $p_T$ divides $m_T^{\,n}$.

The same holds for an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over $F$, with $m_A$ and $p_A(t) = \det(tI - A)$.

## Remarks

The proof is in [[the proof by the Cayley-Hamilton theorem and by a determinant|PPC7ZEFPQE]].
Part 1 is a corollary of the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]] and of the theorem that the [[minimal polynomial divides every annihilating polynomial|0VTAD25A3G]].
Part 2 has a proof that does not use the Cayley-Hamilton theorem.
It takes determinants in the identity $(tI - A)H(t) = m_A(t)I$ for a matrix $H(t)$ of polynomials.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $V$ must be finite-dimensional, so that $p_T$ and $m_T$ exist.
- The exponent $n$ in part 2 cannot be lowered in general. For $A = I_n$, $m_A = t - 1$ and $p_A = (t - 1)^n$, so $p_A$ divides $m_A^k$ only for $k \ge n$.
- The divisibility in part 1 can be strict. For [[the identity matrix|MSQTBBM86Q]], $m = t - 1$ and $p = (t - 1)^2$.

### Consequences

- $m_T$ and $p_T$ have the same irreducible factors in $F[x]$. Each irreducible factor of $m_T$ divides $p_T$ by part 1, and each irreducible factor of $p_T$ divides $m_T$ by part 2.
- In particular $m_T$ and $p_T$ have the same roots in $F$, which agrees with the theorem that the [[eigenvalues are the roots of the minimal polynomial|0QF4ZEFG52]].
- If $p_T$ has $n$ distinct roots in $F$, then $m_T = p_T$.
- To find $m_A$, it suffices to test the monic divisors of $p_A$ that contain every irreducible factor of $p_A$.

### Generalizations

In the invariant factor decomposition of $V$ as an $F[x]$-module, $m_T$ is the largest invariant factor and $p_T$ is the product of all invariant factors.
This gives both parts at once.
This decomposition belongs to the theory of modules over a principal ideal domain.

## History

The divisibility is a standard corollary of the Cayley-Hamilton theorem in textbooks such as Hoffman and Kunze.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The minimal polynomial is a divisor of the characteristic polynomial.
So its degree is at most the size of the matrix.
Conversely, every factor of the characteristic polynomial appears in the minimal polynomial, maybe with a smaller power.

### L2 human

We check both parts for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, studied in the [[worked 3 by 3 example|7XFGVVTZ2X]].

1. $p_A = (t - 3)^2 (t - 6)$ and $m_A = (t - 3)(t - 6)$.
2. Part 1: $p_A = m_A \cdot (t - 3)$.
3. Part 2: $m_A^3 = (t - 3)^3 (t - 6)^3 = p_A \cdot (t - 3)(t - 6)^2$.
4. Here already $p_A$ divides $m_A^2 = (t - 3)^2(t - 6)^2$. The exponent $n = 3$ is an upper bound, not always needed.

The typical use is to list the candidates for $m_A$ once $p_A$ is factored.
A trap: $m_A$ need not equal $p_A$, even when all eigenvalues are known.

### L3 human

Part 2 holds over any commutative ring for any monic $m$ with $m(A) = 0$, by the same determinant argument.
Over a field, both parts together say that the radicals of the ideals $(m_A)$ and $(p_A)$ in $F[x]$ are equal.
