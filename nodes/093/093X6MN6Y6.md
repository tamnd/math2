## Statement

The minimal polynomial and the characteristic polynomial divide each other up to a power, so they are built from the same irreducible factors.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of dimension $n \ge 1$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be a linear map.
Let $m_T$ be the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] and $p_T$ the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] of $T$.

1. $m_T$ divides $p_T$, and so $\deg m_T \le n$.
2. $p_T$ divides $m_T^{\,n}$.

The same holds for an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over $F$, with $m_A$ and $p_A(t) = \det(tI - A)$.

## Remarks

### Where the two parts come from

Part 1 is a short corollary.
The [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]] says that $p_T$ kills $T$, and the [[minimal polynomial divides every annihilating polynomial|0VTAD25A3G]].
Part 2 needs a separate argument, and it does not use the Cayley-Hamilton theorem.
It builds a matrix $H(t)$ of polynomials with $(tI - A) H(t) = m_A(t) I$ and then takes determinants.
The [[proof|PPC7ZEFPQE]] gives both parts in full.

### A worked check

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, the [[3 by 3 example with 4 on the diagonal|7XFGVVTZ2X]].
Here $A = 3I + J$, where $J$ is the all-ones matrix, and $J^2 = 3J$.
So $(A - 3I)(A - 6I) = J(J - 3I) = J^2 - 3J = 0$, and neither factor alone is zero, so $m_A = (t - 3)(t - 6)$.
The eigenvalues are $3, 3, 6$, because $J$ has rank $1$ and trace $3$, so $p_A = (t - 3)^2 (t - 6)$.
Part 1 holds since $p_A = m_A \cdot (t - 3)$.
Part 2 holds since $m_A^3 = (t - 3)^3 (t - 6)^3 = p_A \cdot (t - 3)(t - 6)^2$, and already $m_A^2$ is a multiple of $p_A$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The space must be finite-dimensional, so that $p_T$ exists and $T$ has a minimal polynomial at all.
The exponent $n$ in part 2 cannot be lowered in general.
For $A = I_n$ we have $m_A = t - 1$ and $p_A = (t - 1)^n$, so $p_A$ divides $m_A^k$ only when $k \ge n$.
The divisibility in part 1 can be strict, as [[the identity matrix of size 2|MSQTBBM86Q]] shows with $m = t - 1$ and $p = (t - 1)^2$.

### What it gives you

The two polynomials have the same irreducible factors in $F[t]$.
Each irreducible factor of $m_T$ divides $p_T$ by part 1, and each irreducible factor of $p_T$ divides $m_T^n$ by part 2 and so divides $m_T$, because it is irreducible.
In particular they have the same roots in $F$, which agrees with the theorem that the [[eigenvalues are the roots of the minimal polynomial|0QF4ZEFG52]].

This gives a finite search for $m_A$.
Factor $p_A$, list its monic divisors that contain every irreducible factor at least once, and test them from the lowest degree up.
If $p_A$ has $n$ distinct roots in $F$, the only candidate is $p_A$ itself, so $m_A = p_A$.

### Generalizations

View $V$ as a module over $F[t]$ with $t$ acting as $T$.
The structure theorem for modules over a principal ideal domain writes $V$ as a sum of cyclic modules $F[t]/(d_i)$ with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$.
Then $m_T = d_r$ and $p_T = d_1 d_2 \cdots d_r$, which gives both parts at once, with the sharper bound that $p_T$ divides $m_T^r$.

## History

Part 1 is a direct consequence of the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, which Cayley stated in 1858 and Frobenius proved in general in 1878; see the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The minimal polynomial is the shortest polynomial that the matrix satisfies, and the characteristic polynomial is the one built from the determinant.
The theorem says that the short one is a factor of the long one, so it is never longer than the size of the matrix.
It also says that no factor goes missing: every factor of the long polynomial appears in the short one, maybe with a smaller power.
For the $2 \times 2$ identity, the long polynomial is $(t - 1)^2$ and the short one is $t - 1$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: find the minimal polynomial of
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The matrix is triangular, so $p_A = (t - 2)^3$.
By the theorem, $m_A$ is one of $t - 2$, $(t - 2)^2$ and $(t - 2)^3$.
The first fails, since $A - 2I$ has the entry $1$ in place $(1, 2)$ and so is not zero.
The second works, because $A - 2I$ has a single nonzero entry, in place $(1, 2)$, and its square is zero.
So $m_A = (t - 2)^2$, a proper divisor of $p_A$.
Watch out: knowing all the eigenvalues does not fix $m_A$; the matrix $2I$ has the same eigenvalues and $m = t - 2$.

### L3 human

Over a field, the two parts say that the ideals $(m_A)$ and $(p_A)$ of $F[t]$ have the same radical.
Part 2 holds over any commutative ring for any polynomial $m$ with $m(A) = 0$, since its proof only takes determinants of matrices over $K[t]$.
The full relation between $m_A$ and $p_A$ is given by the invariant factors and the elementary divisors, which the sub-area on canonical forms studies.
