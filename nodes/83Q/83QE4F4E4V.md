## Statement

The product $Q^* Q$ is the table of all inner products of the columns of $Q$, so the columns are orthonormal exactly when this table is the identity.
Let $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and give $\mathbb{K}^n$ the [[standard inner product|TFZ8DYVJ1J]] $\ip{x}{y} = y^* x$.
Let $Q$ be an $n \times k$ matrix over $\mathbb{K}$ with columns $q_1, \dots, q_k$, and let $Q^*$ be its [[conjugate transpose|0DXKE10PHF]].

1. For all $i, j$, the entry $(Q^* Q)_{ij}$ equals $\ip{q_j}{q_i}$.
2. The columns $q_1, \dots, q_k$ form an [[orthonormal family|09VYHEF2VT]] if and only if $Q^* Q = I_k$, the [[identity matrix|06DW9BAWB1]].
3. Let $k = n$.
   Then the following are equivalent: the columns form an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of $\mathbb{K}^n$; $Q^* Q = I_n$; $Q Q^* = I_n$; the rows of $Q$, read as vectors of $\mathbb{K}^n$, form an orthonormal family.
4. If the conditions of part 3 hold, then $Q$ is invertible and $Q^{-1} = Q^*$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Part 3 needs a square matrix.
For the $2 \times 1$ matrix $Q = (1, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$, the single column is a unit vector and $Q^{\mathsf{T}} Q = (1) = I_1$, but
$$Q Q^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \neq I_2.$$

Over $\mathbb{C}$ the conjugate transpose is needed, not the plain transpose.
For $Q = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ i & -i \end{pmatrix}$, the columns are orthonormal and $Q^* Q = I_2$, but the plain transpose gives
$$Q^{\mathsf{T}} Q = \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 1 + i^2 & 1 - i^2 \\ 1 - i^2 & 1 + i^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The inner product must be the standard one.
For $\ip{x}{y} = 2x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, the identity matrix satisfies $I^{\mathsf{T}} I = I$, but its columns $e_1$ and $e_2$ are not orthonormal, since $\ip{e_1}{e_1} = 2$.

### What it gives you

A square real matrix is [[orthogonal|0W02QESPJQ]] exactly when its columns form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and a square complex matrix is [[unitary|0AR68JTMDT]] exactly when its columns form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^n$.
For such a matrix the inverse costs nothing: it is the conjugate transpose.

Even when $Q$ is not square, $Q^* Q = I_k$ has two uses.
First, $Q$ preserves inner products, since
$$\ip{Qx}{Qy} = (Qy)^* (Qx) = y^* Q^* Q x = y^* x = \ip{x}{y}.$$
Second, for a vector $v$ in the column space, the coordinates of $v$ in the basis $q_1, \dots, q_k$ form the vector $Q^* v$, and $Q Q^*$ is the matrix of the [[orthogonal projection|0B8KZRT28M]] onto the column space.

### The converse

Parts 1 to 3 are equivalences, so their converses are part of the statement.
Part 4 has no converse in general.
An invertible matrix need not satisfy $Q^{-1} = Q^*$: for $Q = 2I_n$ the inverse is $\tfrac12 I_n$, while $Q^* = 2I_n$.

### Generalizations

With an inner product on $\mathbb{K}^n$ of the form $\ip{x}{y} = y^* A x$, for a positive definite matrix $A$, the same computation gives $\ip{q_j}{q_i} = (Q^* A Q)_{ij}$.
So the columns are orthonormal for this inner product exactly when $Q^* A Q = I_k$.
For vectors $v_1, \dots, v_k$ in any inner product space, the [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]] plays the role of $Q^* Q$, and orthonormality means that it is $I_k$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Put the vectors as the columns of a matrix $Q$.
The product $Q^* Q$ is then a table: its entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is the inner product of column $j$ with column $i$.
The columns are orthonormal when this table has $1$ on the diagonal and $0$ everywhere else, that is, when it is the identity.

### L2 human

Here is a test on a $3 \times 2$ matrix:
$$Q = \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Multiply $Q^{\mathsf{T}}$ by $Q$:
$$Q^{\mathsf{T}} Q = \frac19 \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 4 + 4 & 2 + 2 - 4 \\ 2 + 2 - 4 & 4 + 1 + 4 \end{pmatrix} = I_2.$$
So the two columns are orthonormal.
But $Q Q^{\mathsf{T}}$ is a $3 \times 3$ matrix of rank at most $2$, so it cannot be $I_3$; its first entry is $\tfrac{1 + 4}{9} = \tfrac59$.
This is the typical use: a computer tests orthonormality by computing $Q^* Q - I$ and checking that it is close to zero.
Watch out: for a matrix that is not square, $Q^* Q = I$ does not give $Q Q^* = I$.

### L3 human

The statement identifies orthonormal families of $k$ vectors in $\mathbb{K}^n$ with linear maps $\mathbb{K}^k \to \mathbb{K}^n$ that preserve inner products.
For square matrices these maps form the [[orthogonal group|0TKYAEJ8N7]] and the [[unitary group|0R9R0Y1HDK]].
The [[QR decomposition|0ERWG4NXQ9]] writes a matrix with independent columns as $QR$ with $Q^* Q = I$ and $R$ upper triangular, which is the matrix form of the Gram-Schmidt process.
