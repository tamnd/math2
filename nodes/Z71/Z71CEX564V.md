## Statement

Taking annihilators reverses inclusions, exchanges sums with intersections, and in finite dimension matches the subspaces of $V$ with those of $V^*$.
Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, of any dimension, and let $U$ and $W$ be [[subspaces|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$.
Write $U^0$ for the [[annihilator|0R6M158SQ9]] of $U$.
For a subspace $X$ of $V^*$, write $X_0 = \{ v \in V : f(v) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in X \}$.

1. $U^0$ is a subspace of $V^*$, with $\{0\}^0 = V^*$ and $V^0 = \{0\}$.
2. $U \subseteq W$ if and only if $W^0 \subseteq U^0$.
3. $(U^0)_0 = U$, so the map $U \mapsto U^0$ is injective.
4. $(U + W)^0 = U^0 \cap W^0$, for the [[sum|0JMTY8MF54]] and the [[intersection|0V33SPH7RR]] of subspaces.
5. $(U \cap W)^0 = U^0 + W^0$.
6. If $V$ is [[finite-dimensional|018W3V3TCT]], then $U \mapsto U^0$ is a bijection from the subspaces of $V$ onto the subspaces of $V^*$, its inverse is $X \mapsto X_0$, and it reverses inclusions.

## Remarks

### Why finite dimension is needed in part 6

Parts 1 to 5 hold in every dimension, but part 6 fails for every infinite-dimensional space.
Take $V = F^{(\mathbb{N})}$, the finitely supported sequences, and let $X$ be the span of the coordinate forms $e_k^*$.
A vector killed by every $e_k^*$ has all coordinates $0$, so $X_0 = \{0\}$ and $(X_0)^0 = V^*$.
The form that adds all coordinates is [[not in X|JCMJDY59CH]], so $(X_0)^0 \ne X$.
If some $U$ had $U^0 = X$, part 3 would give $U = X_0 = \{0\}$ and then $X = \{0\}^0 = V^*$, which is false.
The same argument works with the coordinate forms of any infinite basis, so part 6 holds exactly when $V$ is finite-dimensional.

### Why part 5 needs subspaces

Part 4 holds for any subsets $S$ and $T$ in place of $U$ and $W$, with $S + T$ read as the span of $S \cup T$.
Part 5 does not.
In $\mathbb{R}^2$ take $S = \{(1, 0)\}$ and $T = \{(2, 0)\}$.
Then $S \cap T$ is empty, so $(S \cap T)^0 = (\mathbb{R}^2)^*$, while $S^0$ and $T^0$ are both the line spanned by the form $y$, so $S^0 + T^0$ is only that line.
The span of $S$ equals the span of $T$, and that common span is what the annihilators see.

### What it gives you

Part 3 says that every subspace is the common kernel of the forms that vanish on it.
So every subspace of $F^n$ is the solution set of a homogeneous system of linear equations.

With the theorem on the [[dimension of the annihilator|06Q1EFVH0Q]], part 5 gives $\dim(U^0 + W^0) = \dim V - \dim(U \cap W)$ in finite dimension.
For two different planes $U$ and $W$ through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the intersection is a line, so $\dim(U^0 + W^0) = 3 - 1 = 2$.
Each plane has a line of equations, and the two lines together span a plane of forms.

In finite dimension, parts 2, 4, 5 and 6 together say that the lattice of subspaces of $V$ and the lattice of subspaces of $V^*$ are anti-isomorphic, with sums matched to intersections.

### Generalizations

On a finite-dimensional space with a [[nondegenerate bilinear form|01NHFZF2SK]], the same statements hold for orthogonal complements, which are the annihilators carried back to $V$ by the form.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The annihilator turns a subspace into the set of linear equations that it satisfies.
A bigger subspace satisfies fewer equations, so inclusions turn around.
Two subspaces together satisfy exactly the equations that each one satisfies, and that is part 4.
In finite dimension the equations determine the subspace, and every collection of equations that is closed under combinations comes from exactly one subspace.

### L2 human

We check parts 4 and 5 for the two coordinate axes in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
Let $U = \mathbb{R} \cdot (1, 0)$ and $W = \mathbb{R} \cdot (0, 1)$, so $U^0 = \mathbb{R} \cdot y$ and $W^0 = \mathbb{R} \cdot x$.
Their sum is $U + W = \mathbb{R}^2$, so $(U + W)^0 = \{0\}$, and indeed $U^0 \cap W^0 = \mathbb{R} y \cap \mathbb{R} x = \{0\}$.
Their intersection is $U \cap W = \{0\}$, so $(U \cap W)^0 = (\mathbb{R}^2)^*$, and indeed $U^0 + W^0 = \mathbb{R} y + \mathbb{R} x = (\mathbb{R}^2)^*$.
The typical use is to compute the annihilator of a sum or an intersection from annihilators that you already know.
Watch out: part 5 is harder than part 4, because its [[proof|1WTK1GHPZG]] must extend a form from a subspace to all of $V$.
