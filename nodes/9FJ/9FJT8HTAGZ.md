## Problem

In the [[Einstein field equations|3MCZ97SW6V]], the energy density of the vacuum acts in the same way as a [[cosmological constant|WVKKDY9V61]].
Quantum field theory predicts a vacuum energy density from the zero-point energies of all fields.
If these contributions are summed up to an energy cutoff $E_{\mathrm{c}}$, the estimate grows like $E_{\mathrm{c}}^4$ in units with $c = \hbar = 1$.
Take any cutoff at or above the energies of known particle physics.
The observed cosmological constant corresponds to a vacuum energy density many orders of magnitude smaller than the estimate.
The problem is to explain why the observed value is so small, and why it is not zero.

## Remarks

The problem is open.
With the cutoff at the Planck energy, the naive estimate exceeds the observed value by about $120$ orders of magnitude.
With a cutoff near the electroweak scale, the gap is still more than $50$ orders of magnitude.
A bare cosmological constant can be chosen to cancel the vacuum energy.
But the cancellation must then hold to an extreme precision, and corrections from particle physics at each energy scale upset it.
Supersymmetry makes the bosonic and fermionic zero-point energies cancel, but it is broken in nature, so this cancellation is not exact.
Steven Weinberg reviewed the problem in 1989 in an article called "The cosmological constant problem", and he gave an anthropic bound on the cosmological constant.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Empty space is not truly empty in quantum physics: every field has a minimum energy, even with no particles.
General relativity says that such energy should make the universe expand faster and faster.
When physicists estimate this energy from known physics, the result is enormously larger than the small acceleration that astronomers actually see.
Nobody knows why the true value is so tiny, and this mismatch is often called the worst prediction in theoretical physics.
