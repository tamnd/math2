## Definition

Let $G$ be a [[locally compact group|AHV05ZBCYW]], and let $\pi$ be a [[unitary representation|74BPYJNZTM]] of $G$ on a [[Hilbert space|HM2HHE3J51]] $\mathcal{H}$.
Let $\pi(G)''$ be the [[von Neumann algebra|PSFWQVZ0FS]] generated by the operators $\pi(g)$, the double [[commutant|1VC7EJ1ZBZ]] of $\set{\pi(g) : g \in G}$.
The representation $\pi$ is a *factor representation* if the center of $\pi(G)''$ consists of the scalar multiples of the identity.
The group $G$ is of *type I* if, for every factor representation $\pi$, the algebra $\pi(G)''$ is a type I factor.
This means that $\pi(G)''$ is isomorphic to the algebra $B(\mathcal{K})$ of all bounded operators on some Hilbert space $\mathcal{K}$.

## Remarks

Equivalently, every factor representation of $G$ is a multiple of one [[irreducible|FX3NEFYFFN]] representation.
For second countable $G$, Glimm's theorem gives further equivalent conditions; one of them is that two irreducible representations with the same kernel in $C^*(G)$ are unitarily equivalent.

Example: every abelian locally compact group is a type I group.
If $G$ is abelian, $\pi(G)''$ is commutative, so a factor representation has $\pi(G)'' = \C I$.
Hence each factor representation is a multiple of a one-dimensional representation, a character, and $\pi(G)'' = \C I \cong B(\C)$.
Compact groups, connected real semisimple Lie groups and connected nilpotent Lie groups are also type I groups.

Non-example: the free group $F_2$ on two generators is not a type I group.
Its left regular representation generates a factor of type $\mathrm{II}_1$, which is not a type I factor.
More generally, Thoma proved that a discrete group is of type I if and only if it has an abelian normal subgroup of finite index.

For type I unimodular groups there is a Plancherel theorem that decomposes the left regular representation over the unitary dual.
