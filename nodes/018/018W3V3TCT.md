## Definition

A finite-dimensional space is one that finitely many vectors can span.
Let $F$ be a field and let $V$ be a vector space over $F$.
The space $V$ is *finite-dimensional* if there is a finite subset $S \subseteq V$ with $\operatorname{span}(S) = V$, that is, if $V$ has a finite [[spanning set|0BJKVF90Z6]].
Otherwise $V$ is *infinite-dimensional*.

## Notation

We write $\dim V < \infty$ when $V$ is finite-dimensional.
In Mathlib, `FiniteDimensional F V` is defined as `Module.Finite F V`, which says that $V$ is finitely generated as a module, as here.

## Remarks

The definition asks only for a finite spanning set, not for a basis, because a spanning set is easier to find.
A basis then comes for free, since [[every spanning set contains a basis|06MRNP4BNT]].
Other forms of the condition, such as "has a finite basis" or "is isomorphic to $F^n$", are the [[equivalent conditions|HW9HN5A5HJ]].

For example, $\mathbb{R}^3$ is finite-dimensional, since $e_1, e_2, e_3$ span it, and so is the zero space, which the empty set spans.
The space $F[x]$ of all polynomials fails the condition.
A finite set of polynomials has a largest degree $d$, every combination of them has degree at most $d$, and so $x^{d + 1}$ is not in their span.

Every [[subspace|00YC1D652K]] of a finite-dimensional space is finite-dimensional, but this needs a proof, given in the [[dimension of a subspace|0QW084460E]], because a spanning set of the big space need not lie in the subspace.
The [[concept page|0ZHWKQMVX5]] gives more examples on both sides.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A space is finite-dimensional when a finite list of vectors is enough to build every vector in it.
The plane needs two vectors, and ordinary space needs three.
The space of all polynomials needs infinitely many, because the powers of $x$ have no bound.

### L2 human

To prove that a space is finite-dimensional, write down a finite spanning set.
For the $2 \times 2$ real matrices of trace $0$, every such matrix is
$$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & -a \end{pmatrix} = a \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} + b \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + c \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
so three matrices span the space, and it is finite-dimensional.
Watch out: "finite-dimensional" does not mean that the space has finitely many vectors; this space has infinitely many.
