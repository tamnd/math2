## Summary

Mathematics is written in a mix of ordinary English and symbols, and the mix follows rules that ordinary English does not follow.
Take the sentence "every continuous function on $[0, 1]$ is bounded".
It looks like plain English, but each part does exact work.
The word "every" is a [[quantifier|EGTZPA6QH3]], the words "continuous" and "bounded" are defined terms, and the symbol $[0, 1]$ names a set.

The language of mathematics is this combination of a natural language, most often English, with symbols, formulas and diagrams.
Mathematicians use it to state definitions, theorems and proofs.
The words carry the logical structure: "let", "suppose", "if ... then", "for every", "there is", "hence".
The symbols name the objects and carry the calculations: $x$, $\sqrt{2}$, $\int_0^1 f$, $G/H$.
Mathematical English differs from ordinary English in three ways.
Its small words, such as "or", "if", "any" and "the", have fixed meanings.
Many of its ordinary words, such as "group", "open", "normal" and "field", are defined terms.
Each of its sentences is a [[statement|S9MHMRAX88]] with one truth value, or an instruction that fixes a name or an assumption.

## Motivation

A theorem is useful only if its readers agree on what it says.
Ordinary English is flexible, and one sentence can mean different things to different listeners.
A purely formal language, such as the language of a proof assistant, removes this problem, but it is slow to read, and a proof written only in symbols hides its ideas.
Mathematical English is a compromise between the two.
It keeps the readability of English for the structure of an argument, and it uses symbols where precision and brevity matter most.

So a student who learns mathematics also learns a second dialect of English.
The pages that follow take the dialect apart one piece at a time.
[[Connectives|12QQ51VK01]] fix the meanings of "and", "or" and "not".
[[Conditional statements|FA6YZGR2SS]] fix "if", "only if" and "if and only if".
[[Quantifiers|EGTZPA6QH3]] fix "every", "some" and "any", and [[negation|K930V33BSC]] shows how all of these behave under "not".
The last pages treat [[existence and uniqueness|ACSC75R7R5]], which stands behind the word "the", and the [[ambiguities|8BZPKYF912]] that remain.

## Notation

Every symbol in a mathematical text can be read aloud, and a formula is a part of a sentence.
So "$x^2 - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1)$" reads "$x$ squared minus one equals $x$ minus one times $x$ plus one", and a formula at the end of a sentence takes a full stop.
Symbols play the roles of nouns, as $\pi$ or $f(x)$, of verbs, as $=$, $<$ or $\in$, and of whole clauses, as $x \in A$.
A few logical symbols, $\forall$, $\exists$, $\Rightarrow$, $\iff$, $\land$, $\lor$ and $\neg$, stand for the words of the logical structure.
Many authors avoid these symbols in running text and keep them for displayed formulas.
The conventions of notation and jargon have their own topic, [[notation, conventions and jargon|Z2WNQ1DNTX]].

## Remarks

### Taking a sentence apart

Go back to "every continuous function on $[0, 1]$ is bounded" and make each hidden part visible.
First, "every ... is" hides a conditional: for every function $f \colon [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$, if $f$ is continuous, then $f$ is bounded.
Second, "bounded" is a defined term, and its definition contains two more quantifiers: there is a number $M$ such that $|f(x)| \le M$ for every $x$ in $[0, 1]$.
Put together, the short English sentence says:
$$\forall f \colon [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}, \quad f \text{ continuous} \;\Rightarrow\; \exists M \in \mathbb{R} \;\; \forall x \in [0, 1] \;\; |f(x)| \le M.$$
So a sentence of seven words, one of them a symbol, carries three quantifiers and a conditional.
The statement is true, and it is a part of the [[extreme value theorem|0WS1A8E2P9]].

Check: $f(x) = 1/x$ is continuous on $(0, 1]$ and is not bounded there.
This does not contradict the sentence, because $(0, 1]$ is not $[0, 1]$, and $1/x$ has no value at $0$.

### Small words with fixed meanings

The small words of English carry the logic, so mathematics gives each one a single meaning.
The word "or" is inclusive: "$x \ge 0$ or $x \le 0$" is true for $x = 0$, where both parts hold.
The word "some" means "at least one", so "some prime is even" is true although only one prime, $2$, is even.
The word "if" in a theorem is one-way, but the "if" in a definition means "if and only if", as [[the "if" of a definition|FBCKHZPS3Y]] shows.
The word "the" claims that exactly one object fits, so "the identity element of a group" needs a proof of uniqueness first.

In ordinary speech each of these words can shift.
A waiter who offers "soup or salad" does not expect you to take both.
A mathematician who writes "$n$ is even or $n$ is a multiple of $3$" allows $n = 6$.

### Ordinary words with new meanings

Many words of mathematics are borrowed from ordinary English and then given a definition that overrides the old sense.
A *field* is a set with two operations, not a piece of land.
A *normal* subgroup is a subgroup with a special property, and most subgroups are not normal.
An *imaginary* number is a well-defined object, no less than $2$ or $\pi$.

The trap is to bring the everyday sense back.
In everyday English, "closed" is the opposite of "open".
For sets of real numbers this fails: $[0, 1)$ is neither open nor closed, and $\mathbb{R}$ is both.
So read a defined word by its definition, and only by its definition.

### Words and symbols in one sentence

A good mathematical sentence uses words for the logic and symbols for the objects.
Compare two versions of one short argument.
The first is a bare chain:
$$x^2 = 4 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 2.$$
The second is a sentence: "if $x$ is a positive real number with $x^2 = 4$, then $x = 2$."
Read with the usual hidden "for every real $x$", the bare chain is false, since $x = -2$ satisfies $x^2 = 4$.
The sentence is true, and the words "positive real number" carry the condition that the chain left out.

Two rules of style follow from the same idea.
A sentence should not start with a symbol, because the reader cannot see where the previous sentence ended.
Two formulas should not stand next to each other with only a comma between them, so write "for $x > 0$ we have $x^2 > 0$" and not "for $x > 0$, $x^2 > 0$".

### What it is not

The sentence "the bank is near the river" is ordinary English.
The word "bank" has two meanings, and the context picks one, as ordinary English allows.
Mathematical English does not allow a word with two live meanings in one text.

A proof written for the proof assistant Lean is not mathematical English either.
It is a text in a formal language, with a fixed grammar and no room for interpretation.
It can say the same thing as an English proof, but a computer checks it, and a human reader often finds it hard to follow.

### Watch out

The first error is a proof that is a chain of formulas with no words.
The reader cannot tell which lines are assumptions, which are claims, and how one line follows from the next.
The second error is to use $\Rightarrow$ as a general "so".
The arrow means "implies" between two statements, and it does not claim that the first statement is true.
The third error is to read a defined word in its everyday sense, as in "a closed set is a set that is not open".
The fourth error is to use a letter that was never introduced, so that the reader does not know what it stands for.

### History

Early algebra was written entirely in words, as in the work of al-Khwarizmi in the 9th century.
Robert Recorde introduced the sign $=$ in 1557.
François Viète used letters both for unknowns and for given quantities near the end of the 16th century.
René Descartes, in La Géométrie of 1637, wrote $x$, $y$ and $z$ for unknowns and $a$, $b$ and $c$ for known quantities, close to the modern use.
Gottlob Frege built a formal language for logic in his Begriffsschrift of 1879, and Giuseppe Peano wrote much of mathematics in logical symbols near the end of the 19th century.
In The Language of Mathematics (2013), Mohan Ganesalingam studied mathematical English with the methods of formal linguistics.
The longer story is in the topic [[history of mathematical notation and language|F1P7ZSZ6EM]].

### Try it

First, find the hidden quantifiers in "every positive real number has a square root".
It says: for every real $x > 0$ there is a real $y$ with $y^2 = x$, so it has one "for every" and one "there is".

Second, say why "$[0, 1)$ is not open, so it is closed" is a wrong argument.
"Closed" is not defined as "not open"; a set is closed when its complement is open, and the complement of $[0, 1)$ is not open, since every interval around $1$ meets $[0, 1)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Mathematics has its own way of speaking.
It uses ordinary words, but some words have a special job.
In a mathematics lesson, "or" allows both choices, and "the" means that there is only one.
Mathematics also uses signs, like $+$, $=$ and $<$, which are short ways of writing words.
So "$3 + 4 = 7$" is a full sentence: "three plus four equals seven".
Learning mathematics is partly learning to read and write this kind of sentence.

### L2 human

Read the theorem "if $p$ is a prime and $p$ divides $ab$, then $p$ divides $a$ or $p$ divides $b$" as a sentence of mathematical English.
The letters $p$, $a$ and $b$ have no values, so the theorem speaks about every prime $p$ and all integers $a$ and $b$.
The words "if ... then" make a conditional, "and" joins two hypotheses, and "or" joins two possible conclusions.
The "or" is inclusive, so both conclusions may hold, as for $p = 2$, $a = 2$ and $b = 4$.
The words "prime" and "divides" have exact definitions, and no everyday sense of them enters.

Now test one case, $p = 3$, $a = 4$ and $b = 6$:
$$ab = 24 = 3 \cdot 8, \qquad 6 = 3 \cdot 2.$$
So $3$ divides $ab$, and $3$ divides $b$, as the theorem says.
Watch out: a reader with the everyday "or" in mind may think that the theorem forbids $p$ to divide both $a$ and $b$.
It does not, and the case $p = 2$, $a = 2$, $b = 4$ shows both conclusions at once.
Once the logical words and the defined terms are clear, the plan of a proof often follows from them.

### L3 human

Ganesalingam argues that mathematical English, unlike natural language in general, can be given a full formal analysis, because it is far more restricted in what it says and how it says it.
In his account, the types of mathematical objects, such as numbers, sets and functions, settle many ambiguities that grammar alone leaves open.
The same idea stands behind the controlled natural languages of some proof systems, such as Naproche, which read a restricted mathematical English and check it formally.

Other authors stress what a purely formal language loses.
William Thurston wrote that mathematical understanding passes between people through informal language, pictures and examples, not only through formal statements.
The topic [[the language and typography of mathematics|6WAPT044J8]] treats the written form, and the topic [[proof methods|G9JFJGY4TQ]] treats the structure of arguments.
