## Summary

A polyadic algebra is a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]] with a substitution endomorphism $S(\tau)$ for each map $\tau$ of a set $I$ of variables into itself, and a quantifier $\exists(J)$ for each set $J \subseteq I$; see [[the definition|3GG2T69SSE]].
Halmos introduced them as the algebras of [[first-order logic|086QKPF0A6]] without equality.
The model is the algebra of all subsets of $X^I$, where $S(\tau)$ changes variables and $\exists(J)$ projects along the coordinates in $J$.
Halmos proved that every locally finite polyadic algebra of infinite degree is a subdirect product of such functional algebras, an algebraic form of the completeness theorem.

## Motivation

A first-order formula is acted on in two ways: one can rename its free variables, and one can quantify some of them.
Halmos made both kinds of action primitive operations, for all renamings and all sets of variables at once.
The laws that connect them are the rules of substitution in logic, such as "renaming does not affect bound variables".
The result is an algebraic theory of first-order logic that is close to the usual syntax.

## Notation

We write $(A, I, S, \exists)$; the cardinal $|I|$ is the degree.
A set $J \subseteq I$ is a support of $p$ if $\exists(I \setminus J) p = p$.
The algebra is locally finite if each element has a finite support.
A *functional polyadic algebra* is a polyadic subalgebra of $\mathcal{P}(X^I)$ with $S(\tau) p = \set{x : x \circ \tau \in p}$ and $\exists(J) p = \set{x : \exists y \in p,\ y = x \text{ off } J}$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The functional polyadic algebra on $\set{0, 1}^{\set{1, 2}}$|W1CR668N0Z]], with $16$ elements.
2. [[The polyadic algebra of a first-order theory|2KGFXASYWA]], locally finite of infinite degree.
3. Every full functional algebra $\mathcal{P}(X^I)$ for a nonempty set $X$.
4. For $|I| = 1$, a polyadic algebra is a monadic algebra: a Boolean algebra with one quantifier. These are the [[modal algebras|3H85NSFWHK]] of S5.
5. Every Boolean algebra with $S(\tau) = \mathrm{id}$ and $\exists(J) = \mathrm{id}$ for all $\tau$ and $J$.

### Non-examples

1. [[Universal quantification in place of existential quantification|783HAVZ1MV]] fails $p \le \exists(J) p$.
2. On a Boolean algebra with $0 \ne 1$, $\exists(J) p = 1$ for all $p$ fails $\exists(J) 0 = 0$.

### Basic properties

1. Each $\exists(J)$ is a quantifier: $\exists 0 = 0$, $p \le \exists p$, and $\exists(p \land \exists q) = \exists p \land \exists q$.
2. Quantifiers commute: $\exists(J)\exists(K) = \exists(J \cup K) = \exists(K)\exists(J)$.
3. If $J$ is a support of $p$ and $\sigma = \tau$ on $J$, then $S(\sigma) p = S(\tau) p$.
4. Every locally finite polyadic algebra of infinite degree is isomorphic to a subdirect product of functional algebras with values in $\set{0, 1}$.
5. Polyadic algebras with equality add constants or operations for $v_i = v_j$; in the locally finite case of dimension $\omega$, they are closely related to [[cylindric algebras|1MDT9BF81H]].

### History

Halmos introduced polyadic algebras in a series of papers in the 1950s.
He collected them in his book "Algebraic Logic" of 1962, with the representation theorems for locally finite algebras.
Monadic algebras, the case of one variable, were his starting point.

### Common errors

1. Confusing polyadic and cylindric algebras. Polyadic algebras quantify over arbitrary sets of variables and have substitutions as primitive operations; cylindric algebras quantify one variable at a time and have diagonals.
2. Dropping the injectivity condition in the law that moves a quantifier past a substitution. Without it the law fails, since a substitution can identify a bound variable with a free one.
3. Taking the support of an element to be unique. Every set that contains a support is a support, so an element has many supports.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A polyadic algebra is an algebra of statements with variables.
It can rename the variables of a statement, and it can say "there exist values of these variables".
The main example is the algebra of sets of assignments of values to variables.

### L2 human

1. Take $X = \set{0, 1}$ and $I = \set{1, 2}$; an assignment is a pair $(x_1, x_2)$.
2. Let $p = \set{(0, 1)}$, the set of "$v_1 = 0$ and $v_2 = 1$".
3. The swap $\tau(1) = 2$, $\tau(2) = 1$ gives $S(\tau) p = \set{(1, 0)}$: the variables trade places.
4. $\exists(\set{1}) p = \set{(0, 1), (1, 1)}$, the set of "$v_2 = 1$".

The typical use: polyadic algebras make the substitution rules of first-order logic into algebraic identities.
The trap: $S(\tau)$ acts on assignments by composition on the right, so $S(\sigma\tau) = S(\sigma) S(\tau)$ holds in this order.

### L3 human

A polyadic algebra is a Boolean algebra with operators indexed by the monoid of maps $I \to I$ and by the subsets of $I$.
Each first-order theory without equality gives a locally finite polyadic algebra of infinite degree, and the models of the theory give homomorphisms into functional algebras.
Halmos's representation theorem is the algebraic Gödel completeness theorem, and its proof is an ultrafilter version of the Henkin construction.
Categorical logic offers a third approach, in which Lawvere's hyperdoctrines play the role of polyadic algebras.
