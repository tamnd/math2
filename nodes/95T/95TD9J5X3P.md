## Statement

Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix with integer entries.
For $b \in \R^m$ let $P(b) = \set{x \in \R^n : A x \le b, \ x \ge 0}$, a [[polyhedron|0325SGSJAR]].
Then the following are equivalent.

1. $A$ is [[totally unimodular|VSD67A49XC]].
2. For every $b \in \Z^m$, every [[vertex|R7V3S9V891]] of $P(b)$ lies in $\Z^n$.

## Remarks

Idea of the proof.
From 1 to 2: a vertex of $P(b)$ is the unique solution of a nonsingular square subsystem of $A x \le b$, $-x \le 0$ taken with equality.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} A \\ -I \end{pmatrix}$ is again totally unimodular, so this subsystem has determinant $\pm 1$, and Cramer's rule gives an integer solution.
From 2 to 1: let $B$ be a nonsingular square submatrix of $A$.
One chooses integer vectors $b$ so that suitable vertices of $P(b)$ have the columns of $B^{-1}$ as differences, so $B^{-1}$ is integral.
Then $\det B$ and $\det B^{-1}$ are both integers, and $\det B = \pm 1$.

Example: the incidence matrix of a [[bipartite graph|0ZVQKTRKVQ]] is totally unimodular.
So the linear program for a maximum-weight matching, with constraints $\sum_{e \ni v} x_e \le 1$ and $x \ge 0$, has integral vertices, and its optimum is attained at a matching.

The hypothesis "for every $b$" is needed in 2: the matrix $A = (2)$ is not totally unimodular, yet $P(b) = [0, b/2]$ has integral vertices for every even $b$.
For the odd $b = 1$ the vertex $1/2$ is not integral.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Linear programs are easy, and integer programs are hard.
The theorem names exactly the constraint matrices for which the two agree whatever the whole-number right-hand side: those in which every square piece has determinant $0$, $1$ or $-1$.
For such matrices one may drop the integrality conditions and still get whole-number optimal corners.

### L2 human

The theorem was proved by Alan Hoffman and Joseph Kruskal in 1956.
It explains why bipartite matching, the [[transportation problem|2B9QWY4APJ]], the [[assignment problem|K65YS73SBW]] and network flow problems with integer data have integral optimal solutions.
In the direction from 1 to 2 it is often used together with the observation that appending rows or columns of the identity, or negating rows, keeps total unimodularity.
A finer notion is [[total dual integrality|HC0HPYQBD1]], a property of a system of inequalities rather than of a matrix, which also certifies integral polyhedra.
