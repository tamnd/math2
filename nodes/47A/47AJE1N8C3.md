## Proof

Part 1.

1. $0(T) = 0$, so $0 \in I_T$, by the definition of an [[annihilating polynomial|052M4SX57M]].
2. Let $p, q \in I_T$. Evaluation at $T$ preserves sums, by the remarks on the [[value of a polynomial at an endomorphism|0BNEXSKNRV]]. So $(p - q)(T) = p(T) - q(T) = 0$.
3. Let $p \in I_T$ and $f \in F[x]$. Evaluation preserves products, so $(fp)(T) = f(T)p(T) = f(T) \circ 0 = 0$.
4. By steps 1 to 3 and the definition of an [[ideal|0MYSA6MYNA]], $I_T$ is an ideal of $F[x]$.

Part 2.

5. By the theorem on the [[dimension of Hom(V, W)|0Z9N1ER0W0]], $\dim \End_F(V) = n^2$.
6. The list $I, T, T^2, \dots, T^{n^2}$ has $n^2 + 1$ entries in a space of dimension $n^2$. By the [[Steinitz exchange lemma|08ZDRN6H41]], it is linearly dependent.
7. So there are $a_0, \dots, a_{n^2} \in F$, not all zero, with $\sum_k a_k T^k = 0$.
8. Let $p = \sum_k a_k x^k$. Then $p \ne 0$, $\deg p \le n^2$, and $p(T) = 0$ by the definition of $p(T)$. So $p \in I_T$.

Part 3.

9. By the theorem that [[K[x] is a Euclidean domain|0DQG39K2NH]], and since [[Euclidean domains are principal ideal domains|0QKVE1GD9A]], every ideal of $F[x]$ is principal. So $I_T = h\,F[x]$ for some $h \in F[x]$.
10. Since $I_T \ne 0$, $h \ne 0$. Let $c$ be the leading coefficient of $h$ and put $g = c^{-1}h$. Then $g$ is monic, and $g\,F[x] = h\,F[x] = I_T$, because $c$ is a unit.
11. Every nonzero element of $I_T$ is $fg$ with $f \ne 0$, of degree $\deg f + \deg g \ge \deg g$. So $g$ has the least degree among nonzero elements of $I_T$.
12. Let $g'$ be a monic element of $I_T$ with $\deg g' = \deg g$. By step 11, $g' = fg$ with $\deg f = 0$, so $f$ is a constant. Comparing leading coefficients gives $f = 1$, so $g' = g$.
13. If $g'$ is a monic generator of $I_T$, then $g'$ has the least degree by step 11 applied to $g'$. So $g' = g$ by step 12. This proves uniqueness.

Part 4.

14. Let $p \in F[x]$. By the [[division algorithm|0MES3KS0ZC]], $p = qg + r$ with $r = 0$ or $\deg r < d$.
15. Then $p(T) = q(T)g(T) + r(T) = r(T)$, because $g(T) = 0$. So $p(T)$ is a combination of $I, T, \dots, T^{d-1}$, and these maps span $F[T]$.
16. Suppose $\sum_{k < d} b_k T^k = 0$. Then $r = \sum_{k < d} b_k x^k$ lies in $I_T$ and has degree less than $d$. By step 11, $r = 0$, so every $b_k = 0$.
17. So $I, T, \dots, T^{d-1}$ is linearly independent, and it is a basis of $F[T]$.

## Idea

The annihilating polynomials are closed under sums and under multiplication by any polynomial, because evaluation at $T$ is a ring homomorphism.
In finite dimension the powers of $T$ are dependent, which gives a nonzero element.
Every ideal of $F[x]$ is generated by one polynomial, and scaling makes it monic.

## Variants

- Part 3 can be proved without the theory of principal ideal domains. Take a monic $g \in I_T$ of least degree, and divide any $p \in I_T$ by $g$. The remainder lies in $I_T$ and has smaller degree, so it is $0$. This is the proof that the [[minimal polynomial divides every annihilating polynomial|0VTAD25A3G]].
- Part 2 has a second proof that works with vectors instead of maps. For each basis vector $e_i$, the vectors $e_i, Te_i, \dots, T^n e_i$ give a nonzero $p_i$ of degree at most $n$ with $p_i(T)e_i = 0$. The product $p_1 \cdots p_n$ kills each $e_i$, because polynomials in $T$ commute. So it annihilates $T$, with degree at most $n^2$. The Cayley-Hamilton theorem gives degree $n$.
