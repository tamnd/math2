## Proof

We use that evaluation at $T$ respects sums and products for part 1, count dimensions for part 2, and use division with remainder in $F[x]$ for parts 3 and 4.

*Part 1: $I_T$ is an ideal.*

1. The zero polynomial gives the zero map, so $0 \in I_T$, by the definition of an [[annihilating polynomial|052M4SX57M]].
2. Let $p, q \in I_T$.
   Evaluation at $T$ respects sums, by the remarks on the [[value of a polynomial at an endomorphism|0BNEXSKNRV]], so $(p - q)(T) = p(T) - q(T) = 0 - 0 = 0$.
3. Let $p \in I_T$ and $f \in F[x]$.
   Evaluation also respects products, so $(fp)(T) = f(T) \circ p(T) = f(T) \circ 0 = 0$.
4. By steps 1 to 3 and the definition of an [[ideal|0MYSA6MYNA]], $I_T$ is an ideal of $F[x]$.

*Part 2: a nonzero element in finite dimension.*

5. Let $\dim V = n$.
   By the theorem on the [[dimension of Hom(V, W)|0Z9N1ER0W0]], $\dim \operatorname{End}_F(V) = n \cdot n = n^2$.
6. The list $I, T, T^2, \dots, T^{n^2}$ has $n^2 + 1$ entries in a space of dimension $n^2$, so it is linearly dependent by the [[Steinitz exchange lemma|08ZDRN6H41]].
7. So there are $a_0, \dots, a_{n^2} \in F$, not all zero, with
$$a_0 I + a_1 T + \dots + a_{n^2} T^{n^2} = 0.$$
8. Put $p = a_0 + a_1 x + \dots + a_{n^2} x^{n^2}$.
   Then $p \ne 0$ because some $a_k \ne 0$, $\deg p \le n^2$, and $p(T) = 0$ by step 7.
   So $p$ is a nonzero element of $I_T$.

*Part 3: one monic generator.*

9. By the theorem that [[K[x] is a Euclidean domain|0DQG39K2NH]], and since [[Euclidean domains are principal ideal domains|0QKVE1GD9A]], every ideal of $F[x]$ is principal.
   So $I_T = h\,F[x]$ for some $h \in F[x]$.
10. Since $I_T \ne 0$, we have $h \ne 0$.
    Let $c$ be the leading coefficient of $h$ and put $g = c^{-1} h$.
    Then $g$ is monic, and $g\,F[x] = h\,F[x] = I_T$, because $c$ is a unit of $F$.
11. Every nonzero element of $I_T$ is $fg$ with $f \ne 0$, and its degree is $\deg f + \deg g \ge \deg g$, because $F$ has no zero divisors.
    So $g$ has the least degree among the nonzero elements of $I_T$.
12. Let $g'$ be a monic element of $I_T$ with $\deg g' = \deg g$.
    By step 11, $g' = fg$ with $\deg f = 0$, so $f$ is a nonzero constant.
    Both $g$ and $g'$ are monic, so comparing leading coefficients gives $f = 1$ and $g' = g$.
13. If $g'$ is any monic generator of $I_T$, then step 11 applied to $g'$ shows that $g'$ has the least degree, and step 12 gives $g' = g$.
    This proves that the monic generator is unique, and that it is the unique monic element of least degree.

*Part 4: a basis of $F[T]$.*

14. Let $d = \deg g$ and let $p \in F[x]$.
    By the [[division algorithm|0MES3KS0ZC]], $p = qg + r$ with $r = 0$ or $\deg r < d$.
15. Then $p(T) = q(T)\, g(T) + r(T) = r(T)$, because $g(T) = 0$.
    So $p(T)$ is a linear combination of $I, T, \dots, T^{d-1}$, and these maps span $F[T]$.
16. Suppose that $b_0 I + b_1 T + \dots + b_{d-1} T^{d-1} = 0$.
    Then $r = b_0 + b_1 x + \dots + b_{d-1} x^{d-1}$ lies in $I_T$ and has degree less than $d$ or is zero.
    By step 11, $r = 0$, so every $b_k$ is $0$.
17. So $I, T, \dots, T^{d-1}$ is linearly independent and spans $F[T]$, and it is a basis of $F[T]$.

## Idea

Evaluation at $T$ is a ring homomorphism, and the polynomials that it sends to zero form its kernel, which is always an ideal.
In finite dimension the space of maps is finite-dimensional, so the powers of $T$ cannot stay independent forever, and a dependence among them is a nonzero annihilating polynomial.
Every ideal of $F[x]$ is generated by one polynomial, and dividing by the leading coefficient makes it monic.
Finally, division by $g$ turns every polynomial in $T$ into a remainder of degree less than $d$, which gives the basis.

## Variants

Part 3 can be proved without the theory of principal ideal domains.
Take a monic $g \in I_T$ of least degree and divide any $p \in I_T$ by it.
The remainder lies in $I_T$ and has smaller degree, so it is $0$, and this is the [[proof that the minimal polynomial divides every annihilating polynomial|BRFPQM1MMW]].

Part 2 has a proof with vectors in place of maps.
For each basis vector $e_i$, the $n + 1$ vectors $e_i, T e_i, \dots, T^n e_i$ are dependent, which gives a nonzero $p_i$ of degree at most $n$ with $p_i(T) e_i = 0$.
The product $p_1 \cdots p_n$ kills every $e_i$, because polynomials in $T$ commute, so it annihilates $T$ and has degree at most $n^2$.
The [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]] improves the degree to $n$.
