## Example

Take the unit square with corners $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(1, 1)$ and $(0, 1)$, and its diagonal $D$ from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$.
For each integer $n \ge 1$, let $S_n$ be the staircase from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$ with $n$ steps.
Step $k$, for $k = 0, \dots, n - 1$, goes right from $(\frac{k}{n}, \frac{k}{n})$ to $(\frac{k+1}{n}, \frac{k}{n})$ and then up to $(\frac{k+1}{n}, \frac{k+1}{n})$.
A picture of $S_1, S_2, S_4, S_8$ seems to show the staircases turning into the diagonal.
The false argument says that each staircase has length $2$, that the staircases become the diagonal, and so that the diagonal has length $2$ and $\sqrt{2} = 2$.
We check each claim in turn.

1. Each step of $S_n$ has a horizontal and a vertical segment, both of length $\frac{1}{n}$.
   So $S_n$ has length $n \cdot \frac{2}{n} = 2$, and this claim is true.
2. By the Pythagorean theorem, $D$ has length $\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$, and this claim is true.
3. A point $(x, y)$ has distance $\frac{|x - y|}{\sqrt{2}}$ from the line $y = x$.
   On step $k$ of $S_n$ both coordinates lie between $\frac{k}{n}$ and $\frac{k+1}{n}$, so $|x - y| \le \frac{1}{n}$.
   Each point of $S_n$ is within $\frac{1}{n\sqrt{2}}$ of $D$, which tends to $0$, so this claim is true.
4. Each point $(t, t)$ of $D$ is within $\frac{1}{n}$ of $S_n$: choose $k$ with $\frac{k}{n} \le t \le \frac{k+1}{n}$, and take the point $(t, \frac{k}{n})$ of step $k$.
   This claim is true as well.
5. The conclusion needs one more claim: if curves approach a curve $D$ in the sense of steps 3 and 4, then their lengths approach the length of $D$.
   This claim is false here, because the lengths $2, 2, 2, \dots$ do not approach $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$.
6. So the argument fails at the hidden step 5, and $\sqrt{2} \ne 2$.

## Remarks

The picture is not a [[proof without words|V6ESFEFKJC]], although each staircase in it is drawn correctly.
The condition that fails is that every step the picture suggests must be valid.
The picture shows the finite stages, and the eye adds the limit, which is the one false step.

The reason lies in the definition of [[arc length|0TH78S7GBZ]].
The length of a curve is the least upper bound of the lengths of polygons whose corners lie on the curve.
The corners $(\frac{k+1}{n}, \frac{k}{n})$ of $S_n$ are not on $D$, so the lengths of the staircases say nothing about the length of $D$.
In the language of analysis, length is not continuous under uniform convergence of curves.
It is only lower semicontinuous: the length of the limit curve is at most the limit inferior of the lengths, and here $\sqrt{2} \le 2$.

Replace the diagonal by a quarter circle of radius $1$ and the same picture seems to show that $\pi = 4$.
Staircases between the ends of the arc all have length $2$, but the quarter circle has length $\frac{\pi}{2}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Walk from one corner of a square room to the opposite corner along the walls: $1$ meter east and $1$ meter north, $2$ meters in all.
Now walk in many small steps east and north, close to the diagonal line.
Each zigzag path still has length $2$ meters, because the east parts add up to $1$ meter and the north parts add up to $1$ meter.
The zigzag looks more and more like the diagonal, but its length never changes, and the diagonal itself is only about $1.41$ meters.
So "looks like the diagonal" does not mean "has the length of the diagonal", and the picture misleads.

### L2 human

We compare $S_4$ with a polygon that has its corners on $D$.
The staircase $S_4$ has $8$ segments of length $\frac{1}{4}$, so its length is $2$, and each of its points is within $\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.18$ of the diagonal.
The polygon through $(0, 0)$, $(\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{4})$, $(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$, $(\frac{3}{4}, \frac{3}{4})$ and $(1, 1)$ has its corners on $D$, and its length is
$$4 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4} = \sqrt{2}.$$
That polygon is $D$ itself, divided into four pieces, while the staircase stays near $D$ and still has length $2$.
So a path can be close to $D$ at every point and still have a length far from the length of $D$.
The typical use of the example is to show why arc length is defined with polygons whose corners lie on the curve.
Watch out: a closer picture does not give a better length, because closeness of curves does not control the directions of their segments, and length depends on those directions.
