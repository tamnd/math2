## Example

Take the unit square with corners $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(1, 1)$ and $(0, 1)$, and its diagonal $D$ from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$.
For each integer $n \ge 1$, let $S_n$ be the staircase from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$ with $n$ steps.
Step $k$, for $k = 0, \ldots, n - 1$, goes right from $(\frac{k}{n}, \frac{k}{n})$ to $(\frac{k+1}{n}, \frac{k}{n})$, and then up to $(\frac{k+1}{n}, \frac{k+1}{n})$.
A picture of $S_1, S_2, S_4, S_8, \ldots$ seems to show the staircases turning into the diagonal.
The false argument reads: "each staircase has length $2$, the staircases become the diagonal, so the diagonal has length $2$, and $\sqrt{2} = 2$."
We check each claim of the argument.

1. The length of $S_n$: each step has a horizontal segment of length $\frac{1}{n}$ and a vertical segment of length $\frac{1}{n}$. So $S_n$ has length $n \cdot \frac{2}{n} = 2$. This claim is true.
2. The length of $D$: by the Pythagorean theorem, $D$ has length $\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$. This claim is true.
3. The staircases approach the diagonal: a point $(x, y)$ has distance $\frac{|x - y|}{\sqrt{2}}$ from the line $y = x$. On step $k$ of $S_n$, both coordinates lie between $\frac{k}{n}$ and $\frac{k+1}{n}$, so $|x - y| \le \frac{1}{n}$. Each point of $S_n$ is within $\frac{1}{n \sqrt{2}}$ of $D$, and this tends to $0$. This claim is true.
4. Each point $(t, t)$ of $D$ is within $\frac{1}{n}$ of $S_n$: take $k$ with $\frac{k}{n} \le t \le \frac{k+1}{n}$, and the point $(t, \frac{k}{n})$ of step $k$. This claim is true.
5. The conclusion needs one more claim: if curves approach a curve $D$ in the sense of steps 3 and 4, then their lengths approach the length of $D$. This claim is false, because the lengths $2, 2, 2, \ldots$ do not approach $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$.
6. So the argument fails at the hidden step 5, and $\sqrt{2} \ne 2$.

## Remarks

The picture is not a [[proof without words|V6ESFEFKJC]], although each drawn staircase is correct.
The condition that fails is that each step that the picture suggests must be valid.
The picture shows the finite stages, and the eye adds the limit, which is the one step that is false.

The reason is the definition of [[arc length|0TH78S7GBZ]].
The length of a curve is the least upper bound of the lengths of polygons whose corners lie on the curve.
The corners of $S_n$ at $(\frac{k+1}{n}, \frac{k}{n})$ do not lie on $D$, so the lengths of the staircases say nothing about the length of $D$.
In the language of analysis, length is not a continuous function of the curve under uniform convergence.
Length is only lower semicontinuous: the length of the limit curve is at most the limit inferior of the lengths, and here $\sqrt{2} \le 2$.

The same picture with a quarter circle instead of a diagonal seems to show that $\pi = 4$.
Staircases inside the square of side $1$ around a quarter circle of radius $1$ all have length $2$, but the quarter circle has length $\frac{\pi}{2}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Walk from one corner of a square room to the opposite corner along the walls: we walk $1$ metre east and $1$ metre north, $2$ metres in all.
Now walk in many small steps east and north, close to the diagonal line.
Each small zig-zag path still has total length $2$ metres, because all the east parts add up to $1$ metre and all the north parts add up to $1$ metre.
The zig-zag looks more and more like the diagonal, but its length never changes.
The diagonal itself is shorter, about $1.41$ metres.
So "looks like the diagonal" does not mean "has the length of the diagonal", and the picture misleads.

### L2 human

We compute the case $n = 4$ and compare.

1. The staircase $S_4$ has $4$ steps, each with a horizontal and a vertical segment of length $\frac{1}{4}$. Its length is $8 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = 2$.
2. Each point of $S_4$ is within $\frac{1}{4 \sqrt{2}} \approx 0.18$ of the diagonal.
3. The polygon through the corners $(0, 0)$, $(\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{4})$, $(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$, $(\frac{3}{4}, \frac{3}{4})$, $(1, 1)$ has its corners on $D$. Its length is $4 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4} = \sqrt{2}$.
4. The staircase and this polygon are equally close to $D$, but only the polygon has the length of $D$.

The typical use of the example is to show why arc length is defined with polygons whose corners lie on the curve.
A trap is to think that a closer picture gives a better length: closeness of the curves does not control the directions of the segments, and length depends on the directions.
