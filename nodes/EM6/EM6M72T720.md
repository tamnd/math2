## Example

Three cards $1$, $2$ and $3$ are dealt to two agents $a$ and $b$, one card each, and the third card stays face down.
A deal is a pair $xy$ with $x \neq y$, where agent $a$ holds $x$ and agent $b$ holds $y$.
So there are $3 \cdot 2 = 6$ deals: $12$, $13$, $21$, $23$, $31$ and $32$.
The variable $a_i$ says "$a$ holds $i$", and $b_i$ says "$b$ holds $i$".
This is a [[multimodal|ERG3KAQWYF]] model with two boxes $K_a$ and $K_b$.

1. $xy \mathrel{R_a} x'y'$ iff $x = x'$, and $xy \mathrel{R_b} x'y'$ iff $y = y'$, since each agent sees only its own card.
2. Both are [[equivalence relations|0G8VEZ3WTK]], since equality of one coordinate is reflexive, symmetric and transitive.
3. The [[partition|B5ZMBQ3HK7]] of $R_a$ is $\{ 12, 13 \}$, $\{ 21, 23 \}$, $\{ 31, 32 \}$, and that of $R_b$ is $\{ 21, 31 \}$, $\{ 12, 32 \}$, $\{ 13, 23 \}$.

We evaluate at the actual deal $12$.

4. $K_a \neg b_1$ is true, since in the $R_a$-class $\{ 12, 13 \}$ agent $b$ holds $2$ or $3$, never $1$.
5. $K_a b_2$ is false, since the deal $13$ is in the class and there $b$ holds $3$.
6. $K_b a_1$ is false, since the $R_b$-class of $12$ is $\{ 12, 32 \}$ and $a$ holds $3$ at $32$.
7. $K_b \neg a_2$ is true, since in $\{ 12, 32 \}$ agent $a$ holds $1$ or $3$.
8. For $K_a \neg K_b a_1$ we need $\neg K_b a_1$ at $12$ and at $13$.
   At $12$ this is step 6.
   At $13$ the $R_b$-class is $\{ 13, 23 \}$, and $a$ holds $2$ at $23$, so $K_b a_1$ is false.
9. So $K_a \neg K_b a_1$ is true: $a$ knows that $b$ does not know $a$'s card.
10. $K_a (b_2 \vee b_3)$ is true, since $b_2$ holds at $12$ and $b_3$ holds at $13$.
11. $\neg K_a \neg b_3$ is true, since $13$ is in the class, so $a$ considers it possible that $b$ holds $3$.

## Remarks

Each box alone obeys the laws of [[S5|VC3A2VWXND]], since its relation is an equivalence relation.
The logic of all frames with two equivalence relations is the fusion $\mathsf{S5} \otimes \mathsf{S5}$, often written $\mathsf{S5}_2$.
There is no interaction axiom between $K_a$ and $K_b$, and steps 4 and 6 show that one agent can know what the other does not.

Nested formulas such as step 9 are the reason for having several boxes, since they describe knowledge about the knowledge of others.
[[Common knowledge|7BCMKF2P7X]] adds a box for the transitive closure of $R_a \cup R_b$.
Here that closure joins all six deals, through the chain $12, 13, 23, 21, 31, 32$ and back to $12$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Each player sees only their own card.
So a player cannot tell apart two deals that give them the same card.
Holding card $1$, player $a$ knows that $b$ does not hold $1$.
But $a$ cannot know whether $b$ holds $2$ or $3$.
And $a$ knows that $b$ cannot know $a$'s card, since $b$ always has two cards left to guess from.

### L2 human

To compute in this model, list the six deals and group them by $a$'s card for $K_a$ and by $b$'s card for $K_b$.
A formula $K_i\varphi$ is true at a deal iff $\varphi$ is true at every deal in its group for agent $i$.
Nested knowledge is computed from the inner box outward, as in step 8.
Try it: is $K_b K_a \neg b_1$ true at $12$?
The $R_b$-class of $12$ is $\{ 12, 32 \}$, and $K_a \neg b_1$ holds at $12$ by step 4.
At $32$ the $R_a$-class is $\{ 31, 32 \}$, and $b$ holds $1$ at $31$, so $K_a \neg b_1$ is false at $32$.
So the answer is no: $b$ does not know that $a$ knows that $b$ does not hold card $1$.
Watch out: $K_a \neg K_b a_1$ is not the same as $\neg K_a K_b a_1$, and the first is stronger, since it needs $\neg K_b a_1$ at every deal of $a$'s group.

### L3 human

The model is a finite structure for dynamic epistemic logic.
A public announcement deletes the deals where it is false, and the knowledge of each agent changes with the remaining classes.
For $\mathsf{S5}_n$, satisfiability is PSPACE-complete for $n \ge 2$, in contrast with the NP-complete case of one agent.
