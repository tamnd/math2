## Summary

A density argument proves a claim for easy elements, which lie close to every element, and then lets continuity carry the claim to all the rest.

Here is the pattern on a real goal: a [[continuous|0X3WGX95FE]] $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ with $f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y)$ for all $x, y$ is $f(x) = cx$, where $c = f(1)$.
The equation alone gives $f(n) = cn$ for integers $n$, then $n f(m/n) = f(m) = cm$, so $f(q) = cq$ for every rational $q$.
Now both $f(x)$ and $cx$ are continuous in $x$, and they agree on the rationals.
Each real $x$ is a limit of rationals $q_k$, so $f(x) = \lim f(q_k) = \lim c q_k = cx$.

In general, to prove $P(x)$ for every $x$ in a [[metric space|0VKD1TP7F3]] or normed space $X$, you prove it on a [[dense|0BSJSX7ME2]] subset $D$ and show that $P$ survives limits.
A second form extends a map: a uniformly continuous map on $D$, for example a bounded linear map, has exactly one continuous extension to $X$ when its target is complete.

## Motivation

Many theorems of analysis are easy for nice functions and hard to check for general ones.
The Fourier transform of the [[indicator function|0RCQZ36MME]] of an interval can be computed by hand, but the transform of an arbitrary integrable function cannot.
A continuous function with compact support moves only a little when you translate it, by [[uniform continuity|0M4JJT780X]], but an arbitrary element of $L^p$ has no values you can control.

The way out is approximation.
If the nice functions are dense, each general function is close to a nice one.
If both sides of the claim change only a little when the function changes a little, the claim passes from the nice function to the general one.
In analysis the general objects (integrable functions, elements of $L^p$) are hard to compute with, while the dense classes (step functions, polynomials, smooth functions with compact support) are explicit.

## Notation

The [[closure|0QEAJB91GZ]] of $D$ is $\overline{D}$, and $D$ is dense in $X$ when $\overline{D} = X$, that is, when each point of $X$ is a limit of a sequence in $D$.
$L^p(\mathbb{R})$, for $1 \le p \le \infty$, is the [[Lebesgue space|SASP14MA1Z]] with the norm $\lVert f \rVert_p$.
$C_c(\mathbb{R})$ is the space of continuous functions that vanish outside a bounded interval, and a step function is a finite linear combination of indicators $\mathbf{1}_{[a, b]}$ of bounded intervals.
The translate of $f$ by $h$ is $\tau_h f(x) = f(x - h)$.
Many books say "by approximation", "by a limiting argument" or "by continuity" for the same method.

## Remarks

### The two ingredients

A density argument rests on two facts, and both must hold for the same notion of closeness.
First, the easy class $D$ must be dense in $X$ for the norm or metric of the claim.
Second, both sides of the claim must be continuous for that same norm, often in the strong form of a bound such as $\lvert T f \rvert \le C \lVert f \rVert$.

The closed-set form is the cleanest.
If $f, g \colon X \to Y$ are continuous and agree on a dense set $D$, then $\{x : f(x) = g(x)\}$ is closed and contains $D$, so it is all of $X$.
In the same way, $f \le g$ on $D$ gives $f \le g$ on $X$ for real-valued continuous $f$ and $g$.
Density is transitive: if $D_1$ is dense in $D_2$ and $D_2$ is dense in $X$, then $D_1$ is dense in $X$.
This is how step functions become dense in $L^1(\mathbb{R})$, through the [[simple functions|B6RYM7QTYZ]].

### Extension by continuity

Let $D$ be dense in $X$, let $Y$ be a [[complete metric space|0BY52JW6NK]], and let $f \colon D \to Y$ be uniformly continuous.
Then $f$ has exactly one continuous extension $\bar f \colon X \to Y$, given by $\bar f(x) = \lim f(x_n)$ for any sequence $x_n \to x$ in $D$.
Uniform continuity makes $f(x_n)$ a Cauchy sequence, completeness gives the limit, and density makes the extension unique.

The linear case is the bounded linear transformation theorem.
A bounded [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] from a dense subspace of a normed space into a Banach space extends to a unique [[continuous linear map|03TM135G0N]] on the whole space, with the same norm.
The [[Plancherel theorem|HPZHRZ0502]] uses it: the Fourier transform keeps the $L^2$ norm on the dense subspace $L^1 \cap L^2$, so it extends to an isometry of $L^2(\mathbb{R})$.

### Where the pattern appears

[[The Riemann-Lebesgue lemma|SVQESC9PTS]] computes the decay of the Fourier transform for indicators of intervals and passes it to all of $L^1(\mathbb{R})$.
[[Continuity of translation in Lp|EPP5T6MSD5]] proves $\lVert \tau_h f - f \rVert_p \to 0$ on $C_c(\mathbb{R})$ by uniform continuity and passes it to $L^p(\mathbb{R})$, $1 \le p < \infty$, by an [[epsilon over three argument|VXV7M0QDH6]].

A continuous $f$ on $[0, 1]$ with $\int_0^1 f(x) x^k \, dx = 0$ for every $k \ge 0$ is zero.
By the [[Weierstrass approximation theorem|0JQE130DHW]], polynomials $p_n \to f$ uniformly, so $\int_0^1 f^2 = \lim_n \int_0^1 f p_n = 0$, and a continuous function with $\int_0^1 f^2 = 0$ vanishes.
For the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]] over $\mathbb{C}$, matrices with distinct eigenvalues are diagonalizable, so $p_A(A) = 0$ is direct for them; they are dense, and $A \mapsto p_A(A)$ is continuous.

### When it fails

Each of the two ingredients can fail.
[[Translation is not continuous in L-infinity|WT1CMRARAY]]: the claim $\lVert \tau_h f - f \rVert_\infty \to 0$ holds on $C_c(\mathbb{R})$, but $C_c(\mathbb{R})$ is not dense in $L^\infty(\mathbb{R})$, and the claim fails for $\mathbf{1}_{[0, 1]}$.
Polynomials are differentiable and dense in the continuous functions on $[0, 1]$, yet the [[Weierstrass function|01A0MK1730]] is continuous and nowhere differentiable; differentiability does not survive uniform limits.
Every element of $\mathbb{Q}$ is rational and $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$, but $\sqrt{2}$ is not rational, because the set of rationals is not closed.
Without continuity, additive functions need not be linear, as [[Cauchy's functional equation|ZBMEVWN100]] shows.

### Watch out

The most common slip uses density in one norm and continuity in another.
$C_c(\mathbb{R})$ is dense in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, but the value $f(0)$ is not continuous for the $L^2$ norm, so a claim about $f(0)$ does not pass to $L^2$.
A second slip uses a class that is not dense in the space of the claim, such as $C_c(\mathbb{R})$ in $L^\infty(\mathbb{R})$.
A third forgets that convergence in $L^p$ gives no convergence at each point; a pointwise statement needs a subsequence that converges almost everywhere.

### History

Karl Weierstrass proved in 1885 that polynomials are dense in the continuous functions on a closed bounded interval, and this supplies the dense class of many density arguments.
Michel Plancherel proved in 1910 that the Fourier transform preserves the $L^2$ norm, and the transform on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ is defined today by extending it from $L^1 \cap L^2$ with that identity.

### Try it

Show that $2^x \cdot 3^x = 6^x$ for every real $x$, given that it holds for rational $x$.
Both sides are continuous functions of $x$ and agree on the dense set $\mathbb{Q}$, so they agree everywhere.

Does a continuous $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ with $f(q) \ge 0$ for every rational $q$ satisfy $f \ge 0$?
Yes: the set $\{x : f(x) \ge 0\}$ is closed and contains $\mathbb{Q}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every real number can be approximated by fractions as closely as we like, for example $3$, $3.1$, $3.14$, $3.141$ for $\pi$.
Suppose a rule is easy to check for fractions, and a small change in the input gives only a small change in both sides of the rule.
Then the rule holds for every real number too.
For example, $2^x \cdot 2^x = 4^x$ holds for each fraction $x$, by the rules for powers.
Both sides change only a little when $x$ changes a little, so the rule also holds for $x = \pi$.

### L2 human

We show that a continuous $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ that vanishes at each rational number is zero.

1. Let $Z = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : f(x) = 0\}$, so $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq Z$.
2. $Z$ is the preimage of the closed set $\{0\}$ under the continuous map $f$, so $Z$ is [[closed|0ZCK8DQYN1]].
3. Let $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
   The decimal truncations $q_n$ of $x$ are rational, and $\lvert q_n - x \rvert \le 10^{-n}$, so $q_n \to x$.
4. Each $q_n$ lies in $Z$, and $Z$ is closed, so $x \in Z$, that is, $f(x) = 0$.

In practice the argument takes two lines: "both sides are continuous in $f$, and the claim holds on a dense class, so it holds everywhere".
Watch out: the continuity of $f$ is essential, since the indicator function of the irrationals vanishes on $\mathbb{Q}$ but is not zero.

### L3 human

The structure behind the method is the universal property of the completion.
A uniformly continuous map from a metric space to a complete metric space extends uniquely to the [[completion|06RW4RKWH7]], so an object defined on a dense subspace by a uniformly continuous formula is defined on the whole space.
The Fourier transform on $L^2$, the trace of a Sobolev function on the boundary of a domain, and the stochastic integral are all defined this way.
In algebraic geometry the [[Zariski topology|8SQK7RTB63]] plays the same role: a polynomial identity that holds on a non-empty Zariski open subset of an irreducible variety holds everywhere.
The method often combines with an [[epsilon over three argument|VXV7M0QDH6]], the quantitative form of "the property survives limits", and with [[bootstrapping|YKA0ADKYFR]] and the [[open-closed argument|1JJCDJB7FM]], which also pass a property from a small set to a large one.
