## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$.
Let $\det$ be the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]], and let $k \neq l$ be two row indices.

1. Exchanging rows $k$ and $l$ multiplies $\det$ by $-1$.
2. Multiplying row $k$ by $c \in K$ multiplies $\det$ by $c$.
3. Adding $c$ times row $l$ to row $k$, with $c \in K$, does not change $\det$.
4. For a [[permutation|05RWFKVTJ7]] $\sigma$ of $\set{1, \dots, n}$, let $B$ be the matrix with $b_{ij} = a_{\sigma(i) j}$. Then $\det B = \sgn(\sigma) \det A$, where $\sgn$ is the [[sign|0SK90238BH]].
5. For $c \in K$, $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

In part 3 the two rows must be different: replacing row $k$ by $(1 + c)$ times itself multiplies $\det$ by $1 + c$.
In part 2 the factor $c$ may be $0$ or a non-unit; then the operation is not invertible, and Gaussian elimination uses only units $c$.

### Consequences

- For the elementary matrices $E$ of the three types, $\det E$ is $-1$, $c$ and $1$. So $\det(EA) = \det E \det A$ for elementary $E$.
- Gaussian elimination computes $\det A$: reduce $A$ to triangular form, then multiply the diagonal entries of the result and correct for swaps and scalings, by the [[triangular rule|0K1KKMFHYH]].
- For a [[permutation matrix|08SP3DF8NB]] $P$, $\det P = \pm 1$.
- The same rules hold for column operations, by the [[determinant of the transpose|0ZZQ2E1MTE]].

### Converse

Part 1 says that $\det$ is antisymmetric in the rows.
Antisymmetry implies the alternating property only when $2$ is not a zero divisor; the [[alternating map theorem|VJZVSTWYYW]] gives the counterexample in characteristic $2$.

### Generalizations

Every [[alternating multilinear map|0MSWYWHYF5]] satisfies parts 1 to 4.
Part 5 is the case of homogeneity of degree $n$; compare the [[counterexample on sums|D5VBC7621G]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

There are three moves on the rows of a square table.
Swapping two rows changes the sign of the determinant.
Multiplying one row by a number multiplies the determinant by that number.
Adding a multiple of one row to another row does not change the determinant.
These moves let us bring the table to a triangle, where the determinant is easy to read off.

### L2 human

We compute $\det A$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. Swap rows $1$ and $2$. The sign changes: $\det A = -\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Subtract $2$ times row $1$ from row $3$. The new row $3$ is $(0, 2, 5)$, and $\det$ does not change.
3. Subtract row $2$ from row $3$. The new row $3$ is $(0, 0, 1)$.
4. The triangular matrix has diagonal $1, 2, 1$, so its determinant is $2$. Hence $\det A = -2$.
5. Check by expansion along row $1$: $0 - 2 (7 - 2) + 4 (4 - 2) = -10 + 8 = -2$.

The typical use is the hand computation of determinants of size $4$ and more.
A trap: $\det(2A) = 2^n \det A$, not $2 \det A$; for a $3 \times 3$ matrix the factor is $8$.

### L3 human

Parts 1 to 3 say that $\det(EA) = \det E \det A$ for every elementary matrix $E$.
Over a field every invertible matrix is a product of elementary matrices, and this gives one proof of [[multiplicativity|04CPJ6AF00]].
Over a Euclidean ring the same idea computes $\det$ with exact divisions, as in the [[Bareiss algorithm|ZDBW3AB587]].
