## Statement

Each of the three [[elementary row operations|04XWH28452]] changes the determinant in a simple, known way, so elimination can keep track of it.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$.
Let $\det$ be the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]], and let $k \ne l$ be two row indices.

1. Exchanging rows $k$ and $l$ multiplies $\det$ by $-1$.
2. Multiplying row $k$ by $c \in K$ multiplies $\det$ by $c$.
3. Adding $c$ times row $l$ to row $k$, with $c \in K$, does not change $\det$.
4. Let $\sigma$ be a [[permutation|05RWFKVTJ7]] of $\{1, \dots, n\}$, and let $B$ be the matrix with entries $b_{ij} = a_{\sigma(i) j}$. Then $\det B = \sgn(\sigma) \det A$, where $\sgn$ is the [[sign|0SK90238BH]].
5. For $c \in K$, $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$.

## Remarks

### The three moves on one matrix

Start from $\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = 4 - 6 = -2$ and apply each move once.
Exchanging the two rows gives
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 6 - 4 = 2,$$
which is $-1$ times the old value.
Multiplying the first row by $5$ gives
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 10 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = 20 - 30 = -10,$$
which is $5$ times the old value.
Subtracting $3$ times row $1$ from row $2$ gives
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix} = -2 - 0 = -2,$$
the same value as before.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Part 3 needs two different rows, $k \ne l$.
Adding $c$ times row $k$ to row $k$ itself is the same as multiplying that row by $1 + c$, and that multiplies $\det$ by $1 + c$.
In part 2 the factor $c$ may be $0$ or a non-unit, and the rule still holds.
The operation is then not invertible, so elimination uses only factors $c$ that are units.

### What it gives you

The determinants of the three kinds of elementary matrices are $-1$, $c$ and $1$.
So parts 1 to 3 say that $\det(EA) = \det E \det A$ for every elementary matrix $E$.
This is the first step towards [[multiplicativity|04CPJ6AF00]].
Every [[permutation matrix|08SP3DF8NB]] has determinant $\pm 1$ by part 4, since it is $I_n$ with its rows permuted.
The same rules hold for column operations, by the [[determinant of the transpose|0ZZQ2E1MTE]].

The main use is [[Gaussian elimination|0JVJ2WCXZH]].
Reduce $A$ to a triangular matrix $U$ by the three moves, and record a factor $-1$ for each exchange and a factor $c$ for each scaling.
The [[triangular rule|0K1KKMFHYH]] gives $\det U$ as the product of the diagonal, and the recorded factors give $\det A$.
The number of arithmetic operations grows like $n^3$, far more slowly than the $n!$ terms of the Leibniz formula.

### The converse

Part 1 says that $\det$ is antisymmetric in the rows.
Antisymmetry gives the alternating property only when $2$ is not a zero divisor in $K$, as the theorem on [[alternating and antisymmetric maps|VJZVSTWYYW]] shows.
So part 1 alone cannot replace property 2 of the definition in every ring.

### Watch out

A common slip is $\det(2A) = 2 \det A$.
Part 5 gives $\det(2A) = 2^n \det A$, because each of the $n$ rows carries one factor $2$.
For $A = I_3$ the wrong rule gives $2$, but $2 I_3$ is diagonal with determinant $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8$.

### Try it

A $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$ has $\det A = 3$.
Exchange rows $1$ and $3$ and then double row $2$; what is the new determinant?
The exchange gives $-3$ and the doubling gives $2 \cdot (-3) = -6$.

What is $\det(2A)$ for the same $A$?
By part 5 it is $2^3 \cdot 3 = 24$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

There are three moves on the rows of a square table.
Swapping two rows flips the sign of the determinant.
Multiplying one row by a number multiplies the determinant by that number.
Adding a multiple of one row to another row leaves the determinant alone.
For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $-2$, and after the swap the rows give $2$.
With these moves you can turn the table into a triangle, where the determinant is the product of the diagonal.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: compute $\det A$ for
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 7 \end{pmatrix}.$$
First exchange rows $1$ and $2$, so that the corner entry is not $0$, and record a factor $-1$:
$$\det A = - \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 7 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Subtract $2$ times row $1$ from row $3$, which gives the new row $(0, 2, 5)$ and keeps the determinant.
Subtract row $2$ from row $3$, which gives the new row $(0, 0, 1)$ and keeps the determinant again.
The matrix is now triangular with diagonal $1, 2, 1$, so
$$\det A = -(1 \cdot 2 \cdot 1) = -2.$$
Check by expansion along row $1$: $0 - 2 (1 \cdot 7 - 1 \cdot 2) + 4 (1 \cdot 4 - 1 \cdot 2) = -10 + 8 = -2$.
The problem shows the typical use: elimination is the practical way to compute determinants of size $4$ and more.
Watch out: forgetting the factor $-1$ of an exchange is the most common error in this computation.

### L3 human

Parts 1 to 3 say that $\det(EA) = \det E \det A$ for each elementary matrix $E$.
Over a field every invertible matrix is a product of elementary matrices, and this gives the [[proof of multiplicativity by elementary matrices|3WPWGE036Q]].
Every [[alternating multilinear map|0MSWYWHYF5]] obeys parts 1 to 4, so the same bookkeeping works for any such map.
Over the integers, elimination with fractions can be replaced by a scheme in which every division is exact, the [[Bareiss algorithm|ZDBW3AB587]].
