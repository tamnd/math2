## Example

Let $F$ be a field, $A = J_2(1) \oplus J_2(1)$ and $B = J_2(1) \oplus I_2$, as in the [[example of two non-similar matrices with equal invariants|J4Q3B9ZNHF]].
Both have characteristic polynomial $(x - 1)^4$ and minimal polynomial $(x - 1)^2$.
We compute their [[invariant factors|F7GJPMVBXA]].

1. For $A$: $A e_2 = e_1 + e_2$ and $A e_4 = e_3 + e_4$. So $Z(e_2) = \operatorname{span}\set{e_1, e_2}$ and $Z(e_4) = \operatorname{span}\set{e_3, e_4}$, each with annihilator $(x - 1)^2$.
2. So $F^4 = Z(e_2) \oplus Z(e_4)$ with annihilators $(x - 1)^2 \mid (x - 1)^2$. The invariant factors of $A$ are $(x - 1)^2, (x - 1)^2$.
3. For $B$: $Z(e_2) = \operatorname{span}\set{e_1, e_2}$ with annihilator $(x - 1)^2$, and $Z(e_3) = F e_3$, $Z(e_4) = F e_4$ with annihilator $x - 1$.
4. So $F^4 = Z(e_3) \oplus Z(e_4) \oplus Z(e_2)$ with annihilators $x - 1 \mid x - 1 \mid (x - 1)^2$. The invariant factors of $B$ are $x - 1, x - 1, (x - 1)^2$.
5. The lists differ, even in length. By the uniqueness in the [[cyclic decomposition theorem|R3Z20J19BR]], $A$ and $B$ are not similar.
6. The elementary divisors are $(x - 1)^2, (x - 1)^2$ for $A$ and $(x - 1)^2, x - 1, x - 1$ for $B$; they also differ.

## Remarks

The example shows that the invariant factors are finer than the pair $(\chi, m)$.
The number of invariant factors, $2$ for $A$ and $3$ for $B$, is $\dim \ker(M - I)$ for $M = A, B$.
In size $3$ or less, the pair $(\chi, m)$ already fixes the invariant factors, since $\sum \deg d_i = n$ and $d_r = m$ leave one choice.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Both matrices have the number one four times on the diagonal, and both need the square of $x$ minus one to become zero.
But the first matrix is made of two blocks of size two, and the second of one block of size two and two blocks of size one.
The invariant factors record these sizes, so they tell the two matrices apart.

### L2 human

1. $\dim \ker(A - I) = 4 - \operatorname{rank}(A - I) = 4 - 2 = 2$.
2. $\dim \ker(B - I) = 4 - 1 = 3$.
3. The number of invariant factors is $2$ and $3$, as computed above.

The typical use is a proof that two matrices are not similar.
The trap is to stop at the minimal polynomial: it gives only the last invariant factor.
