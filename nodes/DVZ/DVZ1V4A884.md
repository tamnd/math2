## Statement

The only matrices that commute with every matrix are the multiples of the identity by central elements.
Exactly: let $R$ be a ring with identity, and let $n \ge 1$.
Then the [[center|MVT64B3B6H]] of the matrix ring $M_n(R)$ is
$$Z(M_n(R)) = \{ cI_n : c \in Z(R) \}.$$
In particular, if $R$ is commutative, for example a field, then the center of $M_n(R)$ is the set of all [[scalar matrices|0FRM62SG7D]] $cI_n$ with $c \in R$.

## Remarks

### A check in size 2

Take $R = \mathbb{R}$ and $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$.
Commuting with the [[matrix unit|4H93XF4RVH]] $E_{12}$ means
$$E_{12} A = \begin{pmatrix} c & d \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & a \\ 0 & c \end{pmatrix} = A E_{12},$$
so $c = 0$ and $d = a$.
Commuting with $E_{21}$ gives $b = 0$ in the same way.
So $A = aI$, as the theorem says, and the [[proof|NVW7KDY7PN]] does the same for every $n$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The identity of $R$ is needed to form the matrix units $E_{ij}$, which serve as test matrices.
The condition $c \in Z(R)$ cannot be dropped when $R$ is not commutative.
Over the quaternions, the scalar matrix $iI$ does not commute with $jI$, since $ij = k$ but $ji = -k$.
For $n = 1$ the theorem says $Z(M_1(R)) = Z(R)$, which matches the fact that [[1 by 1 matrices|WYJ95EEQFJ]] behave like their entries.

### The converse inclusion

Every $cI$ with $c \in Z(R)$ is central.
The entries of $(cI)B$ are $c\,b_{ij}$ and those of $B(cI)$ are $b_{ij}\,c$, and these agree because $c$ commutes with each $b_{ij}$.

### Consequences

For $n \ge 2$ and $R \ne 0$, the center is a proper subring, because $E_{11}$ is not central; so $M_n(R)$ is not commutative.
For fields $F$ and $K$, the rings $M_n(F)$ and $M_m(K)$ can be isomorphic only if $F \cong K$, because a ring isomorphism carries center to center.
A linear map of $F^n$ that commutes with every linear map is a multiple of the identity.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Most pairs of matrices do not commute.
The matrices that commute with every other matrix are only the multiples of the identity matrix, such as $3I$.
Any other matrix fails to commute with at least one matrix that has a single entry $1$ and zeros elsewhere.

### L2 human

The [[proof|NVW7KDY7PN]] tests a matrix $A$ against the matrix units $E_{ij}$, which have a single $1$.
The [[lemma on matrix units|TX4J5JR205]] shows that this test already forces $A = cI$.
Commuting with the scalar matrices $bI$ then forces $c$ to commute with every $b \in R$.
The typical use is to show that some matrix is not central: find one matrix unit that it fails to commute with.
Watch out: commuting with all diagonal matrices is not enough, since every diagonal matrix commutes with every other diagonal matrix.

### L3 human

The map $c \mapsto cI_n$ is a ring isomorphism from $Z(R)$ onto $Z(M_n(R))$.
This is one case of a general fact: [[Morita equivalent|JEBXXWHW8M]] rings have isomorphic centers.
For a field $F$, the theorem together with simplicity shows that $M_n(F)$ is a central simple $F$-algebra.
