## Summary

A group isomorphism is a [[group homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]] $\varphi \colon G \to H$ that is [[bijective|0GG9TVW90M]].
Two groups $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic, written $G \cong H$, when there is an isomorphism from $G$ to $H$.
The inverse map of an isomorphism is again a homomorphism, so it is an isomorphism from $H$ to $G$.
Isomorphic groups have the same structure and differ only in the names of their elements.

## Motivation

The same group appears in many places with different names for its elements.
The integers modulo $2$ under addition and the set $\{1, -1\}$ under multiplication have the same table: the non-identity element squares to the identity.
An isomorphism is a renaming of the elements that turns one table into the other.
Each statement about the group operation that holds in one group then holds in the other group.
So group theory studies groups up to isomorphism, and it asks which properties can tell two groups apart.

## Notation

We write $G \cong H$ when $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic.
An isomorphism from $G$ to itself is an automorphism of $G$.
The automorphisms of $G$ form a group under composition, the [[automorphism group|0K1SCPBNKJ]] $\operatorname{Aut}(G)$.
We write $V_4$ for the [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]] and $\mathbb{R}^\times$ for the nonzero real numbers under multiplication.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The map $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \to \{1, -1\}$ with $0 \mapsto 1$ and $1 \mapsto -1$ is an isomorphism ([[the integers modulo 2 and the signs|7FCN2KNWC0]]).
- The [[exponential function|0J0FCWV32D]] is an isomorphism from $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ to $(\mathbb{R}_{>0}, \cdot)$, and its inverse is the natural logarithm ([[the exponential map|7GEXM87S0H]]).
- The map $t + \mathbb{Z} \mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$ is an isomorphism from $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ to the [[circle group|04EAFVFTF7]] ([[the real numbers modulo the integers|FV86TFARY4]]).
- Doubling $u \mapsto 2u$ is an isomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$ onto the even integers $2\mathbb{Z}$ ([[doubling on the integers|PVR5EX71PD]]).
- For each $g \in G$, conjugation $x \mapsto gxg^{-1}$ is an automorphism of $G$ ([[inner automorphism|0RN00NTM3S]]).
- Each [[cyclic group of order n|0G0B3DRG65]] is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ ([[classification of cyclic groups|0736AXH2ME]]).

### Non-examples

- The groups $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and $V_4$ both have order $4$, but they are not isomorphic. The first has an element of order $4$, and the second has none ([[Z/4Z and the Klein four-group|MXJMVKYEPZ]]).
- Doubling $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ is an injective homomorphism, but it is not surjective, so it is not an isomorphism ([[doubling on the integers|PVR5EX71PD]]).
- The translation $u \mapsto u + 1$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ is a bijection, but it is not a homomorphism ([[a translation of the integers|3A3JJPJHQR]]).
- The groups $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$ and $(\mathbb{Q}_{>0}, \cdot)$ are not isomorphic. If $\varphi(a) = 2$ for an isomorphism $\varphi$, then $\varphi(a/2)^2 = 2$, but $\sqrt{2}$ is not rational.

### Basic properties

- The inverse $\psi = \varphi^{-1}$ of an isomorphism is a homomorphism. For $x, y \in H$, let $a = \psi(x)$ and $b = \psi(y)$, so $\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b) = xy$. Then $\psi(xy) = ab = \psi(x)\psi(y)$.
- The relation $\cong$ is an [[equivalence relation|0G8VEZ3WTK]]. The identity map, the inverse map and the [[composite|0THBK9V825]] of two isomorphisms are isomorphisms.
- An injective homomorphism $\varphi \colon G \to H$ is an isomorphism from $G$ onto its [[image|0S21GNW9ZB]] $\operatorname{im} \varphi$.
- An isomorphism preserves the order of each element. If $\varphi$ is injective, then $\varphi(a)^n = e_H$ holds if and only if $\varphi(a^n) = \varphi(e_G)$, that is, $a^n = e_G$.
- Isomorphic groups share these invariants: the [[order|0B85S2V2DR]], being [[abelian|082GHNPJ53]], being [[cyclic|0KVGH0J5B3]], and the number of elements of each order.
- They also share the number of subgroups of each order, and an isomorphism maps the [[centre|0DZ93493VN]] of $G$ onto the centre of $H$.
- Two groups with the same order need not be isomorphic. The smallest instance is $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and $V_4$.

### History

The word comes from the Greek words isos, "equal", and morphe, "shape".
Eilenberg and Mac Lane introduced categories in 1945.
In a category an isomorphism is a morphism with a two sided inverse, and for groups these are exactly the bijective homomorphisms.

### Common errors

- A bijection between two groups need not be an isomorphism. The bijection of $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ that swaps $0$ and $1$ and fixes $2$ sends $0$ to $1$, so it is not a homomorphism.
- That one bijection fails does not show that the groups are not isomorphic. The identity map shows $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$.
- Equal orders do not give an isomorphism. To show $G \not\cong H$, find an invariant that differs, such as the number of elements of order $4$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two groups are isomorphic when they are the same group with different names for the elements.
Think of the same game played with two sets of pieces.
The pieces look different, but the moves follow the same rules.
An isomorphism is a dictionary that translates each piece of one set to a piece of the other set.
Translating a move gives a correct move in the other set.

### L2 human

We show that $(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^\times = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ under multiplication modulo $5$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ under addition.

1. Define $\varphi \colon \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \to (\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^\times$ by $\varphi(k) = 2^k \bmod 5$.
2. The map is well defined, because $2^4 = 16 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$. So $2^{k+4} \equiv 2^k \pmod 5$.
3. The map is a homomorphism, because $2^{k + l} = 2^k \cdot 2^l$.
4. The values are $\varphi(0) = 1$, $\varphi(1) = 2$, $\varphi(2) = 4$ and $\varphi(3) = 8 \equiv 3$.
5. The four values are different, and they are all the elements of $(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^\times$. So $\varphi$ is a bijection and an isomorphism.

As a check, the element $2$ of $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has order $2$, and its image $4$ also has order $2$, because $4 \cdot 4 = 16 \equiv 1$.

The typical use of an isomorphism is to move a computation to a group where it is easier.
For example, $3 = \varphi(3)$, so $3^{10} \bmod 5$ is $\varphi(30 \bmod 4) = \varphi(2) = 4$.
A direct check agrees: $3^2 = 9 \equiv 4$ and $3^4 \equiv 1$, so $3^{10} = 3^8 \cdot 3^2 \equiv 4 \pmod 5$.

A trap is a homomorphism that is not injective.
The map $k \mapsto 4^k \bmod 5$ is also a homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.
Its values are $1, 4, 1, 4$, so it is not an isomorphism.

### L3 human

In the [[category of groups|2CX7RPPXA9]], an isomorphism is a homomorphism with a two sided inverse homomorphism.
For groups this is the same as a bijective homomorphism, because the inverse map is automatically a homomorphism.
This can fail for groups with extra structure, such as a topology.
The identity map from $\mathbb{R}$ with the discrete topology to $\mathbb{R}$ with the usual topology is a continuous bijective homomorphism, but its inverse is not continuous.

The [[automorphism group|0K1SCPBNKJ]] $\operatorname{Aut}(G)$ measures the symmetry of $G$.
The [[inner automorphisms|0RN00NTM3S]] form a normal subgroup of $\operatorname{Aut}(G)$ that is isomorphic to $G/Z(G)$.
Deciding whether two groups given by finite presentations are isomorphic is the [[group isomorphism problem|MP3F49QT3C]], and no algorithm solves it in general.
