## Proof

We prove parts 1, 2, 3 and 5 directly, and part 4 from part 1 by writing $\sigma$ as a product of transpositions.

1. Part 1. Let $x$ and $y$ be rows $k$ and $l$ of $M$. For rows $u, v$, let $D(u, v)$ be $\det M$ with row $k$ replaced by $u$ and row $l$ replaced by $v$.
2. Then $D(x, y) = \det M$, and $D(y, x)$ is the determinant after the exchange. By the rule for [[equal rows|0K4VQB7HWR]], $D(u, u) = 0$ for every row $u$.
3. By [[multilinearity|0PPG793642]] in row $k$ and then in row $l$, $D(x + y, x + y) = D(x, x) + D(x, y) + D(y, x) + D(y, y)$.
4. By step 2, three terms of step 3 are $0$. So $D(y, x) = -D(x, y)$, which is part 1.
5. Part 2 is multilinearity in row $k$ with the scalar $c$.
6. Part 3. The new row $k$ is $x + c y$. Multilinearity in row $k$ gives $D(x + cy, y) = D(x, y) + c D(y, y)$.
7. By step 2, $D(y, y) = 0$. So $D(x + cy, y) = D(x, y) = \det M$, which is part 3.
8. Part 5. The matrix $cA$ arises from $A$ by multiplying each of the $n$ rows by $c$. By part 2 applied $n$ times, $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$.
9. Part 4. For a matrix $M$ and a permutation $\pi$, let $M_\pi$ have row $i$ equal to row $\pi(i)$ of $M$. Then $B = A_\sigma$.
10. Row $i$ of $(M_\pi)_\rho$ is row $\rho(i)$ of $M_\pi$, which is row $\pi(\rho(i))$ of $M$. So $(M_\pi)_\rho = M_{\pi \rho}$.
11. For a [[transposition|0FWVRHBRWM]] $\tau$ of $k$ and $l$, $M_\tau$ is $M$ with rows $k$ and $l$ exchanged. By part 1, $\det M_\tau = -\det M$.
12. If $n = 1$, then $\sigma$ is the identity, $B = A$ and $\sgn(\sigma) = 1$. From now on let $n \ge 2$.
13. The group $S_n$ is [[generated by transpositions|0AQNX7G443]]. So $\sigma = \tau_1 \tau_2 \cdots \tau_m$ with transpositions $\tau_r$.
14. Let $\pi_0$ be the identity and $\pi_r = \tau_1 \cdots \tau_r$. Then $A_{\pi_0} = A$ and $A_{\pi_m} = B$.
15. By step 10, $A_{\pi_r} = (A_{\pi_{r-1}})_{\tau_r}$. So step 11 gives $\det A_{\pi_r} = -\det A_{\pi_{r-1}}$ for $1 \le r \le m$.
16. By induction on $r$, $\det A_{\pi_r} = (-1)^r \det A$. For $r = m$, this is $\det B = (-1)^m \det A$.
17. By the [[definition of the sign|0SK90238BH]], $(-1)^m = \sgn(\sigma)$. So $\det B = \sgn(\sigma) \det A$.

## Idea

Putting the sum of two rows into both places gives a determinant zero.
Expanding it shows that one exchange of two rows changes the sign.
Adding a multiple of a row splits off a determinant with two equal rows, which is zero.
A general permutation of the rows is a chain of exchanges, so the determinant picks up one factor $-1$ for each.

## Variants

With the Leibniz sum as the definition, part 4 follows by the substitution $\pi = \sigma^{-1} \rho$ in the sum, using $\sgn(\sigma \pi) = \sgn(\sigma) \sgn(\pi)$.
Part 1 then needs no use of the equal-rows rule, which is why the Leibniz approach also works in characteristic $2$.
