## Proof

We prove parts 1, 2, 3 and 5 of the [[statement|09YB3WQG38]] from multilinearity and the rule for equal rows, and part 4 from part 1 by writing $\sigma$ as a product of transpositions.

*Parts 1 to 3 and 5.*

1. Let $x$ and $y$ be rows $k$ and $l$ of $A$.
   For rows $u$ and $v$, let $D(u, v)$ be the determinant of $A$ with row $k$ replaced by $u$ and row $l$ replaced by $v$.
2. Then $D(x, y) = \det A$, and $D(y, x)$ is the determinant after the exchange of rows $k$ and $l$.
   By the rule for [[equal rows|0K4VQB7HWR]], $D(u, u) = 0$ for every row $u$.
3. By [[multilinearity|0PPG793642]] in row $k$ and then in row $l$,
$$D(x + y, x + y) = D(x, x) + D(x, y) + D(y, x) + D(y, y).$$
4. By step 2, the left side and the terms $D(x, x)$ and $D(y, y)$ are $0$.
   So $D(y, x) = -D(x, y)$, which is part 1.
5. Part 2 is multilinearity in row $k$, applied to the scalar $c$.
6. For part 3, the new row $k$ is $x + cy$, and multilinearity in row $k$ gives
$$D(x + cy, y) = D(x, y) + c\, D(y, y).$$
7. By step 2, $D(y, y) = 0$.
   So $D(x + cy, y) = D(x, y) = \det A$, which is part 3.
8. For part 5, the matrix $cA$ arises from $A$ when each of the $n$ rows is multiplied by $c$.
   Part 2 applied $n$ times gives $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$.

*Part 4.*

9. For a matrix $M$ and a permutation $\pi$, let $M_\pi$ be the matrix whose row $i$ is row $\pi(i)$ of $M$.
   Then $B = A_\sigma$.
10. Row $i$ of $(M_\pi)_\rho$ is row $\rho(i)$ of $M_\pi$, and that is row $\pi(\rho(i))$ of $M$.
    So $(M_\pi)_\rho = M_{\pi \rho}$.
11. For the [[transposition|0FWVRHBRWM]] $\tau$ of $k$ and $l$, the matrix $M_\tau$ is $M$ with rows $k$ and $l$ exchanged.
    By part 1, $\det M_\tau = -\det M$.
12. If $n = 1$, then $\sigma$ is the identity, $B = A$ and $\sgn(\sigma) = 1$, so the claim holds.
    From now on let $n \ge 2$.
13. The group $S_n$ is [[generated by transpositions|0AQNX7G443]].
    So $\sigma = \tau_1 \tau_2 \cdots \tau_m$ for some transpositions $\tau_1, \dots, \tau_m$.
14. Let $\pi_0$ be the identity and $\pi_r = \tau_1 \cdots \tau_r$ for $1 \le r \le m$.
    Then $A_{\pi_0} = A$ and $A_{\pi_m} = A_\sigma = B$.
15. Since $\pi_r = \pi_{r-1} \tau_r$, step 10 gives $A_{\pi_r} = (A_{\pi_{r-1}})_{\tau_r}$.
    So step 11 gives $\det A_{\pi_r} = -\det A_{\pi_{r-1}}$ for $1 \le r \le m$.
16. By [[induction|0ASJVEFA9J]] on $r$, $\det A_{\pi_r} = (-1)^r \det A$.
    For $r = m$ this is $\det B = (-1)^m \det A$.
17. By the [[definition of the sign|0SK90238BH]], $(-1)^m = \sgn(\sigma)$.
    So $\det B = \sgn(\sigma) \det A$.

## Idea

Put the sum of two rows into both places: the determinant is $0$, because two rows are equal.
Expand it by linearity, and two of the four pieces are $0$ for the same reason.
The two pieces that remain are the determinant before and after the exchange, and they add up to $0$.
Adding a multiple of a row splits off a piece with two equal rows, which is again $0$.
A general permutation of the rows is a chain of exchanges, and each exchange contributes one factor $-1$.

## Variants

If the Leibniz sum is the definition, part 4 follows by a change of the summation index.
The Leibniz sum for $B$ is $\sum_\pi \sgn(\pi) \prod_i a_{\sigma(i) \pi(i)}$.
Put $k = \sigma(i)$ in the product, which turns it into $\prod_k a_{k\, \pi\sigma^{-1}(k)}$.
Now put $\rho = \pi \sigma^{-1}$; as $\pi$ runs over $S_n$, so does $\rho$, and $\sgn(\pi) = \sgn(\rho) \sgn(\sigma)$.
The sum becomes $\sgn(\sigma) \sum_\rho \sgn(\rho) \prod_k a_{k \rho(k)} = \sgn(\sigma) \det A$.
Part 1 is then the case of a transposition, and part 2 is the linearity of each Leibniz term in row $k$.
Part 3 also needs the vanishing for two equal rows, which the Leibniz sum gives by cancelling its terms in pairs.
