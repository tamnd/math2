## Example

Let $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product, and let $T(x_1, x_2) = (x_2, 0)$, so $T(x) = Ax$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Let $B = (b_1, b_2)$ with $b_1 = (1, 0)$ and $b_2 = (1, 1)$.
This basis is not orthonormal, since $b_1 \cdot b_2 = 1$.
We show that the [[matrix|0YYMY95FWS]] of $T^*$ in the basis $B$ is not the transpose of the matrix of $T$ in $B$, and we find the correct rule.

1. We find the adjoint. The standard basis is orthonormal, so the [[matrix of the adjoint|0DBTE6HVBD]] gives $T^*(y) = A^{\mathsf{T}} y$, that is, $T^*(y_1, y_2) = (0, y_1)$.
2. We find the matrix of $T$ in $B$. We have $T(b_1) = (0, 0)$ and $T(b_2) = (1, 0) = b_1$, so
$$[T]_B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
3. We find the matrix of $T^*$ in $B$. We have $T^*(b_1) = (0, 1) = -b_1 + b_2$ and $T^*(b_2) = (0, 1) = -b_1 + b_2$, so
$$[T^*]_B = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
4. We compare. The transpose of $[T]_B$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which differs from $[T^*]_B$, for example in the entry $(1, 1)$.
5. We apply the correct rule. Let $H$ have the entries $H_{ij} = b_j \cdot b_i$, so $H = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, the [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]] of $B$ (over $\mathbb{R}$ it is symmetric, so the order of the indices does not matter), with $\det H = 1$ and $H^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
6. Then
$$[T]_B^{\mathsf{T}} H = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad H^{-1} [T]_B^{\mathsf{T}} H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
This is $[T^*]_B$ from step 3.

## Remarks

The rule "the adjoint is the conjugate transpose" is a rule about orthonormal bases.
In a general basis the [[matrix of the adjoint|0DBTE6HVBD]] is $[T^*]_B = H^{-1} [T]_B^* H$, and the matrix $H$ records how far the basis is from orthonormal.
If we change $b_2$ to $(0, 1)$, the basis becomes orthonormal, $H = I$, and the formula reduces to the transpose.

Some things do not depend on the basis.
The trace and the rank of $[T^*]_B$ and $[T]_B$ agree, because $\operatorname{tr} T^* = \overline{\operatorname{tr} T}$ and $\rank T^* = \rank T$ are facts about the maps.
Here both traces are $0$ and both ranks are $1$.
Check: the trace of $[T^*]_B$ is $-1 + 1 = 0$, and its two columns are equal and not zero, so its rank is $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For the dot product, the adjoint of a matrix is its transpose.
This rule works only when the coordinates come from perpendicular unit vectors.
Here the two basis vectors $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$ make an angle of $45$ degrees.
In these coordinates the adjoint has a matrix with four nonzero entries, while the transpose of the matrix of the map has only one.

### L2 human

The computation has three parts.
First we find the adjoint in an orthonormal basis, where the transpose rule holds.
Then we write the images of the new basis vectors in the new basis, one column at a time.
Last we compare with the transpose and correct it with $H$.
This example is a standard warning for numerical work, where bases are often not orthonormal.
Watch out: in the same way, the matrix of a self-adjoint map in a basis that is not orthonormal need not be symmetric.
