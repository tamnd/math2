## Summary

The Kelvin–Helmholtz timescale of a star is the time it could keep shining at its present luminosity using only its gravitational energy.
It is of order $G M^2 / (R L)$ for a star of mass $M$, radius $R$ and luminosity $L$, with $G$ the gravitational constant.
For the Sun it is about thirty million years.
It is the time scale on which a star adjusts its thermal structure.
It also sets the duration of the contraction of young stars towards the main sequence.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Divide the store of gravitational energy of a star by the rate at which it spends energy, and you get how long the store would last.
For the Sun this is tens of millions of years.
In the nineteenth century Kelvin used this to estimate the age of the Sun, but geology and later radioactive dating showed the Earth is billions of years old.
The gap was solved when nuclear fusion was found to be the real energy source of the Sun.
