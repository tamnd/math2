## Summary

Some ordinary statements about numbers, trees and graphs are true, yet a standard system of axioms cannot prove them.
For example, every [[Goodstein sequence|XQMNJF241J]] reaches $0$, and the sequence of $3$ does so in five steps, but Peano arithmetic cannot prove the general statement.

A *concrete incompleteness result* is a theorem of the following form.
A specific sentence $\varphi$ is given that is arithmetic, usually $\Pi_1$ or $\Pi_2$, that speaks about finite objects such as numbers, finite sets, trees or graphs, and that uses no coding of syntax, proofs or truth.
The theorem says that $\varphi$ is not provable in a stated theory $T$, and that it is provable in a stated stronger theory $T'$.
Such results show that [[Gödel's incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]] reach ordinary combinatorics, and they measure how much strength ordinary statements need.

## Motivation

Gödel's unprovable sentences speak about their own provability, so they look far from ordinary mathematics.
A working mathematician can fairly ask whether any statement of the usual kind, about numbers or finite structures, needs more than the usual axioms.
Concrete incompleteness answers this with explicit statements and explicit theories.

The answers also put theorems on a common scale.
Goodstein's theorem needs ordinals up to $\varepsilon_0$, the finite Kruskal theorem needs more than $\mathsf{ATR}_0$, and the graph minor theorem needs more than $\Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}_0$.
This scale comes from [[ordinal analysis|C5E5TG8PGP]] and from the growth of [[provably total functions|Z742YPHPNS]], and every page on independence in arithmetic places its statement on it.

## Notation

We write $T \nvdash \varphi$ when $T$ does not prove $\varphi$, and $T' \vdash \varphi$ when $T'$ proves it.
The classes $\Pi_n$ and $\Sigma_n$ are the levels of the [[arithmetical hierarchy|CV2E6JZHVP]].
$\mathrm{Con}(T)$ is the [[consistency|VM8TQ70CVJ]] statement of $T$, and $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(T)$ says that every $\Sigma_1$ sentence provable in $T$ is true.
The stronger theory $T'$ is often $T + 1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(T)$, or $T$ plus transfinite induction up to its proof-theoretic ordinal, or $\mathsf{ZFC}$ plus a [[large cardinal|X1FG7PSCTY]] axiom.

## Remarks

### Three results worked as examples

Each result names the theory $T$ that fails and a stronger theory $T'$ that succeeds.

The first is [[Goodstein's theorem|XQMNJF241J]].
The sequence of $3$ is $3, 3, 3, 2, 1, 0$, and every sequence reaches $0$, but $T = $ [[Peano arithmetic|MPSCREYC3M]] $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove it.
$T'$ can be $\mathsf{PA}$ plus transfinite induction up to $\varepsilon_0$, or $\mathsf{ZFC}$.

The second is the [[Paris-Harrington theorem|509ZWA0R26]].
The strengthened finite Ramsey theorem asks for a homogeneous set $H$ with $\lvert H \rvert \ge \min H$, so $\{ 3, 5, 8 \}$ qualifies and $\{ 5, 8, 9 \}$ does not.
Here $T = \mathsf{PA}$ and $T' = \mathsf{PA} + 1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$.

The third is [[Friedman's finite form of Kruskal's theorem|12XRGBWH59]].
With $3$ vertices in the first tree and one more in each later tree, every list of $3$ trees has a tree inside a later one, and a bound exists for every start.
Here $T = \mathsf{ATR}_0$ and $T' = \Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}_0$.

### More results on the same scale

The pattern repeats at several levels of strength.

- The [[Kirby-Paris hydra|C028FF4TWM]], the [[Kanamori-McAloon theorem|NM5WYBK6BG]] and [[Beklemishev's worm principle|MHDZGDNEPS]] are unprovable in $\mathsf{PA}$.
- The finite form of the [[graph minor theorem|WXNTPQGJ38]] is unprovable in $\Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}_0$, and $\mathsf{ZFC}$ proves it.
- The [[Buchholz hydra theorem|9APRFM0G7F]] is unprovable in $(\Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}) + \mathsf{BI}$, and $\mathsf{ZFC}$ proves it.

### What does not count

A Gödel sentence of $\mathsf{PA}$ is unprovable in $\mathsf{PA}$, but it codes provability, so it fails the condition of no coding.
The [[continuum hypothesis|0MN2MER9WN]] is independent of $\mathsf{ZFC}$, but it is about sets of reals, so it fails the condition of being arithmetic.
The [[finite Ramsey theorem|YSZ82MRXT8]] is a natural statement about finite sets, but $\mathsf{PA}$ proves it, so it fails the condition of being unprovable in $T = \mathsf{PA}$.
[[Fermat's Last Theorem|0Y5X3SDBCZ]] is a $\Pi_1$ sentence whose known proof uses heavy tools, but no unprovability result for it is known, so it is not an incompleteness result.

### The rules you can use

A true $\Sigma_1$ sentence is provable in $\mathsf{PA}$, because a witness exists and its check is a finite computation that $\mathsf{PA}$ proves.
So concrete incompleteness for $\mathsf{PA}$ starts at the level $\Pi_1$, and most natural examples are $\Pi_2$.
For a $\Pi_2$ sentence $\forall x\, \exists y\, \theta(x, y)$, unprovability is often shown by growth: the least $y$ grows faster than every provably total function of $T$.

If $\varphi$ is true and $T$ proves only true sentences, then $T$ does not prove $\neg \varphi$ either, so a true unprovable sentence is independent of $T$.
The truth value of an arithmetic sentence does not change under [[forcing|F2DTWW9HP7]], so the independence of an arithmetic sentence from $\mathsf{ZFC}$ cannot come from forcing alone.

### Watch out

"Independent of $\mathsf{PA}$" does not mean "neither true nor false".
The Paris-Harrington sentence is true, and $\mathsf{ZFC}$ proves it.
Independence from $\mathsf{PA}$ does not give independence from $\mathsf{ZFC}$ either: every example above for $\mathsf{PA}$ is a theorem of $\mathsf{ZFC}$.

"Natural" is not a formal notion.
A result states the exact sentence and the exact theories, and naturalness is a judgment about the sentence.
A claim of independence without the theory $T$ is not a precise statement.

### History

Kurt Gödel gave the first unprovable arithmetic sentences in 1931, by coding provability.
In 1977 Paris and Harrington published a combinatorial statement unprovable in $\mathsf{PA}$, often described as the first natural example.
In 1982 Kirby and Paris showed that $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove Goodstein's theorem of 1944.
In the early 1980s Harvey Friedman showed that the finite form of Kruskal's theorem is unprovable in $\mathsf{ATR}_0$, an early success of [[reverse mathematics|TQTDKMBJ0F]].
Friedman's later program, Boolean relation theory, proposes concrete statements whose proofs need large cardinal axioms.

### Try it

First, is "every even number greater than $2$ is a sum of two primes" a concrete incompleteness result?
No: it is a $\Pi_1$ sentence about numbers, but nobody has shown that $\mathsf{PA}$ fails to prove it, and it is not even known to be true.

Second, why can a true sentence $\exists x\, \theta(x)$ with $\theta$ bounded never be independent of $\mathsf{PA}$?
Because $\mathsf{PA}$ proves $\theta(n)$ for the witness $n$ by a finite computation, and then it proves $\exists x\, \theta(x)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Mathematics starts from a list of basic rules, called axioms.
Gödel showed that every reasonable list leaves some true statements unproved, but his statements talk about proofs themselves.
Concrete incompleteness looks for ordinary statements, for example about coloring numbers or about growing trees, that a given list of axioms cannot prove.
Several such statements are known, and each of them can be proved with a stronger list of axioms.
So the answer to "is this provable?" always depends on which axioms are allowed.

### L2 human

We read Goodstein's theorem as a concrete incompleteness result, step by step.
The sentence says that every Goodstein sequence reaches $0$.
It is $\Pi_2$, since it says that for every start $m$ there is a step $k$ at which the value is $0$.
The theory $T = \mathsf{PA}$ does not prove it, by the theorem of Kirby and Paris.
The stronger theory $T' = \mathsf{ZFC}$, or $\mathsf{PA}$ with transfinite induction up to $\varepsilon_0$, proves it with ordinals below $\varepsilon_0$.
The method is growth: the length of the sequence of $m$ grows faster than every function that $\mathsf{PA}$ proves total.

So the sentence is true and independent of $\mathsf{PA}$.
The typical use of this pattern is to compare the strength of theorems on one scale of theories.
The trap is to drop $T$ or $T'$ from the statement, which leaves nothing precise.

### L3 human

Concrete incompleteness has two sides.
The proof-theoretic side measures each statement by the theories that prove it, through ordinal analysis and the growth of provably total functions.
In the results of this kind the scale goes from $\mathsf{PA}$ with ordinal $\varepsilon_0$, through $\mathsf{ATR}_0$ with ordinal $\Gamma_0$, to $(\Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}) + \mathsf{BI}$ and beyond.

The foundational side asks how far ordinary mathematics depends on strong axioms.
Friedman's grand conjecture says that every theorem published in the Annals of Mathematics whose statement involves only finitary objects is provable in [[elementary function arithmetic|2JD80TE553]], a theory much weaker than $\mathsf{PA}$, and it is open.
At the other end, Friedman's Boolean relation theory gives concrete statements about functions on the natural numbers whose proofs need large cardinal axioms beyond $\mathsf{ZFC}$.
