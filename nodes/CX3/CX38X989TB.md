## Summary

You cannot check infinitely many numbers one at a time, but you can reason about one number that you refuse to choose.
If the argument uses nothing special about that number, it works for every number at once, and that is how a claim "for every $x$" is proved.

Here is a small case: for every real number $x$, $x^2 + 1 \ge 2x$.
Let $x$ be an arbitrary real number.
Then
$$x^2 + 1 - 2x = (x - 1)^2 \ge 0,$$
because the square of a real number is never negative, so $x^2 + 1 \ge 2x$.
The proof never said which $x$ it meant, so it holds for each real $x$.

In general, to prove a universal statement "for every $x$ in a set $X$, $P(x)$", we let $x$ be an arbitrary element of $X$ and prove $P(x)$ for that $x$.
"Arbitrary" means two things: $x$ is a new letter that occurs in no hypothesis still in force, and the proof uses no property of $x$ except $x \in X$.
This is the *generic element argument*, and in logic it is the rule of [[universal generalization|BKQ3YD6SVZ]].

## Motivation

Most theorems are universal statements, so the generic element is the first line of most proofs.
The integers and the real numbers have infinitely many elements, and a finite list of checks proves nothing about the others.
The polynomial $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for $n = 0, 1, \dots, 39$, and still [[fails at $n = 40$|H75AB4EJT5]], where it equals $41^2$.
The way out is to make the argument a template: one element with a name, $x$, and only facts that are true of every element.
Then each element of the set can be put into the template.

The same move returns in every later proof method.
A [[direct proof|0NTF6DMVAP]] of "for all $x$, if $P(x)$ then $Q(x)$" starts with a generic $x$ that satisfies $P(x)$, and [[mathematical induction|0ASJVEFA9J]] proves its inductive step for a generic $n$.

## Notation

A universal statement is written $\forall x \in X, \ P(x)$ with the [[universal quantifier|0B88PQTJ66]].
Often the quantifier is not written, as in the identity $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$; see [[quantifiers in mathematical writing|EGTZPA6QH3]].
A proof opens with "Let $x \in X$.", "Let $x$ be an arbitrary element of $X$." or "Fix $x \in X$."
For "for all $x$, if $P(x)$ then $Q(x)$", the opening is "Let $x$ be such that $P(x)$."
In Lean the tactic `intro x` starts a proof of a goal `∀ x, P x`.

## Remarks

### Why the method is valid

A universal statement is true when every element passes the test $P$.
The generic proof is a recipe: give it any element $a$ of $X$, replace the letter $x$ by $a$ in every line, and each line stays correct.
That is exactly why the proof must not use a special property of $x$.
If a line said "$x$ is even", the recipe would break for $a = 3$.

The formal rule has a side condition: the letter $x$ must not be free in any assumption still open.
Without it, from the assumption $x = 0$ we could derive $x = 0$ and then "for every $x$, $x = 0$", which is false.

### Several quantifiers

Nested quantifiers are opened in their order, one "let" for each.
To show that [[the intersection of two sets is a subset of each|SAHE0068ZZ]], we let $A$ and $B$ be arbitrary sets, and then we let $x$ be arbitrary with $x \in A \cap B$.
The definition of intersection gives $x \in A$ and $x \in B$, so $x \in A$, and the inclusion $A \cap B \subseteq A$ follows.

A "there is" inside a "for every" lets its witness depend on the generic element.
In "for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ with ...", the proof says "Let $\varepsilon > 0$." and then may choose $\delta = \varepsilon / 2$.
In "there is $y$ such that for every $x$ ...", the $y$ comes first and may not depend on $x$; see the [[order of quantifiers|0FYQMKFE77]].

### More examples

[[The polynomial x^2 - 2x + 2 is positive|77SJHGNFR8]] for every real $x$, because for arbitrary $x$ it equals $(x - 1)^2 + 1 \ge 1$.
For every integer $n$, the product $n(n + 1)$ is even: let $n$ be arbitrary and use a [[proof by cases|0WCP0PVET6]] on the parity of $n$.
If $n = 2k$, then $n(n + 1) = 2k(n + 1)$, and if $n = 2k + 1$, then $n + 1 = 2(k + 1)$ and $n(n + 1) = 2n(k + 1)$.
A case split is allowed inside a generic proof, because the cases cover every integer.

A universal statement over the empty set is true, since no element can fail the test.
This is [[vacuous truth|0V4QXPGJQK]].

### What is not a proof of a universal statement

A list of checks is not.
In [[checking cases for 2^n > n^2|3K78AT7X0R]], a student checks $n = 1, 5, 6, 7$ and concludes the claim for all $n$, but it fails at $n = 2, 3, 4$.
An argument that starts "let $x = 3$" and computes $x^2 - 2x + 2 = 5 > 0$ proves the claim for $x = 3$ only.

An element that is called arbitrary but is not also fails.
"Let $n$ be an arbitrary integer, so $n = 2k$." uses evenness, which an arbitrary integer does not have.
The same error appears in geometry as [[a special figure used as a generic one|4KAG9W328V]].

### Watch out

The most common error is the [[proof by example|ZX6ERT3SW7]]: ten or a million checks prove only the cases checked.
The cure is to ask, for each line, whether it is true for every element of the set.

A second error hides in an open hypothesis.
If the proof assumed "$x > 0$" earlier, a later conclusion about $x$ holds only for $x > 0$.
For example, from $x > 0$ we get $\sqrt{x^2} = x$, but $x = -2$ gives $\sqrt{4} = 2 \ne -2$.

To disprove a universal statement, one [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]] is enough, by the [[negation of a universal statement|0P3A06QNDP]].

### History

Euclid's proofs speak of one figure, as in "let $ABC$ be a triangle", and the reader understands that the argument applies to every triangle.
Gerhard Gentzen's system of natural deduction stated the rule exactly, with the side condition on open assumptions, and that side condition is the formal meaning of "arbitrary".

### Try it

First, prove that for every integer $n$, $(n + 1)^2 - n^2$ is odd.
Let $n$ be an arbitrary integer; then $(n + 1)^2 - n^2 = 2n + 1$, which is odd by definition.

Second, a student takes $n = 4$, notes that $n^2 = 16$ is even, and concludes that every square is even; find the error.
The proof fixes one value, so it proves a fact about $4$ only, and the claim is false, since $3^2 = 9$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose someone claims that each whole number times the next one is even.
We cannot check every number, because there are infinitely many.
Instead we think of "some number", call it $n$, and we do not say which one.
If $n$ is even, the product $n(n + 1)$ has an even factor, and if $n$ is odd, then $n + 1$ is even.
Either way the product is even.
We used nothing special about $n$, so the argument works for $5 \cdot 6 = 30$, for $8 \cdot 9 = 72$, and for every other pair.

### L2 human

Let us prove that for all real $x$ and $y$ with $x < y$, the midpoint satisfies $x < \frac{x + y}{2} < y$.
Let $x$ and $y$ be arbitrary real numbers with $x < y$, which opens both quantifiers and the hypothesis.
Add $x$ to both sides, and separately add $y$ to both sides:
$$2x < x + y, \qquad x + y < 2y.$$
Divide each inequality by the positive number $2$, and the two halves of the claim appear.
Check: $x = 1$ and $y = 3$ give $1 < 2 < 3$.
We used only $x < y$ and the rules of inequalities, so the result holds for all such pairs, including negative numbers and numbers very close together.
The typical use is the one shown: open every quantifier and hypothesis at the start, then finish with a direct proof.

### L3 human

In type theory a proof of $\forall x : X, \, P(x)$ is a dependent function that sends each $x$ to a proof of $P(x)$, so the generic element is the bound variable of that function.
In category theory, the Yoneda lemma says that a natural transformation out of a representable functor is fixed by its value on one universal element, the identity arrow, which is a generic element in a precise sense.
In algebraic geometry, the generic point of an irreducible variety makes the informal idea exact, and the topic [[almost all, generic and in general|N1XSAXCQY3]] treats the looser uses of the word.
When no generic argument works directly, [[mathematical induction|0ASJVEFA9J]] and the [[well-ordering principle|01BX15HSRR]], from the topic [[induction and well-ordering|EEH2F76CYQ]], give other ways to prove statements about all natural numbers.
