## Statement

A linear map is diagonalizable exactly when there are enough eigenvectors to make a basis, and then the eigenvectors are the columns of the change of basis matrix.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite dimension $n$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear.
Let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ be the pairwise distinct [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $T$ in $F$, and let $E_{\lambda_i} = \ker(T - \lambda_i \id_V)$ be the [[eigenspaces|0G0RFPG8HR]].
Then the following conditions are equivalent.

1. $T$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]].
2. $V$ has a [[basis|0KCQ9Y3QKA]] of [[eigenvectors|0N6960W0BV]] of $T$.
3. $V = E_{\lambda_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus E_{\lambda_k}$, a [[direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]].
4. $\dim E_{\lambda_1} + \cdots + \dim E_{\lambda_k} = n$, that is, the [[geometric multiplicities|099TWVJ909]] add up to $n$.

For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over $F$, apply this to the map $x \mapsto A x$ on $F^n$.
If $v_1, \dots, v_n$ is a basis of eigenvectors of $A$ with $A v_i = \mu_i v_i$, and $P$ is the matrix with the columns $v_1, \dots, v_n$, then $P$ is invertible and
$$P^{-1} A P = \operatorname{diag}(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n), \qquad A = P \operatorname{diag}(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n) P^{-1}.$$
Conversely, if $P$ is invertible and $P^{-1} A P = D$ is diagonal, then each column of $P$ is an eigenvector of $A$ for the matching diagonal entry of $D$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The dimension of $V$ must be finite for condition 4 to make sense.
On the space of all real polynomials, the derivative has the single eigenvalue $0$, whose eigenspace is the line of constants.
So conditions 2 and 3 fail there, and condition 4 compares $1$ with an infinite dimension.

The eigenvalues must lie in $F$.
Over $\R$, the rotation by a right angle has no eigenvalue, so $k = 0$, the sum in condition 4 is $0$, and the rotation is not diagonalizable over $\R$.

The list $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ must contain every eigenvalue.
For $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$, the eigenspace of $1$ alone has dimension $1 < 2$, although the matrix is diagonal.

### What it gives you

Condition 4 is the practical test: find each eigenspace, take its dimension, and add.
For example, $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalues $3$ and $2$.
The eigenspace of $3$ is spanned by $e_1$, and $A - 2I$ has rank $2$, so the eigenspace of $2$ is the line spanned by $e_2$.
The dimensions add up to $2 < 3$, so $A$ is not diagonalizable.

Several later results start here.
If $T$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues, each eigenspace contributes at least $1$, so condition 4 holds, which is the rule that [[n distinct eigenvalues imply diagonalizable|0Y95Z7MT02]].
Combined with the bound of a geometric multiplicity by the algebraic one, condition 4 gives the [[test by multiplicities|CDB8KCNNG9]].
The matrix form $A P = P D$, read column by column, is the [[eigendecomposition|D0AP2R7272]] $A v_i = \mu_i v_i$.

### The converse and the choices

All four conditions are equivalent, so every converse holds.
The matrix $P$ is far from unique.
Each column can be multiplied by a nonzero scalar, the basis of each eigenspace can be changed, and the columns can be reordered.
Only one rule is fixed: the order of the columns of $P$ must match the order of the eigenvalues in $D$.

### Generalizations

In infinite dimension, we call $T$ diagonalizable when $V$ has a basis of eigenvectors, and then conditions 2 and 3 are still equivalent.
For a family of commuting diagonalizable maps there is a common basis of eigenvectors, by [[simultaneous diagonalization|0FK17N4DKA]].
When $V$ is the direct sum of the eigenspaces, the restriction of $T$ to every [[invariant subspace|0ACX5QT8TE]] is again diagonalizable, and the [[minimal polynomial criterion|0MPAJRB23T]] gives the shortest proof.

## History

The characterization grew out of the nineteenth-century study of normal forms of matrices, in which Weierstrass and Jordan took part.
It is now a standard theorem of first courses in linear algebra, and it has no single first source that we can name.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To make a matrix diagonal, we need as many independent special directions as the dimension.
A special direction is one that the matrix only stretches.
In the plane, we need two special directions that do not lie on one line.
If we use them as axes, the matrix becomes a list of stretch factors, one for each axis.

### L2 human

Let us decide whether $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is diagonalizable over $\R$.
The matrix is lower triangular, so its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries, $1$ and $2$.
For the eigenvalue $2$ we row reduce
$$A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
which has rank $1$, so $\dim E_2 = 2$, with the basis $e_2$ and $e_3$.
For the eigenvalue $1$ the matrix $A - I$ has the rows $(0, 0, 0)$, $(0, 1, 0)$ and $(3, 0, 1)$, of rank $2$, and its kernel is the line spanned by $(1, 0, -3)$.
The dimensions add up to $2 + 1 = 3$, so condition 4 holds and $A$ is diagonalizable.
Check: $A(1, 0, -3) = (1, 0, 3 - 6) = (1, 0, -3)$.
So we take the columns $(1, 0, -3)$, $e_2$, $e_3$ for $P$, and multiply back:
$$P D P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ -3 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ -3 + 6 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = A,$$
where the first factor is $P D$ for $D = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2, 2)$ and the second is $P^{-1}$.
Watch out: the count must use the dimensions of the eigenspaces, not the number of times each eigenvalue appears on the diagonal.

### L3 human

Condition 3 says that $V$, as a module over $F[x]$ with $x$ acting as $T$, is a direct sum of submodules on which $x$ acts as a scalar.
In representation theory this says that the representation of $F[x]$ on $V$ is a direct sum of one-dimensional representations.
For a commutative algebra of operators, the common eigenspaces are called weight spaces, and diagonalizability of the algebra means that the weight spaces fill $V$.
When diagonalization fails, the [[generalized eigenspaces|0AJ035ZTZE]] and the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]] measure how far $T$ is from diagonal.
