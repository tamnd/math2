## Example

Let $g > 0$ be the acceleration of gravity, and let $\ell > 0$, $m > 0$, $a > 0$ and $\nu > 0$.
A rigid pendulum of length $\ell$ with a point mass $m$ hangs from a pivot that moves up and down with height $a \cos \nu t$.
Let $\theta$ be the angle of the rod from the downward vertical.
In the frame of the pivot the effective gravity is $g - a \nu^2 \cos \nu t$, so the motion satisfies
$$\theta'' + \frac{g - a \nu^2 \cos \nu t}{\ell} \sin \theta = 0.$$
This is the [[pendulum equation|0SHPXM2HWH]] with a periodically varying coefficient, so it is a nonlinear [[parametric oscillator|HF4K06Q6XX]].

Assume fast and small vibrations: $a \ll \ell$ and $\nu \gg \sqrt{g / \ell}$.
Averaging over the fast vibration, the slow part of $\theta$ moves in the effective potential
$$U_{\mathrm{eff}}(\theta) = - m g \ell \cos \theta + \frac{m a^2 \nu^2}{4} \sin^2 \theta.$$
Check at the upright position: put $\theta = \pi + \psi$.
For small $\psi$, $U_{\mathrm{eff}} = m g \ell + \left( \frac{m a^2 \nu^2}{4} - \frac{m g \ell}{2} \right) \psi^2 + O(\psi^4)$.
So $\theta = \pi$ is a minimum of $U_{\mathrm{eff}}$, and the inverted pendulum is [[stable|0F2SYCKDT2]], when $a^2 \nu^2 > 2 g \ell$.

## Remarks

Without the vibration ($a = 0$) the upright position is unstable, as for the ordinary pendulum.
The downward position $\theta = 0$ stays stable for all $a$ and $\nu$ in this approximation.
Pyotr Kapitza analysed the effect in 1951, and it is a standard example of the averaging method for fast forcing.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Normally a pendulum turned upside down falls over at the smallest push.
If its pivot is shaken up and down quickly enough, the upside-down position becomes stable, and the pendulum stands upright.
The fast shaking creates an average force that pushes the rod back toward the vertical.
