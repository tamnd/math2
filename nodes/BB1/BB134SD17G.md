## Definition

Consider a fluid occupying an open set $\Omega \subseteq \R^3$, with density $\rho > 0$, velocity [[vector field|0299VCW1TH]] $u$ and pressure $p$, all depending on position and time.
The flow is *inviscid* if the Cauchy stress tensor is isotropic, $\sigma = -p I$, at every point and time.
Equivalently, the fluid has zero [[viscosity|NM5Q4845Z4]], and the balance of momentum takes the form
$$\rho \Big( \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + (u \cdot \nabla) u \Big) = -\nabla p + \rho f,$$
where $f$ is the body force per unit mass.
Together with conservation of mass, these are the [[Euler equations|RW0JK9VDZE]].

## Remarks

Example: with $\rho = 1$ and $f = 0$, the rigid rotation $u = (-y, x, 0)$ with $p = (x^2 + y^2)/2$ satisfies the momentum equation, since $(u \cdot \nabla) u = (-x, -y, 0) = -\nabla p$.

Non-example: in a viscous plane Couette flow $u = (\dot\gamma y, 0, 0)$ of a Newtonian fluid with viscosity $\mu > 0$, the stress has the off-diagonal entry $\sigma_{12} = \mu \dot\gamma \ne 0$, so $\sigma$ is not isotropic.

Viscous effects cannot be dropped near solid walls, where the no-slip condition holds; there one uses boundary layer theory.
An ideal fluid is a fluid that is inviscid and also has no heat conduction.
