## Summary

The Jaffe-Quinn and Thurston debate is an exchange of essays in the Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society in 1993 and 1994 on the role of speculation and of proof in mathematics.
Arthur Jaffe and Frank Quinn proposed that mathematics should recognize "theoretical mathematics", work that reaches results by intuition and heuristic argument, and should label it explicitly as not proved.
William Thurston replied that proofs serve human understanding, and that mathematical knowledge lives in a community more than in written proofs.
Fifteen other mathematicians responded, and the debate is still a standard reference on how [[rigour|E1PE9R8DBB]] relates to discovery and to credit.

## Motivation

In the 1980s and early 1990s ideas from theoretical physics produced many striking predictions in geometry and topology.
Physicists and some mathematicians stated results with strong evidence and no proof.
At the same time, some major results in topology were announced with proofs that were not fully written.
This raised practical questions.
One question was who deserves credit for a result: the person who first stated it, or the person who proved it.
Another was how a reader can tell which claims in the literature are proved, and which are only [[conjectures|WMEMMHZ3BH]] with good evidence.

## Notation

Jaffe and Quinn borrowed terms from physics.
"Theoretical mathematics" means speculative work, by analogy with theoretical physics.
"Rigorous mathematics" means work with complete proofs, by analogy with experimental physics, which tests the theory.
They proposed that theoretical work use the words "conjecture" for "theorem", "predict" for "show", and "motivation" or "supporting argument" for "proof".

## Remarks

### Jaffe and Quinn, 1993

The essay "'Theoretical mathematics': Toward a cultural synthesis of mathematics and theoretical physics" appeared in volume 29 of the Bulletin in 1993.
Jaffe and Quinn saw speculation as valuable, and they listed four dangers when it is not marked as such.
Without the test of proof, theory can go astray.
Readers cannot tell which parts of the literature are reliable.
When a speculator claims full credit, a field can become "dead", because nobody has a reason to supply the proofs.
Students can be misled about what is known.

Their remedies were labels and credit.
Speculative papers should say so, for example with "theoretical" or "speculative" in the title.
A major share of the credit should go to the people who supply the proofs.
Research announcements should appear only as summaries of full versions that have been accepted.

They discussed several cases.
Dehn's lemma was stated in 1910 with a flawed proof, and Papakyriakopoulos proved it in 1957.
Other cases were the Italian school of algebraic geometry, Poincaré's Analysis Situs of 1895, and Thurston's geometrization theorem for Haken manifolds, whose full proof they described as never fully published.
They also discussed work of Thom, Sullivan and Witten.

### Thurston, 1994

Thurston's reply, "On proof and progress in mathematics", appeared in volume 30 of the Bulletin in April 1994.
He began with the question of how mathematicians advance human understanding of mathematics, and not with the question of how they produce theorems.
He argued that a proof is a social act: its level of detail depends on the audience, and a proof that takes two minutes for one group of experts can take an hour for another.
He described his own work on foliations, after which, he said, other mathematicians left the field, because his results seemed to leave little to do and the ideas were not well communicated.
In his later work on 3-manifolds he put more effort into communicating ideas, through notes that he sent to many people.
He said that his proof for Haken manifolds was accepted quickly through the trust of experts, while the written proof remained incomplete.

### The responses

The same volume printed responses by fifteen mathematicians: Atiyah, Borel, Chaitin, Friedan, Glimm, Gray, Hirsch, Mac Lane, Mandelbrot, Ruelle, Schwarz, Uhlenbeck, Thom, Witten and Zeeman, with a reply by Jaffe and Quinn.
Atiyah objected that Jaffe and Quinn gave a cleaned-up picture of mathematics that would make the subject stiff and old.
Other responses supported the call for clear labels, or questioned the analogy with physics.
The debate did not end with an agreement, and later writers on mathematical practice still cite all sides.

### Examples

- Mirror symmetry: physicists predicted numbers of rational curves on a quintic threefold, and mathematicians later proved these predictions. Jaffe and Quinn would call the prediction "theoretical" and the proof "rigorous".
- The [[geometrization conjecture|B27HB43K86]]: Thurston proved the Haken case and stated the general conjecture. Perelman's proof of the general case, from 2002 and 2003, was checked in detail by three teams, as the page [[acceptance of proofs|MJ3EHQJJHX]] describes.

### Non-examples

- A conjecture stated as a conjecture, such as the [[Goldbach conjecture|063JNBSMJ0]], is not the problem that Jaffe and Quinn described. The condition that fails: the status is labelled correctly.
- A complete proof that is short and checked at once raises no question of credit. The condition that fails: no speculative stage separates the claim from the proof.

### Basic properties

- Both sides agreed that speculation and proof are both needed. They disagreed about the weight of each, and about how to mark and reward them.
- The debate concerns practice and credit, not logic. It does not question that a theorem needs a proof.
- The questions return with each new source of evidence, such as computation; experimental methods are treated in [[experimental mathematics|DME0TPC348]].

### History

The Jaffe-Quinn essay appeared in 1993, Thurston's reply and the fifteen responses in 1994.
Thurston received the Fields Medal in 1982 for his work on 3-manifolds.
Perelman's work of 2002 and 2003 proved the geometrization conjecture, so the case at the centre of the debate is now settled.

### Common errors

- To think that Jaffe and Quinn opposed speculation. They valued it, and they asked for labels and for a fair division of credit.
- To think that Thurston rejected proof. He argued that proof serves understanding, and that understanding is the main aim.
- To read the debate as physics against mathematics. The essays concern the practice of mathematics, and physics enters as a source of examples.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some mathematicians are good at guessing true results, and others are good at proving them.
In 1993 two mathematicians, Arthur Jaffe and Frank Quinn, wrote that a guess must carry the label of a guess.
They also said that the people who later prove it should get a fair share of the credit.
In 1994 William Thurston answered that the real aim of mathematics is understanding, and that proofs are one way to share it.
Fifteen other mathematicians then wrote short replies, and they did not all agree.

### L2 human

We apply the labels of Jaffe and Quinn to a sample abstract.
The first sentence of the abstract: "We show that every space of type X has property P."
The second sentence: "The proof uses a new correspondence with physics and computer evidence."

1. Ask whether each step is proved. Suppose that the correspondence with physics is not proved; then the result is not proved.
2. Replace "show" by "predict", and "proof" by "supporting argument".
3. Mark the work as "theoretical" in the title or the abstract.
4. The abstract becomes: "We predict that every space of type X has property P." The second sentence uses "supporting argument" and "conjectural correspondence".
5. A later paper that proves the claim states it as a theorem, and it cites the first paper as the source of the conjecture.

The typical use of these labels is in a refereeing report, to ask that a claim match its evidence.
A trap is to apply the labels to every heuristic remark: a heuristic inside a paper with full proofs needs no label, since the theorems are proved.

### L3 human

The debate connects to three questions of the philosophy of mathematical practice.
The first is the purpose of proof: verification, explanation, or communication, which the page on [[functions of proof|ENY0GKRJS8]] lists.
The second is the social nature of acceptance, also raised by De Millo, Lipton and Perlis in 1979; see [[acceptance of proofs|MJ3EHQJJHX]].
The third is the epistemic status of strong evidence without proof, which also arises for [[computer-assisted proof|MC18C7BMFT]] and for heuristic and experimental work.
Formal proof assistants give a new reply to Jaffe and Quinn: a claim can now be marked as proved by a checked formal proof, as opposed to accepted by experts; see [[formal and informal proofs|8VZ77R4R8S]].
