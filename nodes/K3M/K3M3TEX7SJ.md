## Summary

In 1910 Max Dehn published a proof of a basic lemma about surfaces inside three-dimensional manifolds, now called Dehn's lemma.
In 1929 Hellmuth Kneser found a gap in it, and the lemma stayed unproved until Christos Papakyriakopoulos proved it in 1957.
For almost twenty years the field had treated as known a result that nobody had proved.
Cases like this one lie behind a debate about speculation and proof in the 1990s.

In 1993 Arthur Jaffe and Frank Quinn proposed, in the Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society, that mathematics should recognize "theoretical mathematics": work that reaches results by intuition and heuristic argument, and that is openly labeled as not proved.
In 1994 William Thurston replied in the same journal, in "On proof and progress in mathematics", that the aim of mathematics is human understanding, and that proofs serve that aim.
Fifteen other mathematicians responded in the same issue.
The exchange is still a standard reference on how [[rigor|E1PE9R8DBB]] relates to discovery and to credit.

## Motivation

In the 1980s and early 1990s ideas from theoretical physics produced striking predictions in geometry and topology.
Physicists and some mathematicians stated results with strong evidence and no proof, and some major results in topology circulated with proofs that were not fully written.
This raised practical questions.
Who deserves credit for a result: the person who first states it, or the person who proves it?
And how can a reader tell which claims in the literature are proved and which are only [[conjectures|WMEMMHZ3BH]] with good evidence?

## Notation

Jaffe and Quinn borrowed their terms from physics.
"Theoretical mathematics" means speculative work, by analogy with theoretical physics.
"Rigorous mathematics" means work with complete proofs, by analogy with experimental physics, which tests the theory.
They suggested that speculative work use words such as "conjecture" and "predict" where a proved result would use "theorem" and "show".

## Remarks

### The proposal of Jaffe and Quinn

The essay "'Theoretical mathematics': toward a cultural synthesis of mathematics and theoretical physics" appeared in volume 29 of the Bulletin in 1993.
Jaffe and Quinn valued speculation, and they saw dangers when it is not marked as such.
Without the test of proof, a theory can go astray.
Readers and students cannot tell which parts of the literature are reliable.
When the first speculator receives all the credit, nobody has a reason to supply the proofs, and a field can stall.

Their remedies were labels and a fair share of credit.
Speculative work should say that it is speculative, and a major share of the credit should go to those who supply the proofs.
Their cases included the Italian school of algebraic geometry and Thurston's geometrization theorem for Haken manifolds, whose complete proof they described as not yet published.

### Thurston's reply

Thurston's essay appeared in volume 30 of the Bulletin in 1994.
He began from the question of how mathematicians advance human understanding of mathematics, not from the question of how they produce theorems.
He argued that a proof is a form of communication between people, and that its level of detail depends on its readers.
He described his early work on foliations, after which, in his account, other mathematicians left the field, because his results seemed to leave little to do and the ideas were not well communicated.
In his later work on three-dimensional manifolds he put more effort into sharing ideas, for example through lecture notes that he sent to many people.
He also discussed the Haken case, which experts accepted through their understanding of the ideas while the written proof remained incomplete.

### The responses

The 1994 volume printed responses by Atiyah, Borel, Chaitin, Friedan, Glimm, Gray, Hirsch, Mac Lane, Mandelbrot, Ruelle, Schwarz, Uhlenbeck, Thom, Witten and Zeeman, followed by a reply from Jaffe and Quinn.
Atiyah argued that the proposals would give a cleaned-up and too rigid picture of mathematics, and would discourage the speculative work from which new ideas come.
Other responses supported the call for clear labels, or questioned the analogy with physics.
The debate did not end in agreement, and later writers on mathematical practice cite all sides.

### Two cases to test the labels

In 1991 a group of physicists used mirror symmetry to predict the numbers of rational curves of each degree on a quintic threefold.
Alexander Givental in 1996, and Bong Lian, Kefeng Liu and Shing-Tung Yau in 1997, proved the predictions.
In the terms of Jaffe and Quinn, the prediction was theoretical and the proof rigorous, and both deserved credit.

The [[geometrization conjecture|B27HB43K86]] is the second case.
Thurston proved the Haken case and stated the general conjecture.
Perelman's proof of the general case, in preprints of 2002 and 2003, was checked in detail by three teams, as [[acceptance of proofs|MJ3EHQJJHX]] describes.

A conjecture stated as a conjecture, such as [[Goldbach's conjecture|063JNBSMJ0]], is not the problem that Jaffe and Quinn described, because its status is labeled correctly.
A short complete proof checked at once raises no question of credit either, because no speculative stage separates claim from proof.

### Watch out

Do not read Jaffe and Quinn as opponents of speculation.
They valued it, and they asked for labels and for a fair division of credit.
Do not read Thurston as rejecting proof either.
He argued that proof serves understanding, and that understanding is the main aim.
And do not read the debate as physics against mathematics, since the essays concern the practice of mathematics and physics enters as a source of examples.

### Try it

First, an abstract says "we show that every space of type X has property P", but its argument uses an unproved correspondence from physics.
How would Jaffe and Quinn have it rewritten?
As "we predict that every space of type X has property P", with the argument called a supporting argument and the paper marked as speculative.

Second, does a heuristic remark inside a paper with complete proofs need such a label?
No, because the theorems of the paper are proved, and the remark claims nothing more.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some mathematicians are good at guessing true results, and others are good at proving them.
In 1993 Arthur Jaffe and Frank Quinn wrote that a guess must carry the label of a guess.
They also said that the people who later prove it should get a fair share of the credit.
In 1994 William Thurston answered that the real aim of mathematics is understanding, and that proofs are one way to share it.
Fifteen other mathematicians then wrote short replies, and they did not all agree.

### L2 human

We apply the two emphases of the debate to Dehn's lemma.
Dehn published his proof in 1910 as a theorem, so for 19 years later authors could build on it in good faith.
After Kneser found the gap in 1929, the lemma and every result that used it had no proof until 1957.
The labels that Jaffe and Quinn proposed would have helped after 1929, when the honest status of the lemma was "conjecture with strong evidence".
The emphasis on understanding points to a second lesson: a statement that experts believe for good reasons still guides research while its proof is missing.
The case shows the typical use of the debate: it separates what is known from what is believed, and it asks who does the work of the step between them.
Watch out: the label belongs to the claim, not to the person, and a strong mathematician's claim needs the same label as anyone else's.

### L3 human

The debate connects to three questions of the philosophy of mathematical practice.
The first is the purpose of proof, whether verification, explanation or communication, which [[functions of proof|ENY0GKRJS8]] surveys.
The second is the social nature of acceptance, raised also by De Millo, Lipton and Perlis in 1979 and discussed in [[acceptance of proofs|MJ3EHQJJHX]].
The third is the status of strong evidence without proof, which returns for [[computer-assisted proof|MC18C7BMFT]] and for [[experimental mathematics|DME0TPC348]].
Proof assistants give a new answer to Jaffe and Quinn: a claim can now be marked as proved by a checked [[formal proof|8VZ77R4R8S]], as opposed to accepted by experts.
