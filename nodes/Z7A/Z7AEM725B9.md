## Example

Let $q = \frac{1}{2}(1 + i + j + k)$, a [[quaternion|AM07TESJED]] with $a = b = c = d = \frac{1}{2}$.
We compute its matrix $\phi(q)$ from [[the quaternion theorem|A763JSEVW8]], and we check it.

1. $\abs{q}^2 = 4 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = 1$, so $q$ is a unit quaternion.
2. $\alpha = a + bi = \frac{1 + i}{2}$ and $\beta = c + di = \frac{1 + i}{2}$.
3. So $U = \phi(q) = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ -\overline{\beta} & \overline{\alpha} \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 + i & 1 + i \\ -1 + i & 1 - i \end{pmatrix}$.
4. $\det U = \frac{1}{4}\left((1 + i)(1 - i) - (1 + i)(-1 + i)\right) = \frac{1}{4}(2 - (-2)) = 1$.
5. The columns $\frac{1}{2}(1 + i, -1 + i)$ and $\frac{1}{2}(1 + i, 1 - i)$ have length $\sqrt{\frac{2 + 2}{4}} = 1$.
6. Their inner product is $\frac{1}{4}\left((1 + i)\overline{(1 + i)} + (1 - i)\overline{(-1 + i)}\right) = \frac{1}{4}(2 + (1 - i)(-1 - i)) = \frac{1}{4}(2 - 2) = 0$.
7. So $U$ is unitary with determinant $1$, and $U \in SU(2)$.
8. $\tr U = 1$ and $\det U = 1$, so the characteristic polynomial is $t^2 - t + 1$, with roots $e^{\pm i\pi/3}$.
9. These roots satisfy $\lambda^3 = -1$. $U$ is diagonalizable, so $U^3 = -I_2$, and $q^3 = -1$ in $\mathbb{H}$.

## Remarks

- The example illustrates [[the quaternion theorem|A763JSEVW8]] in both directions: from $U$, the entries $\alpha = \frac{1 + i}{2}$ and $\beta = \frac{1 + i}{2}$ give back $a = b = c = d = \frac{1}{2}$.
- The map $v \mapsto qv\overline{q}$ on the pure quaternions sends $i \mapsto j$, $j \mapsto k$ and $k \mapsto i$. It is the rotation by $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ about the axis $(1, 1, 1)$, see [[this example|E6SGA0X325]].
- $q$ and $-q$ give the same rotation, but $\phi(-q) = -U$ is a different element of $SU(2)$.
- $q^6 = 1$, so $q$ generates a cyclic subgroup of order $6$ in $SU(2)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A unit quaternion is a list of four numbers whose squares add up to $1$.
Here all four numbers are $\frac{1}{2}$.
The theorem turns this list into a $2 \times 2$ complex matrix.
The matrix keeps all lengths and has determinant $1$.
Three copies of it multiply to minus the identity.

### L2 human

The worked computation is in the Example.
The typical use: to multiply rotations of space, multiply their unit quaternions, or their matrices in $SU(2)$.
A trap: $U^3 = -I_2$, not $I_2$, although the rotation of space by $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ repeated three times is the identity.
