## Summary

A reflection is the linear form of a mirror.
It keeps every vector of a hyperplane through the origin where it is, and it reverses the one direction that is perpendicular to that hyperplane.
Reflections are the simplest length-preserving maps that are not rotations, and every orthogonal map is a product of them.
Let $V$ be a real [[inner product space|01EK923SKP]], and let $u \in V$ with $u \neq 0$.
The *reflection* in the hyperplane $u^\perp$ is the linear map
$$s_u(x) = x - 2\frac{\ip{x}{u}}{\ip{u}{u}}u.$$
When $u$ is a unit vector, the formula is $s_u(x) = x - 2\ip{x}{u}u$.
The [[formal definition|025158VEYZ]] states this, and [[the reflection theorem|FEER10D15S]] proves that $s_u$ is a [[linear isometry|07SJXTWVQW]] with $s_u \circ s_u = \id$ and $\det s_u = -1$.

## Motivation

Hold a flat mirror through the origin of space.
A point on the mirror stays where it is, and every other point goes to the point at the same distance straight behind the mirror.
This map keeps all lengths and angles, but it turns a right hand into a left hand.
So it is an isometry that no rotation can imitate, and the sign of its determinant records this.

Reflections are also the building blocks of the [[orthogonal group|WWK2E8BRGF]].
The [[Cartan-Dieudonné theorem|0VMWGYY35Q]] says that each element of $O(n)$ is a product of at most $n$ reflections.
In numerical linear algebra, reflections written as Householder matrices bring a matrix to triangular form in the [[QR decomposition|0ERWG4NXQ9]], because one reflection sends a whole column onto a coordinate axis.
In group theory, the groups generated by reflections are the [[Coxeter groups|G6FND7X69J]].

## Notation

Books write $s_u$, $\sigma_u$, $R_u$ or $H_u$ for the reflection in $u^\perp$.
The hyperplane $u^\perp$ is the *mirror*, and $u$ is a *normal vector* of the mirror.
For a unit vector $u \in \R^n$, the matrix of $s_u$ is the *Householder matrix* $I_n - 2uu^{\mathsf{T}}$, the matrix of the [[Householder transformation|JDH0THTVGM]].
Some books call the map $2P_W - \id$ the reflection in a subspace $W$ of any dimension, where $P_W$ is the [[orthogonal projection|0Q8HZTJC7G]] onto $W$.
In the orthogonal group, "reflection" means the case of a hyperplane, and we keep to that meaning.

## Remarks

### A reflection of the plane, step by step

Take the line $y = 2x$ in $\R^2$ as the mirror.
A normal vector is $u = (2, -1)$, because $(1, 2)$ spans the line and $(1, 2) \cdot (2, -1) = 0$.
Here $\ip{u}{u} = 5$.
Apply the formula to the first basis vector, where $\ip{e_1}{u} = 2$:
$$s_u(1, 0) = (1, 0) - \frac{2 \cdot 2}{5}(2, -1) = \left(-\tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{4}{5}\right).$$
Apply it to the second basis vector, where $\ip{e_2}{u} = -1$:
$$s_u(0, 1) = (0, 1) + \frac{2}{5}(2, -1) = \left(\tfrac{4}{5}, \tfrac{3}{5}\right).$$
So the matrix of $s_u$ is $\frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} -3 & 4 \\ 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, and its determinant is $\frac{1}{25}(-9 - 16) = -1$.
Check: the point $(1, 2)$ lies on the mirror, and $\frac{1}{5}(-3 + 8, 4 + 6) = (1, 2)$.

### More examples

In $\R^2$, the reflection in the line through $0$ at the angle $\alpha$ to the $x$-axis has the matrix
$$S(\alpha) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\alpha & \sin 2\alpha \\ \sin 2\alpha & -\cos 2\alpha \end{pmatrix}.$$
For the line $y = 2x$ above, $\tan\alpha = 2$ gives $\cos 2\alpha = \frac{1 - 4}{5} = -\frac{3}{5}$ and $\sin 2\alpha = \frac{4}{5}$, which matches.
The swap $(x, y) \mapsto (y, x)$ is $S(\pi/4)$, the reflection in the line $y = x$ with normal vector $(1, -1)$.
The diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1, \dots, 1, -1)$ is the reflection of $\R^n$ in the hyperplane $x_n = 0$.
In space, [[the Householder matrix of the vector (1, 2, 2)/3|BE65CTF1HQ]] is the reflection in the plane $x + 2y + 2z = 0$.
Reflections need not live in $\R^n$: on the polynomials of degree at most $2$ with $\ip{p}{q} = \int_{-1}^{1} p(x)q(x)\,dx$, [[the substitution p(x) to p(-x)|W4MHG5MZ1S]] is the reflection whose mirror is the even polynomials and whose normal vector is $x$.

### What a reflection is not

The [[half turn diag(-1, -1, 1)|EKK87GQF5W]] is orthogonal and squares to $I_3$, but it reverses a whole plane, not a single line.
Its determinant is $+1$, so it is a rotation and not a reflection.
The [[point reflection -I_3|X77QN4Y9VD]] has determinant $-1$ and squares to $I_3$, but it fixes only $0$, so there is no mirror.
An orthogonal projection onto a hyperplane also fixes the hyperplane, but it sends $u$ to $0$ rather than to $-u$, so it is not even invertible.
In each case one condition fails: a reflection must fix a hyperplane pointwise and must send the normal vector to its negative.

### The rules you can use

The reflection depends only on the line through $u$: $s_{cu} = s_u$ for every $c \neq 0$, because the factor $c^2$ cancels between numerator and denominator.
It is an involution, $s_u \circ s_u = \id$, so $s_u^{-1} = s_u$.
It is self-adjoint and keeps inner products, so its matrix in an orthonormal basis is symmetric and orthogonal.
In a basis $u, e_2, \dots, e_n$ with $e_2, \dots, e_n$ in the mirror, its matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, \dots, 1)$, so $\det s_u = -1$.
When $\norm{x} = \norm{y}$ and $x \neq y$, the reflection $s_{x - y}$ swaps $x$ and $y$.
For $Q \in O(n)$, the conjugate $Q s_u Q^{-1}$ is $s_{Qu}$, so the reflections form one conjugacy class.
The product of two reflections is a rotation, and in the plane $S(\alpha)S(\beta) = R(2(\alpha - \beta))$, as [[the composition of two plane reflections|SRN03D73AJ]] shows.
All these rules are proved in [[the reflection theorem|FEER10D15S]].

### Watch out

A matrix with $Q^2 = I$ and $\det Q = -1$ need not be a reflection, since $-I_3$ has both properties.
For an orthogonal $Q$, the correct test is that $Q$ fixes a hyperplane pointwise and is not the identity.
In terms of eigenvalues, $Q$ must have the eigenvalue $1$ with multiplicity $n - 1$ and the eigenvalue $-1$ once.
A second trap is the normalization.
With $u = (1, 1)$, the map $x \mapsto x - 2\ip{x}{u}u$ sends $(1, 1)$ to $(1, 1) - 4(1, 1) = (-3, -3)$, which is not a reflection.
Either divide by $\ip{u}{u}$ or first scale $u$ to length $1$.
A third trap: the vector $u$ is perpendicular to the mirror, not a vector along it.

### History

Householder introduced the matrices $I - 2uu^{\mathsf{T}}$ as a tool of numerical linear algebra in a paper of 1958 on the triangularization of matrices.

### Try it

First, apply the reflection in the line $y = x$ to $(3, 1)$.
With $u = (1, -1)$, $\ip{(3, 1)}{u} = 2$ and $\ip{u}{u} = 2$, so the image is $(3, 1) - 2(1, -1) = (1, 3)$.

Second, find a reflection of $\R^3$ that sends $e_1$ to $e_2$.
Take $u = e_1 - e_2 = (1, -1, 0)$; then $I - \frac{2}{2}uu^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the matrix that swaps the first two coordinates and keeps the third.

Third, compute $s_u$ for $u = (0, 0, 2)$ in $\R^3$.
Here $\ip{x}{u} = 2z$ and $\ip{u}{u} = 4$, so $s_u(x, y, z) = (x, y, z) - (0, 0, 2z) = (x, y, -z)$, the same map as for $u = e_3$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A reflection is a mirror image.
The mirror is a line through the origin in the plane, or a flat plane through the origin in space.
Points on the mirror stay where they are.
Every other point jumps straight across the mirror and lands at the same distance on the other side.
For the mirror along the $x$-axis, the point $(2, 3)$ goes to $(2, -3)$.
Two mirror images in the same mirror bring every point back to its start.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: find an orthogonal matrix that sends $x = (2, 1, 2)$ onto the first coordinate axis.
Since $\norm{x} = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 4} = 3$, the target is $y = (3, 0, 0)$, and the swap rule suggests the normal vector $u = x - y = (-1, 1, 2)$.
Here $\ip{u}{u} = 6$ and $\ip{x}{u} = -2 + 1 + 4 = 3$, so
$$s_u(x) = x - 2 \cdot \frac{3}{6}u = x - u = (3, 0, 0).$$
The matrix is $H = I - \frac{2}{6}uu^{\mathsf{T}}$:
$$H = I - \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & -2 \\ -1 & 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & -2 \\ 2 & -2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $Hx = \frac{1}{3}(4 + 1 + 4, \ 2 + 2 - 4, \ 4 - 2 - 2) = (3, 0, 0)$.
The columns of $H$ are orthonormal, for example the first two have dot product $\frac{1}{9}(2 + 2 - 4) = 0$.
This one step is the start of the Householder method for the QR decomposition: the first column of a matrix becomes $(\norm{x}, 0, 0)$, and the method repeats on a smaller block.

### L3 human

The reflections generate $O(n)$, and they form a single conjugacy class in it, so a property that holds for one reflection and is stable under products and conjugation holds on all of $O(n)$.
The finite groups generated by reflections are the finite Coxeter groups, such as the symmetry groups of the regular polyhedra and the Weyl groups of root systems.
For a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form $B$ over a field of characteristic not $2$, the same formula $s_u(x) = x - 2\frac{B(x, u)}{B(u, u)}u$ defines a reflection whenever $B(u, u) \neq 0$.
The general [[Cartan-Dieudonné theorem|0VMWGYY35Q]] says that these reflections generate the orthogonal group of $B$, for example the [[Lorentz group|QWK3BYVJ9W]] of special relativity.
Over $\C$ one also meets complex reflections, which multiply the normal vector by a unit complex number $\lambda \neq 1$ in place of $-1$.
Reflections in affine hyperplanes that miss the origin are not linear, and they belong to Euclidean geometry.
