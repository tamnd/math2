## Definition

The annihilator of a subspace is the set of linear forms that are zero at every vector of the subspace.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $U$ be a [[subspace|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$.
The *annihilator* of $U$ is the subset of the [[dual space|0M856Q1AMZ]] $V^*$ given by
$$U^0 = \{ f \in V^* : f(u) = 0 \text{ for all } u \in U \}.$$

## Notation

Other notations are $U^\circ$, $\operatorname{Ann}(U)$ and $U^\perp$.
The last one also names the orthogonal complement in an inner product space, so read the context.
Mathlib writes `Submodule.dualAnnihilator`.

## Remarks

The definition has two parts, and each has a reason.
The elements of $U^0$ are taken from $V^*$, so they are linear forms on all of $V$ and not only on $U$.
The condition asks for the value $0$ at every vector of $U$, and this is what makes $U^0$ a subspace.
It contains the zero form, and if $f$ and $g$ vanish on $U$, then $(af + bg)(u) = a f(u) + b g(u) = 0$ for every $u \in U$.

For example, in $F^2$ let $U$ be the line spanned by $(1, 1)$.
A form $(x, y) \mapsto ax + by$ vanishes on $U$ exactly when $a + b = 0$, so $U^0$ is the line spanned by the form $x - y$.
If we asked instead for the value $1$ at $(1, 1)$, the set of such forms would miss the zero form, so it would not be a subspace.

The same formula makes sense for any subset $S$ of $V$, and $S^0 = (\operatorname{span} S)^0$, because a form that vanishes on $S$ vanishes on every combination of elements of $S$.
A larger subspace gives a smaller annihilator: if $U_1 \subseteq U_2$, then $U_2^0 \subseteq U_1^0$.
In finite dimension, $\dim U + \dim U^0 = \dim V$, by the theorem on the [[dimension of the annihilator|06Q1EFVH0Q]].
The concept page on the [[annihilator|0ASHNYF7AG]] gives the examples and the rules.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The annihilator of a subspace collects the linear rules that give zero on all of it.
In space, the rules that give zero on a plane through the origin are the multiples of one rule, the equation of the plane.
A larger subspace obeys fewer rules, so it has a smaller annihilator.
