## Definition

The *planetary boundary layer*, or *atmospheric boundary layer*, is the lowest part of the troposphere, directly influenced by the Earth's surface.
It is characterised by its response to surface forcings, namely frictional drag, heat transfer and evaporation, on time scales of about an hour or less.
Its top, the boundary-layer height, separates it from the free atmosphere, in which surface friction is negligible.

## Remarks

The layer is usually divided into a surface layer, roughly its lowest tenth, in which the turbulent fluxes are nearly constant with height, and an outer layer above.
In the outer layer of a neutral, rotating boundary layer the wind turns with height towards the geostrophic wind, as in the [[Ekman layer|H5GNFWEW80]].
Its structure depends strongly on stratification: a convective boundary layer during the day contains a well-mixed layer capped by an inversion, and a stable boundary layer forms at night.

Example: over land on a sunny afternoon the boundary layer is typically $1$ to $2$ km deep, and the potential temperature is nearly constant with height in its mixed layer.

Non-example: on a calm night with a stable boundary layer a few hundred metres deep, the air at $5$ km height is part of the free atmosphere.
Its wind is close to [[geostrophic balance|CZ1S1P465Y]] and does not respond to the surface within hours.

The layer is a [[turbulent|VY307F8Z0S]] [[boundary layer|YCFPD0GE5G]] with very large Reynolds number.
Molecular viscosity is negligible except within millimetres of the surface, and turbulent fluxes must be parametrised in models.
