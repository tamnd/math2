## Definition

Let $k$ be a field.
A *(left) Leibniz algebra* over $k$ is a vector space $L$ over $k$ with a bilinear product $[\cdot, \cdot] \colon L \times L \to L$ such that
$$[a, [b, c]] = [\,[a, b], c] + [b, [a, c]]$$
for all $a, b, c \in L$.

## Remarks

The identity says that each left multiplication $b \mapsto [a, b]$ is a derivation of the product.
A *right* Leibniz algebra satisfies instead $[\,[a, b], c] = [a, [b, c]] + [\,[a, c], b]$, saying that each right multiplication is a derivation; the opposite product turns one kind into the other.

Example: every [[Lie algebra|5JJYBJ6A8K]] is a Leibniz algebra, since by antisymmetry the Jacobi identity can be rewritten as $[a, [b, c]] = [\,[a, b], c] + [b, [a, c]]$.
A Leibniz algebra that is not a Lie algebra: $L = k^2$ with basis $x, y$, $[x, x] = y$ and all other products of basis vectors zero.
Every product lies in $k y$ and every product with $y$ is zero, so all three terms of the identity vanish, while $[x, x] \neq 0$.

Non-example: $L = k$ with basis $x$ and $[x, x] = x$.
Then $[x, [x, x]] = x$ while $[\,[x, x], x] + [x, [x, x]] = 2x$, and these differ because $x \neq 0$.

A Leibniz algebra is a Lie algebra if and only if $[x, x] = 0$ for all $x$.
The span of all squares $[x, x]$ is a two-sided ideal, and the quotient by it is a Lie algebra.
