## Summary

Feasible interpolation turns a short refutation of two conflicting sets of clauses into a small circuit that says, for each value of the shared variables, which set fails.
Read backwards, it turns lower bounds for circuits into lower bounds for proofs.

Here is the smallest case.
Let $A = \{q, \ \neg q \vee p\}$ and $B = \{\neg p\}$, which share only the variable $p$.
The set $A$ forces $p = 1$ and the set $B$ forces $p = 0$, so the circuit $I(p) = p$ answers the question: at $p = 0$ the set $A$ fails, and at $p = 1$ the set $B$ fails.

In general, let $p$, $q$, $r$ be disjoint lists of variables, $A(p, q)$ a CNF in $p, q$, and $B(p, r)$ a CNF in $p, r$.
An *interpolating circuit* for the pair is a [[Boolean circuit|SQXNFGVADX]] $I(p)$ such that, for every $0$-$1$ assignment $a$ of $p$:
$$I(a) = 0 \implies A(a, q) \text{ is unsatisfiable}, \qquad I(a) = 1 \implies B(a, r) \text{ is unsatisfiable}.$$
A [[propositional proof system|3V24CYWDT6]] $P$ has *feasible interpolation* if there is a polynomial $\sigma$ such that, whenever $A \wedge B$ has a $P$-refutation of size $S$, the pair has an interpolating circuit with at most $\sigma(S)$ gates.
It has *monotone feasible interpolation* if, in addition, the circuit can be chosen monotone whenever no variable of $p$ occurs negatively in $A$.

## Motivation

By [[Craig's interpolation theorem|TRS2HY57EG]], every unsatisfiable $A \wedge B$ has an interpolant in the variables $p$ alone.
But the interpolant from the proof of that theorem can be exponentially large.
Proof complexity asks a finer question: does a short refutation give a small interpolant?

A yes answer is a tool for lower bounds.
Suppose that every circuit separating the sets $\{a : A(a, q) \text{ is satisfiable}\}$ and $\{a : B(a, r) \text{ is satisfiable}\}$ is large.
If $P$ has feasible interpolation, then every $P$-refutation of $A \wedge B$ is large too.
For general circuits no strong lower bounds are known, but for monotone circuits they are, so monotone feasible interpolation gives lower bounds with no unproved assumption.
This is the route to the exponential lower bounds for [[resolution|6JSH907W5R]] and for [[cutting planes|KKCX2HQP2K]] on the clique-coloring formulas.

## Notation

Many authors state the tautology $A(p, q) \to \neg B(p, r)$ instead of refuting $A \wedge B$.
The *uniform* version asks for an algorithm that computes $I(a)$ from the refutation and $a$ in time polynomial in the size of the refutation, and it implies the version with circuits.
The measure must be named each time.
For resolution we count the clauses of the refutation, for cutting planes the lines, and for the circuit the gates.

## Remarks

### A first interpolant, read off a proof

Take $A = \{q, \ \neg q \vee p\}$ and $B = \{\neg p \vee r, \ \neg r\}$.
A resolution refutation derives $p$ from the two clauses of $A$, then $\neg p$ from the two clauses of $B$, then the empty clause, so it has $7$ clauses.
Label each clause of $A$ with $0$, each clause of $B$ with $1$, take OR at the step on $q$ and AND at the step on $r$.
The step on the shared variable $p$ then chooses a label by the value of $p$:
$$I = (p \wedge 1) \vee (\neg p \wedge 0) = p.$$
Check: at $p = 0$ the set $A(0, q) = \{q, \neg q\}$ is unsatisfiable, and at $p = 1$ the set $B(1, r) = \{r, \neg r\}$ is unsatisfiable.
[[An interpolant from a refutation of seven clauses|KH4T0G84TD]] works through every step, and the [[interpolation theorem for resolution|YHGCN7BBEP]] proves that the labels always work, with at most $4$ gates per clause.

### Lower bounds by contraposition

The transfer is one line of logic.
If $P$ has feasible interpolation with the polynomial $\sigma$, and every interpolating circuit for $A, B$ has more than $T$ gates, then every $P$-refutation of $A \wedge B$ has size $S$ with $\sigma(S) > T$.

The main use takes $A$ to say "the graph $p$ has a $k$-clique" and $B$ to say "the graph $p$ is $(k-1)$-colorable".
The edge variables occur only positively in $A$, so the interpolant is monotone, and for a suitable $k$ the monotone circuits that separate the two kinds of graphs need $2^{\Omega((n/\log n)^{1/3})}$ gates.
[[Monotone interpolation and the clique-coloring formulas|F8GJ402PH2]] draws the conclusion for resolution, measured in clauses.
[[Pudlák's theorem|5RYPXFF0G2]] does the same for cutting planes, measured in lines, with monotone real circuits.

### What is not feasible interpolation

Trying every value of the private variables always gives an interpolant, but not a feasible one.
In [[interpolation by elimination of the private variables|7JYMW3G20G]] a refutation with $2m + 3$ clauses meets an elimination procedure with $2^m$ cases.

The monotone form needs the sign condition.
For $A = \{\neg p\}$ and $B = \{p\}$, the set $A$ holds at $p = 0$, so $I(0) = 1$, and $B$ holds at $p = 1$, so $I(1) = 0$.
Thus every interpolating circuit computes $\neg p$, which no monotone circuit computes.

### Which systems have it

Resolution has monotone feasible interpolation, by Krajíček's theorem.
Cutting planes has a monotone form with real circuits, by Pudlák.
The [[truth-table system|67MYPBSHCJ]] has it for a cheap reason: a proof on $N$ variables already has size at least $2^N$, so a circuit may try every value of $q$ within the allowed size.

Strong systems lack it under cryptographic assumptions.
Krajíček and Pudlák showed in 1998 that [[extended Frege|5YSE04SHFH]] has no feasible interpolation if [[RSA|0HM584C465]] is secure against circuits of polynomial size.
Bonet, Pitassi and Raz gave a result of the same kind for TC0-Frege in 2000, from the security of the [[Diffie-Hellman key exchange|01TKKESHSB]].
These results are conditional, and they do not say that Frege proofs are long.

### Interpolation and automatizability

Suppose that $P$ is [[automatizable|WBECDG163W]] and that putting constants into a $P$-refutation gives a $P$-refutation that is no larger.
Given a refutation $\pi$ of $A \wedge B$ and a value $a$, run the search for a refutation of $A(a, q)$ for the time that the automating algorithm needs on refutations of size $\lvert \pi \rvert$.
If $B(a, r)$ has a solution $c$, putting $a$ and $c$ into $\pi$ gives such a refutation, so the search succeeds.
So output $0$ when the search succeeds and $1$ when it fails, and this is a uniform interpolant.
In particular, a system that lacks feasible interpolation, and has this closure property, is not automatizable.

### Watch out

Craig's theorem gives an interpolant for every unsatisfiable $A \wedge B$, so existence is never the question.
The question is the size of the interpolant compared with the size of a given refutation.
A second slip is to apply the monotone form when some variable of $p$ occurs negatively in $A$, as in the example $A = \{\neg p\}$ above.
A third slip is to read the results for extended Frege as unconditional lower bounds: they are conditional, and they are not lower bounds at all.

### History

Krajíček introduced feasible interpolation and proved it for resolution in a paper published in 1997.
Pudlák proved the monotone form with real circuits for cutting planes in 1997.
Krajíček and Pudlák showed in 1998 that extended Frege lacks feasible interpolation if RSA is secure.

### Try it

First, find an interpolating circuit for $A = \{q, \ \neg q\}$ and $B = \{p\}$.
Here $A(a, q)$ is unsatisfiable for every $a$, so the constant $0$ works.

Second, for $A = \{p_1 \vee q, \ p_2 \vee \neg q\}$ and $B = \{\neg p_1, \ \neg p_2\}$, show that $I = p_1 \vee p_2$ works.
If $I(a) = 0$, then $p_1 = p_2 = 0$, and $A$ becomes $\{q, \neg q\}$, which fails.
If $I(a) = 1$, then one of $\neg p_1$, $\neg p_2$ is false, so $B(a)$ fails.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two sets of rules, $A$ and $B$, share some switches $p$, and they cannot both be obeyed.
So for each setting of the shared switches, at least one of the two sets is impossible to obey.
An interpolant is a small machine that looks at the shared switches and names a set that fails.
Feasible interpolation means that a short proof of the conflict always gives a small machine.
So if every such machine is large, every proof is long.

### L2 human

We find an interpolant for $A = \{q, \ \neg q \vee p\}$ and $B = \{\neg p\}$ by reasoning about values.
At $p = 0$, the set $A$ becomes $\{q, \neg q\}$, which fails, so we may output $0$.
At $p = 1$, the set $B$ becomes $\{\neg 1\}$, the empty clause, which fails, so we may output $1$.
So $I(p) = p$ is an interpolating circuit with no gates at all.
For two variables this reasoning is fast, but for $n$ shared variables it needs $2^n$ cases.
Feasible interpolation replaces the case analysis by a walk through a refutation, one gate group per line.
Watch out: when both $A(a, q)$ and $B(a, r)$ fail, the circuit may answer either way.

### L3 human

Feasible interpolation links proof complexity with disjoint pairs of [[NP|SBDQH0TJ5X]] sets.
If $P$ has it and proves in polynomial size that a pair is disjoint, then circuits of polynomial size separate the pair.
So a pair that is hard to separate, such as a pair built from a cryptographic function, gives either a lower bound for $P$ or a proof that $P$ lacks feasible interpolation.
For strong systems the second outcome holds under standard assumptions, so the method gives no route to lower bounds for [[Frege systems|ANSV13M4FS]].
