## Summary

The paraxial approximation treats rays that stay close to the optical axis and make small angles with it.
It replaces $\sin\theta$ and $\tan\theta$ by $\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ by $1$.
Then the laws of reflection and refraction become linear, and so does the passage of rays through a whole optical system.
It is the basis of first-order (Gaussian) optics: focal lengths, image positions and magnifications.
See the [[definition|BQH7VFR6XH]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Most rays in a camera or a telescope travel almost along the central axis.
For small angles, the sine of an angle is almost the angle itself, measured in radians.
Using this shortcut, every lens and mirror acts by simple linear rules.
At an angle of $0.1$ radians, about $6^\circ$, the shortcut is off by less than $0.2$ percent.
The errors it ignores for larger angles are the aberrations of the system.
