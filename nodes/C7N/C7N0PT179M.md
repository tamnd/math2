## Example

We read off the diagonals of one square matrix and then multiply it by itself.
Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. The matrix $A$ has 3 rows and 3 columns, so it is a [[square matrix|0CP7YY6NAG]] of order 3.
2. The main diagonal runs from the top left to the bottom right: $a_{11} = 1$, $a_{22} = 5$, $a_{33} = 9$. Its sum is the [[trace|0XC1RDBY7A]], $1 + 5 + 9 = 15$.
3. The antidiagonal runs from the top right to the bottom left: $a_{13} = 3$, $a_{22} = 5$, $a_{31} = 7$. Only the middle entry is shared with the main diagonal.
4. The [[product|076JGP6R3T]] $AA$ is defined, because the first factor has 3 columns and the second has 3 rows. Each entry of $A^2$ is a row of $A$ times a column of $A$.
5. Row 1 of $A$ is $(1, 2, 3)$, and the columns of $A$ are $(1, 4, 7)$, $(2, 5, 8)$ and $(3, 6, 9)$. So row 1 of $A^2$ is
$$(1 + 8 + 21,\ 2 + 10 + 24,\ 3 + 12 + 27) = (30, 36, 42).$$
6. Row 2 of $A$ is $(4, 5, 6)$, so row 2 of $A^2$ is
$$(4 + 20 + 42,\ 8 + 25 + 48,\ 12 + 30 + 54) = (66, 81, 96).$$
7. Row 3 of $A$ is $(7, 8, 9)$, so row 3 of $A^2$ is
$$(7 + 32 + 63,\ 14 + 40 + 72,\ 21 + 48 + 81) = (102, 126, 150).$$
8. Together,
$$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 30 & 36 & 42 \\ 66 & 81 & 96 \\ 102 & 126 & 150 \end{pmatrix},$$
and $A^2$ is again a $3 \times 3$ matrix.

Check: the entries of $A$ increase by $1$ along a row, and the entries of $A^2$ increase by $6$, $15$ and $24$ along the rows, which are the row sums of $A$.

## Remarks

The diagonal of $A^2$ is $30, 81, 150$, not the squares $1, 25, 81$ of the diagonal of $A$.
The entry $(2, 2)$ of $A^2$ is $4 \cdot 2 + 5 \cdot 5 + 6 \cdot 8 = 81$, and only the middle term $5 \cdot 5$ comes from the diagonal.
So the diagonal of a product depends on all the entries in general.
For [[triangular matrices|0N4XY7Z3NN]] the off-diagonal terms vanish, and then the diagonal of $A^2$ is the list of squared diagonal entries.

If we delete the last column of $A$, we get a $3 \times 2$ matrix, and its square is no longer defined, as in [[the 2 × 3 example|TVNYJ89WDJ]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A $3 \times 3$ matrix is a square grid of nine numbers.
Its main diagonal is the line of entries from the top left corner to the bottom right corner, here $1$, $5$ and $9$.
Because the grid is square, we can multiply it by itself, and the answer is again a $3 \times 3$ grid.

### L2 human

Each entry of $A^2$ is the [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]] of a row of $A$ with a column of $A$.
For the entry in row 3 and column 1, take row 3, $(7, 8, 9)$, and column 1, $(1, 4, 7)$:
$$7 \cdot 1 + 8 \cdot 4 + 9 \cdot 7 = 7 + 32 + 63 = 102.$$
This needs the rows of the first factor to be as long as the columns of the second factor, which is the case for every square matrix.
The typical use is to compute powers $A^k$, for example to follow a process that repeats the same linear step.
The trap is to square each entry instead, which gives $1, 4, 9$ in the first row, not $30, 36, 42$.
