## Example

Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. The matrix $A$ has 3 rows and 3 columns, so it is a [[square matrix|0CP7YY6NAG]] of order 3.
2. The main diagonal is $a_{11} = 1$, $a_{22} = 5$, $a_{33} = 9$. The [[trace|0XC1RDBY7A]] is $1 + 5 + 9 = 15$.
3. The antidiagonal is $a_{13} = 3$, $a_{22} = 5$, $a_{31} = 7$. It is not the main diagonal.
4. The product $AA$ is defined, because $A$ has 3 columns and 3 rows. Row 1 of $A^2$ is $(1 + 8 + 21,\ 2 + 10 + 24,\ 3 + 12 + 27) = (30, 36, 42)$.
5. Row 2 of $A^2$ is $(4 + 20 + 42,\ 8 + 25 + 48,\ 12 + 30 + 54) = (66, 81, 96)$.
6. Row 3 of $A^2$ is $(7 + 32 + 63,\ 14 + 40 + 72,\ 21 + 48 + 81) = (102, 126, 150)$.
7. So
$$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 30 & 36 & 42 \\ 66 & 81 & 96 \\ 102 & 126 & 150 \end{pmatrix},$$
and $A^2$ is again a $3 \times 3$ matrix.

## Remarks

The diagonal of $A^2$ is $(30, 81, 150)$, and not the squares $(1, 25, 81)$ of the diagonal of $A$.
The diagonal of a product depends on all the entries, unless the matrices are [[triangular|0N4XY7Z3NN]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A $3 \times 3$ matrix has a main diagonal from the top left to the bottom right.
We can multiply it by itself, and the result is again a $3 \times 3$ matrix.

### L2 human

Each entry of $A^2$ is the [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]] of a row of $A$ with a column of $A$.
Only a square matrix has a row length equal to its column length for this to work.
