## Summary

A conservative extension adds new symbols or axioms to a theory but proves nothing new in the old language.
Take the theory of groups in the language $\{ \cdot, e \}$ and add a constant $c$ with the axiom $c \cdot c = c$.
The new theory proves $c = e$, a sentence that the old theory cannot even state.
But every group becomes a model of the new theory when we read $c$ as $e$, so every sentence about $\cdot$ and $e$ that the new theory proves was already a theorem of group theory.

Let $T$ be a [[theory|92SWXST1ZM]] in a first-order language $L$, and $T'$ a theory in a language $L' \supseteq L$.
As in [[formal system|439M8B4DY2]], a theory is any set of sentences, not necessarily closed under consequence.
$T'$ is an extension of $T$ when $T' \vdash \sigma$ for every $L$-sentence $\sigma$ with $T \vdash \sigma$.
The extension is conservative when, conversely, every $L$-[[sentence|DKFGQTRYZE]] $\sigma$ with $T' \vdash \sigma$ has $T \vdash \sigma$.
For a class $\Gamma$ of $L$-sentences, $T'$ is $\Gamma$-conservative over $T$ when this holds for the sentences in $\Gamma$.

## Motivation

Mathematicians add new symbols and new objects all the time: the order $\le$ in arithmetic, sets of numbers in analysis, imaginary numbers to solve real equations.
A natural worry is that the new tools change the old subject, so that a theorem about numbers proved with sets is not really a theorem of number theory.
Conservativity is the precise form of the answer "the new tools change nothing in the old language".

The idea was the goal of [[Hilbert's program|P1RW6JWX73]]: show by finitary means that ideal mathematics proves no finitary statement that finitary mathematics cannot prove.
The second incompleteness theorem blocks that plan in its full form, but conservation results survive as a central tool of proof theory and of [[reverse mathematics and subsystems|BY8AAFN7C5]].
The simplest case is the [[extension by definitions|GCK0NN60E4]], where each new symbol abbreviates a formula of the old language.

## Notation

Authors write "$T'$ is conservative over $T$", and some write $T \preceq_{\mathrm{cons}} T'$.
Some authors require $T \subseteq T'$; the definition above asks only that $T'$ proves every theorem of $T$.
For arithmetic the classes $\Gamma$ are usually levels of the arithmetical hierarchy, such as $\Pi^0_1$ and $\Pi^0_2$, or of the analytical hierarchy, such as $\Pi^1_1$.
The word "conservativity" also names an unrelated property of determiners in the theory of generalized quantifiers.

## Remarks

### Four conservative extensions

Adding a [[new constant symbol with no new axioms|RC9ERBRWZV]] is conservative, because every model of $T$ can interpret the constant by any element.
Every extension by definitions is conservative; for example, arithmetic with $\le$ defined by $x \le y \leftrightarrow \exists z\, (x + z = y)$.
The second-order system [[ACA0 is conservative over Peano arithmetic|HPA6HX3114]], so its sets of numbers prove no new arithmetic sentence.
Skolemization is conservative: add a function symbol $f$ and the axiom $\forall \bar x\, (\exists y\, \varphi(\bar x, y) \to \varphi(\bar x, f(\bar x)))$, and every model of $T$ expands by a choice of witnesses.

A deeper example comes from set theory.
ZFC is conservative over [[ZF|0HE5WDQ9FJ]] for arithmetic sentences.
If ZFC proves such a sentence $\sigma$, then ZF proves $\sigma$ relativized to the [[constructible universe|6TF2JBTPPJ]] $L$, and an arithmetic sentence means the same in $L$ as in the whole universe.

### Extensions that are not conservative

[[PA plus the consistency of PA|EQH7XTZ29V]] is not conservative over [[Peano arithmetic|0QFT7Z76N5]]: it proves the arithmetic sentence $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, which PA does not prove, by the [[second incompleteness theorem|4PZM5GJTR5]].
Peano arithmetic is not conservative over [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] $\mathsf{Q}$, since it proves $\forall x\, (0 + x = x)$ by induction and $\mathsf{Q}$ does not.
ZFC is not conservative over ZF in the full language, if ZF is consistent: ZFC proves the [[axiom of choice|0HTGB13FE2]], and Cohen showed that ZF does not.

### How to test conservativity

The model-theoretic test: if every [[model|MVCH6N9SNW]] of $T$ expands to a model of $T'$, then $T'$ is conservative over $T$.
Suppose $T' \vdash \sigma$ for an $L$-sentence $\sigma$.
Each model of $T$ expands to a model of $T'$, where $\sigma$ is true by soundness, and the truth of $\sigma$ does not depend on the new symbols.
So $\sigma$ is true in every model of $T$, and [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]] gives $T \vdash \sigma$.

The proof-theoretic test is to transform each derivation in $T'$ of an $L$-sentence into a derivation in $T$.
For a new constant, replace it by a fresh variable; for a defined symbol, replace each atomic formula with the symbol by its definition.

### First properties

A conservative extension of a [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]] theory is consistent.
If $T'$ were inconsistent, it would prove the $L$-sentence $\exists x\, (x \neq x)$, so $T$ would prove it too.
Conservativity is transitive.
The union of a chain of conservative extensions of $T$ is conservative over $T$, because each derivation uses only finitely many axioms.

### Watch out

A conservative extension does prove new sentences, but only sentences that use new symbols.
A true extension need not be conservative: $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ is true in $\mathbb{N}$ and not conservative over PA.
The model-theoretic test is sufficient but not necessary.
Let $T$ be the set of all sentences true in $\mathbb{N}$ in the language $\{ 0, S, +, \cdot, < \}$, and $T' = T \cup \{ c > \bar n : n \in \mathbb{N} \}$, where $\bar n$ is the numeral of $n$.
$T'$ is consistent by the [[compactness theorem|1NR3HAWG9D]], and $T$ is negation complete, so any consistent extension proves no new $L$-sentence.
Yet $\mathbb{N}$ has no element above all numerals, so it does not expand to a model of $T'$.

### History

Hilbert's program in the 1920s asked for a finitary proof that the ideal parts of mathematics are conservative over the finitary part for finitary statements.
Gödel's second incompleteness theorem of 1931 showed that this cannot be done in the form Hilbert wanted.
Partial forms survive as conservation results; for example, Friedman proved that $\mathsf{WKL}_0$ is $\Pi^0_2$-conservative over primitive recursive arithmetic.

### Try it

Is the theory of groups with a new constant $c$ and the axiom $c \neq e$ conservative over the theory of groups?
No: it proves $\exists x\, (x \neq e)$, which fails in the trivial group, so the theory of groups does not prove it.

Is $\mathsf{PA} + \neg \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ conservative over PA?
No: it proves $\neg \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, and PA does not, because PA is true in $\mathbb{N}$ and $\neg \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ is false there.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose a club has a rule book, and you add a new word to it: "senior member" means "a member for more than ten years".
The new word makes some rules shorter to write.
But it cannot create a new rule about the old words, so everything you can now prove about members you could prove before.
Such an addition is called conservative.
An addition such as "every member pays 5 dollars" is not conservative, because it says something new in the old words.

### L2 human

Here is the full check for the opening example.
Let $T$ be the theory of groups in the language $\{ \cdot, e \}$, and $T'$ the theory $T$ with a new constant $c$ and the axiom $c \cdot c = c$.
Let $G$ be any group, and read $c$ as $e$.
Then
$$c \cdot c = e \cdot e = e = c,$$
so the expansion is a model of $T'$.
Every model of $T$ expands, so by the model-theoretic test $T'$ proves no new sentence of the language $\{ \cdot, e \}$.
Inside $T'$ we can prove $c = e$ by multiplying $c \cdot c = c$ on the right by the inverse of $c$; this sentence is new, but it uses $c$.
The typical use is to add a convenient symbol or axiom and then check that every old model expands.
Watch out for an axiom such as $c \cdot c = c \wedge c \neq e$: it contradicts the group axioms, so no group expands, and the extension proves every sentence.

### L3 human

Conservation results measure the strength of theories by the sentences of a fixed class that they prove.
In reverse mathematics, $\mathsf{WKL}_0$ is $\Pi^1_1$-conservative over $\mathsf{RCA}_0$, and $\mathsf{ACA}_0$ is conservative over PA, so these systems prove the same arithmetic sentences as weaker-looking theories.
Cut elimination, the Dialectica interpretation and realizability give conservation results for intuitionistic and classical systems, collected in [[proof interpretations and conservation results|8XY7JYZKFW]].
In set theory, Gödel-Bernays class theory is conservative over ZF, so classes can serve as a convenient language.
The model-theoretic side, the expansion of models, links conservativity to [[Beth's definability theorem|RJBDSA2ETP]] and to Robinson's joint consistency theorem.
