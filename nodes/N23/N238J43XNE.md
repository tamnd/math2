## Summary

Let $T$ be a [[theory|92SWXST1ZM]] in a first-order language $L$ and $T'$ a theory in a language $L' \supseteq L$; here a theory is a set of sentences, not necessarily closed under consequence.
$T'$ is an *extension* of $T$ when $T' \vdash \sigma$ for every $L$-sentence $\sigma$ with $T \vdash \sigma$.
The extension is *conservative* when, conversely, every $L$-sentence $\sigma$ with $T' \vdash \sigma$ already has $T \vdash \sigma$.
So $T$ and $T'$ prove the same [[sentences|DKFGQTRYZE]] of the old language, although $T'$ can prove many new sentences that use new symbols.
For a class $\Gamma$ of $L$-sentences, $T'$ is *$\Gamma$-conservative* over $T$ when this holds for the sentences in $\Gamma$.
Conservative extensions matter because new axioms, symbols or higher objects can then be used freely: they shorten or simplify proofs, but they never prove a new fact in the old language.

## Motivation

Mathematicians add new symbols and new objects all the time: a symbol $\le$ in arithmetic, sets of numbers in analysis, imaginary numbers to solve real equations.
A natural worry is that the new tools change the old subject, so that a theorem about numbers proved with sets is not a theorem of number theory.
Conservativity is the precise form of the answer "the new tools change nothing in the old language".
It was the goal of [[Hilbert's program|P1RW6JWX73]]: show by finitary means that ideal mathematics proves no finitary statement that finitary mathematics cannot prove.
Conservation results are now a central tool of proof theory and of reverse mathematics.

## Notation

Some authors write $T' \supseteq_{\mathrm{cons}} T$ or say "$T'$ is conservative over $T$".
Other authors call $T'$ conservative over $T$ only when $L' \neq L$ or only when $T \subseteq T'$; on this page the second condition is replaced by "$T'$ proves every theorem of $T$".
For arithmetic, the classes $\Gamma$ are usually levels of the arithmetical hierarchy, such as $\Pi^0_1$ or $\Pi^0_2$, or of the analytical hierarchy, such as $\Pi^1_1$.
The word "conservativity" has another meaning in linguistics, for determiners in generalized quantifier theory; that meaning is not the subject of this page.

## Remarks

### Examples

- Adding a [[new constant symbol with no new axioms|RC9ERBRWZV]] gives a conservative extension, because every model of $T$ can interpret the constant by any element.
- Every [[extension by definitions|GCK0NN60E4]] is conservative; for example the symbol $\le$ defined in arithmetic by $x \le y \leftrightarrow \exists z \, (x + z = y)$.
- The second-order system [[ACA0 is conservative over Peano arithmetic|HPA6HX3114]]: the sets of numbers in ACA0 prove no new arithmetic sentence.
- ZFC is conservative over [[ZF|0HE5WDQ9FJ]] for arithmetic sentences. If ZFC proves such a sentence $\sigma$, then ZF proves $\sigma$ relativized to the [[constructible universe|6TF2JBTPPJ]], and arithmetic sentences mean the same in $L$ as in $V$.
- Skolemization is conservative. Adding a function symbol $f$ and the axiom $\forall \bar x \, (\exists y \, \varphi(\bar x, y) \to \varphi(\bar x, f(\bar x)))$ to $T$ is conservative, since every model of $T$ expands by a choice of witnesses.

### Non-examples

- [[PA plus the consistency of PA|EQH7XTZ29V]] is not conservative over [[Peano arithmetic|0QFT7Z76N5]]: it proves the arithmetic sentence $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, which PA does not prove, by the [[second incompleteness theorem|X9WYNYE29C]].
- Peano arithmetic is not conservative over [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] Q: it proves $\forall x \, (0 + x = x)$ by induction, and Q does not prove this sentence.
- ZFC is not conservative over ZF, if ZF is consistent: ZFC proves the [[axiom of choice|0HTGB13FE2]], and Cohen showed that ZF does not prove it.
- Adding $\lnot\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ to PA gives a consistent theory, again by the second incompleteness theorem, but not a conservative one.

### Basic properties

- A conservative extension of a [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]] theory is consistent. If $T'$ were inconsistent, it would prove $\exists x \, (x \neq x)$, an $L$-sentence, so $T$ would prove it and be inconsistent.
- Model-theoretic test: if every [[model|MVCH6N9SNW]] of $T$ expands to a model of $T'$, then $T'$ is conservative over $T$. If $T' \vdash \sigma$ for an $L$-sentence $\sigma$, then each model of $T$ expands to a model of $T'$, where $\sigma$ is true by soundness; so $\sigma$ is true in every model of $T$, and [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]] gives $T \vdash \sigma$.
- The converse of the test is false. Let $T$ be the set of all sentences true in $\N$ in the language $\set{0, S, +, \cdot, <}$, and $T' = T \cup \set{c > \bar n : n \in \N}$, where $\bar n$ is the numeral of $n$.
- $T'$ is consistent by the [[compactness theorem|1NR3HAWG9D]]. $T$ is a [[complete theory|655K8Y7VS8]], and a consistent extension of a complete theory is conservative: if $T' \vdash \sigma$ and $T \nvdash \sigma$, then $T \vdash \lnot\sigma$ and $T'$ is inconsistent. But $\N$ has no element above all numerals, so it does not expand to a model of $T'$.
- Conservativity is transitive, and the union of a chain of conservative extensions of $T$ is conservative over $T$, because each derivation uses finitely many axioms.

### History

[[Hilbert's program|P1RW6JWX73]] in the 1920s asked for a finitary proof that the ideal parts of mathematics are conservative over its finitary part for finitary statements.
Gödel's second incompleteness theorem of 1931 showed that this cannot be done in the form Hilbert wanted.
Partial forms survive as conservation results; for example, Friedman proved that WKL0 is $\Pi^0_2$-conservative over primitive recursive arithmetic.

### Common errors

- Thinking that a conservative extension proves nothing new. It proves new sentences of $L'$; only the sentences of $L$ stay the same.
- Thinking that a true extension is conservative. $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ is true in $\N$, and it is not conservative over PA.
- Confusing "conservative" with "every model expands". Expansion of all models is sufficient, but not necessary, as the example with true arithmetic shows.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose a club has a rule book, and we add a new word to it, say "senior member" for "a member for more than ten years".
The new word makes some rules shorter to write.
But it cannot create a new rule about the old words: everything that we can now prove about members, we could prove before.
Such an addition is called conservative.
An addition such as "every member pays 5 euros" is not conservative, because it says something new in the old words.

### L2 human

We show that the theory $T'$ of groups with a new constant $c$ and the axiom $c \cdot c = c$ is conservative over the theory $T$ of groups in the language $\set{\cdot, e}$.

1. Let $G$ be any group. Interpret $c$ as $e$. Then $c \cdot c = e \cdot e = e = c$, so the expansion is a model of $T'$.
2. So every model of $T$ expands to a model of $T'$.
3. By the model-theoretic test, $T'$ proves no new sentence of the language $\set{\cdot, e}$.
4. In $T'$ we can prove $c = e$: multiply $c \cdot c = c$ on the right by the inverse of $c$. This is a new sentence, but it uses $c$.

The typical use is to add a convenient symbol or axiom and then check that every old model expands.
A trap is to add the axiom $c \cdot c = c \land c \neq e$.
Step 4 shows that it contradicts the group axioms, so no group expands, and the extension proves every sentence.

### L3 human

Conservation results measure the strength of theories by the sentences of a fixed class that they prove.
In reverse mathematics, WKL0 is $\Pi^1_1$-conservative over RCA0, and ACA0 is conservative over PA; so these systems prove the same arithmetic sentences as much weaker-looking theories.
Proof-theoretic methods such as cut elimination, the Dialectica interpretation and realizability give conservation results for intuitionistic and classical systems, collected in [[proof interpretations and conservation results|8XY7JYZKFW]] and [[reverse mathematics and subsystems|BY8AAFN7C5]].
In set theory, Gödel-Bernays class theory is conservative over ZF, so classes can be used as a convenient language.
The model-theoretic counterpart is the expansion of models, which links conservativity to [[Beth's definability theorem|RJBDSA2ETP]] and to Robinson's joint consistency theorem.
