## Statement

Let $\nu > 0$ and let $\Omega \subseteq \R^2$ be either the whole plane or a bounded open set with smooth boundary.
Let $u_0 \in H^1(\Omega; \R^2)$ satisfy $\nabla \cdot u_0 = 0$, and if $\Omega$ is bounded let $u_0 = 0$ on $\partial \Omega$.
Here $H^1$ is the [[Sobolev space|M4CEZ5K9HP]] of square-integrable fields with square-integrable first derivatives.
Then there is a unique pair $(u, p)$ with the following properties.

1. It solves the [[Navier–Stokes equations|18KXZHVF4M]] $\partial_t u + (u \cdot \nabla) u = \nu \Delta u - \nabla p$, $\nabla \cdot u = 0$ in $\Omega \times (0, \infty)$.
2. $u(\cdot, 0) = u_0$, and if $\Omega$ is bounded, $u$ satisfies the [[no-slip condition|DF0M8YNQ3D]] $u = 0$ on $\partial \Omega$.
3. $u \in C([0, \infty); H^1)$ and $u \in L^2(0, T; H^2)$ for every $T > 0$.
4. $u$ and $\nabla p$ are smooth in $\Omega \times (0, \infty)$.

So in two space dimensions no singularity forms in finite time.

## Remarks

The energy identity gives a bound on $\norm{u(t)}_{L^2}$ for all time, as in three dimensions.
In two dimensions the Ladyzhenskaya inequality $\norm{v}_{L^4}^2 \le C \norm{v}_{L^2} \norm{\nabla v}_{L^2}$ holds for $v \in H^1_0(\Omega)$, with a constant $C$ independent of $v$.
With it the nonlinear term is controlled in the estimate for $\norm{\nabla u(t)}_{L^2}$, and Gronwall's inequality gives a bound for all finite times.
Higher derivatives then follow by the usual bootstrap, and uniqueness follows from an energy estimate for the difference of two solutions.
On the whole plane one can also use the vorticity $\omega = \partial_1 u_2 - \partial_2 u_1$, which solves $\partial_t \omega + u \cdot \nabla \omega = \nu \Delta \omega$, so that $\norm{\omega(t)}_{L^2}$ never increases.
In three dimensions the corresponding inequality is only $\norm{v}_{L^4}^2 \le C \norm{v}_{L^2}^{1/2} \norm{\nabla v}_{L^2}^{3/2}$, the estimate no longer closes for large data, and the question is open.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For a flat, two-dimensional viscous fluid, a smooth start always gives a smooth flow for all time.
In the plane, swirls are carried along by the flow but never stretched, so viscosity keeps them from getting infinitely sharp.
This is the planar answer to the famous open problem about three-dimensional flows.

### L2 human

The theorem is the two-dimensional counterpart of the [[Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness|46Z1TVS2K6]] problem.
The key difference is scaling: in two dimensions the energy $\norm{u}_{L^2}^2$ is invariant under the scaling of the equations, so the a priori bound is critical and can be closed.
In three dimensions the energy is supercritical, and only [[Leray weak solutions|DHHR5PZZ7E]] are known to exist globally.
A related argument, based on the transport of vorticity, gives global smooth solutions of the two-dimensional Euler equations with smooth data.
