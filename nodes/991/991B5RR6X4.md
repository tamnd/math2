## Summary

Take a red phase of a traffic light on the interval $[0, 4]$ of the integers, and a car crossing on the interval $[2, 3]$.
The interval formula $[D]\,\neg\mathit{cross}$, "no crossing interval lies strictly inside this one", is false at $[0, 4]$, because $0 < 2 < 3 < 4$.
If green holds on $[4, 7]$, then $\langle A \rangle\,\mathit{green}$, "green holds on some interval that starts where this one ends", is true at $[0, 4]$.

In general, interval temporal logics are [[temporal logics|8XB117FKKV]] whose formulas are true or false of time intervals rather than of time points.
Over a [[linear order|0FRXBVVR03]] $(T, <)$, an interval is a pair $[a, b]$ with $a < b$, and two intervals stand in exactly one of the thirteen relations of Allen.
The logic HS of Halpern and Shoham has one modal operator for each of these relations except equality.
HS is undecidable over every class of linear orders that contains an infinite order, while some of its fragments are decidable.
The ITL of Moszkowski, with the binary "chop" operator, is the other main interval logic.

## Motivation

Events take time: a meeting, a transaction, the rise of a signal.
A point-based logic describes an event by its start and end points and the points between them.
Many statements are more direct about intervals, such as "during every red phase, no car crosses".
Allen proposed thirteen relations between intervals for planning in artificial intelligence in 1983.
Halpern and Shoham made these relations into the accessibility relations of a [[multimodal logic|ERG3KAQWYF]] in 1986.
The price is high: an interval is a pair of points, so the logic behaves like a two-dimensional modal logic, and the natural logics are undecidable.

## Notation

1. Intervals are $[a, b]$ with $a < b$ in the strict semantics, or with $a \le b$ in the non-strict semantics, which allows point intervals.
2. The relations of $[a, b]$ to $[c, d]$ are before ($b < c$), meets ($b = c$), overlaps ($a < c < b < d$), starts ($a = c$, $b < d$), during ($c < a$, $b < d$), finishes ($c < a$, $b = d$), equals, and the six inverses.
3. $\langle X \rangle \varphi$ is true at $[a, b]$ when $\varphi$ is true at some $[c, d]$ related to $[a, b]$ by $X$.
   The basic choices are $A$, "after" ($c = b$), $B$, "begins" ($c = a$, $d < b$), and $E$, "ends" ($a < c$, $d = b$).
   Also $D$ is "during" ($a < c$, $d < b$), $O$ is "overlaps" and $L$ is "later" ($b < c$).
4. $\langle \bar X \rangle$ is the modality of the inverse relation, and $[X] = \neg\langle X \rangle\neg$.
5. In ITL, $\varphi \mathbin{;} \psi$ ("chop") holds at $[a, b]$ when some $c$ with $a \le c \le b$ has $\varphi$ at $[a, c]$ and $\psi$ at $[c, b]$.

## Remarks

### Examples and a non-example

The thirteen relations for concrete intervals of integers, and why they are exhaustive and exclusive, are in [[the example of Allen's relations|ET6CYR090M]].
Over $(\mathbb{N}, <)$, $[B]\bot$ is true exactly at the intervals $[a, a + 1]$ of length one, by [[the unit-interval example|ZM3ZKMWMPK]].
In a partially ordered time, two intervals need not stand in any of the relations of Allen, by [[the non-example|TPK0GPTJAW]].
A point formula such as $Fp$ of LTL is not directly an interval formula, and it needs a translation that reads $p$ as "true at the left end".

### Building all relations from six

Every relation of Allen is a composition of $A$, $B$, $E$ and their inverses.
For example, "during" is $B$ followed by $E$: from $[a, b]$ go to $[a, d]$ with $d < b$, then to $[c, d]$ with $a < c$.
"Later" is $A$ followed by $A$: from $[a, b]$ go to $[b, c]$, then to $[c, d]$.
So HS can be built from the six modalities $\langle A \rangle$, $\langle B \rangle$, $\langle E \rangle$ and their inverses.

### Decidability

HS validity is undecidable over every class of linear orders that contains an order with an infinite ascending or descending sequence, by [[the undecidability theorem|9BE0H3W9QG]].
The fragment with only $\langle A \rangle$ and $\langle \bar A \rangle$, called propositional neighborhood logic, is decidable over the natural numbers and over all linear orders.

### Watch out

Intervals are not sets of points.
Two different intervals can satisfy different formulas even if no point formula tells them apart.

Fix the semantics before you compute.
With point intervals allowed, $[B]\bot$ holds at the point intervals $[a, a]$, not at the unit intervals.

The relation "during" is strict.
It requires both endpoints to lie strictly inside, so an interval is not during itself.

### History

James Allen introduced the interval algebra in "Maintaining knowledge about temporal intervals", Communications of the ACM, 1983.
Ben Moszkowski developed ITL with the chop operator in his Stanford thesis of 1983, for hardware verification.
Halpern and Shoham introduced HS in 1986, and the journal version appeared in the Journal of the ACM in 1991.

### Try it

Over $\mathbb{N}$, let $p$ be true exactly at the interval $[1, 2]$.
Is $\langle D \rangle p$ true at $[0, 3]$?
Yes: $0 < 1 < 2 < 3$, so $[1, 2]$ lies strictly inside $[0, 3]$.

Check the same answer with $\langle B \rangle \langle E \rangle p$ at $[0, 3]$.
$\langle B \rangle$ moves to $[0, 2]$, since $2 < 3$, and then $\langle E \rangle$ moves to $[1, 2]$, since $0 < 1$, where $p$ holds.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Most temporal logics speak about moments: "at 3 o'clock the light is on".
Interval logics speak about stretches of time: "during the whole meeting the light is on".
They have operators like "at some interval that starts when this one ends" or "at some interval inside this one".
Such logics fit planning and hardware design, where things take time.

### L2 human

We evaluate $\langle B \rangle p$ at $[0, 5]$ over $\mathbb{N}$, with $p$ true exactly at the intervals $[a, a + 2]$.

1. $\langle B \rangle p$ asks for an interval $[0, d]$ with $0 < d < 5$ and $p$ true there.
2. The interval $[0, 2]$ qualifies.
3. At $[0, 2]$ itself, $\langle B \rangle p$ fails, because the only candidate is $[0, 1]$, of length one.

The typical use is to describe the shape of a behavior in time, such as "every request interval begins with an acknowledgment interval".
Watch out: the strict and the non-strict semantics give different logics.

### L3 human

An interval $[a, b]$ is a point $(a, b)$ above the diagonal in $T \times T$, so HS is a two-dimensional modal logic.
Its frames contain grid-like structures, and undecidability follows from [[tiling problems|AJH5HPD2B7]].
The decidable fragments avoid grids, for example by having only modalities that move in one direction.
The logic CDT of Venema, built from chop and two related binary operators, extends this view.
