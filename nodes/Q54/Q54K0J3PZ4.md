## Example

Let $f(n) = n^2 + n + 41$.
The claim is "$f(n)$ is a [[prime number|01826KMG2H]] for every natural number $n$", and the argument offered is "we checked $f(0), f(1), \ldots, f(39)$, and all of them are prime".
We show that this argument is not a proof by [[mathematical induction|0ASJVEFA9J]], and that the claim is false.

1. A proof by induction needs a base case and an induction step.
2. The argument has a base case, since $f(0) = 41$ is prime.
3. The argument has no induction step.
   It never shows that "$f(n)$ is prime" implies "$f(n + 1)$ is prime" for an arbitrary $n$.
4. Forty checked cases are finitely many cases, and they say nothing about $n = 40$.
5. At $n = 40$ every term has the factor $41$:
   $$f(40) = 40 \cdot 40 + 40 + 41 = 40 \cdot 41 + 41 = 41 \cdot 41 = 1681.$$
   So $f(40)$ is not prime, and the claim is false.
6. The next value fails too:
   $$f(41) = 41^2 + 41 + 41 = 41 \cdot 43 = 1763.$$

Check: $41 \cdot 41 = 1681$ and $41 \cdot 43 = 1763$.

## Remarks

The example separates two meanings of the word "induction".
[[Inductive reasoning|5V35SGEN1T]] passes from many cases to a guess, and mathematical induction proves a rule that carries each case to the next.
The first kind of induction suggests the claim here, and only the second kind could prove it.

No induction step can exist, because the claim is false.
The step from $n = 39$ to $n = 40$ fails: $f(39) = 1601$ is prime, and $f(40)$ is not.
So the forty cases were not a base case waiting for a step, but evidence for a false guess.

The failure is not bad luck.
No non-constant polynomial with integer coefficients takes only prime values at the natural numbers, so a finite check can never become a proof of a claim of this form.
The same polynomial, seen as a lesson on evidence rather than on induction, is the subject of [[forty prime values of $n^2 + n + 41$|H75AB4EJT5]].

Euler noticed in 1772 that this polynomial takes many prime values.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The rule "$n^2 + n + 41$ is prime" works for $n = 0, 1, 2$, and on up to $39$.
Forty successes in a row feel like a proof, but they are not.
At $n = 40$ the value is $1681$, which is $41 \times 41$.
A proof by induction must show why each case leads to the next one, and here no such reason exists.

### L2 human

We try to write an induction step, and we watch it break.
Assume that $f(n)$ is prime, and compute the next value:
$$f(n + 1) = (n + 1)^2 + (n + 1) + 41 = f(n) + 2n + 2.$$
The fact that $f(n)$ is prime says nothing about $f(n) + 2n + 2$, because a prime plus an even number can be prime or composite.
For example, $f(39) + 80 = 1601 + 80 = 1681$, and this is $41^2$.
So no step of the form "$f(n)$ prime implies $f(n + 1)$ prime" follows from the relation between consecutive values.

Checking small cases is the right way to find a conjecture, and then a proof or a counterexample must settle it.
Watch out for "and so on" or "by induction" written after a list of cases with no step: that is not a proof.
