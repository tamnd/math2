## Example

Let $f(n) = n^2 + n + 41$.
Consider the claim "$f(n)$ is a [[prime number|01826KMG2H]] for every natural number $n$", and the argument "we checked $f(0), f(1), \ldots, f(39)$, and all are prime".
We show that this argument is not a proof by [[mathematical induction|0ASJVEFA9J]], and that the claim is false.

1. A proof by induction needs a base case and an induction step.
2. The argument has a base case: $f(0) = 41$ is prime.
3. The argument has no induction step. It never shows that "$f(n)$ is prime" implies "$f(n + 1)$ is prime" for an arbitrary $n$.
4. Forty checked cases are finitely many cases, and they say nothing about $n = 40$.
5. At $n = 40$: $f(40) = 1600 + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41^2$. So $f(40)$ is not prime, and the claim is false.
6. At $n = 41$: $f(41) = 41^2 + 41 + 41 = 41 \cdot 43 = 1763$, which is not prime either.

A computer check confirms that $f(n)$ is prime for each $n$ from $0$ to $39$, and that $n = 40$ is the first exception.

## Remarks

1. The example shows the difference between [[inductive reasoning|5V35SGEN1T]] (a guess from many cases) and mathematical induction (a proof of a rule that carries each case to the next).
2. No induction step can exist here, because the claim is false. The step from $n = 39$ to $n = 40$ fails: $f(39) = 1601$ is prime, and $f(40)$ is not.
3. The failure at $n = 40$ has a reason: $f(40) = 40 \cdot 41 + 41 = 41 \cdot 41$. In the same way, $f(41) = 41 \cdot 43$.
4. In fact, no non-constant polynomial with integer coefficients takes only prime values at all natural numbers. So a finite check can never be extended to a proof for a claim of this form.
5. Euler noticed in 1772 that this polynomial takes many prime values.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The rule "$n^2 + n + 41$ is prime" works for $n = 0, 1, 2$, and on up to $39$.
Forty successes in a row feel like a proof, but they are not.
At $n = 40$ the value is $1681$, which is $41 \times 41$.
A proof by induction must show why each case leads to the next one, and here no such reason exists.

### L2 human

We try to write an induction step, and see where it breaks.

1. Assume that $f(n) = n^2 + n + 41$ is prime.
2. Then $f(n + 1) = (n + 1)^2 + (n + 1) + 41 = f(n) + 2n + 2$.
3. The primality of $f(n)$ says nothing about the primality of $f(n) + 2n + 2$, because the sum of a prime and an even number can be prime or composite.
4. So no step of the form "$f(n)$ prime implies $f(n + 1)$ prime" follows from the relation of step 2.

The typical use of a check of small cases is to find a conjecture, which a proof must then confirm or a counterexample must refute.
A trap: a student who sees a pattern for many cases writes "and so on" or "by induction" without a step.
That is not a proof.
