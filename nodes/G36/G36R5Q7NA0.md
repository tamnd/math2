## Proof

We move the problem into coordinates, find the first maximum with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and then remove one pair of singular vectors and use induction on $p$.

For a real $p \times q$ matrix $M$ with $p \le q$, let $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_p$ be the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix $M M^{\mathsf{T}}$, and put $\sigma_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}$.
The eigenvalues are real by the [[real spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]], and $\lambda_k \ge 0$ because $x^{\mathsf{T}} M M^{\mathsf{T}} x = \norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} x}^2 \ge 0$.

**Reduction to a matrix problem.**

1. The map $a \mapsto Q_1 a$ is a bijection from $\R^p$ onto $U$, and $(Q_1 a) \cdot (Q_1 a') = a^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1 a' = a \cdot a'$ because $Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1 = I_p$.
2. So this map keeps norms and orthogonality, and the same holds for $c \mapsto Q_2 c$ from $\R^q$ onto $W$.
3. For $u = Q_1 a$ and $w = Q_2 c$ we get $u \cdot w = a^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2 c = a^{\mathsf{T}} M c$.
4. By steps 2 and 3, the definition of the [[principal angles|YHVD3773AW]] becomes a statement about $M$: $\cos \theta_k$ is the maximum of $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c$ over unit vectors $a \in \R^p$ and $c \in \R^q$ with $a \perp a_1, \dots, a_{k-1}$ and $c \perp c_1, \dots, c_{k-1}$, where $(a_i, c_i)$ are the earlier maximizing pairs.

**The first maximum.**

5. For unit vectors $a$ and $c$, the [[Cauchy-Schwarz inequality|0RFWW56Y22]] gives $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c = (M^{\mathsf{T}} a) \cdot c \le \norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a}$.
6. The [[Rayleigh quotient|06WHP8SE98]] of a symmetric matrix is at most its largest eigenvalue, so $\norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a}^2 = a^{\mathsf{T}} (M M^{\mathsf{T}}) a \le \lambda_1$, and with step 5, $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c \le \sigma_1$.
7. If $\sigma_1 = 0$, then step 6 gives $M^{\mathsf{T}} a = 0$ for every unit vector $a$, so $M = 0$, and every pair gives the value $0 = \sigma_1$.
8. If $\sigma_1 > 0$, let $a$ be a unit eigenvector of $M M^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $\lambda_1$ and put $c = M^{\mathsf{T}} a / \sigma_1$.
9. Then $\norm{c}^2 = \lambda_1 / \sigma_1^2 = 1$ and $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c = \norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a}^2 / \sigma_1 = \sigma_1$, so the maximum over unit vectors is $\sigma_1$.
10. Now let $\sigma_1 > 0$ and let $(a, c)$ be any unit vectors with $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c = \sigma_1$, so that steps 5 and 6 are equalities.
11. Equality in Cauchy-Schwarz with $\norm{c} = 1$ gives $c = M^{\mathsf{T}} a / \norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a}$, and equality in step 6 gives $\norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a} = \sigma_1$, so $M^{\mathsf{T}} a = \sigma_1 c$.
12. Write $a = \sum_i \alpha_i e_i$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis $e_1, \dots, e_p$ of $M M^{\mathsf{T}}$; then $\sum_i \lambda_i \alpha_i^2 = \lambda_1$ and $\sum_i \alpha_i^2 = 1$ force $\alpha_i = 0$ whenever $\lambda_i < \lambda_1$.
13. Hence $M M^{\mathsf{T}} a = \lambda_1 a$, and then $M c = M M^{\mathsf{T}} a / \sigma_1 = \sigma_1 a$.

**Part 1, by induction on $p$.**

14. We claim that the $k$-th maximum in step 4 is $\sigma_k$, for every choice of the earlier maximizing pairs.
15. For $k = 1$ this is steps 5 to 9, and this settles the case $p = 1$.
16. If $\sigma_1 = 0$, then $M = 0$ by step 7, so all maxima are $0$ and all $\sigma_k$ are $0$; the constraint sets are not empty, because $k - 1 < p \le q$.
17. Let $\sigma_1 > 0$, and let $(a_1, c_1)$ be the first maximizing pair, so that $M c_1 = \sigma_1 a_1$ and $M^{\mathsf{T}} a_1 = \sigma_1 c_1$ by steps 11 and 13.
18. Complete $a_1$ to an orthonormal basis of $\R^p$ by the columns of a $p \times (p - 1)$ matrix $Y'$, and $c_1$ to an orthonormal basis of $\R^q$ by the columns of a $q \times (q - 1)$ matrix $Z'$, and put $M_1 = Y'^{\mathsf{T}} M Z'$.
19. By step 17, $a_1^{\mathsf{T}} M Z' = \sigma_1 c_1^{\mathsf{T}} Z' = 0$ and $Y'^{\mathsf{T}} M c_1 = \sigma_1 Y'^{\mathsf{T}} a_1 = 0$, so for the orthogonal matrices $G = (a_1 \ Y')$ and $H = (c_1 \ Z')$,
$$G^{\mathsf{T}} M H = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 \\ 0 & M_1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
20. Since $H H^{\mathsf{T}} = I$, this gives
$$G^{\mathsf{T}} (M M^{\mathsf{T}}) G = (G^{\mathsf{T}} M H)(G^{\mathsf{T}} M H)^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & 0 \\ 0 & M_1 M_1^{\mathsf{T}} \end{pmatrix}.$$
21. So the eigenvalues of $M M^{\mathsf{T}}$ are $\sigma_1^2$ together with those of $M_1 M_1^{\mathsf{T}}$, and since $\sigma_1^2$ is the largest, the singular values of $M_1$ are $\sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_p$.
22. The unit vectors $a \perp a_1$ are $a = Y' \alpha$ with $\alpha$ a unit vector, the unit vectors $c \perp c_1$ are $c = Z' \gamma$ with $\gamma$ a unit vector, and the maps keep orthogonality as in step 1.
23. For such vectors, $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c = \alpha^{\mathsf{T}} Y'^{\mathsf{T}} M Z' \gamma = \alpha^{\mathsf{T}} M_1 \gamma$.
24. So maxima $2, \dots, p$ of the problem for $M$ are maxima $1, \dots, p - 1$ of the problem for $M_1$, and since $p - 1 \le q - 1$, the induction hypothesis gives them as $\sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_p$ by step 21.
25. This proves the claim of step 14.
26. For $\sigma_1 \le 1$, the [[projection formulas|AK3JJ5HHG2]] give $Q_2 Q_2^{\mathsf{T}} = P_W$, so for $u = Q_1 a$ we get $\norm{M^{\mathsf{T}} a} = \norm{Q_2^{\mathsf{T}} u} = \norm{Q_2 Q_2^{\mathsf{T}} u} = \norm{P_W u}$, by step 2 for $Q_2$.
27. The [[Pythagorean theorem|0BVTDM872V]] gives $\norm{P_W u} \le \norm{u} = \norm{a}$, so $\lambda_1 \le 1$ by step 6.
28. Hence every $\sigma_k$ lies in $[0, 1]$, and step 14 with the definition of $\theta_k$ gives $\cos \theta_k = \sigma_k$, which proves part 1.

**Parts 2 to 5.**

29. Let $M = Y \Sigma Z^{\mathsf{T}}$ be a [[singular value decomposition|000NPTMX6J]]; then $y_k^{\mathsf{T}} M z_k = \sigma_k$, and the $y_k$ and the $z_k$ are orthonormal.
30. By step 4 and part 1, the pairs $(y_k, z_k)$ reach the $k$-th maximum, so $u_k = Q_1 y_k$ and $w_k = Q_2 z_k$ are principal vectors, which proves part 2.
31. For part 3, $\theta_k = 0$ if and only if $\sigma_k = 1$, so the number of zero angles is the multiplicity of the eigenvalue $1$ of $M M^{\mathsf{T}} = Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} P_W Q_1$.
32. By the spectral theorem, this multiplicity is the dimension of $E = \{ a : Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} P_W Q_1 a = a \}$.
33. If $a \in E$ and $u = Q_1 a$, then, since $P_W^{\mathsf{T}} P_W = P_W$, we get $\norm{P_W u}^2 = a^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} P_W Q_1 a = \norm{a}^2 = \norm{u}^2$.
34. The Pythagorean theorem then gives $\norm{u - P_W u}^2 = \norm{u}^2 - \norm{P_W u}^2 = 0$, so $u \in U \cap W$.
35. Conversely, if $u = Q_1 a$ lies in $U \cap W$, then $P_W u = u$ and $Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} P_W Q_1 a = Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1 a = a$, so $a \in E$.
36. So $a \mapsto Q_1 a$ maps $E$ onto $U \cap W$, it is injective by step 1, and $\dim E = \dim (U \cap W)$, which proves part 3.
37. For part 4, $p = 1$ gives $Q_1 = u$ and, by step 26, $M M^{\mathsf{T}} = \norm{Q_2^{\mathsf{T}} u}^2 = \norm{P_W u}^2$, so $\cos \theta_1 = \sigma_1 = \norm{P_W u}$.
38. For part 5, $X = P_U - P_W$ is symmetric, so the definition of the [[Frobenius norm|JT8Q204E4R]] gives
$$\norm{X}_F^2 = \tr (X^{\mathsf{T}} X) = \tr (P_U^2 - P_U P_W - P_W P_U + P_W^2).$$
39. Here $P_U^2 = P_U = Q_1 Q_1^{\mathsf{T}}$, and the cyclic rule for the [[trace|0XC1RDBY7A]] gives $\tr (Q_1 Q_1^{\mathsf{T}}) = \tr (Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1) = \tr (I_p) = p$; in the same way $\tr (P_W^2) = q$.
40. The cyclic rule also gives $\tr (P_U P_W) = \tr (Q_1 Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2 Q_2^{\mathsf{T}}) = \tr (Q_1^{\mathsf{T}} Q_2 Q_2^{\mathsf{T}} Q_1) = \tr (M M^{\mathsf{T}})$, and $\tr (P_W P_U) = \tr (P_U P_W)$.
41. The trace of $M M^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the sum of its eigenvalues, which is $\sum_k \sigma_k^2 = \sum_k \cos^2 \theta_k$, so steps 38 to 40 give $\norm{P_U - P_W}_F^2 = p + q - 2 \sum_k \cos^2 \theta_k$.
42. For $p = q$ this is $2p - 2 \sum_k \cos^2 \theta_k = 2 \sum_k (1 - \cos^2 \theta_k) = 2 \sum_k \sin^2 \theta_k$, which proves part 5.

## Idea

Orthonormal bases turn the geometric search into the maximization of $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c$ over unit vectors $a$ and $c$.
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the Rayleigh quotient show that the largest value is the largest singular value, and every pair that reaches it is a pair of singular vectors.
In bases that start with this pair, $M$ splits into the block $\sigma_1$ and a smaller matrix $M_1$, and the remaining searches only see $M_1$.
So induction peels off the singular values one at a time, and they come out as the cosines in decreasing order.

## Variants

If the singular value decomposition $M = Y \Sigma Z^{\mathsf{T}}$ is known in advance, write $a = Y \alpha$ and $c = Z \gamma$, so that $a^{\mathsf{T}} M c = \sum_k \sigma_k \alpha_k \gamma_k$.
The bound $\sum_k \sigma_k \alpha_k \gamma_k \le \sigma_1 \sum_k \abs{\alpha_k \gamma_k} \le \sigma_1$ then replaces steps 5 and 6, since $\sum_k \abs{\alpha_k \gamma_k} \le 1$ by Cauchy-Schwarz.
The later angles follow from the same bound on the coordinates that the constraints leave free.
This route still needs steps 10 to 13, which show that every maximizing pair is a pair of singular vectors.
