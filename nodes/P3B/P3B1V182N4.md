## Summary

Two [[structures|983GZBQYT4]] $\mathcal{M}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ for the same signature $L$ are *elementarily equivalent*, written $\mathcal{M} \equiv \mathcal{N}$, when they satisfy the same first-order $L$-[[sentences|DKFGQTRYZE]].
Equivalently, they have the same [[theory|~305c87e7]]: $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M}) = \operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{N})$.
[[Isomorphic|PZMGQMDN25]] structures are elementarily equivalent, but the converse fails for infinite structures.
Elementary equivalence is the notion of sameness that first-order logic can see, and much of model theory studies how far it is from isomorphism.

## Motivation

Isomorphism is the usual notion of sameness for structures, but first-order logic cannot detect it.
A first-order sentence cannot say "the domain is countable", so it cannot separate a countable structure from an uncountable one with the same first-order properties.
Elementary equivalence is the coarser relation that exactly matches what sentences can express.
It lets us transfer results: if $\mathcal{M} \equiv \mathcal{N}$ and a first-order statement is proved for $\mathcal{M}$, it holds in $\mathcal{N}$.
For example, a first-order statement about polynomials proved over $\C$ by analysis holds over every algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$.

## Notation

The standard symbol is $\mathcal{M} \equiv \mathcal{N}$, and $\mathcal{M} \equiv_L \mathcal{N}$ shows the signature.
Mathlib calls this relation `FirstOrder.Language.ElementarilyEquivalent`, defined as equality of the complete theories `L.completeTheory M = L.completeTheory N`.
For agreement on sentences of quantifier depth at most $k$, one writes $\mathcal{M} \equiv_k \mathcal{N}$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- $(\Q, <) \equiv (\R, <)$, although the orders are not isomorphic; see [[this example|~aba9d1ce]].
- $(\C, +, \cdot) \equiv (\overline{\Q}, +, \cdot)$, the complex and the algebraic numbers; see [[this example|~81e191f1]].
- Any two models of a [[complete theory|655K8Y7VS8]] are elementarily equivalent, for instance any two infinite sets in the empty signature.

### Non-examples

- $(\Z, <) \not\equiv (\Q, <)$: the density sentence separates them; see [[this non-example|~e22e87be]].
- $(\Z, +) \not\equiv (\Q, +)$: the sentence $\forall x \, \exists y \, (y + y = x)$ holds in $\Q$ and fails in $\Z$ at $x = 1$.
- $(\R, +, \cdot) \not\equiv (\C, +, \cdot)$: the sentence $\exists x \, (x \cdot x + 1 = 0)$ holds only in $\C$.

### Basic properties

- $\equiv$ is an equivalence relation on $L$-structures.
- [[Isomorphic structures are elementarily equivalent|NDTJ2E895C]].
- For finite structures the converse holds: [[elementarily equivalent finite structures are isomorphic|~906b905e]].
- By the [[Löwenheim-Skolem theorem|484M0TZ1S7]], every infinite structure in a countable signature is elementarily equivalent to structures of every infinite cardinality.
- A satisfiable theory is complete if and only if all its models are elementarily equivalent; see [[the characterization of complete theories|~3e32bff6]].
- If $\mathcal{M}$ is an [[elementary substructure|8WZF9SBP39]] of $\mathcal{N}$, then $\mathcal{M} \equiv \mathcal{N}$; the converse fails.

### History

Tarski's quantifier elimination for real closed fields shows that $(\R, +, \cdot, <)$ is elementarily equivalent to the ordered field of real algebraic numbers, an early example of two nonisomorphic elementarily equivalent structures.
The finer notion of elementary substructure first appears in a 1957 paper of Tarski and Vaught.

### Common errors

- To think that elementary equivalence implies isomorphism. It does so only for finite structures.
- To compare structures in different signatures. Both structures must interpret the same symbols.
- To use a property of subsets, such as completeness of the order, as a first-order sentence. Such properties are invisible to $\equiv$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Imagine that you may only ask yes-or-no questions written as first-order sentences.
Two structures are elementarily equivalent when every such question gets the same answer in both.
The ordered sets $(\Q, <)$ and $(\R, <)$ are elementarily equivalent, although one is countable and the other is not.
The ordered sets $(\Z, <)$ and $(\Q, <)$ are not, because "between any two elements there is a third" is true in $\Q$ and false in $\Z$.

### L2 human

We show that $(\Z, +) \not\equiv (\Z \times \Z, +)$, with componentwise addition.

1. Let $\sigma$ say "there are exactly two classes modulo $2$": $\exists u \, \forall x \, \exists y \, (x = y + y \lor x = y + y + u)$ and $\neg\exists y \, (u = y + y)$, joined into one sentence with $\exists u$ in front.
2. In $\Z$, take $u = 1$: every integer is $2y$ or $2y + 1$, and $1$ is odd. So $\Z \models \sigma$.
3. In $\Z \times \Z$, the group modulo doubling is $(\Z/2\Z)^2$, with four elements. For any $u$, one of $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$ is neither $2y$ nor $2y + u$. So $\Z \times \Z \models \neg\sigma$.

The typical use of elementary equivalence is to move a first-order result from a structure where it is easy to one where it is hard.
A trap is to separate structures by cardinality; first-order sentences cannot see it.

### L3 human

Elementary equivalence is the equivalence relation whose classes are the complete theories, so classifying structures up to $\equiv$ is classifying complete theories.
The Keisler-Shelah theorem characterizes it algebraically: $\mathcal{M} \equiv \mathcal{N}$ if and only if $\mathcal{M}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ have isomorphic ultrapowers; see the sub-area on [[ultraproducts|HZG1YP26AB]].
The Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game characterizes $\equiv_k$ combinatorially; see the sub-area on [[back-and-forth methods|N64R1MGDBN]].
Inside one model, the finer relation of [[elementary substructure|8WZF9SBP39]] asks that formulas with parameters also agree.
