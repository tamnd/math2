## Description

The invertible matrices of one size form a group under multiplication, the general linear group.
Let $R$ be a commutative ring and let $n \ge 1$ be an integer.
The *general linear group* $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ is the set of [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] $n \times n$ matrices over $R$, with [[matrix multiplication|032Q9FB8MT]] as operation.
It is a [[group|G5S1M9]]:

1. The product of two invertible matrices is invertible, by the [[inverse of a product|0S4WTRE9GG]].
2. Matrix multiplication is [[associative|073E4XC016]].
3. The identity element is $I_n$.
4. The inverse of $A$ in the group is the inverse matrix $A^{-1}$, which is invertible by the [[rules for inverses|2ZH4X4Q99D]].

Equivalently, $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ is the [[group of units|07C6ECNJET]] of the [[matrix ring|0FHVPRAE60]] $M_n(R)$.
For a field $F$ and a vector space $V$ over $F$, the group $\mathrm{GL}(V)$ is the group of [[linear isomorphisms|0B8T10CE7N]] of $V$ to itself.
The group $\mathrm{GL}(F^n)$ is isomorphic to $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$.

## Remarks

### Small cases

For $n = 1$, the group $\mathrm{GL}_1(R)$ is the group $R^{\times}$ of units of $R$, so $\mathrm{GL}_1(\mathbb{R})$ is the group of nonzero real numbers under multiplication, and it is abelian.
For $n \ge 2$, the group $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is not abelian, as the [[$2 \times 2$ example|DER31Q8FCM]] shows.
Over the field $\mathbb{Z}/2$ with two elements, $\mathrm{GL}_2$ has exactly $6$ elements, as the example [[$\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}/2)$|M170X47PG4]] lists.
More generally, over a finite field with $q$ elements the [[order of the general linear group|0JQ1KNQW2K]] is $(q^n - 1)(q^n - q) \cdots (q^n - q^{n-1})$, since the columns must be chosen one at a time outside the span of the earlier ones.
For $q = 2$ and $n = 2$ this gives $3 \cdot 2 = 6$.

### Which matrices belong

Over a commutative ring, $A$ is in $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ exactly when $\det A$ is a unit, by the [[determinant criterion over rings|03Q7RE2HXR]].
So $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$ is the group of integer matrices with determinant $\pm 1$.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $1$, is in $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, while $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is in $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Q})$ but not in $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$.

### Generators, determinant and center

Over a field, $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is generated by the [[elementary matrices|01REXW3WWE]], by [[invertibility and row reduction|1ZK55FS2BF]].
The [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] is a group homomorphism $\det \colon \mathrm{GL}_n(R) \to R^{\times}$, as the [[determinant homomorphism example|0YZ3T5Q7XN]] explains, and its kernel is the [[special linear group|0V9PMN0RBH]] $\mathrm{SL}_n(R)$.
The center of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is the group of nonzero scalar matrices $cI_n$.

### Watch out

The group is closed under multiplication but not under addition.
For example, $I_n$ and $-I_n$ are both invertible, and their sum is the zero matrix.
So $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is not a subspace of the space of matrices, and it does not contain $0$.

### Continuous groups

Over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$, the group $\mathrm{GL}_n$ is the open subset of the space of matrices where $\det \ne 0$, and it is a [[matrix Lie group|36KSZ7GA5S]]; that view belongs to Lie theory.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The general linear group collects all the invertible matrices of one size.
We can multiply two of them, and we can undo each of them, so they form a group.
Geometrically, its elements are all the ways to transform space linearly without flattening it.

### L2 human

Here is a typical membership check in $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Q})$.
The matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\det A = 2 - 1 = 1 \ne 0$, so $A$ is in the group, and its group inverse is
$$A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $AA^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 & -2 + 2 \\ 1 - 1 & -1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ is not in the group, because its determinant is $4 - 4 = 0$.

### L3 human

$\mathrm{GL}_n$ is a functor from commutative rings to groups: a ring homomorphism $R \to S$ applied to each entry gives a group homomorphism $\mathrm{GL}_n(R) \to \mathrm{GL}_n(S)$.
In algebraic geometry, $\mathrm{GL}_n$ is the affine group scheme with coordinate ring $\mathbb{Z}[x_{ij}, 1/\det]$.
The study of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{F}_q)$ as a finite group, and of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ as a Lie group, belongs to group theory and to Lie theory.
