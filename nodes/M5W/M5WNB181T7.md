## Statement

No finite list of first-order axioms has the same theorems as Peano arithmetic.
The reason in one line: a finite list would fit inside one fragment $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$, and $\mathsf{PA}$ proves that each such fragment is consistent.

Exactly: there is no finite set $F$ of sentences of the [[language of arithmetic|D8SD54M5PN]] $L_A$ such that $F$ and [[Peano arithmetic|HACXBCM7SS]] $\mathsf{PA}$ have the same theorems.
Equivalently, no single sentence $\sigma$ of $L_A$ satisfies both $\mathsf{PA} \vdash \sigma$ and $\sigma \vdash \varphi$ for every axiom $\varphi$ of $\mathsf{PA}$.

## Remarks

### A finite piece of PA

Take the axioms $\mathsf{Q}1$ to $\mathsf{Q}8$ together with two induction axioms.
Let $\iota_1$ be induction for $\exists y \, (y \cdot y = x)$ and $\iota_2$ induction for $\forall y \, \exists z \, (x + y = z)$.
The formula of $\iota_1$ is $\Sigma_1$, so $\iota_1$ is an axiom of the [[fragment|T70PYM1F36]] $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$.
The formula of $\iota_2$ is $\Pi_2$, and $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_2$ proves every $\Pi_2$ induction axiom.
So the whole finite set is provable in $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_2$.

The same happens to every finite set of axioms of $\mathsf{PA}$: it uses induction only up to some level $n$, so it lies inside $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$.
If such a finite set proved all of $\mathsf{PA}$, then $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ would prove all of $\mathsf{PA}$.
But $\mathsf{PA}$ proves $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n)$, so $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ would prove its own consistency, which the [[second incompleteness theorem|4PZM5GJTR5]] forbids.
The [[proof by reflection|BBX212YCR4]] writes this out.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The theorem is about first-order axioms in $L_A$.
The second-order [[Peano axioms|0QFT7Z76N5]] are finitely many, because one induction axiom quantifies over all sets.
In the same way, $\mathsf{ACA}_0$ is finitely axiomatizable in the two-sorted language of second-order arithmetic, and its first-order theorems are exactly those of $\mathsf{PA}$, by [[the conservativity example|HPA6HX3114]].
So a finite axiomatization exists once set variables are allowed.

Full induction is needed too.
The fragments $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$ with $n \ge 1$ are finitely axiomatizable, and so is [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] $\mathsf{Q}$.

The phrase "the same theorems" matters.
$\mathsf{PA}$ is given by an infinite schema, but the theorem says more: no finite set of sentences at all, axioms of $\mathsf{PA}$ or not, generates the same theory.

### What it gives you

The chain of fragments never collapses.
For each $n$ there is $m > n$ with $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n \subsetneq \mathsf{I}\Sigma_m$, since otherwise $\mathsf{PA}$ would equal some finitely axiomatizable $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$.
Every finite subset of $\mathsf{PA}$ has a model that is not a model of $\mathsf{PA}$, by the completeness theorem.

The argument extends to all consistent extensions of $\mathsf{PA}$ in $L_A$, because $\mathsf{PA}$ is essentially reflexive.
Every such extension $T$ proves the consistency of each finite subset of $T$.
So no consistent extension of $\mathsf{PA}$ in $L_A$ is finitely axiomatizable.

### The converse

The converse fails: a theory that is not finitely axiomatizable need not contain $\mathsf{PA}$.
The [[theory of infinite sets|KFWXHKBHYT]] in the empty language is not finitely axiomatizable, by compactness, and it has no arithmetic at all.
Inside arithmetic, the theory $\mathsf{R}$ of Tarski, Mostowski and Robinson, one of the [[weak theories of arithmetic|Q5MT8WY7JM]], is another example.

### Generalizations

The same reflection idea works for set theory.
[[Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory|0HE5WDQ9FJ]] proves the consistency of each of its finite subsets, so, if it is consistent, it is not finitely axiomatizable either.

## History

Czesław Ryll-Nardzewski proved the theorem in 1952.
The proof by reflection below is one of several routes; it uses the fragments $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_n$, truth formulas of bounded complexity and the second incompleteness theorem.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Peano arithmetic has infinitely many axioms, one induction axiom for each property of numbers.
The theorem says that this cannot be avoided.
No finite list of axioms, written in the same language, proves exactly the same statements.
The reason is that any finite part of Peano arithmetic is weak enough that the full theory can prove the part is consistent.

### L2 human

We find the fragment for the finite set $\{ \mathsf{Q}1, \ldots, \mathsf{Q}8, \iota_1, \iota_2 \}$ of the first remark, step by step.

1. The axioms $\mathsf{Q}1$ to $\mathsf{Q}8$ belong to every fragment.
2. The formula $\exists y \, (y \cdot y = x)$ is $\Sigma_1$, so $\iota_1$ is an axiom of $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_1$, and so of $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_2$.
3. The formula $\forall y \, \exists z \, (x + y = z)$ is $\Pi_2$, and $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_2$ proves every $\Pi_2$ induction axiom.
4. So $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_2$ proves the whole set, and $\mathsf{PA}$, even $\mathsf{I}\Sigma_3$, proves $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{I}\Sigma_2)$.

The typical use is the step "finitely many axioms of $\mathsf{PA}$ are proved in one fragment".
Watch out: the theorem does not say that $\mathsf{PA}$ is undecidable or incomplete.
Those are different properties, and the finite theory $\mathsf{Q}$ has them too.
