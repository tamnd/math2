## Proof

We check the three conditions of an equivalence relation, and then each invariant, using only the rules $(XY)^{\mathsf{T}} = Y^{\mathsf{T}} X^{\mathsf{T}}$ of the [[transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]] and $(X^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}} = X$.

Part 1 is the statement that [[congruence|NZJDNC6EP6]] is an [[equivalence relation|0SGTMBEWRF]].

1. Reflexive. We have $A = I^{\mathsf{T}} A I$, and the identity matrix $I$ is invertible, so $A$ is congruent to $A$.
2. Symmetric. Let $A' = P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$ with $P$ invertible, and put $Q = P^{-1}$. The transpose of a product gives
$$Q^{\mathsf{T}} P^{\mathsf{T}} = (P Q)^{\mathsf{T}} = I^{\mathsf{T}} = I.$$
3. Multiply $A' = P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$ by $Q^{\mathsf{T}}$ on the left and by $Q$ on the right, and use step 2 and $PQ = I$:
$$Q^{\mathsf{T}} A' Q = (Q^{\mathsf{T}} P^{\mathsf{T}})\, A\, (P Q) = A.$$
Since $Q$ is invertible, $A$ is congruent to $A'$.
4. Transitive. Let $A' = P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$ and $A'' = Q^{\mathsf{T}} A' Q$ with $P$ and $Q$ invertible. Substitute the first equation into the second:
$$A'' = Q^{\mathsf{T}} P^{\mathsf{T}} A P Q = (PQ)^{\mathsf{T}} A (PQ).$$
5. The product $PQ$ is invertible, with inverse $Q^{-1} P^{-1}$, so $A''$ is congruent to $A$. This proves part 1.

Part 2 is about the rank.

6. The matrix $P^{\mathsf{T}}$ is invertible, with inverse $(P^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}}$, by step 2.
7. By the [[rank of a product|02FDWY55AS]], multiplying by an invertible matrix on either side keeps the rank. Apply this to $P^{\mathsf{T}}$ and then to $P$:
$$\operatorname{rank}(P^{\mathsf{T}} A P) = \operatorname{rank}(A P) = \operatorname{rank} A.$$

Part 3 is about symmetry and skew-symmetry.

8. By the transpose of a product, applied twice,
$$(P^{\mathsf{T}} A P)^{\mathsf{T}} = P^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} (P^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}} = P^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} P.$$
9. If $A^{\mathsf{T}} = A$, step 8 gives $A'^{\mathsf{T}} = P^{\mathsf{T}} A P = A'$, so $A'$ is symmetric.
10. If $A^{\mathsf{T}} = -A$, step 8 gives $A'^{\mathsf{T}} = -P^{\mathsf{T}} A P = -A'$, so $A'$ is skew-symmetric.

Part 4 is about the property $x^{\mathsf{T}} A x = 0$.

11. For $x \in F^n$ put $z = Px$. The transpose of a product gives $z^{\mathsf{T}} = x^{\mathsf{T}} P^{\mathsf{T}}$, so
$$x^{\mathsf{T}} A' x = x^{\mathsf{T}} P^{\mathsf{T}} A P x = z^{\mathsf{T}} A z.$$
12. By the hypothesis of part 4, $z^{\mathsf{T}} A z = 0$. So $x^{\mathsf{T}} A' x = 0$ for every $x \in F^n$.

Part 5 is about the determinant.

13. By the [[multiplicativity of the determinant|04CPJ6AF00]] and the [[determinant of the transpose|0ZZQ2E1MTE]],
$$\det(P^{\mathsf{T}} A P) = \det(P^{\mathsf{T}}) \det A \det P = (\det P)^2 \det A.$$

Steps 1 to 13 prove all five parts of the statement.

## Idea

Congruence is built from multiplication by invertible matrices, and such multiplications can be undone and composed.
That gives the three conditions of an equivalence relation, with $P^{-1}$ for symmetry and $PQ$ for transitivity.
Each invariant then follows from one rule for products: the rank rule, the transpose rule, the substitution $z = Px$, and the product rule for determinants.
The transpose rule is what makes $P^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $P$ fit together, since it reverses the order of the factors.

## Variants

Part 1 also follows from the [[change of basis rule|0SPYAGE7T0]].
Two matrices are congruent exactly when they are the matrices of one bilinear form in two bases, and "being the matrix of the same form" is an equivalence relation.
Parts 2 to 4 can be proved in the same way, as statements about the form: its rank, its symmetry and the values $B(v, v)$ do not mention a basis at all.
