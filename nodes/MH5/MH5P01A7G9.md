## Statement

Let $k$ be a field.
For a [[Lie algebra|5JJYBJ6A8K]] $L$ write $L' = [L, L]$ for the span of all brackets.

1. Every Lie algebra of dimension $1$ over $k$ is [[abelian|N43CM5H99K]].
2. A Lie algebra of dimension $2$ over $k$ is either abelian or isomorphic to the Lie algebra with basis $x, y$ and $[x, y] = y$.
3. Let $L$ be a Lie algebra of dimension $3$ over $\C$. Then $L$ is isomorphic to exactly one of the following.
   1. The abelian Lie algebra $\C^3$.
   2. The [[Heisenberg algebra|HBVZGRKCWQ]], the case $\dim L' = 1$ and $L' \subseteq Z(L)$, where $Z(L)$ is the [[center|JJ9WQWCP9G]].
   3. The direct sum of the nonabelian $2$-dimensional Lie algebra and $\C$, the case $\dim L' = 1$ and $L' \not\subseteq Z(L)$.
   4. A Lie algebra $L_\mu$ with basis $x, y, z$, $[y, z] = 0$, $[x, y] = y$, $[x, z] = \mu z$, for some $\mu \in \C$ with $\mu \ne 0$; here $L_\mu \cong L_\nu$ if and only if $\nu = \mu$ or $\nu = 1/\mu$.
   5. The Lie algebra with basis $x, y, z$, $[y, z] = 0$, $[x, y] = y$, $[x, z] = y + z$.
   6. The [[special linear Lie algebra|1ZAT1Z967A]] $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\C)$, the case $L' = L$.

The cases 4 and 5 are those with $\dim L' = 2$.

## Remarks

In dimension $2$, if $L$ is not abelian then $L'$ is a line $k y$; choosing $x$ with $[x, y] \ne 0$ and rescaling gives $[x, y] = y$.
In dimension $3$ the proof splits by $\dim L'$.
If $\dim L' = 2$, one shows that $L'$ is abelian and that $\operatorname{ad} x$ acts on $L'$ by an invertible map for $x \notin L'$.
Its Jordan form, up to scaling $x$, gives cases 4 and 5.
If $L' = L$, one shows that some $\operatorname{ad} h$ has a nonzero eigenvalue, and its eigenvectors give an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple that spans $L$.
Over $\R$ the list is longer, since $\operatorname{ad} x$ may have non-real eigenvalues and $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\R)$ and $\mathfrak{so}_3(\R)$ are not isomorphic; the real case is the [[Bianchi classification|Q86TANYYX4]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

In small dimensions one can list all Lie algebras completely.
In dimension one or two there are only two kinds: the trivial abelian one and one other in dimension two.
In dimension three over the complex numbers there are a few more, including the Heisenberg algebra and $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, plus one family depending on a number $\mu$.
These lists give a supply of test examples for the general theory.

### L2 human

The invariant that organizes the proof is the derived algebra $L' = [L, L]$ and its relation to the center.
The solvable cases, with $L' \ne L$, reduce to linear algebra: a $2$-dimensional abelian ideal and one operator acting on it.
The case $L' = L$ forces $L \cong \mathfrak{sl}_2$, the smallest simple Lie algebra.
In higher dimensions the lists grow quickly and contain families with several parameters.
