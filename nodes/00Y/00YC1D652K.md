## Summary

A subspace is a smaller vector space that sits inside a larger one and uses the same addition and scaling.
In the plane and in space, the subspaces are the flat pieces through the origin: the origin itself, the lines and planes through it, and the whole space.
In exact terms, let $V$ be a [[vector space|000AYPA9AJ]] over a field $F$.
A *linear subspace* of $V$ is a subset $W \subseteq V$ that contains $0$ and is closed under the two operations:
$$0 \in W, \qquad u + v \in W \text{ and } av \in W \quad \text{for all } u, v \in W, \ a \in F.$$
The [[definition|0EPTJA5QV4]] states these three conditions, and the [[subspace test|Z5XKYXTC0K]] packs them into one: $W$ is nonempty and $u + av \in W$ for all $u, v \in W$ and $a \in F$.

## Motivation

Many vector spaces are born inside larger ones.
The solutions of a [[homogeneous system|04KGXJG1A2]] $Ax = 0$ lie in $F^n$, the polynomials of degree at most $2$ lie among all polynomials, and the continuous functions lie among all functions.
Each of these sets is a vector space in its own right, and we want to know that without checking eight axioms each time.
The axioms that are identities, such as $u + v = v + u$, already hold in the larger space, so they hold in every subset.
What can go wrong is that a sum or a multiple leaves the subset, and the subspace conditions test exactly that.

Subspaces are also the shapes that linear algebra cares about.
The kernel and the image of every [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] are subspaces, and the later pages on span, bases and dimension describe subspaces by a few vectors and one number.

## Notation

We write $W \le V$ or $W \subseteq V$ for "$W$ is a subspace of $V$", and the reader must tell from the context whether $\subseteq$ means a subspace or only a subset.
The *zero subspace* (or *trivial subspace*) is $\{0\}$, and a *proper subspace* is a subspace $W \ne V$.
Some books say "vector subspace" or "linear manifold".
In Mathlib, a subspace of `V` over `F` is a term of type `Submodule F V`.

## Remarks

### A first check

Take the plane $W = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x - 2y + z = 0\}$.
The origin lies in $W$, since $0 - 0 + 0 = 0$.
Let $u = (x, y, z)$ and $v = (x', y', z')$ lie in $W$, and let $a$ be a real number.
The vector $u + av$ has entries $x + ax'$, $y + ay'$ and $z + az'$, so we put them into the equation:
$$(x + ax') - 2(y + ay') + (z + az') = (x - 2y + z) + a(x' - 2y' + z') = 0 + a \cdot 0 = 0.$$
So $u + av \in W$, and the subspace test shows that $W$ is a subspace.
The same computation works for the line $\{(t, 2t) : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$, because $(s, 2s) + a(t, 2t) = (s + at, 2(s + at))$.

### Solution sets of homogeneous systems

The plane above is the solution set of one homogeneous equation, and the argument works for every homogeneous system.
For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$, the [[null space|0PHDYNR24D]] $\{x \in F^n : Ax = 0\}$ is a subspace of $F^n$, because
$$A0 = 0, \qquad A(x + ay) = Ax + aAy = 0 + a \cdot 0 = 0.$$
For example, $A = (1 \ 1 \ 1)$ gives the plane $x + y + z = 0$, which contains $(1, -1, 0)$ and $(0, 1, -1)$ and also their sum $(1, 0, -1)$.
The right side must be $0$: the solutions of $x + y + z = 1$ form a parallel plane that misses the origin.
In fact every subspace of $F^n$ is the null space of some matrix, a fact that the sub-area on duality explains.

### More examples

Every vector space $V$ has the two subspaces $\{0\}$ and $V$.
The polynomials of degree at most $n$ form a subspace of the [[space of polynomials|0JGZCDT5DN]] $F[x]$, since a sum or a multiple of such polynomials has degree at most $n$ again.
The continuous functions on $[0, 1]$ form a subspace of the [[space of all real functions|0GZB2SN0PH]] on $[0, 1]$, because sums and multiples of continuous functions are continuous.
The polynomials with no constant term form a [[proper subspace of the same infinite dimension|DVEZQ8SP64]] as $F[x]$, which cannot happen in finite dimension.

### What it is not

Each of the three conditions can fail on its own, and a small picture in $\mathbb{R}^2$ shows each failure.
The line $\{(t, 1) : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ misses the origin, so it fails the first condition.
The cross formed by the two axes, $\{(x, y) : xy = 0\}$, contains $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ but not their sum $(1, 1)$, so it fails closure under addition.
The first quadrant $\{(x, y) : x \ge 0, \ y \ge 0\}$ contains $(1, 1)$ but not $(-1)(1, 1)$, so it fails closure under scaling.
The lattice $\mathbb{Z}^2$ fails in the same way, since $\tfrac{1}{2}(1, 0) \notin \mathbb{Z}^2$.
Among the $2 \times 2$ matrices, the [[singular matrices do not form a subspace|PDNHFSACCV]], because two singular matrices can add up to $I$.

### The rules you can use

A subspace with the operations of $V$ is itself a vector space over $F$; this is the content of the subspace test.
Any [[intersection of subspaces|0V33SPH7RR]] is a subspace, but the union of two subspaces is a subspace only when one contains the other.
The smallest subspace that contains two subspaces $U$ and $W$ is their [[sum|0JMTY8MF54]] $U + W$, and the smallest one that contains a set $S$ is its [[span|0DTY8B9HMS]].
In a finite-dimensional space, a subspace has [[dimension at most that of the whole space|0QW084460E]], with equality only for the whole space.

### Watch out

The zero vector is the first thing to check, and it settles many questions at once.
A line or plane that does not pass through the origin is an [[affine subspace|0PYW26VNT6]], a shifted copy of a subspace, but not a linear subspace.
Watch out also for sets that pass one closure test and fail the other.
The set $\{(x, y) : x^2 = y^2\}$ is the union of the lines $y = x$ and $y = -x$, so it contains $0$ and is closed under scaling.
But $(1, 1) + (1, -1) = (2, 0)$, and $2^2 \ne 0^2$, so it is not closed under addition.

### History

Grassmann studied the quantities spanned by finitely many given ones in his *Ausdehnungslehre* of 1844, and in today's language these are subspaces.
Peano gave the first axiomatic definition of a real vector space in 1888.

### Try it

First, is $\{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x + y + z = 1\}$ a subspace?
No, because $0 + 0 + 0 = 0 \ne 1$, so the origin is not in the set.

Second, is the set of polynomials $p$ of degree at most $3$ with $p(0) = p(1)$ a subspace?
Yes: the zero polynomial is in it, and if $p(0) = p(1)$ and $q(0) = q(1)$, then $(p + aq)(0) = p(1) + aq(1) = (p + aq)(1)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A subspace is a smaller vector space inside a bigger one.
You cannot leave it by adding two of its vectors or by stretching one of them.
In the plane, take the line through the origin with slope $2$.
The arrows $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 4)$ lie on it, and so does their sum $(3, 6)$.
The line $y = 2x + 1$ is different: it does not pass through the origin, so it is not a subspace.

### L2 human

Here is a typical question: do the $2 \times 2$ real matrices with trace $0$ form a subspace of $M_2(\mathbb{R})$?
The trace of $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ is $a + d$, and the zero matrix has trace $0$.
For two matrices $A$ and $B$ and a real number $t$, the diagonal entries of $A + tB$ are $a + ta'$ and $d + td'$, so
$$\operatorname{tr}(A + tB) = (a + d) + t(a' + d') = \operatorname{tr} A + t \operatorname{tr} B.$$
If both traces are $0$, the right side is $0$, so the set passes the subspace test.
Compare the matrices with determinant $0$: the determinant is not linear, and $\operatorname{diag}(1, 0) + \operatorname{diag}(0, 1) = I$ has determinant $1$.
The lesson is that a set cut out by a linear condition, such as a trace or a value $p(1)$, is a subspace, while a nonlinear condition usually fails.

### L3 human

The subspaces of $V$, ordered by inclusion, form a [[complete lattice|D7A0ZGZT5S]], with intersection as meet and sum as join.
This lattice is [[modular|QX7WZ92QAK]], but it is not distributive when $\dim V \ge 2$, as [[three lines in the plane|38AKN9MG6J]] shows.
Over a ring in place of a field, the same definition gives the submodules of a [[module|0NZ8JA25WK]], and Mathlib uses one structure, `Submodule`, for both.
Subspaces return as kernels and images of linear maps, as quotient spaces $V/W$, and as the invariant subspaces of the theory of eigenvalues.
