## Summary

A linear subspace of a [[vector space|000AYPA9AJ]] $V$ over a field $F$ is a subset $W \subseteq V$ with three properties.
It contains $0$, and it is closed under addition and under scalar multiplication.
With the operations of $V$, such a set $W$ is a vector space over $F$.
The precise conditions are in the [[definition|0EPTJA5QV4]], and the [[subspace test|Z5XKYXTC0K]] gives a short way to check them.
Subspaces are the lines, planes and other flat pieces through the origin, and they are the solution sets of homogeneous linear systems.

## Motivation

Many vector spaces appear inside larger ones.
The solutions of $Ax = 0$ lie in $F^n$, and the polynomials of degree at most $2$ lie in all polynomials.
The continuous functions lie in all functions.
In each case, it is a waste to check the eight axioms again, because they already hold in the larger space.
Only closure must be checked, and the subspace concept records exactly this.

## Notation

We write $W \le V$ or $W \subseteq V$ for "$W$ is a subspace of $V$".
The *trivial subspace* is $\set{0}$, and a *proper subspace* is a subspace $W \ne V$.
In Mathlib, a subspace is a term of type `Submodule F V`.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. In each space $V$, the sets $\set{0}$ and $V$ are subspaces: $0 + 0 = 0$ and $a0 = 0$, and $V$ is closed by definition.
2. The line $L = \set{(t, 2t) : t \in \R}$ in $\R^2$: it contains $(0, 0)$ for $t = 0$; $(s, 2s) + (t, 2t) = (s + t, 2(s + t)) \in L$; and $a(t, 2t) = (at, 2(at)) \in L$.
3. The [[null space|0PHDYNR24D]] $\set{x \in F^n : Ax = 0}$ of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$: $A0 = 0$, $A(x + y) = Ax + Ay = 0$, and $A(ax) = a(Ax) = 0$. For $A = (1\ 1\ 1)$ it is the plane $x + y + z = 0$ in $\R^3$.
4. The polynomials of degree at most $n$ form a subspace of the [[space of polynomials|0JGZCDT5DN]] $F[x]$.
5. The continuous functions form a subspace of the [[space of all functions|0GZB2SN0PH]] $\R^{[0, 1]}$.

### Non-examples

1. The line $\set{(t, 1) : t \in \R}$ fails condition 1, because it does not contain $(0, 0)$.
2. The union of the two axes, $\set{(x, y) \in \R^2 : xy = 0}$, fails condition 2: $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ lie in it, but $(1, 1)$ does not.
3. The first quadrant $\set{(x, y) : x \ge 0, y \ge 0}$ fails condition 3: $(1, 1)$ lies in it, but $(-1)(1, 1) = (-1, -1)$ does not.
4. The set $\Z^2 \subseteq \R^2$ fails condition 3: $\tfrac12 (1, 0) \notin \Z^2$.
5. The singular $2 \times 2$ matrices fail condition 2; see [[this non-example|PDNHFSACCV]].

### Basic properties

1. A subspace is a vector space with the restricted operations; see the [[subspace test|Z5XKYXTC0K]].
2. An intersection of subspaces is a subspace.
   A union of two subspaces is a subspace only when one contains the other; see [[intersection and union of subspaces|0V33SPH7RR]].
3. The smallest subspace that contains a set $S$ is its [[span|0DTY8B9HMS]], and the smallest one that contains $U \cup W$ is the [[sum|0JMTY8MF54]] $U + W$.
4. If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $\dim W \le \dim V$, with equality only for $W = V$; see [[the dimension of a subspace|0QW084460E]].
5. The kernel and the image of a [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]] are subspaces.

### History

Grassmann worked with the spans of finitely many "extensive quantities" in 1844, and these spans are subspaces.
Peano gave the axiomatic definition of a vector space in 1888.
The test "closed under linear combinations" became standard in textbooks of the twentieth century.

### Common errors

1. A subspace must contain $0$; a line or plane that misses the origin is an affine subspace, not a linear subspace.
2. Closure under addition alone is not enough, as the first quadrant shows.
   Closure under scaling alone is not enough, as the union of the axes shows.
3. A subset is a subspace only for the operations of $V$; a subset with other operations is a different question.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A subspace is a smaller vector space inside a bigger one.
We cannot leave it by adding its vectors or by scaling them.
In space, the subspaces are the origin, the lines and planes through the origin, and the whole space.

### L2 human

To check that $W$ is a subspace, we use the [[subspace test|Z5XKYXTC0K]]: $W$ is nonempty, and $u + av \in W$ for all $u, v \in W$ and $a \in F$.
Example: $W = \set{(x, y, z) \in \R^3 : x - 2y + z = 0}$.

1. $(0, 0, 0) \in W$, because $0 - 0 + 0 = 0$.
2. Let $u = (x, y, z)$ and $v = (x', y', z')$ be in $W$, and $a \in \R$.
3. Then $(x + ax') - 2(y + ay') + (z + az') = (x - 2y + z) + a(x' - 2y' + z') = 0 + a \cdot 0 = 0$.
4. So $u + av \in W$, and $W$ is a subspace.

The equation is homogeneous; with $x - 2y + z = 1$, step 1 fails.

### L3 human

The subspaces of $V$, ordered by inclusion, form a complete lattice, with meet $\cap$ and join $+$.
This lattice is modular, but it is not distributive when $\dim V \ge 2$.
For modules over a ring, the same definition gives submodules; see the [[module|0NZ8JA25WK]] concept.
In Mathlib, `Submodule F V` carries this lattice structure, and `W : Submodule F V` coerces to a type with its own module instance.
