## Proof

Here $^*$ is the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]], and $\C^k$ carries the inner product $\ip{x}{y} = y^* x$.

1. A square matrix is [[unitary|0D6RMJ10M9]] exactly when its columns form an [[orthonormal family|09VYHEF2VT]]. Indeed the $(i, j)$ entry of $W^* W$ is $w_i^* w_j = \ip{w_j}{w_i}$ for the columns $w_i$ of $W$.
2. By [[the Gram matrix result|0AM050KCAW]], $A^* A$ is a positive semidefinite Hermitian $n \times n$ matrix.
3. By the [[spectral theorem|0DGZBQWZWN]], $A^* A = V_0 D_0 V_0^*$ with $V_0$ unitary and $D_0$ real diagonal.
4. Choose a [[permutation matrix|0Q5SM60HTH]] $\Pi$ such that $D = \Pi^T D_0 \Pi$ is diagonal with entries $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n$. This permutes the diagonal entries of $D_0$ into decreasing order.
5. The matrix $\Pi$ is real with $\Pi^T \Pi = I$, so it is unitary. By the [[one-sided inverse theorem|0753Y2X5K6]], $\Pi \Pi^T = I$. Let $V = V_0 \Pi$. It is unitary, since $V^* V = \Pi^T V_0^* V_0 \Pi = \Pi^T \Pi = I$.
6. Then $V D V^* = V_0 \Pi \Pi^T D_0 \Pi \Pi^T V_0^* = A^* A$. Also $V V^* = I$ by the one-sided inverse theorem.
7. Let $v_1, \dots, v_n$ be the columns of $V$. They are orthonormal by step 1. From $A^* A V = V D$ we get $A^* A v_i = \lambda_i v_i$.
8. For each $i$, $\lambda_i = \lambda_i v_i^* v_i = v_i^* A^* A v_i = (A v_i)^* (A v_i) \ge 0$.
9. Since $D = V^* (A^* A) V$ and $V^* = V^{-1}$, the matrices $A^* A$ and $D$ have the [[same characteristic polynomial|0TYMASG33Y]]. So $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n$ are the eigenvalues of $A^* A$, with multiplicity.
10. By the definition of [[singular values|0K2TX12X58]], they are $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$, using the [[square roots of nonnegative reals|068BZBXE0C]]. So $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_n \ge 0$.
11. Let $r$ be the number of $i$ with $\lambda_i > 0$. Then $\lambda_i > 0$ for $i \le r$, and $\lambda_i = 0$ for $i > r$.
12. For $i \le r$, let $u_i = A v_i / \sigma_i \in \C^m$. By step 7, $u_i^* u_j = v_i^* A^* A v_j / (\sigma_i \sigma_j) = \lambda_j\, v_i^* v_j / (\sigma_i \sigma_j)$.
13. This is $0$ for $i \ne j$, and $\lambda_i / \sigma_i^2 = 1$ for $i = j$. So $u_1, \dots, u_r$ is an orthonormal family in $\C^m$.
14. By the [[existence of an orthonormal basis|0Y85GYEGSF]], it extends to an orthonormal basis $u_1, \dots, u_m$ of $\C^m$. This basis has $m$ vectors, by the [[dimension theorem|0WK0Y8RH1M]] and [[the dimension of the coordinate space|0QK7FPE6VE]]. So $r \le m$.
15. Let $U$ be the $m \times m$ matrix with columns $u_1, \dots, u_m$. By step 1, $U$ is unitary.
16. For $i > r$, step 8 gives $(A v_i)^* (A v_i) = \lambda_i = 0$. A vector $y$ with $y^* y = \sum_k \abs{y_k}^2 = 0$ is zero, so $A v_i = 0$.
17. Let $\Sigma$ be the $m \times n$ matrix with $\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i$ for $i \le \min(m, n)$ and all other entries $0$.
18. Column $j$ of $U \Sigma$ is $\sum_i \Sigma_{ij} u_i$. This is $\sigma_j u_j$ if $j \le m$, and $0$ if $j > m$.
19. Column $j$ of $AV$ is $A v_j$. For $j \le r$, we have $j \le m$ and $A v_j = \sigma_j u_j$ by step 12.
20. For $j > r$, $A v_j = 0$ by step 16. Column $j$ of $U\Sigma$ is also $0$, since either $j > m$ or $\sigma_j = 0$.
21. By steps 18 to 20, $AV = U \Sigma$. By step 6, $A = A V V^* = U \Sigma V^*$.
22. The diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ are $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_{\min(m,n)} \ge 0$. If $m < n$, the remaining singular values $\sigma_i$ with $i > m$ are $0$, because $r \le m$.
23. So $A = U \Sigma V^*$ with $U$ and $V$ unitary, $\Sigma_{ij} = 0$ for $i \ne j$, and decreasing diagonal entries given by the singular values of $A$. This proves part 1.
24. Reduced form. For any $m \times n$ matrix $\Sigma$ with zero entries off the diagonal, column-row multiplication gives $U \Sigma V^* = \sum_{i \le p} \Sigma_{ii} u_i v_i^*$, with $p = \min(m, n)$.
25. By steps 11 and 22, $\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i = 0$ for $r < i \le p$. So $A = \sum_{i \le r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^*$, and this sum is $U_r \Sigma_r V_r^*$ by the same column-row rule.
26. Four subspaces. For $x \in \C^n$, step 25 gives $Ax = \sum_{i \le r} \sigma_i (v_i^* x) u_i$. So every $Ax$ lies in the span of $u_1, \dots, u_r$. Also $u_i = A(v_i / \sigma_i)$ lies in the [[column space|0F0RPPJ2TT]].
27. So the column space of $A$ is the span of $u_1, \dots, u_r$. These vectors are orthonormal by step 13, so they are an orthonormal basis, and $r = \rank A$ by the definition of the [[rank|0EWQEYRVA8]].
28. If $Ax = 0$, then $\sigma_i (v_i^* x) = 0$ for $i \le r$ by step 26, since the $u_i$ are [[independent|AMFJCC9M7G]]. So $v_i^* x = 0$ for $i \le r$.
29. By [[coordinates in an orthonormal basis|09DZ8CD1AK]], $x = \sum_i (v_i^* x) v_i$. So $Ax = 0$ if and only if $x$ lies in the span of $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$, using step 26 for the converse. So these vectors are an orthonormal basis of the [[null space|0PQ2GGY1QV]] of $A$.
30. Taking $^*$ of step 21 gives $A^* = V \Sigma^T U^*$, where $\Sigma^T$ is $n \times m$ with the same nonzero diagonal entries $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r$. Steps 24 to 29 used only this form, so they apply to $A^*$ with $U$ and $V$ exchanged.
31. So $v_1, \dots, v_r$ are an orthonormal basis of the column space of $A^*$, and $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$ of the null space of $A^*$. This proves part 3. Part 2 follows from step 25 and from $\sigma_r > 0 = \sigma_{r+1}$, since the rank is $r$ by step 27.
32. Uniqueness of $\Sigma$. Let $A = U' \Sigma' V'^*$ be any decomposition as in part 1. Then $A^* A = V' \Sigma'^T \Sigma' V'^*$, and $\Sigma'^T \Sigma'$ is the $n \times n$ diagonal matrix with entries $\Sigma_{ii}'^2$ for $i \le p$ and $0$ after.
33. As in step 9, these entries are the eigenvalues of $A^* A$ with multiplicity. Since the $\Sigma'_{ii}$ are nonnegative and decreasing, $\Sigma'_{ii} = \sigma_i$. So $\Sigma' = \Sigma$.
34. Real case. If $A$ is real, then $A^T A$ is real symmetric, and the real [[spectral theorem|0DGZBQWZWN]] gives a real orthogonal $V_0$ in step 3. Then $V$, the $u_i$ of step 12, and their completion in step 14 can all be taken real. This proves part 4.

## Idea

The matrix $A^* A$ is Hermitian, so it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $v_i$.
The images $A v_i$ are orthogonal, with lengths $\sigma_i$, and normalising them and completing to an orthonormal basis gives $U$.
The four subspaces can be read off the rank-one expansion $A = \sum_{i \le r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^*$ and the same expansion of $A^*$.

## Variants

- The orthogonality of the $A v_i$ and the rank count are also proved in [[the theorem on singular values and rank|6VXM1T8PG7]].
- For a square $A$, the SVD also follows from the [[polar decomposition|0J2C7BDJQA]] $A = WP$: diagonalize $P = V \Sigma V^*$, and put $U = WV$.
- Another proof maximizes $\norm{Ax}$ over unit vectors $x$ to find $v_1$, and then repeats on the orthogonal complement. This proof also works for compact operators.
