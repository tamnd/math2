## Proof

We diagonalize the Hermitian matrix $A^* A$ to get $V$, we normalize the images $A v_i$ to get the first columns of $U$, and we complete them to an orthonormal basis.
Throughout, $^*$ is the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]], and $\C^k$ carries the inner product $\ip{x}{y} = y^* x$.

1. A square matrix $W$ is [[unitary|0D6RMJ10M9]] exactly when its columns $w_1, \dots, w_k$ form an [[orthonormal family|09VYHEF2VT]]. The reason is that the entry $(i, j)$ of $W^* W$ is $w_i^* w_j = \ip{w_j}{w_i}$.
2. By [[the Gram matrix result|0AM050KCAW]], $A^* A$ is a positive semidefinite Hermitian $n \times n$ matrix.
3. By the [[spectral theorem|0DGZBQWZWN]], $A^* A = V_0 D_0 V_0^*$ with $V_0$ unitary and $D_0$ real diagonal.
4. Choose a [[permutation matrix|0Q5SM60HTH]] $\Pi$ such that $D = \Pi^{\mathsf{T}} D_0 \Pi$ is diagonal with entries $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n$. Conjugating by $\Pi$ only reorders the diagonal entries of $D_0$.
5. The matrix $\Pi$ is real with $\Pi^{\mathsf{T}} \Pi = I$, so $\Pi \Pi^{\mathsf{T}} = I$ by the [[one-sided inverse theorem|0753Y2X5K6]]. Put $V = V_0 \Pi$; it is unitary, since $V^* V = \Pi^{\mathsf{T}} V_0^* V_0 \Pi = \Pi^{\mathsf{T}} \Pi = I$.
6. Then $V D V^* = V_0 \Pi \Pi^{\mathsf{T}} D_0 \Pi \Pi^{\mathsf{T}} V_0^* = V_0 D_0 V_0^* = A^* A$. Also $V V^* = I$, by the one-sided inverse theorem.
7. Let $v_1, \dots, v_n$ be the columns of $V$. They are orthonormal by step 1, and comparing columns in $A^* A V = V D$ gives $A^* A v_i = \lambda_i v_i$.
8. For each $i$, we have $\lambda_i = \lambda_i v_i^* v_i = v_i^* A^* A v_i = (A v_i)^* (A v_i) \ge 0$.
9. Since $D = V^{-1} (A^* A) V$, the matrices $A^* A$ and $D$ have the [[same characteristic polynomial|0TYMASG33Y]]. So $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n$ are the eigenvalues of $A^* A$ with multiplicity.
10. By the definition of [[singular values|0K2TX12X58]] and the [[square roots of nonnegative reals|068BZBXE0C]], the singular values are $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$, and $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_n \ge 0$.
11. Let $r$ be the number of $i$ with $\lambda_i > 0$. Since the $\lambda_i$ decrease, $\lambda_i > 0$ for $i \le r$ and $\lambda_i = 0$ for $i > r$.
12. For $i \le r$, put $u_i = A v_i / \sigma_i \in \C^m$. By step 7,
$$u_i^* u_j = \frac{v_i^* A^* A v_j}{\sigma_i \sigma_j} = \frac{\lambda_j \, v_i^* v_j}{\sigma_i \sigma_j}.$$
13. This is $0$ for $i \ne j$, and $\lambda_i / \sigma_i^2 = 1$ for $i = j$. So $u_1, \dots, u_r$ is an orthonormal family in $\C^m$.
14. By the [[existence of an orthonormal basis|0Y85GYEGSF]], this family extends to an orthonormal basis $u_1, \dots, u_m$ of $\C^m$. The basis has $m$ vectors by the [[dimension theorem|0WK0Y8RH1M]] and [[the dimension of the coordinate space|0QK7FPE6VE]], so $r \le m$.
15. Let $U$ be the $m \times m$ matrix with columns $u_1, \dots, u_m$. By step 1, $U$ is unitary.
16. For $i > r$, step 8 gives $(A v_i)^* (A v_i) = \lambda_i = 0$. A vector $y$ with $y^* y = \sum_k \abs{y_k}^2 = 0$ is zero, so $A v_i = 0$.
17. Let $\Sigma$ be the $m \times n$ matrix with $\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i$ for $i \le p = \min(m, n)$ and all other entries $0$.
18. Column $j$ of $U \Sigma$ is $\sum_i \Sigma_{ij} u_i$, which is $\sigma_j u_j$ when $j \le m$ and $0$ when $j > m$.
19. Column $j$ of $AV$ is $A v_j$. For $j \le r$ we have $j \le m$ by step 14, and $A v_j = \sigma_j u_j$ by step 12.
20. For $j > r$, $A v_j = 0$ by step 16, and column $j$ of $U\Sigma$ is also $0$, since either $j > m$ or $\sigma_j = 0$.
21. Steps 18 to 20 give $AV = U \Sigma$. Multiplying on the right by $V^*$ and using step 6 gives $A = A V V^* = U \Sigma V^*$.
22. The diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ are $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_p \ge 0$. If $m < n$, the singular values $\sigma_i$ with $i > m$ are $0$, because $r \le m$, so no nonzero singular value is missing from $\Sigma$. This proves part 1.
23. Reduced form. For any $m \times n$ matrix $\Sigma$ with zeros off the diagonal, the column-row rule for products gives $U \Sigma V^* = \sum_{i \le p} \Sigma_{ii} u_i v_i^*$.
24. By step 11, $\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i = 0$ for $r < i \le p$. So $A = \sum_{i \le r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^*$, and the same column-row rule writes this sum as $U_r \Sigma_r V_r^*$.
25. Four subspaces. For $x \in \C^n$, step 24 gives $Ax = \sum_{i \le r} \sigma_i (v_i^* x) u_i$. So every $Ax$ lies in the span of $u_1, \dots, u_r$, and each $u_i = A(v_i / \sigma_i)$ lies in the [[column space|0F0RPPJ2TT]].
26. So the column space of $A$ is the span of $u_1, \dots, u_r$. These vectors are orthonormal by step 13, so they are an orthonormal basis, and $r = \rank A$ by the definition of the [[rank|0EWQEYRVA8]].
27. If $Ax = 0$, then the [[independence|AMFJCC9M7G]] of $u_1, \dots, u_r$ and step 25 give $\sigma_i (v_i^* x) = 0$, so $v_i^* x = 0$ for $i \le r$.
28. By [[coordinates in an orthonormal basis|09DZ8CD1AK]], $x = \sum_i (v_i^* x) v_i$, so step 27 puts $x$ in the span of $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$. Conversely, step 25 shows that every vector of this span is sent to $0$. So these vectors are an orthonormal basis of the [[null space|0PQ2GGY1QV]] of $A$.
29. Taking $^*$ of step 21 gives $A^* = V \Sigma^{\mathsf{T}} U^*$, where $\Sigma^{\mathsf{T}}$ is $n \times m$ with the same nonzero diagonal entries $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r$. Steps 23 to 28 used only a decomposition of this form, so they apply to $A^*$ with the roles of $U$ and $V$ exchanged.
30. So $v_1, \dots, v_r$ are an orthonormal basis of the column space of $A^*$, and $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$ are one of the null space of $A^*$. This proves part 3, and part 2 follows from step 24 together with $r = \rank A$ from step 26.
31. Uniqueness of $\Sigma$. Let $A = U' \Sigma' V'^*$ be any decomposition as in part 1. Then $A^* A = V' (\Sigma'^{\mathsf{T}} \Sigma') V'^*$, and $\Sigma'^{\mathsf{T}} \Sigma'$ is the $n \times n$ diagonal matrix with entries $(\Sigma'_{ii})^2$ for $i \le p$ and $0$ after.
32. As in step 9, these entries are the eigenvalues of $A^* A$ with multiplicity. The $\Sigma'_{ii}$ are nonnegative and decrease, so $\Sigma'_{ii} = \sigma_i$ for each $i \le p$, and $\Sigma' = \Sigma$.
33. Real case. If $A$ is real, then $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ is real symmetric, and the real [[spectral theorem|0DGZBQWZWN]] gives a real orthogonal $V_0$ in step 3. Then $V$ is real, the $u_i$ of step 12 are real, and the completion in step 14 can be done inside $\R^m$, which proves part 4.

## Idea

The matrix $A^* A$ is Hermitian, so it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $v_i$.
The matrix $A$ sends these to orthogonal vectors $A v_i$ of lengths $\sigma_i$, and normalizing the nonzero ones gives the first $r$ columns of $U$.
Any orthonormal completion gives the rest of $U$, because the remaining $v_i$ go to $0$ and so never meet those columns.
The four subspaces can then be read off the rank-one expansion $A = \sum_{i \le r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^*$ and the same expansion of $A^*$.

## Variants

The orthogonality of the $A v_i$ and the count of the rank are also proved in [[the theorem on singular values and rank|6VXM1T8PG7]].

For a square $A$, the SVD also follows from the [[polar decomposition|0J2C7BDJQA]] $A = WP$.
Diagonalize the positive semidefinite factor as $P = V \Sigma V^*$ and put $U = WV$, so that $A = W V \Sigma V^* = U \Sigma V^*$.

A third proof uses no eigenvalues at first.
It maximizes $\norm{Ax}$ over unit vectors $x$ to find $v_1$ and $\sigma_1$, shows that $A$ maps the orthogonal complement of $v_1$ into the orthogonal complement of $A v_1$, and repeats on the smaller spaces.
This proof by maximization also works for compact operators on Hilbert spaces.
