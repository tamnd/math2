## Example

Suppose that a calculus course defines $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ geometrically, as the coordinates of the point at arc length $x$ on the unit circle.
A student computes the [[limit of $\frac{\sin x}{x}$|01QSSKS5C1]] as follows.

1. As $x \to 0$, both $\sin x \to 0$ and $x \to 0$.
2. By [[L'Hôpital's rule|0JWT4PPQ7B]], $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{1}$.
3. The cosine is continuous, so the last limit is $\cos 0 = 1$.

We check whether this argument is [[circular|JPHE53PABT]] in the given course.

1. Step 2 needs the [[derivative of sine|00R4MWW532]]: $\sin' = \cos$, or at least $\sin'(x)$ for $x$ near $0$.
2. In the course, the proof of $\sin' = \cos$ starts from the addition formula: $\frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} = \sin x \cdot \frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x \cdot \frac{\sin h}{h}$.
3. As $h \to 0$, the right side tends to $\cos x$ only if $\frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1$ and $\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} \to 0$.
4. Even the single value $\sin'(0)$ is the claim: $\sin'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin h - \sin 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin h}{h}$.
5. So step 2 of the argument uses a result whose proof needs the limit that the argument computes. The argument is circular.

The conclusion is true, and each step is a correct use of a true result.
The error is in the order: the argument cannot be the first proof of the limit in this course.

A correct first proof uses areas and the [[squeeze theorem|0XMF00T4XQ]].

1. Let $0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$. In the unit circle, the triangle with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(\cos x, \sin x)$ lies inside the sector of angle $x$, and the sector lies inside the right triangle with legs $1$ and $\tan x$.
2. Their areas give $\frac{\sin x}{2} \le \frac{x}{2} \le \frac{\tan x}{2}$.
3. Divide by $\frac{\sin x}{2} > 0$ and take reciprocals: $\cos x \le \frac{\sin x}{x} \le 1$.
4. The same inequality holds for $-\frac{\pi}{2} < x < 0$, because both $\cos x$ and $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ are even functions.
5. As $x \to 0$, $\cos x \to 1$, so the squeeze theorem gives $\frac{\sin x}{x} \to 1$.

## Remarks

The example shows that circularity is a property of an argument relative to a chosen development, not of the argument alone.
With sine [[defined by its power series|VEVN4JA38E]], the same L'Hôpital computation is correct, because $\sin' = \cos$ then has a proof by term-by-term differentiation.
The area proof in its turn needs the formula $\frac{x}{2}$ for the area of a sector of angle $x$, and a careful course must prove that formula without the limit.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To use a tool, we must first build the tool.
L'Hôpital's rule needs the slope of the sine curve.
But the usual way to find that slope uses exactly the limit that we want.
So using the rule here is like proving that a bridge is safe by driving over it with the bridge already assumed safe.

### L2 human

We find the circle in the argument step by step.

1. List the results that the argument uses: L'Hôpital's rule, $\sin' = \cos$ near $0$, and the continuity of $\cos$.
2. For each one, find its proof in the course. L'Hôpital's rule is proved from the mean value theorem, and the continuity of $\cos$ from the geometry of the circle. Neither uses the limit.
3. The proof of $\sin' = \cos$ uses $\frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1$, which is the claim.
4. So the dependency graph has the cycle "limit, then $\sin'$, then limit".

The typical use of this check is to read an exam answer that uses a strong theorem for a basic fact, and to ask whether the theorem depends on the fact.
A trap is to think that the argument is wrong because L'Hôpital's rule does not apply; the rule applies, and the only error is the circle.
