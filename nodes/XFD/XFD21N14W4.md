## Example

A calculus course defines $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ geometrically, as the coordinates of the point at arc length $x$ on the unit circle.
On the exam, a student computes the [[limit of $\frac{\sin x}{x}$|01QSSKS5C1]] like this; is it a proof?

1. As $x \to 0$, both $\sin x \to 0$ and $x \to 0$.
2. By [[L'Hôpital's rule|0JWT4PPQ7B]], $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{1}$.
3. The cosine is continuous, so the last limit is $\cos 0 = 1$.

In this course the argument is [[circular|JPHE53PABT]], and step 2 is where the circle closes.
We trace it.

1. Step 2 needs the [[derivative of sine|00R4MWW532]], $\sin' = \cos$, at least near $0$.
2. The course proves $\sin' = \cos$ from the addition formula:
   $$\frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} = \sin x \cdot \frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x \cdot \frac{\sin h}{h}.$$
3. The right side tends to $\cos x$ only if $\frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1$ and $\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} \to 0$.
4. Even the single value $\sin'(0)$ is the claim, since by definition $\sin'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin h - \sin 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin h}{h}$.
5. So step 2 uses a result whose proof needs the limit being computed.

The conclusion is true, and each step applies a true result correctly.
The error is in the order: in this course the argument cannot be the first proof of the limit.

The correct first proof uses areas and the [[squeeze theorem|0XMF00T4XQ]].

1. Let $0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$. In the unit circle, the triangle with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(\cos x, \sin x)$ lies inside the sector of angle $x$, and the sector lies inside the right triangle with legs $1$ and $\tan x$.
2. Compare the three areas:
   $$\frac{\sin x}{2} \le \frac{x}{2} \le \frac{\tan x}{2}.$$
3. Divide by $\frac{\sin x}{2} > 0$ and take reciprocals: $\cos x \le \frac{\sin x}{x} \le 1$.
4. Both $\cos x$ and $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ are even functions, so the same bounds hold for $-\frac{\pi}{2} < x < 0$.
5. As $x \to 0$, $\cos x \to 1$, so the squeeze theorem gives $\frac{\sin x}{x} \to 1$.

## Remarks

Circularity belongs to an argument together with a chosen order of results, not to the argument alone.
With sine [[defined by its power series|VEVN4JA38E]], the same L'Hôpital computation is a correct proof, because $\sin' = \cos$ then comes from term-by-term differentiation.
The area proof has its own debt: it needs the area $\frac{x}{2}$ of a sector of angle $x$, and a careful course proves that formula without the limit.
Check the squeeze numerically at $x = 0.1$: $\cos 0.1 \approx 0.99500$, $\frac{\sin 0.1}{0.1} \approx 0.99833$, and both lie below $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To use a tool, you must first build the tool.
L'Hôpital's rule needs the slope of the sine curve.
But the usual way to find that slope uses exactly the limit we want.
Using the rule here is like proving that a bridge is safe by driving over it while assuming that it is safe.

### L2 human

We find the circle by listing what the argument uses and where each piece comes from.
It uses three results: L'Hôpital's rule, $\sin' = \cos$ near $0$, and the continuity of $\cos$.
L'Hôpital's rule is proved from the mean value theorem, and the continuity of $\cos$ from the geometry of the circle, so neither needs the limit.
The proof of $\sin' = \cos$ does need $\frac{\sin h}{h} \to 1$, which is the claim.
So the dependency graph has the cycle "limit, then $\sin'$, then limit".

The typical use of this check is to read an answer that quotes a strong theorem for a basic fact, and to ask whether the theorem rests on the fact.
Watch out: the rule does apply here, since its hypotheses hold; the only error is the circle.
