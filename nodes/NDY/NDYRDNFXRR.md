## Example

The primitive conjecture says: if each function $u_n \colon [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous and the series $s(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} u_n(x)$ converges for each $x$, then $s$ is continuous.
Cauchy published this claim in 1821, and we follow the [[method of proofs and refutations|79P9CTRN0V]] on it.

1. We give the proof.
   The partial sum $s_N = \sum_{n=0}^{N} u_n$ is a finite sum of continuous functions, so it is continuous.
   For a point $a$ and a number $\varepsilon > 0$ we split the error into three parts:
   $$|s(x) - s(a)| \le |s(x) - s_N(x)| + |s_N(x) - s_N(a)| + |s_N(a) - s(a)|.$$
   We choose $N$ so that the third part is less than $\varepsilon/3$, and then $\delta > 0$ so that the second part is less than $\varepsilon/3$ for $|x - a| < \delta$.
   The proof then says that the first part is also less than $\varepsilon/3$.
2. We take the series with $u_n(x) = x(1 - x)^n$, and each $u_n$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous.
3. For $0 < x \le 1$ the geometric sum gives
   $$s_N(x) = x \cdot \frac{1 - (1 - x)^{N+1}}{1 - (1 - x)} = 1 - (1 - x)^{N+1},$$
   and at $x = 0$ every term is $0$, so the same formula gives $s_N(0) = 0$.
4. For $0 < x \le 1$ we have $0 \le 1 - x < 1$, so $(1 - x)^{N+1} \to 0$ and $s(x) = 1$, while $s(0) = 0$.
5. So $s$ is $0$ at $0$ and $1$ on $(0, 1]$, it is not continuous at $0$, and the conjecture is false.
6. Proof analysis asks which part of step 1 fails at $a = 0$.
   The first part is $|s(x) - s_N(x)| = (1 - x)^{N+1}$ for $x$ in $(0, 1]$, and for a fixed $N$ it tends to $1$ as $x \to 0$.
   So no $\delta$ makes it less than $\varepsilon/3$ when $\varepsilon < 3$, and the guilty lemma is "one $N$ makes $|s(x) - s_N(x)|$ small for all $x$ near $a$ at once".
7. Lemma-incorporation adds the guilty lemma as a hypothesis: $\sup_{x \in [0, 1]} |s(x) - s_N(x)| \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$.
   This hypothesis is [[uniform convergence|08VZ57PT26]], and the improved theorem is the [[uniform limit theorem|0X9XT05JVC]].

## Remarks

The counterexample converges [[pointwise|0GFGK40582]] but not uniformly, because $\sup_{x \in [0, 1]} (1 - x)^{N+1} = 1$ for each $N$.
On an interval $[c, 1]$ with $c > 0$ the series does converge uniformly, since $(1 - x)^{N+1} \le (1 - c)^{N+1} \to 0$, so the sum is continuous there, as the improved theorem predicts.
The proof in step 1 is the [[epsilon over three argument|VXV7M0QDH6]], and its first estimate is exactly where uniformity is needed.

Abel gave a counterexample in 1826 with a Fourier series.
The series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{n+1} \frac{\sin nx}{n}$ equals $\frac{x}{2}$ for $-\pi < x < \pi$ and $0$ at $x = \pi$, so its sum jumps at $\pi$.
Seidel and Stokes later found the hidden condition independently.
Lakatos uses this history as a second case study of a concept generated by a proof.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Cauchy believed that adding up infinitely many unbroken curves always gives an unbroken curve.
The sum of the curves $x(1 - x)^n$ is $1$ everywhere except at $x = 0$, where it is $0$, so the sum jumps.
Cauchy's argument was right in every step but one.
That step assumed that the partial sums approach the total at the same speed everywhere.
Near $x = 0$ they approach it more and more slowly, and the repair is to ask for the same speed everywhere, which is called uniform convergence.

### L2 human

We can measure how badly uniformity fails near $0$.
With $N = 10$ and $x = 0.01$, the error is $s(x) - s_{10}(x) = 0.99^{11} \approx 0.895$.
With $N = 100$ and $x = 0.001$, it is $0.999^{101} \approx 0.904$, so a larger $N$ did not help.
In general, the point $x = \frac{1}{N+1}$ gives the error
$$\left( 1 - \frac{1}{N+1} \right)^{N+1},$$
which is $\frac{1}{4}$ for $N = 1$ and increases toward $\frac{1}{e} \approx 0.368$.
So $\sup_x |s(x) - s_N(x)| \ge \frac{1}{4}$ for every $N \ge 1$, and the convergence is not uniform.
To find a guilty lemma, compute the term of the proof that the counterexample makes large.
Watch out: convergence at each point does not bound the error near that point, and the proof needs control on a whole neighborhood.
