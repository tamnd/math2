## Example

Let $F$ be a field and let $M = F[x]$ be the [[polynomial ring|0SDC934R52]], as a module over itself.

1. $M$ is generated by $1$, so it is finitely generated, and it is [[free|0DZ9DKAKZR]] of rank $1$.
2. The polynomials $1, x, x^2, \dots$ are [[linearly independent|0CRMGPAH83]] over $F$, so $\dim_F M$ is infinite.
3. If $f \ne 0$, then $f \cdot 1 = f \ne 0$. So no nonzero polynomial kills $M$, and $M$ is not torsion.
4. For a finite-dimensional $V$ and linear $T$, the module $V_T$ has finite $F$-dimension and is killed by the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] $m_T$.
5. By steps 2 and 3, $M$ is not isomorphic to any $V_T$ with $V$ finite-dimensional.

## Remarks

The example shows why the free part $R^r$ of the structure theorem is $0$ for the modules that come from matrices.
$M$ is still of the form $V_T$ with the infinite-dimensional $V = F[x]$ and $T$ the multiplication by $x$.
The quotient $F[x]/(f)$ with $f \ne 0$ has dimension $\deg f$ and is the module of the companion matrix $C(f)$ when $f$ is monic.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The polynomials form a space with infinitely many independent directions: $1$, $x$, $x$ squared, and so on.
A matrix acts on a space with finitely many directions.
So the polynomials, with "multiply by $x$" as the map, cannot come from a matrix.

### L2 human

1. Assume $F[x] \cong V_T$ with $\dim V = n$.
2. Then $\dim_F F[x] = n$, but $1, x, \dots, x^n$ are $n + 1$ independent elements.
3. This is a contradiction.

The typical use is to see that every module $V_T$ is torsion, so only the torsion part of the structure theorem matters.
The trap is to think that a finitely generated module must be finite-dimensional over $F$: $F[x]$ has one generator and infinite dimension.
