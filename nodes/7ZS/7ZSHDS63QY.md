## Summary

In a tenth-grade geometry class in the United States, a student fills in a table with two columns, "Statements" and "Reasons", to prove that the base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal.
For many students this is the first and only proof they write at school.
In a third-grade class in the same district, the teacher asks why two odd numbers always add to an even number, and expects an explanation with pictures or counters.
Both lessons follow a written curriculum, and the two curricula place proof very differently.

Proof in school curricula is the place that official curricula and standards give to reasoning and [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]].
For a long time, school proof meant the proofs of Euclidean geometry, often in two-column form.
Since about 2000, many curricula have asked for reasoning and proof at all ages and in all parts of school mathematics.
Three examples are the "Reasoning and Proof" standard of the NCTM (2000), the third Standard for Mathematical Practice of the Common Core (2010), and the list of [[proof methods|958EF46EYJ]] in the English A level content.
The place of proof in the curriculum decides whether students meet proof before university at all.

## Motivation

Students who first meet proof at university face a large step, as the page on [[transition-to-proof courses|HQDCSJG379]] describes.
A curriculum can make that step smaller by asking for reasons at every age.
But a written standard does not by itself change what happens in classrooms.
So two questions matter: what do curricula ask for, and how far does classroom practice follow them?

## Notation

Curriculum documents use several words for proof-like work: "reasoning", "justification", "argument", "explanation" and "proof".
They rarely define them, and a teacher has to read from the examples which level of rigor is meant.
Research distinguishes the intended curriculum (the document), the implemented curriculum (what teachers do) and the attained curriculum (what students learn).

## Remarks

### Three documents

The Principles and Standards for School Mathematics of the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics (NCTM, 2000) list five process standards: Problem Solving, Reasoning and Proof, Communication, Connections and Representation.
Each standard is described for grade bands from prekindergarten to grade 12, so reasoning and proof appear from the first years of school.

The Common Core State Standards for Mathematics, released in 2010 in the United States, list eight Standards for Mathematical Practice.
The third is "Construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others", and it applies at every grade, not only in geometry.

The subject content for AS and A level mathematics in England, published by the Department for Education in 2014, opens with a section on proof.
It names proof by deduction, proof by exhaustion and disproof by [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]], and for the full A level adds proof by contradiction, including the irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ and the infinity of primes.

### Geometry as the home of proof

In many countries, school geometry is where students prove theorems, such as the [[angle sum of a triangle|05MDKQBEAF]] and the [[isosceles triangle theorem|056VS31A0J]].
The van Hiele model, from the 1957 dissertations of Dina van Hiele-Geldof and Pierre van Hiele, describes levels of geometric thinking that run from visual recognition to formal deduction and rigor.
On this model, formal proof in geometry needs the earlier levels first, and a course that starts with axioms skips them.
The topic [[geometry education|1D7PZ2TF5Y]] treats the model.

### What is not proof in a curriculum

A curriculum line such as "use the Pythagorean theorem to find the length of a side" asks for a calculation, not a proof, even though the theorem has a proof.
A textbook table of [[forty prime values of n^2 + n + 41|H75AB4EJT5]] with the question "what do you notice?" asks for a conjecture.
Proof enters only when the next question asks for a reason or a counterexample.

### What research says

Patricio Herbst (2002) traced the two-column proof in American school geometry through the early twentieth century.
He described how the format arose as a way to make proof teachable to all students.
The page on [[two-column proofs|P8PCGFASP8]] treats the format itself.

Lulu Healy and Celia Hoyles (2000) surveyed high-attaining students of age 14 to 15 in England and Wales on proof in algebra.
The students were better at choosing correct arguments than at constructing their own, which shows a gap between the intended and the attained curriculum.
Andreas Stylianides (2007) proposed a definition of proof for school classrooms that takes account of what the class knows, as described on the page [[reasoning and proof in the classroom|E77B813MT4]].

### History

In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, school proof meant the study of Euclid's [[Elements|83WHDAV68J]] or of textbooks based on it.
In the United States, the two-column format of geometry proofs appeared in textbooks of the early twentieth century, as Herbst showed.
The New Math of the 1960s brought sets and logic into school, as the page on [[teaching sets and relations|12ZNG0NRY7]] describes.
The NCTM standards of 2000 made reasoning and proof a process for all grades, and the Common Core of 2010 kept the idea as a practice standard.

### Watch out

A curriculum that puts all proof in one geometry course teaches students that proof is a feature of geometry, not of mathematics.
A teacher who reads "construct viable arguments" as "show your working" accepts a list of computations, which is not an argument for a general claim.
And a test that asks for a proof students can memorize, such as the proof that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, measures memory, not the ability to prove.

### Try it

Is "Find the angle $x$ in this triangle with angles $50^\circ$, $60^\circ$ and $x$" a proof task?
No, it applies the angle sum theorem to compute $x = 70^\circ$, and gives no reason why the sum is $180^\circ$.

Rewrite it as a reasoning task in the spirit of the third Common Core practice.
One version asks whether Sam is right when he says that every triangle has an angle of at least $60^\circ$, and asks for an explanation.
Sam is right, since three angles each less than $60^\circ$ would add to less than $180^\circ$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In many schools, students first meet proofs in geometry, for example the proof that the angles of a triangle add up to $180^\circ$.
Newer curricula ask for reasons at all ages and in all topics.
A young student may be asked why the sum of two odd numbers is always even.
In England, students in the last years of school learn proof by contradiction, for example the proof that $\sqrt{2}$ is not a fraction.
A curriculum only helps if teachers and tests also ask for reasons.

### L2 human

We follow one claim through four stages of a curriculum that asks for proof at all ages: the sum of two odd numbers is even.
At age 7 to 9, students pair counters: each odd number is pairs plus one, and the two single counters make a new pair, which is an [[action proof|ECTGXEPC65]].
At age 11 to 13, students argue from a [[generic example|QFMBKN41PW]] such as
$$7 + 9 = (6 + 1) + (8 + 1) = 6 + 8 + 2,$$
and add that every part is even and that the same split works for any two odd numbers.
At age 14 to 16, students write the algebra:
$$(2a + 1) + (2b + 1) = 2(a + b + 1).$$
At age 16 to 18, students meet the related claim "if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even", and prove it by [[contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]] or by contradiction.

A map like this helps a curriculum designer make each stage build on the last.
Watch out for calling the first two stages "not real proofs": each is a valid argument for the claim, at the level of language that the class has.

### L3 human

The study of proof in curricula connects the topic [[school mathematics curricula and standards|9AHB6RQVDE]] with the research in this topic.
The curriculum states the aims, and the research on [[proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]] and on [[student difficulties with proof|5K2969GSWE]] shows how far students reach them.
The [[functions of proof|ENY0GKRJS8]], such as verification, explanation and communication, give a language for what a curriculum wants proof to do at each stage.
