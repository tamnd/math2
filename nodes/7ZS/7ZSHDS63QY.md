## Summary

Proof in school curricula is the place that official curricula and standards give to reasoning and [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] at school.
For a long time, school proof meant the proofs of Euclidean geometry, often written in a two-column form.
Since about 2000, many curricula have asked for reasoning and proof at all ages and in all subjects of school mathematics.
Three examples are the "Reasoning and Proof" standard of the NCTM (2000), the practice "construct viable arguments" of the Common Core (2010), and the list of [[proof methods|958EF46EYJ]] in the English A level (2014).
The topic matters because a curriculum decides whether students meet proof before university at all.

## Motivation

Students who first meet proof at university face a large step, as the page on [[transition-to-proof courses|HQDCSJG379]] describes.
A curriculum can make this step smaller by asking for reasons at each age.
But a written standard does not by itself change what happens in classrooms.
So the questions of this page are: what do curricula ask for, where in the curriculum is proof placed, and what does research say about the gap between curriculum and practice?

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The Principles and Standards for School Mathematics of the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics (NCTM, 2000) list five process standards: Problem Solving, Reasoning and Proof, Communication, Connections and Representation. The expectations are given for grade bands from preschool to grade $12$.
2. The Common Core State Standards for Mathematics in the United States, released on 2 June 2010, list eight Standards for Mathematical Practice. The third is "Construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others".
3. The content for AS and A level mathematics in England, published by the Department for Education on 18 December 2014, has a section on proof. It lists proof by deduction, proof by exhaustion and disproof by [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]], and for the full A level also proof by contradiction, with the irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ and the infinity of primes.
4. Geometry courses in many countries teach proof through the theorems of Euclid, such as the [[angle sum of a triangle|05MDKQBEAF]] and the [[isosceles triangle theorem|056VS31A0J]].

### Non-examples

1. A curriculum that lists "use the Pythagorean theorem to find the length of a side" asks for a calculation. It does not ask for proof, even though the theorem has a proof.
2. A list of [[forty prime values of n^2 + n + 41|H75AB4EJT5]] in a textbook, with the question "what do you notice?", asks for a conjecture. Proof enters only if the next question asks for a reason or a counterexample.

### Research findings

1. Patricio Herbst (2002) traced the history of the two-column proof in American school geometry in the early twentieth century. He described how the format arose as a way to make proof teachable to all students. The page on [[two-column proofs|P8PCGFASP8]] treats the format.
2. Lulu Healy and Celia Hoyles (2000) studied proof in algebra among high-attaining students of age 14 to 15 in England. The students were better at choosing correct arguments than at constructing them.
3. The van Hiele model, from the 1957 dissertations of Dina van Hiele-Geldof and Pierre van Hiele, describes levels of geometric thinking: visualization, analysis, abstraction, deduction and rigour. It suggests that formal proof in geometry needs earlier levels first. The topic [[geometry education|1D7PZ2TF5Y]] treats the model.
4. Andreas Stylianides (2007) proposed a definition of proof for school classrooms that depends on the knowledge of the class, as on the page [[reasoning and proof in the classroom|E77B813MT4]].

### History

In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, school proof was the study of Euclid's [[Elements|83WHDAV68J]] or of textbooks based on it.
In the United States, the two-column format of geometry proofs appeared in textbooks of the early twentieth century, as Herbst showed.
The New Math of the 1960s brought sets and logic into school, as the page on [[teaching sets and relations|12ZNG0NRY7]] describes.
The NCTM standards of 2000 made reasoning and proof a process for all grades, and the Common Core of 2010 kept the idea as a practice standard.

### Common errors

1. A curriculum writer places all proof in a single geometry course. Students then see proof as a feature of geometry, not of mathematics.
2. A teacher reads "construct viable arguments" as "show your working". A list of computations is not an argument for a general claim.
3. A test asks for a proof that students can memorize, such as the proof that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational. Then the test measures memory, not the ability to prove.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In many schools, students first meet proofs in geometry, for example the proof that the angles of a triangle add up to $180^\circ$.
Newer curricula ask for reasons at all ages and in all topics.
For example, a young student may be asked why the sum of two odd numbers is always even.
In England, students in the last years of school learn proof by contradiction, for example the proof that $\sqrt{2}$ is not a fraction.
A curriculum only helps if teachers and tests also ask for reasons.

### L2 human

We map one claim through three school levels, in the style of a curriculum that asks for proof at all ages.
The claim: the sum of two odd numbers is even.

1. Age $7$ to $9$: students pair counters. Each odd number of counters is pairs plus one, and the two single counters make a pair. This is an [[action proof|ECTGXEPC65]].
2. Age $11$ to $13$: students give a [[generic example|QFMBKN41PW]] in words: "$7 + 9 = (6 + 1) + (8 + 1) = 6 + 8 + 2$, and each part is even, and the same works for any two odd numbers".
3. Age $14$ to $16$: students write $(2a + 1) + (2b + 1) = 2(a + b + 1)$ for integers $a$, $b$.
4. Age $16$ to $18$ (A level): students meet the related claim "if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even", and prove it by [[contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]] or contradiction.

The typical use of such a map is in the design of a curriculum, so that each stage builds on the last.
A trap is to call stages 1 and 2 "not real proofs".
Each is a valid argument for the claim, at the level of the language that the class has.

### L3 human

The study of proof in curricula connects the topic [[school mathematics curricula and standards|9AHB6RQVDE]] with the research of this topic.
The curriculum states the aims; the research on [[proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]] and on [[student difficulties with proof|5K2969GSWE]] shows how far students reach them.
A central question is the relation of the intended, the implemented and the attained curriculum: what the standard says, what teachers do, and what students learn.
The [[functions of proof|ENY0GKRJS8]] give a language for the aims of proof in school: verification, explanation, communication and others.
