## Summary

A [[rule|QPD3X471Q1]] $A_1, \dots, A_n / B$ is *admissible* in a logic $L$ if every substitution that makes all premises theorems of $L$ also makes the conclusion a theorem of $L$.
It is *derivable* if $A_1, \dots, A_n \vdash_L B$, that is, if the conclusion follows from the premises inside $L$.
Every [[derivable rule|Z8E2JHECRC]] is admissible, and adding an admissible rule to $L$ gives no new theorems.
In classical logic the two notions agree.
In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]] they differ, and the admissible rules record the constructive content of the logic, such as the [[disjunction property|YTRVX6F3FX]].
The main results are that Visser's rules form a basis of the admissible rules of $\mathsf{IPC}$, and that admissibility in $\mathsf{IPC}$ is decidable.

## Motivation

A logic is usually given by its theorems.
The theorems do not determine which inferences between theorems are safe.
For instance, "if $\neg p \to q \vee r$ is a theorem, then so is $(\neg p \to q) \vee (\neg p \to r)$" holds in $\mathsf{IPC}$, but the corresponding implication is not a theorem.
Admissible rules capture such facts about the set of theorems as a whole.
They also link logic to algebra: a rule is admissible iff every unifier of the premises unifies the conclusion, so admissibility is a question about equations in the free Heyting algebra.

## Notation

We write $\Gamma \mathrel{|\!\sim}_L B$ when the rule $\Gamma / B$ is admissible in $L$, and $\Gamma \vdash_L B$ when it is derivable.
A *substitution* $\sigma$ replaces each variable by a formula, and a *unifier* of $A$ is a substitution $\sigma$ with $\vdash_L \sigma A$.
A logic is *structurally complete* if every admissible rule is derivable.
It is *hereditarily structurally complete* if every extension is structurally complete.
A set $R$ of admissible rules is a *basis* if $\mathrel{|\!\sim}_L$ is the smallest structural consequence relation that contains $\vdash_L$ and $R$.
For $n \geq 1$, the *Visser rule* $V_n$ is
$$\frac{\bigl(\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n} (p_i \to q_i) \to p_{n+1} \vee p_{n+2}\bigr) \vee r}{\bigvee_{j=1}^{n+2} \bigl(\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n} (p_i \to q_i) \to p_j\bigr) \vee r}.$$

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The Kreisel-Putnam rule is admissible but not derivable in IPC|ZBTCC4C61S]].
2. [[Classical logic is structurally complete|14HRKCJEFV]].
3. Mints's rule, from $(p \to q) \to p \vee r$ infer $((p \to q) \to p) \vee ((p \to q) \to r)$, is admissible in $\mathsf{IPC}$ and not derivable.
4. The [[disjunction property|YTRVX6F3FX]] is the admissibility of the multiple-conclusion rule $p \vee q / p, q$.
5. The Visser rules $V_n$ are admissible in $\mathsf{IPC}$; see [[Iemhoff's basis theorem|JNFV2NMNFR]].

### Non-examples

1. [[Double negation elimination is not admissible in IPC|ZRZMVDSFM7]].
2. The rule $p / q$ is not admissible in any consistent logic. The substitution with $\sigma p = p \to p$ and $\sigma q = \bot$ gives a theorem and a non-theorem.

### Basic properties

1. Every derivable rule is admissible, by closure of theorems under substitution and modus ponens.
2. Admissibility is a property of the set of theorems, while derivability depends also on the deductive consequence relation.
3. A rule is admissible in $L$ iff adding it to $L$ yields no new theorems.
4. $\mathsf{IPC}$ is not structurally complete, by example 1.
5. Every disjunction-free rule, and every implication-free rule, admissible in an intermediate logic is derivable there.
6. [[Gödel-Dummett logic|474SGS2N0X]] is hereditarily structurally complete. $\mathsf{IPC}$ and [[the logic KC|H9Z8BWRN69]] are not structurally complete.
7. [[Medvedev's logic|R2FD01J3PC]] is structurally complete.
8. [[Iemhoff's theorem|JNFV2NMNFR]]: the Visser rules form a basis of the admissible rules of $\mathsf{IPC}$.
9. [[Rybakov's theorem|GJN7A5DJHK]]: admissibility in $\mathsf{IPC}$ is decidable, and there is no finite basis.

### History

Harrop observed the admissibility of the rule in example 1 in 1960.
Visser and de Jongh found the rules $V_n$ and conjectured that they form a basis.
Iemhoff proved this in 2001, building on Ghilardi's 1999 work on unification in intuitionistic logic.
Rybakov's 1997 monograph proved that admissibility in $\mathsf{IPC}$ is decidable and has no finite basis.
Iemhoff proved in 2005 that $\mathsf{IPC}$ is the only intermediate logic with the disjunction property that admits all Visser rules.

### Common errors

1. Confusing an admissible rule with the implication between premise and conclusion. The rule may be admissible while the implication is not a theorem.
2. Checking admissibility only for the identity substitution. The definition quantifies over all substitutions, as non-example 1 shows.
3. Assuming that structural completeness passes to sublogics. Classical logic is structurally complete, while its sublogic $\mathsf{IPC}$ is not.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A rule says: "from these formulas, infer that one".
It is derivable if the logic itself proves the inference.
It is admissible if it never takes us from theorems to a non-theorem.
In intuitionistic logic some rules are safe in this second sense without being provable as implications.

### L2 human

1. To show $A / B$ admissible, take any $\sigma$ with $\vdash \sigma A$ and prove $\vdash \sigma B$.
2. To show it derivable, prove $A \vdash B$, for example by the deduction theorem and a derivation of $A \to B$.
3. To separate the two, find a Kripke countermodel to $A \to B$ and an argument that theorems of the form $\sigma A$ always give $\sigma B$.

The typical use is to describe the constructive content of a logic beyond its theorems.
A trap: admissibility is about theorems, so premises are not hypotheses but theorems.

### L3 human

Admissibility in $\mathsf{IPC}$ is equivalent to the validity of quasi-identities in the free Heyting algebra on countably many generators.
Ghilardi's projective formulas give finite complete sets of unifiers, so unification in Heyting algebras is finitary.
Admissible rules then reduce to finitely many derivability checks, one per projective formula in the approximation of the premise.
