## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $U$ and $W$ be subspaces of $V$.
Then the [[sum|0RMKABS2S5]] $U + W$ is a subspace of $V$ with $U \subseteq U + W$ and $W \subseteq U + W$.
If $X$ is a subspace of $V$ with $U \subseteq X$ and $W \subseteq X$, then $U + W \subseteq X$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

If $U$ and $W$ are only subsets, then $U + W$ need not be a subspace: in $\R$, the set $\set{1} + \set{0}$ is $\set{1}$.
If $X$ is only a subset that contains $U \cup W$, the inclusion $U + W \subseteq X$ can fail: take $X = U \cup W$ for the two axes of $\R^2$.

### Consequences

The sum $U + W$ equals the intersection of all subspaces that contain $U \cup W$, so $U + W = \operatorname{span}(U \cup W)$.
The subspaces of $V$ form a lattice, with join $U + W$ and meet $U \cap W$.

### Converse

If a subspace $Y$ contains $U$ and $W$ and lies in every subspace that contains both, then $Y = U + W$, because two smallest elements are equal.

### Generalizations

For a family $(U_i)_{i \in I}$, the set of finite sums $u_{i_1} + \cdots + u_{i_k}$ is the smallest subspace that contains every $U_i$.
The same statement holds for submodules and for normal subgroups $NM$ of a group.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every subspace that holds $U$ and $W$ must hold all sums $u + w$, by closure.
The sum is exactly this set of sums, and it is already a subspace.
So it is the smallest choice.

### L2 human

Check with the axes of $\R^2$: any subspace $X$ with $(1, 0) \in X$ and $(0, 1) \in X$ contains $a(1, 0) + b(0, 1) = (a, b)$.
So $X = \R^2 = U + W$.
