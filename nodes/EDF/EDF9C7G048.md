## Statement

The sum of two subspaces is itself a subspace, and every subspace that holds both pieces holds their sum.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $U$ and $W$ be subspaces of $V$.
Then the [[sum|0RMKABS2S5]] $U + W$ is a subspace of $V$ with $U \subseteq U + W$ and $W \subseteq U + W$.
If $X$ is a subspace of $V$ with $U \subseteq X$ and $W \subseteq X$, then $U + W \subseteq X$.

## Remarks

### A check with the axes

Let $U$ and $W$ be the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
A subspace $X$ that contains both axes contains $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, and by closure it contains every
$$a(1, 0) + b(0, 1) = (a, b).$$
So $X = \mathbb{R}^2$, and the only subspace that holds both axes is $U + W = \mathbb{R}^2$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The pieces $U$ and $W$ must be subspaces.
For subsets the sum need not be a subspace: in $\mathbb{R}$, the set $\{1\} + \{0\} = \{1\}$ does not contain $0$.

The set $X$ must be a subspace, not only a subset that contains $U$ and $W$.
For the two axes of $\mathbb{R}^2$, the subset $X = U \cup W$ contains both axes, but $(1, 1) \in U + W$ is not in $X$.

### What it gives you

The theorem identifies $U + W$ with the intersection of all subspaces that contain $U \cup W$, and that intersection is the [[span|0DTY8B9HMS]] of $U \cup W$.
So $U + W = \operatorname{span}(U \cup W)$, and a spanning list of $U$ joined with a spanning list of $W$ spans $U + W$.
It also makes the subspaces of $V$ into a lattice, with $U + W$ as the least upper bound and $U \cap W$ as the greatest lower bound; the [[intersection theorem|0V33SPH7RR]] gives the second half.

### The converse

The two properties determine $U + W$.
If a subspace $Y$ contains $U$ and $W$ and lies in every subspace that contains both, then $Y \subseteq U + W$ and $U + W \subseteq Y$, so $Y = U + W$.

### Generalizations

For a family $(U_i)_{i \in I}$ of subspaces, the set of finite sums $u_{i_1} + \dots + u_{i_m}$ with $u_{i_j} \in U_{i_j}$ is the smallest subspace that contains every $U_i$, by the same proof.
The statement holds for submodules of a module over a ring, and in group theory for two normal subgroups $N$ and $M$, whose product $NM$ is the smallest normal subgroup that contains both.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every subspace that holds $U$ and $W$ must hold all the sums $u + w$, because a subspace is closed under addition.
The sum $U + W$ is exactly this set of sums, and it is already a subspace.
So nothing smaller can work, and nothing larger is needed.

### L2 human

Two facts need proof.
First, $U + W$ is a subspace: if $x = u + w$ and $y = u' + w'$, then
$$x + ay = (u + au') + (w + aw'),$$
and the two brackets lie in $U$ and in $W$.
Second, a subspace $X$ that contains $U$ and $W$ contains each $u$ and each $w$, so it contains $u + w$.
The [[proof|P1XA7PBR99]] writes out both steps.
In practice the statement is used to compute a sum from spanning lists, as in the concept page on the [[sum of subspaces|0JMTY8MF54]].
