## Summary

Deontic logic is the logic of obligation, permission and prohibition.
Its basic system, standard deontic logic $\mathsf{SDL}$, is the [[normal modal logic|D1TJYZZDCY]] $\mathsf{KD}$ written with a box $O$, read "it is obligatory that".
The other operators are defined: $P\varphi := \neg O\neg\varphi$ ("it is permitted that $\varphi$") and $F\varphi := O\neg\varphi$ ("it is forbidden that $\varphi$").
The axioms of $\mathsf{SDL}$ are all propositional tautologies, $\mathrm{K}$: $O(p \to q) \to (Op \to Oq)$, and $\mathrm{D}$: $Op \to Pp$.
The rules are modus ponens, uniform substitution and necessitation: from $\vdash \varphi$ infer $\vdash O\varphi$.
In [[Kripke semantics|6BQ71KQ6QT]], $w R v$ means that $v$ is an ideal alternative of $w$, a world where every obligation of $w$ is met.
$\mathsf{SDL}$ is sound and complete for the frames whose [[accessibility relation|YHGXV3SQDP]] is [[serial|NRNKV2YYWJ]].
Deontic logic matters because it gives exact rules for reasoning with norms, and its paradoxes show which modal principles fail for "ought".

## Motivation

Laws, contracts and moral rules use the words "must", "may" and "must not".
Von Wright saw that these words relate as "necessary", "possible" and "impossible" do.
"Permitted" is "not obligatory not", just as "possible" is "not necessary not".
So a modal logic with a box for obligation is a natural first model.
Two facts fix the choice of axioms.
What ought to be is often not the case, so the axiom $Op \to p$ must fail.
But a good system of norms should not demand the impossible, so $O\bot$ must be false, and this is the axiom $\mathrm{D}$.

## Notation

1. $O$ (also written $\mathsf{O}$, $\mathit{Ob}$ or $\bigcirc$) is obligation; $P$ is permission; $F$ is prohibition.
2. $\mathsf{SDL}$ is the modal logic $\mathsf{KD}$, also called $\mathsf{D}$, see [[the logics T, D and K4|6M7Q6GFPK4]].
3. Dyadic (conditional) obligation is written $O(q \mid p)$ or $O(q/p)$, read "$q$ is obligatory given $p$".
4. A deontic frame is a pair $(W, R)$ with $R$ serial; $R[w] = \set{v : w R v}$ is the set of ideal worlds for $w$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The derived laws $\vdash \neg(Op \wedge O\neg p)$ and $\vdash O(p \wedge q) \leftrightarrow (Op \wedge Oq)$ are theorems of $\mathsf{SDL}$, see [[the derivation in SDL|RKTY59QFQ9]].
2. Ross's paradox: $\mathsf{SDL}$ proves $Op \to O(p \vee q)$, so "you ought to post the letter" gives "you ought to post or burn it", see [[Ross's paradox|NGDW39A028]].
3. Chisholm's paradox: four plausible sentences about a duty and a contrary-to-duty duty cannot be written consistently in $\mathsf{SDL}$, see [[Chisholm's paradox|TJ9JJADSEQ]].
4. On the frame $W = \set{a, b}$, $R = \set{(a, b), (b, b)}$ with $V(p) = \set{b}$, the formula $Op$ is true at $a$. The reason is that the only ideal world $b$ satisfies $p$.
5. Anderson's reduction: with an alethic box $\square$ and a constant $V$ for "a violation occurs", put $Op := \square(\neg p \to V)$. With $\lozenge\neg V$ as an axiom, this $O$ obeys $\mathsf{KD}$.

### Non-examples

1. $\mathsf{SDL} \nvdash Op \to p$: at world $a$ of example 4, $Op$ holds and $p$ fails, see [[the failure of T|N365EYW624]]. The frame is serial but not reflexive.
2. The logic $\mathsf{K}$ with $O$ for its box is not an adequate deontic logic. A world with no successor makes $O\bot$ true, so it allows conflicting obligations $Op \wedge O\neg p$.
3. The material reading $Op := p$ is not deontic logic: it makes every truth obligatory and every obligation true.

### Basic properties

1. $\mathsf{SDL}$ is sound and complete for serial frames, by [[completeness of K, T, D, K4, S4, B and S5|FXTCKYR3AQ]]. It has the [[finite model property|XNPJK6B921]], so it is decidable.
2. $\mathsf{SDL} \vdash \neg O\bot$, $\mathsf{SDL} \vdash O\top$ and $\mathsf{SDL} \vdash Op \to Pp$.
3. Each of $\mathrm{D}$, $\neg O\bot$ and $\neg(Op \wedge O\neg p)$ axiomatizes $\mathsf{SDL}$ over $\mathsf{K}$.
4. $\mathsf{SDL} \nvdash O(Op \to p)$. This formula is valid exactly on the frames where $w R v$ implies $v R v$, and some authors add it.
5. If $\vdash \varphi \to \psi$, then $\vdash O\varphi \to O\psi$. This monotonicity of $O$ is the source of Ross's paradox.
6. Dyadic deontic logic has a binary operator $O(q \mid p)$. In Hansson's semantics, worlds are ranked by betterness, and $O(q \mid p)$ holds when all best $p$-worlds are $q$-worlds.

### History

Mally proposed the first formal deontic system in Grundgesetze des Sollens (1926).
Von Wright's paper "Deontic Logic" (Mind, 1951) started the modern subject with the analogy between deontic and alethic modalities.
Ross raised his paradox in 1941, and Chisholm published the contrary-to-duty paradox in 1963.
Von Wright proposed a dyadic operator in 1956, and Hansson gave it a preference semantics in 1969.

### Common errors

1. Reading $O$ as truth in all worlds. $O\varphi$ is truth in the ideal worlds only, and the actual world need not be ideal.
2. Confusing "not obligatory" with "forbidden". $\neg Op$ is $P\neg p$, while $Fp$ is $O\neg p$.
3. Writing "if $p$ then $q$ is obligatory" as $O(p \to q)$ or as $p \to Oq$ without checking. The two forms behave differently, see [[Chisholm's paradox|TJ9JJADSEQ]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Deontic logic studies the words "must", "may" and "must not".
Think of a list of ideal situations, where every rule is followed.
A thing is obligatory if it happens in every ideal situation.
A thing is permitted if it happens in at least one ideal situation.
For example, if in both ideal situations the rent is paid, then paying the rent is obligatory.
The real situation can still be one where the rent is not paid.
So "it must be so" does not mean "it is so".

### L2 human

We check that $\neg(Op \wedge O\neg p)$ is valid on every serial frame.

1. Let $(W, R)$ be serial, let $V$ be a valuation, and let $w \in W$.
2. Suppose $w \models Op$ and $w \models O\neg p$.
3. Seriality gives a world $v$ with $w R v$.
4. Then $v \models p$ from $Op$, and $v \models \neg p$ from $O\neg p$.
5. This is impossible, so $Op \wedge O\neg p$ fails at $w$.

The typical use of $\mathsf{SDL}$ is to test whether a set of norms is consistent: write each norm with $O$ and look for a serial model.
A trap: $\mathsf{SDL}$ treats $O$ as "ought to be", a property of states of affairs.
Rules about what an agent ought to do need an agent operator, as in [[STIT logic|WYY2JXWMYY]].

### L3 human

$\mathsf{SDL}$ is the monomodal logic $\mathsf{KD}$, so its metatheory is that of normal modal logic: canonical models, filtration and PSPACE-complete satisfiability.
The interest of the subject lies in the paradoxes, which show that a single normal box is too coarse for norms.
Responses go in three directions: dyadic operators ranked by betterness, as in [[axiological logic|4BRXSBXXWG]]; non-normal or input/output logics; and combinations with agency and time.
Conditional obligation in the Hansson style uses the same minimal-model semantics as [[conditional logic|ZGYG7WN7EC]] and as [[AGM belief revision|Y05YQN7HGQ]].
Anderson's reduction places deontic logic inside alethic modal logic with one constant, and Kanger and others gave related reductions.
Deontic operators also appear in computer science, for access control and for the specification of contracts and of normative multi-agent systems.
