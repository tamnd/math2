## Summary

Deontic logic is the logic of "ought", "may" and "must not", and it treats obligation as truth in the ideal worlds.
If every ideal world is one where the rent is paid, then paying the rent is obligatory, even in an actual world where nobody pays it.
Its basic system, standard deontic logic $\mathsf{SDL}$, is the [[normal modal logic|D1TJYZZDCY]] $\mathsf{KD}$ written with a box $O$, read "it is obligatory that".
Permission and prohibition are defined from it:
$$P\varphi := \neg O \neg \varphi, \qquad F\varphi := O \neg \varphi.$$
The axioms are all propositional tautologies, $\mathrm{K}$: $O(p \to q) \to (Op \to Oq)$, and $\mathrm{D}$: $Op \to Pp$.
The rules are modus ponens, uniform substitution and necessitation, which infers $\vdash O\varphi$ from $\vdash \varphi$.
In [[Kripke semantics|6BQ71KQ6QT]], $w R v$ says that $v$ is an ideal alternative of $w$, and $\mathsf{SDL}$ is sound and complete for the frames whose [[accessibility relation|YHGXV3SQDP]] is [[serial|NRNKV2YYWJ]].

## Motivation

Here is the test case that every deontic logic must face, written in $\mathsf{SDL}$.
Let $h$ be "Jones goes to help his neighbors" and $t$ be "Jones tells them he is coming".
Four sentences sound consistent: $Oh$ (he ought to go), $O(h \to t)$ (if he goes, he ought to tell them), $\neg h \to O\neg t$ (if he does not go, he ought not to tell them), and $\neg h$ (he does not go).
From the second sentence the axiom $\mathrm{K}$ gives $Oh \to Ot$, so the first gives $Ot$.
The third and fourth give $O\neg t$, and the axiom $\mathrm{D}$ forbids $Ot \wedge O\neg t$.
So the four sentences are inconsistent in $\mathsf{SDL}$, and this is [[Chisholm's paradox|TJ9JJADSEQ]].

Why start with a system that fails such a test?
Because $\mathsf{SDL}$ fixes the vocabulary and shows exactly where "ought" differs from "necessarily".
Von Wright saw that "obligatory", "permitted" and "forbidden" relate as "necessary", "possible" and "impossible" do.
Two facts then fix the axioms.
What ought to be is often not the case, so $Op \to p$ must fail.
A good code of norms does not demand the impossible, so $O\bot$ must be false, and that is the axiom $\mathrm{D}$.
Laws, contracts and access-control policies in computing all use this vocabulary.

## Notation

Books write the obligation box as $O$, $\mathsf{O}$, $\mathit{Ob}$ or $\bigcirc$, with $P$ for permission and $F$ for prohibition.
The logic $\mathsf{SDL}$ is the modal logic $\mathsf{KD}$, also called $\mathsf{D}$, as in [[the logics T, D and K4|6M7Q6GFPK4]].
In a deontic frame $(W, R)$ the set $R[w] = \{ v : w R v \}$ is the set of ideal worlds for $w$.
Dyadic obligation is written $O(q \mid p)$ or $O(q / p)$, read "$q$ is obligatory given $p$".
Watch the letter $F$: in [[temporal logic|8XB117FKKV]] it means "at some future time", and a book that mixes the two logics must rename one of them.

## Remarks

### A first model

Take $W = \{a, b\}$, $R = \{(a, b), (b, b)\}$ and $V(p) = \{b\}$.
The frame is serial, since $a$ sees $b$ and $b$ sees $b$.
The only ideal world of $a$ is $b$, and $p$ holds there, so $a \models Op$.
But $p$ is false at $a$ itself, so $Op \to p$ fails at $a$.
This two-world model is the [[failure of the axiom T|N365EYW624]], and it is the picture of a violated norm.

Permission is the dual.
At $a$ the formula $P\neg p$ is false, since the only ideal world satisfies $p$.
So at $a$ the act $\neg p$ is forbidden, and it is also what actually happens.

### The laws you can use

Because $O$ is a normal box, it distributes over conjunction: $O(p \wedge q) \leftrightarrow (Op \wedge Oq)$.
The axiom $\mathrm{D}$ adds the laws that need seriality, such as $\neg O\bot$ and $\neg(Op \wedge O\neg p)$.
Each of $\mathrm{D}$, $\neg O\bot$ and $\neg(Op \wedge O\neg p)$ gives $\mathsf{SDL}$ when it is added to $\mathsf{K}$.
The [[derived laws of SDL|RKTY59QFQ9]] work these out from the axioms, line by line.

The logic is decidable.
It is complete for serial frames by [[completeness of K, T, D, K4, S4, B and S5|FXTCKYR3AQ]], and it has the [[finite model property|XNPJK6B921]].
The formula $O(Op \to p)$, "it ought to be that obligations are met", is not a theorem.
It is valid exactly on the frames where $w R v$ implies $v R v$, and some authors add it as an axiom.

### Ross's paradox

From $\vdash p \to (p \vee q)$, necessitation and $\mathrm{K}$ give $\vdash Op \to O(p \vee q)$.
So "you ought to post the letter" yields "you ought to post it or burn it".
Nothing is inconsistent here, but the English sentence suggests that burning would comply.
The source is monotonicity, the derived rule that infers $\vdash O\varphi \to O\psi$ from $\vdash \varphi \to \psi$.
[[Ross's paradox|NGDW39A028]] works this out and shows what goes wrong if you add free choice permission.

### Conditional obligation

Chisholm's four sentences cannot all be written with the one-place $O$.
Each repair breaks something: $h \to Ot$ follows from $\neg h$, and $O(\neg h \to \neg t)$ follows from $Oh$.
Dyadic deontic logic answers with a two-place operator $O(q \mid p)$.
In Hansson's semantics the worlds are ranked by betterness, and $O(q \mid p)$ holds when every best $p$-world is a $q$-world.
With this operator the four sentences become consistent, and the worked Chisholm example checks this with four ranked worlds.

### What it is not

The logic $\mathsf{K}$ with the box read as $O$ is not an adequate deontic logic.
A world with no successor makes $O\bot$ true there, and so it makes the conflict $Op \wedge O\neg p$ true as well.
The reading $Op := p$ is not deontic logic either, since it makes every truth obligatory and every obligation met.
In the same way, the logic $\mathsf{KT}$ is a logic of necessity, not of obligation, because it proves $Op \to p$.

### Watch out

"Not obligatory" is not "forbidden".
The formula $\neg Op$ is $P\neg p$, which permits $\neg p$, while $Fp$ is $O\neg p$, which requires it.
Take a world with two ideal worlds, one where $p$ holds and one where it fails.
There $\neg Op$ holds and $O\neg p$ fails, so $p$ is not obligatory, and yet it is not forbidden.
The second trap is to read $O\varphi$ as truth in all worlds.
It is truth in the ideal worlds only, and the actual world need not be one of them.

### History

Mally proposed a formal system of "ought" in Grundgesetze des Sollens (1926).
Ross raised his paradox in 1941, in the logic of imperatives.
Von Wright's paper "Deontic Logic" (Mind, 1951) began the modern subject with the analogy to alethic modalities, and he proposed a dyadic operator in 1956.
Chisholm published the contrary-to-duty paradox in 1963, and Hansson gave dyadic obligation a betterness semantics in 1969.

### Try it

First, show that $\mathsf{SDL}$ proves $O\top$.
The formula $\top$ is a tautology, so necessitation gives $O\top$ at once.

Second, on the first model, decide whether $P(p \vee q)$ holds at $a$ when $V(q) = \emptyset$.
It does, because the ideal world $b$ satisfies $p$ and so satisfies $p \vee q$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Deontic logic studies the words "must", "may" and "must not".
Picture a short list of ideal situations, where every rule is followed.
A thing is obligatory if it happens in every ideal situation, and permitted if it happens in at least one.
Say there are two ideal situations, and the rent is paid in both.
Then paying the rent is obligatory.
The real situation can still be one where the rent is not paid, so "it must be so" does not mean "it is so".

### L2 human

We check that $\neg(Op \wedge O\neg p)$ holds on every serial frame.
Take a serial frame $(W, R)$, a valuation and a world $w$, and suppose that $w \models Op$ and $w \models O\neg p$.
Seriality gives a world $v$ with $w R v$.
Then $p$ holds at $v$ because of $Op$, and $\neg p$ holds at $v$ because of $O\neg p$.
That is impossible, so $Op \wedge O\neg p$ fails at $w$.
On a frame with a dead end the argument breaks at the step that picks $v$, and the conflict becomes satisfiable.
This is the typical use of $\mathsf{SDL}$: write each norm with $O$, and test the code for consistency by looking for a serial model.
Watch out that $O$ reads as "ought to be", a property of states of affairs.
Rules about what an agent ought to do need an agent operator, as in [[STIT logic|WYY2JXWMYY]].

### L3 human

Since $\mathsf{SDL}$ is the monomodal logic $\mathsf{KD}$, its metatheory is that of normal modal logic: canonical models, filtration and PSPACE-complete satisfiability.
The interest of the subject lies in the paradoxes, which show that one normal box is too coarse for norms.
The responses go three ways: dyadic operators ranked by betterness, as in [[axiological logic|4BRXSBXXWG]]; non-normal and input/output logics; and combinations with agency and time.

Hansson's conditional obligation uses the same "best worlds" semantics as the counterfactual of [[conditional logic|ZGYG7WN7EC]] and the revision of [[AGM belief revision|Y05YQN7HGQ]].
Anderson's reduction places deontic logic inside alethic modal logic: with a constant $V$ for "a violation occurs", put $Op := \square(\neg p \to V)$.
With the axiom $\lozenge \neg V$, this defined $O$ obeys $\mathsf{KD}$.
