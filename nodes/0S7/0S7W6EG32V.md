## Summary

Let $(V_i)_{i \in I}$ be a family of [[vector spaces|000AYPA9AJ]] over a field $F$.
The *external direct sum* $\bigoplus_{i \in I} V_i$ is the space of families $(v_i)_{i \in I}$ with $v_i \in V_i$ for each $i$ and $v_i = 0$ for all but finitely many $i$, with componentwise operations.
For two spaces, $V \oplus W$ is the space of pairs $(v, w)$.
The external direct sum builds a new space from given spaces, while the [[internal direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] splits a given space into subspaces.

## Motivation

We often want a space that contains copies of given spaces side by side, without any interaction between them.
For example, a state of two independent systems is a pair of states, one for each system.
The external direct sum is this space of pairs, and for infinitely many spaces it keeps only the families with finitely many nonzero entries.
That restriction is what makes linear maps out of the direct sum easy to describe: a map is given by one map on each summand, by the [[universal property|N04WS31KXB]].

## Notation

We write $\bigoplus_{i \in I} V_i$, and $V_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus V_k$ for a finite family.
The direct sum of copies of one space $V$ indexed by $I$ is written $V^{(I)}$, so $F^{(I)}$ is the space of finitely supported functions $I \to F$.
The *canonical injection* $\iota_j \colon V_j \to \bigoplus_i V_i$ sends $v$ to the family with $v$ in place $j$ and $0$ elsewhere.
Some books call this space the direct sum and the [[internal direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] an internal decomposition, and other books do the opposite.
Mathlib writes `DirectSum ι β` with the notation `⨁ i, β i`, and `DirectSum.lof` for the injections.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[Polynomials as a direct sum of lines|P6FQCVRKAD]]: $F[x]$ is isomorphic to $F^{(\N)}$, the direct sum of countably many copies of $F$.
- $F^m \oplus F^n$ is isomorphic to $F^{m + n}$ by writing the two coordinate lists one after the other.
- [[A map out of R² ⊕ R|RWST1DAHFM]] is given by one map on $\R^2$ and one map on $\R$.
- For each $i$, the space $V_i$ is isomorphic to its image $\iota_i(V_i)$, and $\bigoplus_i V_i$ is the internal direct sum of these images.

### Non-examples

- The space of all real sequences is the [[product|0EX2HPXPJ6]] of countably many copies of $\R$, and it is [[not their direct sum|6DEMNQEGG8]], since $(1, 1, 1, \dots)$ has infinitely many nonzero entries.
- The union $V \cup W$ of two spaces is not a direct sum. It is not even a vector space in general.

### Basic properties

- For a finite family, the direct sum and the [[product|0EX2HPXPJ6]] are the same space.
- $\dim(V \oplus W) = \dim V + \dim W$, and more generally the union of bases of the $\iota_i(V_i)$ is a basis of $\bigoplus_i V_i$. So $\dim \bigoplus_i V_i = \sum_i \dim V_i$.
- The [[universal property|N04WS31KXB]]: linear maps $f_i \colon V_i \to X$ come from exactly one linear map $f \colon \bigoplus_i V_i \to X$ with $f \circ \iota_i = f_i$. In the language of categories, the direct sum is the coproduct of vector spaces.
- If $U_1, \dots, U_k$ are subspaces of $V$, then the addition map $U_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_k \to V$ is linear. It is injective exactly when the sum is direct, and it is an isomorphism exactly when $V = U_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_k$.

### History

Direct sums of groups and modules were studied in the early twentieth century, for example in the structure theory of finite abelian groups.
The name and the symbol $\oplus$ became standard with the abstract algebra of Noether and van der Waerden in the 1920s and 1930s.

### Common errors

- Allowing infinitely many nonzero entries. That gives the product, which is larger than the direct sum for infinite families.
- Confusing $V \oplus W$ with $V + W$. The sum $V + W$ needs a common space that contains $V$ and $W$, and the external direct sum does not.
- Thinking that $V \oplus V$ equals $V$. The space $\R \oplus \R$ is $\R^2$, of dimension $2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The external direct sum puts several spaces side by side.
A vector of $\R^2 \oplus \R$ is a pair: a vector of the plane and a number.
We add pairs part by part and scale both parts.
So $\R^2 \oplus \R$ is just $\R^3$ written in two blocks.

### L2 human

Let $V = \R^2$ and $W = P_1$, the polynomials of degree at most $1$.

1. A vector of $V \oplus W$ is a pair such as $((1, 2), 3 + x)$.
2. Addition: $((1, 2), 3 + x) + ((0, 1), 1 - x) = ((1, 3), 4)$.
3. A basis is $((1, 0), 0)$, $((0, 1), 0)$, $(0, 1)$, $(0, x)$. So $\dim(V \oplus W) = 4 = 2 + 2$.
4. The injections are $\iota_1(v) = (v, 0)$ and $\iota_2(p) = (0, p)$. Their images meet only in $0$ and sum to the whole space.

The trap for infinite families is to allow all families, which gives the product.

### L3 human

In the category of vector spaces over $F$, finite direct sums are biproducts: $V \oplus W$ is both the coproduct and the product.
For infinite families, the coproduct is $\bigoplus_i V_i$ and the product is $\prod_i V_i$, and the canonical map from the first to the second is injective but not surjective.
Every vector space is isomorphic to $F^{(B)}$ for a basis $B$, so every vector space is a direct sum of copies of $F$.
The duality $\left(\bigoplus_i V_i\right)^* \cong \prod_i V_i^*$ exchanges sums and products, which explains why the dual of an infinite-dimensional space is larger than the space.
