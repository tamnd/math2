## Summary

The external direct sum builds one new vector space out of several given spaces by placing them side by side.
A vector of the new space chooses one vector from each given space, and we add and scale these choices part by part.
For two spaces $V$ and $W$ over a field $F$, the direct sum $V \oplus W$ is the set of pairs $(v, w)$ with $v \in V$ and $w \in W$, with the operations
$$(v, w) + (v', w') = (v + v', w + w'), \qquad a (v, w) = (a v, a w).$$
For a family $(V_i)_{i \in I}$ of [[vector spaces|000AYPA9AJ]] over $F$, the [[external direct sum|02WZB422CE]] $\bigoplus_{i \in I} V_i$ is the set of families $(v_i)_{i \in I}$ with $v_i \in V_i$ for each $i$ and $v_i = 0$ for all but finitely many $i$, again with componentwise operations.
The [[internal direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] splits a space that we already have, while the external direct sum makes a new space from pieces that need not lie in a common space.

## Motivation

Linear algebra often needs one space that holds two kinds of data at once.
A point of the plane together with a polynomial, or the states of two systems that do not interact, are pairs.
The [[sum|0JMTY8MF54]] $U + W$ of subspaces cannot describe such pairs, because it needs one space that already contains both $U$ and $W$.
The external direct sum supplies that common space: $V$ sits inside $V \oplus W$ as the pairs $(v, 0)$, and $W$ sits inside as the pairs $(0, w)$.

For an infinite family, the condition of finite support is what makes linear maps out of the direct sum easy to build.
Such a map is given by one linear map on each summand, and its value on a family is the finite sum of the values of the parts, by the [[universal property|N04WS31KXB]].
This makes the direct sum the coproduct of vector spaces.
The idea returns in the sub-area on [[duality|9K7FFF26QR]], where the dual of a direct sum turns out to be a product.

## Notation

We write $\bigoplus_{i \in I} V_i$ for a family and $V_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus V_k$ for finitely many spaces.
The direct sum of copies of one space $V$, indexed by a set $I$, is written $V^{(I)}$, so $F^{(I)}$ is the space of functions $I \to F$ that vanish outside a finite set.
The *canonical injection* $\iota_j \colon V_j \to \bigoplus_i V_i$ sends $v$ to the family with $v$ in place $j$ and $0$ in every other place.
Watch the words: some books use "direct sum" only for the internal notion, and other books use it only for this external one.
The two agree up to isomorphism, because $\bigoplus_i V_i$ is the internal direct sum of the images $\iota_i(V_i)$.
Mathlib writes `DirectSum ι β`, with the notation `⨁ i, β i`, and `DirectSum.lof` for the injections.

## Remarks

### Pairs of coordinate vectors

Start with $\R^2 \oplus \R$.
A vector is a pair $((x, y), t)$, and two pairs add part by part:
$$((1, 2), 3) + ((0, -1), 4) = ((1, 1), 7).$$
If we drop the inner brackets, the pair $((x, y), t)$ becomes the triple $(x, y, t)$, and the operations become those of $\R^3$.
So $\R^2 \oplus \R \cong \R^3$, and in the same way $F^m \oplus F^n \cong F^{m + n}$, by writing the two coordinate lists one after the other.

The pieces need not be coordinate spaces.
Take $V = \R^2$ and $W = P_1$, the real polynomials of degree at most $1$.
Then
$$((1, 2), 3 + x) + ((0, 1), 1 - x) = ((1, 3), 4),$$
and the four vectors $((1, 0), 0)$, $((0, 1), 0)$, $((0, 0), 1)$ and $((0, 0), x)$ form a basis, so $\dim(V \oplus W) = 4 = 2 + 2$.

### Infinitely many summands

For countably many copies of $F$, the direct sum $F^{(\N)}$ is the space of sequences $(a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots)$ with only finitely many nonzero terms.
Such a sequence is the same as the list of coefficients of a polynomial, and [[the polynomials form this direct sum|P6FQCVRKAD]]: $F[x] \cong F^{(\N)}$.
For instance, $3 - x + 5x^3$ corresponds to $(3, -1, 0, 5, 0, 0, \dots)$.

The sequence $(1, 1, 1, \dots)$ is not in $F^{(\N)}$, since all its terms are nonzero.
It lies in the [[direct product|0EX2HPXPJ6]] $F^{\N}$ of all sequences, and so [[the product of countably many lines is not their direct sum|6DEMNQEGG8]].
For a finite family the direct sum and the product are the same space, so the difference appears only for infinite families.

### Maps out of a direct sum

[[A linear map out of R² ⊕ R|RWST1DAHFM]] is built from one map on each piece.
With $f_1(x, y) = x + y$ on $\R^2$ and $f_2(t) = 2t$ on $\R$, the combined map is
$$f((x, y), t) = f_1(x, y) + f_2(t) = x + y + 2t.$$
It is the only linear map that agrees with $f_1$ on the pairs $((x, y), 0)$ and with $f_2$ on the pairs $((0, 0), t)$.
In general, linear maps $f_i \colon V_i \to X$ come from exactly one linear map $f \colon \bigoplus_i V_i \to X$ with $f \circ \iota_i = f_i$, and $f$ sends $(v_i)_i$ to the finite sum $\sum_i f_i(v_i)$.
This is the [[universal property of the external direct sum|N04WS31KXB]], and it says that the direct sum is the [[coproduct|4FJ0S71P58]] of vector spaces.

### The rules you can use

Each canonical injection $\iota_j$ is linear and injective.
The images $\iota_j(V_j)$ together span the direct sum, and each of them meets the sum of the others only in $0$, since a family is zero when all its entries are zero.
So if we choose a basis of each $V_i$ and place each basis vector in its own slot, we get a basis of $\bigoplus_i V_i$, and
$$\dim \bigoplus_{i \in I} V_i = \sum_{i \in I} \dim V_i,$$
where the sum is a sum of cardinals when the family or the dimensions are infinite.

For subspaces $U_1, \dots, U_k$ of one space $V$, the addition map $U_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_k \to V$, $(u_1, \dots, u_k) \mapsto u_1 + \cdots + u_k$, is linear.
It is injective exactly when the sum $U_1 + \cdots + U_k$ is direct, and it is an isomorphism exactly when $V = U_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_k$ as an internal direct sum.
This is the precise link between the external and the internal notion.

### What it is not

The union of two subspaces is not their direct sum, and it is usually not even a subspace.
The union of the two axes of $\R^2$ contains $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, but not their sum $(1, 1)$.
The sum $U + W$ of two subspaces is not the external sum either.
With $U = W = \R$, the sum $U + W$ is $\R$, while $\R \oplus \R$ is $\R^2$.

### Watch out

The first common mistake is to allow infinitely many nonzero entries.
That gives the product, which is strictly larger when infinitely many factors are nonzero: for countably many copies of $\R$, the sequence $(1, 1, 1, \dots)$ is in the product but not in the sum.
The second mistake is to think that $V \oplus V$ is $V$.
The external sum keeps the two copies apart, so $\dim(\R \oplus \R) = 2$, and only the sum $V + V$ of a subspace with itself collapses to $V$.

### Try it

First, compute $((2, 0), 1) - 3((1, 1), -1)$ in $\R^2 \oplus \R$.
Work part by part: the answer is $((2 - 3, 0 - 3), 1 + 3) = ((-1, -3), 4)$.

Second, find the dimension of $\R^3 \oplus P_2 \oplus \R$, where $P_2$ is the space of real polynomials of degree at most $2$.
The dimensions add, so the answer is $3 + 3 + 1 = 7$.

Third, decide whether the sequence $(1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, \dots)$ lies in $\R^{(\N)}$.
It does not, because infinitely many of its entries equal $1$, so it lies only in the product $\R^{\N}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The external direct sum puts spaces side by side.
A vector of $\R^2 \oplus \R$ is a vector of the plane together with one extra number, such as $((1, 2), 3)$.
We add two such vectors part by part, and we scale both parts by the same number.
If we drop the inner brackets, $((1, 2), 3)$ is just $(1, 2, 3)$, so $\R^2 \oplus \R$ is $\R^3$ written in two blocks.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: we build a linear map $g \colon \R^2 \oplus P_1 \to \R$ that adds the first coordinate of the plane vector to the value of the polynomial at $1$.
The two pieces are $g_1(a, b) = a$ on $\R^2$ and $g_2(p) = p(1)$ on $P_1$, and both are linear.
The universal property gives one linear map on the whole sum:
$$g((a, b), p) = g_1(a, b) + g_2(p) = a + p(1).$$
At the vector $((1, 2), 3 + x)$, this gives $1 + (3 + 1) = 5$.
Check: $g((a, b), 0) = a$ and $g((0, 0), p) = p(1)$, so $g$ agrees with both pieces.
On the basis $((1, 0), 0)$, $((0, 1), 0)$, $((0, 0), 1)$, $((0, 0), x)$, the values of $g$ are $1, 0, 1, 1$, so the matrix of $g$ is the row $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the block row of $g_1$ and $g_2$.
So a direct sum turns the design of one linear map into separate designs on each piece.
Watch out: for an infinite family the recipe works only because each vector has finitely many nonzero parts, and it fails on the product.

### L3 human

In the category of vector spaces over $F$, the external direct sum is the coproduct, and the [[direct product|0EX2HPXPJ6]] is the product.
For a finite family one space has both properties, so finite direct sums are biproducts, and linear maps between them are block matrices.
When infinitely many $V_i$ are nonzero, the canonical map $\bigoplus_i V_i \to \prod_i V_i$ is injective but not surjective.
[[Every vector space has a basis|00MS9VMQ9Z]] $B$, and then it is isomorphic to $F^{(B)}$, so every vector space is a direct sum of copies of $F$; for modules over a ring, the spaces of this form are the free modules.
Duality exchanges the two constructions, $\left(\bigoplus_i V_i\right)^* \cong \prod_i V_i^*$, and the sub-area on [[duality|9K7FFF26QR]] uses this to compare an infinite-dimensional space with its dual.
