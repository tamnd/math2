## Summary

The negation of a [[proposition|0ZGC7ZESYN]] $p$ is the proposition $\neg p$, read "not $p$", which is true when $p$ is false and false when $p$ is true.
Negation is a [[logical connective|QS0XVAN9BG]] with one input, and its truth table has two rows; the precise definition is on the page [[negation (definition)|0XR7A7RSXB]].
In a proof, $\neg p$ is the same as "$p$ leads to a contradiction": to prove $\neg p$, we assume $p$ and derive a contradiction.
Negation is the tool for saying that a claim fails, so it is needed for refutations, counterexamples, the contrapositive and proof by contradiction.

## Motivation

Mathematics needs to say that something does not hold: "$\sqrt{2}$ is not rational", "$7$ is not divisible by $3$", "the sequence does not converge".
Each of these sentences denies a claim, and its truth value is the opposite of the truth value of the claim.
To check a proof that uses such a sentence, we must know exactly which sentence is the denial.
For a simple claim this is easy, but for a compound claim with "and", "or" or "for every", the denial has a precise form that is often not the first guess.
The connective $\neg$ makes the denial exact, and the laws of negation tell how to move it inside a compound formula.

## Notation

The negation of $p$ is written $\neg p$ in this sub-area.
Other notations are $\sim p$, $p'$, $\overline{p}$ and, in programming, `!p`.
In the arithmetic of the truth values $1$ and $0$, the negation of $p$ is $1 - p$.
For relations, a slash marks the negation: $a \neq b$ is $\neg(a = b)$, $x \notin A$ is $\neg(x \in A)$, and $a \nmid b$ is $\neg(a \mid b)$.
The symbol $\neg$ binds more strongly than the binary connectives, so $\neg p \land q$ means $(\neg p) \land q$ and not $\neg(p \land q)$.
In Lean's Mathlib, the negation of `a` is `Not a`, written `¬a`, and `Not a` is defined as `a → False`.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The negation of $2 < x \leq 5$|~feb52056]] is "$x \leq 2$ or $x > 5$", by De Morgan's law.
2. [[Proving "$\sqrt{2}$ is not rational" by negation introduction|~c72c717b]]: we assume that $\sqrt{2}$ is rational and reach a contradiction.
3. The negation of "$7$ is prime" is "$7$ is not prime". The first is true, so the second is false.
4. The negation of "$x \in \mathbb{Q}$" is "$x \notin \mathbb{Q}$". For $x = \pi$, the first is false and the second is true.
5. The negation of "$f$ is continuous and $f$ is bounded" is "$f$ is not continuous or $f$ is not bounded".

### Non-examples

1. [[The proposition "$n$ is negative" is not the negation of "$n$ is positive"|~1aa33acb]]: for $n = 0$ both are false.
2. "No prime is even" is not the negation of "every prime is even". Both are false, because $2$ is an even prime and $3$ is an odd prime. The negation is "some prime is not even"; see [[negating mathematical statements|K930V33BSC]].
3. "$x > 3$" is not the negation of "$x < 3$": for $x = 3$ both are false. The negation is "$x \geq 3$".

### Negating compound propositions

Negation moves inside a compound proposition by fixed laws.
The symbol $\equiv$ means [[logical equivalence|0JMR2HM2N2]].

1. $\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q$ and $\neg(p \lor q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q$. These are [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]].
2. $\neg(p \to q) \equiv p \land \neg q$. An [[implication|0NMG7W7Q11]] fails exactly when its hypothesis holds and its conclusion fails.
3. $\neg(p \leftrightarrow q) \equiv p \oplus q$, the [[exclusive or|~777e424f]].
4. $\neg \forall x\, P(x) \equiv \exists x\, \neg P(x)$ and $\neg \exists x\, P(x) \equiv \forall x\, \neg P(x)$. See [[negation of a universal statement|0P3A06QNDP]] and [[negation of an existential statement|05SZQKH75K]].

For $\land$, $\lor$ and the quantifiers the pattern is the same: push $\neg$ inward, change $\land$ to $\lor$, $\lor$ to $\land$, $\forall$ to $\exists$ and $\exists$ to $\forall$, and stop at the atomic propositions.

### Double negation

In classical logic $\neg\neg p \equiv p$; this is the [[double negation law|0NFAGWH84K]].
The direction $p \to \neg\neg p$ holds in [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]] too.
The direction $\neg\neg p \to p$ does not hold there; in Mathlib, `Classical.not_not` states $\neg\neg a \leftrightarrow a$ with classical logic.
A triple negation reduces to a single one in both logics: $\neg\neg\neg p \equiv \neg p$.

### Rules of inference for negation

In [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]], negation has an introduction rule and an elimination rule.

1. Negation introduction: if the assumption $p$ leads to a contradiction, we conclude $\neg p$ and discharge the assumption.
2. Negation elimination: from $p$ and $\neg p$ we conclude a contradiction, written $\bot$ or `False`.
3. From a contradiction any proposition follows. This is the principle of [[explosion|TTKZGJEEK7]]; in Mathlib, `absurd` turns proofs of `a` and `¬a` into a proof of any `b`.

Mathlib's definition of `Not a` as `a → False` makes these rules special cases of the rules for implication.
A proof of `¬a` is a function that turns every proof of `a` into a proof of `False`, so negation introduction is the introduction of an implication, and negation elimination is the application of that function.
Negation introduction proves $\neg p$ from a contradiction; it is valid intuitionistically.
[[Proof by contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]] in the strict sense proves $p$ from a contradiction with $\neg p$; it needs $\neg\neg p \to p$, so it is a classical rule.

### Basic properties

1. $\neg p$ is true exactly when $p$ is false; by [[bivalence|~e6dead3d]], $p$ and $\neg p$ have opposite truth values.
2. $p \land \neg p$ is false under every valuation. This is the [[law of noncontradiction|~f0f20560]].
3. $p \lor \neg p$ is true under every valuation. This is the [[law of excluded middle|0RYYK8PG4S]].
4. $p \to q \equiv \neg q \to \neg p$. This is the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]].
5. Negation of sets is the [[complement|0RKF1ZT5JJ]]: $x \notin A$ means $x \in A^c$.

### History

Aristotle stated the law of noncontradiction: a claim and its negation are not both true.
George Boole wrote "not $x$" as $1 - x$ in his algebra of logic.
Gottlob Frege took negation and the conditional as the basic connectives of his Begriffsschrift of 1879.
Luitzen Egbertus Jan Brouwer read $\neg p$ as "$p$ leads to an absurdity", and intuitionistic logic, formalized by Arend Heyting in 1930, keeps this reading.

### Common errors

1. A student takes the opposite statement for the negation. The negation of "$x > 0$" is "$x \leq 0$", not "$x < 0$".
2. A student negates the parts of a conjunction and keeps "and". The negation of "$n$ is even and $n > 10$" is "$n$ is odd or $n \leq 10$".
3. A student negates a universal statement by negating its predicate only. The negation of "every $x$ satisfies $P(x)$" is "some $x$ does not satisfy $P(x)$", not "no $x$ satisfies $P(x)$".

## Explanations

### L1 human

The negation of a claim says that the claim is wrong.
The negation of "$9$ is a square number" is "$9$ is not a square number".
The first claim is true, because $9 = 3 \times 3$, so the negation is false.
The negation of "$8$ is odd" is "$8$ is not odd", and this negation is true.
A claim and its negation always have opposite answers: one is true and the other is false.

### L2 human

We negate the statement "$n$ is even and $n > 10$, or $n = 3$" for an integer $n$, and we check one value.

1. Give the parts letters. Let $p$ be "$n$ is even", $q$ be "$n > 10$" and $r$ be "$n = 3$". The statement is $(p \land q) \lor r$.
2. Apply De Morgan's law to the outer "or": $\neg((p \land q) \lor r) \equiv \neg(p \land q) \land \neg r$.
3. Apply De Morgan's law to the inner "and": $\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q$.
4. The negation is $(\neg p \lor \neg q) \land \neg r$: "$n$ is odd or $n \leq 10$, and $n \neq 3$".
5. Check $n = 4$. The statement: $p$ T, $q$ F, so $p \land q$ is F, and $r$ is F, so the statement is F. The negation: $\neg q$ is T, so $\neg p \lor \neg q$ is T, and $\neg r$ is T, so the negation is T.

The typical use is in a proof by contradiction or a disproof: we write the exact negation of the claim before we try to prove it.

A trap: in step 4, "$n \leq 10$" is the negation of "$n > 10$"; "$n < 10$" would miss the case $n = 10$.

### L3 human

In a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]], negation is the complement: $\neg a$ is the unique element with $a \land \neg a = 0$ and $a \lor \neg a = 1$.
In a [[Heyting algebra|NG943XNQWH]], $\neg a$ is defined as $a \to 0$, the largest element whose meet with $a$ is $0$; it need not satisfy $a \lor \neg a = 1$, and this is the algebraic form of the failure of excluded middle in intuitionistic logic.
Mathlib's definition `Not a := a → False` is the same definition in type theory.
In hardware, negation is the NOT [[logic gate|AMH914MF2A]], and in arithmetic modulo $2$ it is $p \mapsto 1 + p$.
In [[paraconsistent logics|C7YWCR87QJ]], negation is weakened so that $p$ and $\neg p$ together do not imply every proposition.
The negation of quantified statements, which exchanges $\forall$ and $\exists$, is the first-order form of De Morgan's laws, and it reappears in the duality between the two quantifiers in the later sub-areas on first-order logic.
