## Summary

To deny a claim is to say that it fails, and negation is the logical tool for that.
The negation of "$91$ is prime" is "$91$ is not prime", and since $91 = 7 \cdot 13$ the first is false and the second is true.
For a [[proposition|0ZGC7ZESYN]] $p$, the negation $\neg p$, read "not $p$", is true when $p$ is false and false when $p$ is true.
It is the [[logical connective|QS0XVAN9BG]] with one input, and the [[formal definition|0XR7A7RSXB]] gives its two-row truth table.
In a proof, $\neg p$ means "$p$ leads to a contradiction", and Lean's Mathlib defines `Not a` as `a → False`.

## Motivation

Mathematics says often that something does not hold: "$\sqrt{2}$ is not rational", "$7$ is not divisible by $3$", "the sequence does not converge".
To check a proof that uses such a sentence, we must know exactly which sentence is the denial.
For a simple claim this is easy, but for a claim built with "and", "or", "if" or "for every", the exact denial is often not the first guess.
The connective $\neg$ makes the denial exact, and a few laws tell us how to move it inside a compound formula.
Every refutation, every counterexample, the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] and [[proof by contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]] depend on getting the negation right.

## Notation

Most books write $\neg p$; you will also see $\sim p$, $p'$, $\overline{p}$ and, in programming, `!p`.
With truth values $1$ and $0$, the negation of $p$ is $1 - p$.
For relations a slash marks the negation: $a \ne b$ is $\neg(a = b)$, $x \notin A$ is $\neg(x \in A)$ and $a \nmid b$ is $\neg(a \mid b)$.
The symbol $\neg$ binds more strongly than the two-input connectives, so $\neg p \land q$ means $(\neg p) \land q$, not $\neg(p \land q)$.
In Lean, `¬a` is notation for `Not a`.

## Remarks

### The truth table

Let $p$ be "$9$ is a square".
Then $p$ is T, because $9 = 3^2$, and $\neg p$, "$9$ is not a square", is F.
Let $p$ be "$8$ is odd" instead; now $p$ is F and $\neg p$ is T.
These two cases are the whole truth table:

| $p$ | $\neg p$ |
|---|---|
| T | F |
| F | T |

By the [[principle of bivalence|5DYNDW9526]] there is no third case, so $p$ and $\neg p$ always have opposite truth values.
That gives two laws at once: $p \land \neg p$ is false in every row, the [[law of noncontradiction|MP4R7T546Z]], and $p \lor \neg p$ is true in every row, the [[law of excluded middle|0RYYK8PG4S]].

### Negating compound statements

Negating "$2 < x \le 5$" means negating the conjunction "$2 < x$ and $x \le 5$".
It fails when either part fails, so [[its negation|1FKKCBBQTG]] is "$x \le 2$ or $x > 5$".
That is one of [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]]:
$$\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q, \qquad \neg(p \lor q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q.$$
An [[implication|0NMG7W7Q11]] fails exactly when its hypothesis holds and its conclusion fails, so $\neg(p \to q) \equiv p \land \neg q$.
The negation of a [[biconditional|0S6GMNJ62Y]] is the [[exclusive or|PTMR3P9J6C]], $\neg(p \leftrightarrow q) \equiv p \oplus q$.
For quantifiers the pattern continues: $\neg \forall x\, P(x) \equiv \exists x\, \neg P(x)$ and $\neg \exists x\, P(x) \equiv \forall x\, \neg P(x)$, as in [[negation of a universal statement|0P3A06QNDP]] and [[negation of an existential statement|05SZQKH75K]].
So you push $\neg$ inward, swap $\land$ with $\lor$ and $\forall$ with $\exists$, and stop at the atomic parts.

### Negation is not the opposite

"$n$ is negative" sounds like the denial of "$n$ is positive", but [[it is not|6EAWB0HSJZ]]: for $n = 0$ both are false.
The negation of "$n > 0$" is "$n \le 0$".
In the same way, "no prime is even" is not the negation of "every prime is even", since both are false: $2$ is an even prime and $3$ is an odd prime.
The negation is "some prime is not even", as [[negating mathematical statements|K930V33BSC]] explains.
Two opposites cannot both be true, but they can both be false, while a statement and its negation always split T and F.

### Double negation and the intuitionistic view

Classically $\neg\neg p \equiv p$; this is the [[double negation law|0NFAGWH84K]], and Mathlib states it as `Classical.not_not`.
The direction $p \to \neg\neg p$ holds in [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]] as well, since from $p$ any refutation of $p$ gives a contradiction.
The direction $\neg\neg p \to p$ fails there: a proof that $\neg p$ is absurd does not by itself produce a proof of $p$.
A triple negation collapses in both logics, $\neg\neg\neg p \equiv \neg p$.

### The rules of proof

In [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]], negation introduction says: if the assumption $p$ leads to a contradiction, conclude $\neg p$.
[[The proof that $\sqrt{2}$ is not rational|RZA4QFV5B8]] has this shape.
Negation elimination says: from $p$ and $\neg p$, conclude a contradiction, written $\bot$ or `False`.
From a contradiction every proposition follows, the principle of [[explosion|TTKZGJEEK7]], and Mathlib's `absurd` turns proofs of `a` and `¬a` into a proof of any `b`.
Because `Not a` is `a → False`, both rules are rules of implication, and both are intuitionistic.
Proof by contradiction in the strict sense proves $p$ by refuting $\neg p$, and that step needs $\neg\neg p \to p$, so it is classical.

### Watch out

Do not negate the predicate inside a quantifier and stop.
The negation of "every $x$ satisfies $P(x)$" is "some $x$ fails $P(x)$", not "no $x$ satisfies $P(x)$".
Do not keep "and" when you negate a conjunction either.
At $n = 4$ the statement "$n$ is even and $n > 10$" is F, so its negation must be T.
The correct negation "$n$ is odd or $n \le 10$" is T there, but the wrong form "$n$ is odd and $n \le 10$" is F.

### History

Aristotle stated the law of noncontradiction: a claim and its negation are not both true.
George Boole wrote "not $x$" as $1 - x$ in his algebra of logic.
Gottlob Frege took negation and the conditional as the basic connectives of his Begriffsschrift of 1879.
Brouwer read $\neg p$ as "$p$ leads to an absurdity", and Arend Heyting's formal intuitionistic logic of 1930 keeps this reading.

### Try it

Negate "$x \ge 0$ and $x^2 < 4$".
By De Morgan's law the negation is "$x < 0$ or $x^2 \ge 4$".

Negate "if $n$ is prime, then $n$ is odd" and decide which version is true.
The negation is "$n$ is prime and $n$ is even"; it is true for $n = 2$, so the implication fails for $n = 2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The negation of a claim says that the claim is wrong.
The negation of "$9$ is a square number" is "$9$ is not a square number".
The first claim is true, because $9 = 3 \times 3$, so the negation is false.
The negation of "$8$ is odd" is "$8$ is not odd", and this negation is true.
A claim and its negation always get opposite answers: one is true and the other is false.

### L2 human

Negate "$n$ is even and $n > 10$, or $n = 3$" for an integer $n$.
Name the parts: $p$ is "$n$ is even", $q$ is "$n > 10$" and $r$ is "$n = 3$", so the statement is $(p \land q) \lor r$.
De Morgan's law for the outer "or", and then for the inner "and", gives
$$\neg((p \land q) \lor r) \equiv \neg(p \land q) \land \neg r \equiv (\neg p \lor \neg q) \land \neg r.$$
In words, the negation is "$n$ is odd or $n \le 10$, and $n \ne 3$".
Check $n = 4$: $p$ is T and $q$ is F, so $p \land q$ is F, and $r$ is F, so the statement is F.
The negation has $\neg q$ T and $\neg r$ T, so it is T, as it must be.

Writing the exact negation is the first line of every disproof and every proof by contradiction.
Watch out: "$n \le 10$" is the negation of "$n > 10$", while "$n < 10$" would miss the case $n = 10$.

### L3 human

In a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]], negation is the complement: $\neg a$ is the unique element with $a \land \neg a = 0$ and $a \lor \neg a = 1$.
In a [[Heyting algebra|NG943XNQWH]], $\neg a$ is defined as $a \to 0$, the largest element whose meet with $a$ is $0$, and $a \lor \neg a = 1$ can fail; this is the algebraic face of the intuitionistic failure of excluded middle.
Mathlib's `Not a := a → False` is the same definition in type theory.
In hardware negation is the NOT [[logic gate|AMH914MF2A]], and modulo $2$ it is $p \mapsto 1 + p$.
[[Paraconsistent logics|C7YWCR87QJ]] weaken negation so that $p$ and $\neg p$ together do not imply everything.
The exchange of $\forall$ and $\exists$ under negation returns as the duality of the two quantifiers in the sub-areas on first-order logic.
