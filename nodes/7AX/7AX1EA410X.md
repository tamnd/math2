## Statement

Consider a statistically steady [[turbulent|VY307F8Z0S]] flow of an incompressible fluid along a smooth flat wall, with the [[no-slip condition|DF0M8YNQ3D]] at the wall.
Let $\rho$ be the density, $\nu$ the kinematic [[viscosity|NM5Q4845Z4]], $\tau_w$ the mean shear stress at the wall and $u_\tau = \sqrt{\tau_w / \rho}$ the friction velocity.
Let $U(y)$ be the mean velocity parallel to the wall at distance $y$, in the sense of the [[Reynolds decomposition|V47TX9AFKF]], and set $u^+ = U / u_\tau$ and $y^+ = y u_\tau / \nu$.
Let $\delta$ be the thickness of the boundary layer, or the half-width of the channel.

1. In the viscous sublayer, roughly $y^+ < 5$, one has $u^+ = y^+$.
2. In the overlap region, roughly $y^+ > 30$ and $y$ small compared with $\delta$, one has
$$u^+ = \frac{1}{\kappa} \ln y^+ + B,$$
with constants $\kappa \approx 0.41$, the von Kármán constant, and $B \approx 5$.

## Remarks

The linear law follows from the fact that very close to the wall the viscous stress $\rho \nu\, dU/dy$ carries all of $\tau_w$.
The logarithmic law comes from an overlap argument.
Near the wall $U$ depends on $y$ only through $y^+$, and far from it the velocity defect depends only on $y/\delta$.
The two forms can agree on a common range only if $dU/dy = u_\tau / (\kappa y)$ there.
The buffer layer between the two regions has no simple formula.
On a rough wall the logarithmic law survives but with a different additive constant, and in strong pressure gradients or near separation it fails.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Very close to a wall a turbulent flow slows down to zero speed.
Once one measures speed and distance in units made from the wall friction and the viscosity, the velocity profile is the same for pipes, channels and boundary layers.
Right at the wall the speed grows linearly with the distance, and a little farther out it grows like the logarithm of the distance.
This universal profile is one of the most robust facts of turbulence.

### L2 human

The law of the wall is the basis of wall functions in turbulence models, which avoid resolving the thin viscous sublayer.
Integrated across a pipe, it gives the logarithmic friction law for smooth pipes.
The values of $\kappa$ and $B$ are empirical, and their exact values and universality are still debated.
