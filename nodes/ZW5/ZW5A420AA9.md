## Example

"If it rains, the street is wet; the street is wet; so it rains."
The argument sounds fine, but it is not a [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]], and one row of a truth table shows why.
Let $p$ and $q$ be propositional variables.
We show $p \to q, \; q \not\models p$.

1. Take the truth assignment $v$ with $v(p) = 0$ and $v(q) = 1$.
2. The first premise has $\bar v(p \to q) = 1$, because an [[implication|0NMG7W7Q11]] with a false antecedent is true.
3. The second premise has $\bar v(q) = 1$.
4. The conclusion has $\bar v(p) = 0$.
5. So $v$ makes both premises true and the conclusion false, and $v$ is a [[countermodel|FWHBT4HABW]].
6. Hence the condition in the definition of $\models$ fails, and $p \to q, \; q \not\models p$.

Check: in the table of $p \to q$, the row $p = 0$, $q = 1$ has the value $1$, so step 2 is right.

## Remarks

The form "if $p$ then $q$; $q$; so $p$" is the fallacy of [[affirming the consequent|J7KFCFQRBT]]; [[another worked case|2XXTDXK1ZC]] shows it with a mathematical statement.
The countermodel above is the only one.
Of the other three assignments, $(1, 1)$ makes $p$ true, $(1, 0)$ makes $p \to q$ false, and $(0, 0)$ makes $q$ false.

If we add the converse $q \to p$ as a premise, the consequence holds, since $q, \; q \to p \models p$ by [[modus ponens|H8H9ZYZZEJ]].
By the semantic deduction theorem the failure also says that $(p \to q) \to (q \to p)$ is not a tautology.

Watch out.
A countermodel shows that the form is invalid, not that the conclusion is false.
In the assignment $v(p) = v(q) = 1$ the premises and the conclusion are all true, and still the form is invalid.

Try it.
Is "if $p$ then $q$; not $p$; so not $q$" valid?
No: the same assignment $v(p) = 0$, $v(q) = 1$ makes $p \to q$ and $\neg p$ true and $\neg q$ false.
This form is called denying the antecedent.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Consider the argument "if it rains, the street is wet; the street is wet; so it rains".
It does not follow.
The street could be wet because someone washed it while the sky was dry.
That one situation, no rain and a wet street, makes both premises true and the conclusion false, and one such situation is enough to break the argument.

### L2 human

We search for all countermodels of $p \to q, \; q \models p$.

1. A countermodel needs $\bar v(p) = 0$.
2. It needs $\bar v(q) = 1$.
3. Then $\bar v(p \to q) = 1$, so the first premise holds as well.
4. So $v(p) = 0$, $v(q) = 1$ is a countermodel, and it is the only assignment that meets steps 1 and 2.

To refute an argument form, one countermodel is enough.
There is no need to show that the conclusion is false in general.
