## Example

Color a pair of points by whether they are comparable, and Ramsey's theorem for pairs hands you an infinite chain or an infinite antichain.
We check that this gives $\mathrm{RT}^2_2 \to \mathrm{CAC}$ over [[RCA0|XQENB067R3]], an arrow of the [[reverse mathematics zoo|E3X5B175ZR]].
$\mathrm{CAC}$ says that every infinite [[partial order|0QV2JS9RJ3]] $(P, \preceq)$ with $P \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ has an infinite chain or an infinite [[antichain|03HF498P1J]].
A *chain* is a set of pairwise comparable elements, and an *antichain* is a set of pairwise incomparable elements.
We argue in $\mathsf{RCA}_0 + \mathrm{RT}^2_2$.

1. Let $(P, \preceq)$ be a partial order with $P$ infinite.
   $\mathsf{RCA}_0$ proves that $P$ is the range of a strictly increasing function $p \colon \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$, the enumeration of $P$ in order.
2. For $m < n$, put $c(\{m, n\}) = 0$ if $p(m) \preceq p(n)$ or $p(n) \preceq p(m)$, and $c(\{m, n\}) = 1$ otherwise.
   The definition uses only $p$ and $\preceq$ and no unbounded quantifier, so $c$ exists by $\Delta^0_1$ comprehension.
3. By $\mathrm{RT}^2_2$ there is an infinite set $H$ on whose pairs $c$ is constant.
4. Let $G = \{p(m) : m \in H\}$.
   Since $p(m) \ge m$, we have $y \in G$ if and only if some $m \le y$ has $m \in H$ and $p(m) = y$, so $G$ exists by $\Delta^0_1$ comprehension.
5. $G$ is infinite, because $p$ is one-to-one and $H$ is infinite.
6. If the constant color is $0$, any two elements of $G$ are comparable, so $G$ is an infinite chain.
7. If the constant color is $1$, any two distinct elements of $G$ are incomparable, so $G$ is an infinite antichain.

Steps 6 and 7 cover both colors, so $(P, \preceq)$ has an infinite chain or an infinite antichain, which is $\mathrm{CAC}$.

## Remarks

The proof uses one application of $\mathrm{RT}^2_2$ and no induction beyond the $\Sigma^0_1$ induction of $\mathsf{RCA}_0$.
The converse fails: Hirschfeldt and Shore showed in 2007 that $\mathrm{CAC}$ does not imply $\mathrm{RT}^2_2$ over $\mathsf{RCA}_0$.
The colorings that come from partial orders are special, since comparability interacts with transitivity, and a general coloring has no such structure.
If you color a pair by the order between $p(m)$ and $p(n)$ in a linear order instead, the same steps prove $\mathrm{RT}^2_2 \to \mathrm{ADS}$ directly.
Step 4 is easy to forget: the homogeneous set $H$ is a set of indices, and the chain or antichain must be a set of elements of $P$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a large group of people, where some pairs can be ranked against each other and some cannot.
Color a pair red when the two can be ranked and blue when they cannot.
Ramsey's theorem gives an endless group in which all pairs have one color.
If all pairs are red, everyone in the group can be ranked against everyone else, and this is a chain.
If all pairs are blue, no two of them can be ranked, and this is an antichain.

### L2 human

Take the positive integers ordered by divisibility, so $P = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ and $p(m) = m + 1$.
The indices $1$ and $3$ point to the elements $2$ and $4$, so $c(\{1, 3\}) = 0$, because $2$ divides $4$.
The indices $1$ and $2$ point to $2$ and $3$, so $c(\{1, 2\}) = 1$.
The indices $m$ with $p(m)$ a power of $2$ form a homogeneous set of color $0$, and step 4 turns it into the chain $1 \mid 2 \mid 4 \mid 8 \mid \cdots$.
The indices $m$ with $p(m)$ prime form a homogeneous set of color $1$, and they give the antichain of primes, since no prime divides another.
The typical use of the argument is to show that a combinatorial principle is at most as strong as $\mathrm{RT}^2_2$.
The trap is to apply $\mathrm{RT}^2_2$ directly to $P$ as if it were $\mathbb{N}$; the enumeration $p$ of step 1 is what makes the coloring a coloring of $[\mathbb{N}]^2$.
