## Definition

Consider the flow of a viscous fluid governed by the [[Navier–Stokes equations|18KXZHVF4M]].
The flow is *turbulent* when its velocity field has the following properties.

1. It is irregular in space and time, and sensitive to small changes in the initial and boundary data, so that only statistical quantities are reproducible.
2. It is rotational, with strong three-dimensional fluctuations of the [[vorticity|B3SXT9TWFA]].
3. Its fluctuations cover a wide continuous range of length and time scales, which widens as the [[Reynolds number|JH9EJ0HA0R]] grows.
4. It mixes momentum, heat and dissolved substances much faster than molecular diffusion does.
5. It dissipates kinetic energy into heat through [[viscosity|NM5Q4845Z4]] at the smallest scales, so it needs a continuous supply of energy to persist.

## Remarks

This is a descriptive definition; there is no accepted definition of turbulence as a precise mathematical property of solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations.
Example: the flow in a pipe at a Reynolds number of $10^5$, based on mean speed and diameter, has all five properties.
Non-example: the Kármán vortex street behind a cylinder at a Reynolds number near $100$ is unsteady but periodic and regular, so it fails clause 1 and is laminar.
Random but non-rotational motions, such as small-amplitude random water waves, also fail clause 2 and are not called turbulence.
Two-dimensional turbulence, studied as a model for large-scale atmospheric flows, keeps most of these properties but not the three-dimensional vortex stretching of clause 2.
