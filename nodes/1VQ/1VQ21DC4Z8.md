## Example

Let $L = \{E\}$ be the [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]] with one binary relation symbol $E$ and no other symbols.
Let $M = \{1, 2, 3\}$ and let $E^{\mathcal{M}} = \{ (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 1) \}$.
We check that $\mathcal{M} = (M, E^{\mathcal{M}})$ is an $L$-[[structure|983GZBQYT4]].

1. The domain is nonempty, because $1 \in M$.
2. The signature has no constant symbols and no function symbols, so there is nothing to check for them.
3. The symbol $E$ is binary, so its interpretation must be a subset of $M^2$.
   Each of the pairs $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$ and $(3, 1)$ has both entries in $M$, so $E^{\mathcal{M}} \subseteq M^2$.

So $\mathcal{M}$ is an $L$-structure.
As a [[directed graph|0KFYGSHHF5]], it is the directed cycle $1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1$.

## Remarks

A directed graph with vertex set $V$ is the same thing as an $\{E\}$-structure with domain $V$.
The vertices form the domain, and the edges form the [[binary relation|05AVARPXA2]] $E^{\mathcal{M}}$.
Since every subset of $M^2$ is allowed, the domain $\{1, 2, 3\}$ carries $2^{3^2} = 2^9 = 512$ different $\{E\}$-structures, from the graph with no edges to the graph with all nine pairs, loops included.

A simple undirected graph is the special case in which $E^{\mathcal{M}}$ is symmetric and irreflexive.
These two conditions are sentences that a structure may or may not satisfy, not parts of the definition of a structure.
If we add the pair $(3, 4)$ to $E^{\mathcal{M}}$ but keep $M = \{1, 2, 3\}$, we no longer have a structure, because $4 \notin M$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A directed graph is a set of dots with arrows between some of them.
To see it as a structure, take the dots as the domain and read $E(x, y)$ as "there is an arrow from $x$ to $y$".
Here the dots are $1$, $2$ and $3$, and the arrows go from $1$ to $2$, from $2$ to $3$ and from $3$ back to $1$.
Any choice of arrows between the three dots gives a structure, and there are $512$ such choices.

### L2 human

We use the structure to read the sentence $\forall x \, \exists y \, E(x, y)$, "each vertex has an outgoing edge".
For each vertex we look for a witness $y$:
$$1 \mapsto 2, \qquad 2 \mapsto 3, \qquad 3 \mapsto 1,$$
and each of the pairs $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$, $(3, 1)$ lies in $E^{\mathcal{M}}$.
So the sentence is true in $\mathcal{M}$ by the clauses of [[satisfaction|2RBQPV02AS]].
If we remove the edge $(3, 1)$, the vertex $3$ has no outgoing edge, and the sentence becomes false.
The typical use is to express graph properties as first-order sentences in the signature $\{E\}$.
Watch out: $E$ need not be symmetric, and here $E(1, 2)$ holds while $E(2, 1)$ does not.
