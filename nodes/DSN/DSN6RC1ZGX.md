## Problem

Let $A$ be a finite algebra of finite signature, and let $V(A)$ be the [[variety|AJNZJD8442]] generated by $A$.
Suppose that for every natural number $n$, the variety $V(A)$ contains a finite [[subdirectly irreducible|DASF939439]] algebra with more than $n$ elements.
Must $V(A)$ contain an infinite subdirectly irreducible algebra?

## Remarks

Quackenbush raised the question in 1971, in the paper "Equational classes generated by finite algebras".
In the same paper he proved the converse: if $V(A)$ contains an infinite subdirectly irreducible algebra, then it contains arbitrarily large finite ones.
The proof uses that $V(A)$ is [[locally finite|8QPW65PZVW]].

The version without the finite signature, the unrestricted Quackenbush problem, has a negative answer.
McKenzie, in 1993 (published 1996), built a finite algebra with infinitely many operations whose variety has arbitrarily large finite subdirectly irreducible members but no infinite one.

The restricted problem, stated above, has a positive answer in many cases.
The answer is yes for finite groups, for finite semigroups, and for finite algebras that generate a [[congruence-modular|QAQAS3JWXW]] variety.
For finite algebras that generate a [[congruence-distributive|9GZ7F6ETC6]] variety the answer is yes vacuously, since by [[Jónsson's lemma|VT72G3BA99]] the subdirectly irreducible members have at most $\abs{A}$ elements.
Willard listed the restricted problem as open in 2017.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every algebra in a variety is built from irreducible pieces, the subdirectly irreducible algebras.
Start with one finite algebra with finitely many operations and look at all the pieces in the variety it generates.
If the finite pieces get bigger and bigger, it seems natural that an infinite piece should exist too.
Quackenbush asked whether this is always true.
With infinitely many operations it can fail, but with finitely many the question is still a hard test of the theory.
