## Summary

The polynomial $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for $n = 0, 1, \ldots, 39$, and at $n = 40$ it equals $41^2$.
Forty successes did not make a proof, and one case ended the pattern.
The pages here study evidence of that kind: what it can do, what it cannot do, and how mathematicians use it before a proof exists.
They are part of [[reasoning and proof|SGSVYFDSYK]].

The story starts with Pólya's [[plausible reasoning|8DHD3H1CJ9]], the patterns by which a checked consequence or an analogy makes a conjecture more credible.
[[Experimental mathematics|BPEPTN0TT9]] turns those patterns into a method: compute many digits, detect a relation, test it at higher precision, and then prove it.
[[Numerical evidence|JFD3QWCX8Z]] can be enormous and still mislead, as the Pólya and Mertens conjectures show.
Small cases mislead even more often, which is Guy's [[strong law of small numbers|8AFXPXCE3N]], and [[patterns that eventually fail|A2KSJSQSF6]] collects the classic cases.

Evidence also comes from arguments that are not proofs.
[[Heuristic arguments|VCQQNJ3SEZ]] model the primes as random and predict counts such as the number of twin primes.
[[Forming conjectures|R2T42N5XGK]] describes how a precise statement grows out of tables and special cases.
[[Machine-assisted discovery|D1YQW43N3K]] now proposes formulas and constructions, and its claims need the same tests.
[[Physical arguments|THJV0NFPDQ]] let a lever or a soap film suggest a result, as Archimedes did for the volume of the sphere.
In every case the result is a conjecture until a proof arrives.

## Remarks

### Reading order

1. [[Plausible reasoning|8DHD3H1CJ9]]: Pólya's patterns, by which a verified consequence or an analogy makes a conjecture more credible.
2. [[Experimental mathematics|BPEPTN0TT9]]: computation to find patterns and closed forms, with integer relation algorithms such as PSLQ.
3. [[Numerical evidence for conjectures|JFD3QWCX8Z]]: how much checking supports a conjecture, and the Pólya, Mertens and Skewes cases where it misleads.
4. [[Strong law of small numbers|8AFXPXCE3N]]: Guy's law that small cases agree by coincidence, and his second law.
5. [[Patterns that eventually fail|A2KSJSQSF6]]: $n^2 + n + 41$, the Fermat numbers, the circle regions $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 31$ and the Borwein integrals.
6. [[Heuristic arguments in mathematics|VCQQNJ3SEZ]]: probabilistic models of the primes, dimension counts and scaling arguments, with their limits.
7. [[Forming conjectures|R2T42N5XGK]]: from tables, differences and special cases to a precise statement that is tested before a proof.
8. [[Machine-assisted mathematical discovery|D1YQW43N3K]]: conjecture generators and search systems from Graffiti to AlphaEvolve, and how their claims are checked.
9. [[Physical arguments in mathematics|THJV0NFPDQ]]: levers, strings, soap films and currents that suggest results, and the proofs they still need.

### Prerequisites

The pages assume [[mathematical proof|0B94TKFQJX]], [[conjecture|WMEMMHZ3BH]] and [[deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning|5V35SGEN1T]], from the start of [[proof, theorems and mathematical method|N4P3E53HM0]].
They also use the [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]], which settles many conjectures that numerical evidence had supported.
Pages 1, 4, 5, 7 and 9 need only school algebra, first-year calculus and elementary number theory.
Pages 2, 3, 6 and 8 use more: the prime number theorem, power series and some linear algebra.
