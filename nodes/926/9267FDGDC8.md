## Problem

Let $G$ be a finite connected [[graph|00C0BR7D7H]], viewed as a network of vertices joined by edges along which one can move.
An evader hides at a point of this network, on a vertex or inside an edge.
The evader moves along the network arbitrarily fast and always knows where the searchers are.
The evader cannot pass through a point occupied by a searcher.
A team of searchers makes moves one at a time.
A move places a searcher on a vertex, removes a searcher from a vertex, or slides a searcher along an edge.
The evader is caught when a searcher reaches the point where the evader is.
The *edge search number* $s(G)$ is the least number $k$ with the following property.
Some finite sequence of moves of $k$ searchers catches the evader, whatever the evader does.
Determine $s(G)$ for a given graph $G$, and describe the search strategies that achieve it.

## Remarks

This is the discrete, graph form of the pursuit–evasion problem; continuous forms in the plane or in a region are studied as differential games.
Small cases are easy: $s(G) = 1$ for a path, and $s(G) = 2$ for a cycle, since a single searcher sliding around a cycle leaves the cleared part open at both ends.
LaPaugh proved in 1993 that searchers never need to let a cleared edge become contaminated again, so optimal strategies can be taken monotone.
Megiddo, Hakimi, Garey, Johnson and Papadimitriou proved in 1988 that computing $s(G)$ is [[NP-hard|MHA48AWBXN]] for general graphs, and gave a linear-time algorithm for [[trees|062D9KXJB1]].
Many variants exist, such as the game of cops and robbers, in which pursuers and evader move along edges in turns and see each other.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Imagine a dark cave system with tunnels and junctions, and a very fast lost person or thief somewhere inside.
How many searchers do you need to be sure of finding them, if the person can run past any tunnel that you leave unguarded?
A single straight tunnel needs one searcher who walks from one end to the other.
A tunnel that forms a loop needs two, because one searcher alone always leaves a gap behind.
For complicated networks, finding the smallest team is a hard computational problem.
