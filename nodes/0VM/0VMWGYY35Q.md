## Statement

1. Euclidean case. Let $V$ be a real inner product space of finite dimension $n$. Then each [[linear isometry|0717XXPWM6]] $T \colon V \to V$ is a composition of at most $n$ [[reflections|06BRDF3F0E]] in hyperplanes of $V$.
2. Matrix form. Each $Q \in O(n)$ is a product of at most $n$ matrices $I_n - 2uu^T$ with $u \in \R^n$ a unit vector.
3. General case. Let $V$ be a vector space of finite dimension $n$ over a field of characteristic not $2$, with a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form $B$. Then each element of the orthogonal group $O(V, B)$ is a composition of at most $n$ reflections $x \mapsto x - 2\frac{B(x, u)}{B(u, u)}u$ with $B(u, u) \neq 0$.

The identity is the composition of zero reflections.
The proof node proves parts 1 and 2.
Part 3 is stated here with the reference, and its proof is longer, because a vector $u \neq 0$ can have $B(u, u) = 0$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The bound $n$ is sharp. A product of $k$ reflections fixes each vector of the intersection of $k$ hyperplanes, a subspace of dimension at least $n - k$. The map $-\id$ fixes only $0$, so it needs $n$ reflections.
- Finite dimension is needed. On the space $\ell^2$ of square-summable sequences, the right shift keeps norms but is not surjective, and each finite product of reflections is invertible.
- Part 3 needs characteristic not $2$. In characteristic $2$, the coefficient $2$ is $0$, and the formula gives the identity map.

### Consequences

- Each element of [[$SO(n)$|6WBVP2PB1Y]] is a product of an even number of reflections, at most $n$, because each reflection has determinant $-1$.
- Each rotation of $\R^3$ is a product of two reflections in planes through its axis. An element of $O(3)$ with determinant $-1$ is a product of one or three reflections.
- Each rotation $R(\theta)$ of $\R^2$ is the product of two reflections in lines at the angle $\theta/2$, see [[this example|SRN03D73AJ]].

### Converse

Each composition of reflections is a linear isometry, because each reflection is one, by [[the reflection theorem|FEER10D15S]].
So the linear isometries of $V$ are exactly the compositions of at most $n$ reflections.

### Generalizations

- Part 3 applies to the [[indefinite orthogonal groups|M2900DMEW6]] over $\R$, for example to the Lorentz group, with reflections in vectors $u$ with $B(u, u) \neq 0$.
- Each element of $U(n)$ is a product of at most $n$ complex reflections $x \mapsto x - (1 - \lambda)\frac{\ip{x}{u}}{\ip{u}{u}}u$ with $\abs{\lambda} = 1$. The proof uses an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $u_1, \dots, u_n$, one reflection for each $u_j$.

## History

The theorem is named after Cartan and Dieudonné.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A mirror flips space across a flat wall through the origin.
Each motion that keeps lengths and the origin is a chain of such flips.
In space of dimension $n$, at most $n$ flips are needed.
In the plane, a turn is two flips in mirrors that meet at half the angle of the turn.

### L2 human

Worked example: $T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, see [[this example|YXTM3P2YV8]].

1. $Te_1 = -e_2$, so $u = Te_1 - e_1 = -(1, 1, 0)$. The reflection $s_{(1, 1, 0)}$ swaps $e_1$ and $-e_2$.
2. $S = s_{(1, 1, 0)}T$ fixes $e_1$, and on the plane $e_1^\perp$ it is a rotation by $-\frac{\pi}{2}$.
3. $Se_2 = -e_3$, so the next reflection is $s_{(0, 1, 1)}$, and $s_{(0, 1, 1)}S = s_{(0, 0, 1)}$.
4. So $T = s_{(1, 1, 0)}s_{(0, 1, 1)}s_{(0, 0, 1)}$, with three reflections.

The typical use: a fact that holds for each reflection and is kept under products holds for each orthogonal map.
A trap: the reflections in a decomposition are not unique, but the parity of their number is fixed by $\det T$.

### L3 human

The theorem says that $O(V, B)$ is generated by involutions whose fixed spaces are hyperplanes.
The function that sends $T$ to the least number of reflections is the reflection length on $O(V, B)$.
For the Euclidean case, the reflection length of $T$ is the codimension of the fixed space $\ker(T - \id)$.
The theory of reflection groups and Coxeter groups studies the finite subgroups generated by reflections, and it belongs to group theory.
