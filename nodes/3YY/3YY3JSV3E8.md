## Example

Let $G$ be a connected [[topological group|92700QAB5Y]] with identity $e$, and let $U$ be an open set with $e \in U$.
Let $H$ be the [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] generated by $U$: the set of all finite products $u_1^{\pm 1} u_2^{\pm 1} \cdots u_k^{\pm 1}$ with $u_i \in U$.
We prove $H = G$ by the [[open-closed argument|1JJCDJB7FM]].

1. For each $g \in G$, the left translation $x \mapsto gx$ is a [[homeomorphism|0QT00PB5G2]] of $G$. Its inverse is $x \mapsto g^{-1}x$, and both maps are continuous because multiplication is continuous.
2. So for each $g \in G$ and each open set $W$, the set $gW$ is open.
3. Non-empty: $e \in U \subseteq H$.
4. Open: let $h \in H$. Then $hU$ is open by step 2, and $h = he \in hU$. Each element $hu$ with $u \in U$ is a product of two elements of $H$, so $hU \subseteq H$. So $H = \bigcup_{h \in H} hU$ is a union of open sets, and $H$ is open.
5. Closed: the left [[cosets|564VVAQTNR]] $gH$ of $H$ partition $G$, and each coset $gH$ is open by steps 2 and 4.
6. The complement $G \setminus H$ is the union of the cosets $gH$ with $g \notin H$, so it is open. Hence $H$ is closed.
7. $G$ is connected, and $H$ is a non-empty clopen subset. So $H = G$.

A check in the group $(\mathbb{R}, +)$, with $U = (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$ for some $\varepsilon > 0$:
for a real number $x$, choose an integer $n \ge 1$ with $\lvert x \rvert / n < \varepsilon$.
Then $x = \tfrac{x}{n} + \cdots + \tfrac{x}{n}$ ($n$ terms), and each term lies in $U$, so $x \in H$.

## Remarks

1. The proof shows more: each open subgroup of a topological group is also closed. This uses steps 5 and 6 only.
2. Connectedness is needed. In the multiplicative group $\mathbb{R}^\times$ of non-zero reals, the open set $U = (\tfrac12, 2)$ contains $1$, and it generates the group of positive reals, which is not all of $\mathbb{R}^\times$. The group $\mathbb{R}^\times$ is not connected.
3. In a group that is not connected, the subgroup generated by a neighbourhood of $e$ contains the [[identity component|QMYZ798EAR]], because the identity component is connected and the subgroup is clopen.
4. For a Lie group, the example says that a connected Lie group is generated by any neighbourhood of $e$, for example by the image of a small ball under the exponential map.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of the real number line with addition.
Take only the small numbers between $-0.1$ and $0.1$.
By adding enough of them we reach any number: $5$ is the sum of fifty copies of $0.1$.
The example says the same for every group that is one connected piece: small steps near the identity, repeated, reach every element.

### L2 human

We see why the closed step works in the circle group.

1. Let $G = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \lvert z \rvert = 1 \}$ with multiplication. It is connected, because it is the continuous image of $[0, 2\pi]$ under $t \mapsto e^{it}$.
2. Let $U = \{ e^{it} : \lvert t \rvert < 0.1 \}$. It is open in $G$ and contains $1$.
3. The subgroup $H$ generated by $U$ contains $e^{it}$ for $\lvert t \rvert < 0.1$, and so $e^{ikt}$ for each integer $k$.
4. Each $e^{is}$ with $s \in [0, 2\pi)$ equals $(e^{is/n})^n$, and $\lvert s/n \rvert < 0.1$ for $n \ge 63$, because $2\pi / 63 < 0.0998$.
5. So $H = G$, as the example predicts.

The typical use is to reduce a statement about all elements of a connected group to elements near the identity, for example to show that a continuous homomorphism is fixed by its values near $e$.
A trap is to apply the result to a disconnected group, such as an orthogonal group $O(n)$, where a small neighbourhood of $e$ inside $SO(n)$ generates only the rotations $SO(n)$.
