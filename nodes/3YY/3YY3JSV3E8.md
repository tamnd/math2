## Example

Let $G$ be a connected [[topological group|92700QAB5Y]] with identity $e$, and let $U$ be an open set with $e \in U$.
Let $H$ be the [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] generated by $U$, the set of all finite products $u_1^{\pm 1} u_2^{\pm 1} \cdots u_k^{\pm 1}$ with $u_i \in U$.
We prove $H = G$ by the [[open-closed argument|1JJCDJB7FM]].

1. For each $g \in G$, the left translation $x \mapsto gx$ is a [[homeomorphism|0QT00PB5G2]] of $G$, with inverse $x \mapsto g^{-1}x$, since multiplication is continuous.
   So $gW$ is open for every open $W$.
2. Non-empty: $e \in U \subseteq H$.
3. Open: for $h \in H$, the set $hU$ is open, contains $h = he$, and lies in $H$, since each $hu$ is a product of elements of $H$.
   So $H = \bigcup_{h \in H} hU$ is open.
4. Closed: the left [[cosets|564VVAQTNR]] $gH$ partition $G$, and each is open by steps 1 and 3.
   The complement $G \setminus H$ is the union of the cosets $gH$ with $g \notin H$, so it is open, and $H$ is closed.
5. $G$ is connected and $H$ is a non-empty clopen subset, so $H = G$.

Check in $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ with $U = (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$: for real $x$, choose an integer $n \ge 1$ with $\lvert x \rvert / n < \varepsilon$.
Then $x$ is the sum of $n$ copies of $\tfrac{x}{n}$, each in $U$, so $x \in H$.

## Remarks

Steps 3 and 4 prove more: every open subgroup of a topological group is also closed.
Connectedness is needed for the last step.
In the multiplicative group $\mathbb{R}^\times$, the open set $(\tfrac12, 2)$ contains $1$ and generates only the positive reals, and $\mathbb{R}^\times$ is not connected.

In a group that is not connected, the subgroup generated by a neighborhood of $e$ is clopen, so it contains the [[identity component|QMYZ798EAR]], which is connected and contains $e$.
For a connected Lie group, any neighborhood of $e$ generates the group, for example the image of a small ball under the exponential map.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of the real number line with addition.
Take only the small numbers between $-0.1$ and $0.1$.
By adding enough of them you reach any number: $5$ is the sum of fifty copies of $0.1$.
The example says the same for every group that is one connected piece: small steps near the identity, repeated, reach every element.

### L2 human

We check the result in the circle group.

1. Let $G = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \lvert z \rvert = 1 \}$ with multiplication.
   It is connected, as the continuous image of $[0, 2\pi]$ under $t \mapsto e^{it}$.
2. Let $U = \{ e^{it} : \lvert t \rvert < 0.1 \}$, which is open in $G$ and contains $1$.
3. Each $e^{is}$ with $s \in [0, 2\pi)$ equals $(e^{is/n})^n$.
4. For $n \ge 63$, $s/n < 2\pi / 63 < 0.0998$, so $e^{is/n} \in U$ and $e^{is} \in H$.
5. So $H = G$, as the example predicts.

The result reduces a statement about a connected group to elements near the identity, for example to show that a continuous homomorphism is determined by its values near $e$.
Watch out for disconnected groups: in the orthogonal group $O(n)$, a small neighborhood of $e$ lies in $SO(n)$ and generates only $SO(n)$.
