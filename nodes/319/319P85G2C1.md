## Summary

Take the axioms of a group and ask whether every group is commutative.
The axioms cannot answer: the integers under addition are commutative, the permutations of three objects are not, and both are groups.
A theory that never gets stuck in this way, because it proves either the answer "yes" or the answer "no" to every question in its language, is called negation complete.

Exactly: a [[theory|92SWXST1ZM]] $T$ is negation complete when, for every [[sentence|DKFGQTRYZE]] $\varphi$ of its language,
$$T \vdash \varphi \quad \text{or} \quad T \vdash \neg \varphi.$$
For a [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]] theory this is the same as being a [[complete theory|655K8Y7VS8]] in the sense of model theory: all models of $T$ satisfy the same sentences.

## Motivation

An axiom system is written to settle the questions of its subject, and negation completeness says that it settles all of them.
In the 1920s Hilbert hoped for an axiom system for arithmetic that is consistent and negation complete, with axioms that a machine can check.
[[Gödel's first incompleteness theorem|X9WYNYE29C]] shows that this hope fails.
No consistent theory with a decidable set of axioms that contains [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] is negation complete.

The property is also the first step of many decision procedures.
If a consistent theory is negation complete and its theorems can be listed by a program, then the program decides every sentence.
This is how the decidability of [[Presburger arithmetic|4W0E9G7PPV]] and of the theory of [[real closed fields|DDY3Q3PJ6W]] is often organized.

## Notation

A theory that is not negation complete is called incomplete.
A sentence $\varphi$ with $T \nvdash \varphi$ and $T \nvdash \neg \varphi$ is called independent of $T$, or undecided by $T$.
Books on incompleteness often say just "complete" for "negation complete".
Do not confuse this with the completeness of first-order logic, which is a different property, of a proof system and not of a theory.

## Remarks

### Theories that decide everything

The simplest negation complete theory has the single axiom $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x = y)$, "there is exactly one thing".
All its models are isomorphic, so they agree on every sentence, and the [[completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]] turns this agreement into proofs; the [[one-element example|8C3H1JGG40]] checks this in steps.

[[True arithmetic|9YQFEPHWJW]], the set of all sentences true in $\mathbb{N}$, is negation complete for a cheaper reason.
Each sentence is true or false in $\mathbb{N}$, so the sentence or its negation is already an axiom.

Some natural theories with simple axioms are negation complete too.
The theory of [[dense linear orders|2W4S2H4VYA]] without endpoints, Presburger arithmetic and the theory of real closed fields are examples.
For dense linear orders, the axioms refute "there is a least element", since the axiom "no endpoints" says that below every element there is a smaller one.

Finally, every inconsistent theory is negation complete, because it proves every sentence.
This is why every incompleteness result must assume consistency.

### Theories that leave questions open

[[Robinson arithmetic is not negation complete|F14YSZ3XKZ]]: it proves neither $\forall x \, (0 + x = x)$ nor its negation.
The proof builds a model of $\mathsf{Q}$ with two extra elements in which $0 + a \ne a$, and compares it with $\mathbb{N}$.
The theory of groups leaves the commutative law open, as the Summary showed.
Peano arithmetic is incomplete as well, but here no simple pair of models is known in advance, and the first incompleteness theorem is needed.

### The rules you can use

For a consistent theory $T$, negation completeness holds exactly when all models of $T$ satisfy the same sentences.
The reason is the completeness theorem: $T \nvdash \varphi$ exactly when $T + \neg \varphi$ has a model.

The theory of a structure $M$, the set of all sentences true in $M$, is always negation complete, by the argument for true arithmetic.

A consistent, negation complete theory whose theorems are computably enumerable is [[decidable|PPGD5KW8JC]].
To decide $\varphi$, list the theorems until $\varphi$ or $\neg \varphi$ appears; one of them must appear, and consistency says that only the correct one does.

Every consistent theory has a consistent, negation complete extension in the same language, by [[Lindenbaum's lemma|SC0BSJ3GT6]].
The incompleteness theorem says that, for extensions of $\mathsf{Q}$, such an extension never has a decidable set of axioms.

### Watch out

Do not read "$T$ is incomplete" as "some true sentence is unprovable in $T$".
Negation completeness does not mention truth at all.
The theory of groups is incomplete, but the commutative law is neither true nor false until you choose a group.

Do not forget the language either.
The theory "there is exactly one thing" is negation complete in the language of pure equality.
Add a new constant $c$ and a unary predicate $P$, and the same axioms no longer decide the sentence $P(c)$.

### History

Gödel proved the completeness of first-order logic in his dissertation of 1929, published in 1930.
His incompleteness theorem of 1931, in "Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze der Principia Mathematica und verwandter Systeme I", is about negation completeness of theories.
The two words sound alike, but the theorems are about different things.

### Try it

Is the theory of fields negation complete?
No: it does not decide $1 + 1 = 0$, which holds in the field with two elements and fails in $\mathbb{Q}$.

Is the theory with the single axiom $\forall x \, (x = x)$, in the language of pure equality, negation complete?
No: it does not decide "there are at least two elements", which holds in a two-element set and fails in a one-element set.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A list of rules is negation complete if, for every yes-or-no question in its language, the rules prove "yes" or prove "no".
The rule "there is exactly one thing" is like that: every question about which things are equal has a fixed answer.
The rules for groups are not like that: they do not say whether $a \cdot b$ always equals $b \cdot a$.
Gödel showed that every list of rules for whole-number arithmetic that a computer can check, and that has no contradiction, leaves some question open.

### L2 human

We show that the theory of groups is incomplete, by the usual method: find two models that disagree.
The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ with addition form a group, and $a + b = b + a$ for all integers.
The permutations of $\{1, 2, 3\}$ form a group under composition.
Let $s$ swap $1$ and $2$, and let $t$ swap $2$ and $3$.
Then $s t$ sends $1$ to $2$, while $t s$ sends $1$ to $3$, so $s t \ne t s$.
So one model satisfies $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x y = y x)$ and the other satisfies its negation, and by soundness the theory proves neither sentence.
This is the typical way to show incompleteness when you know two models.
To show negation completeness you need the opposite: that all models agree, for example by the [[Łoś-Vaught test|ZR7Y5C235P]] or by quantifier elimination.

### L3 human

For consistent theories, negation completeness is the syntactic face of the model-theoretic notion of a complete theory, and the completeness theorem links the two.
Complete theories with computably enumerable theorems are decidable, so the undecidability of $\mathsf{Q}$ already implies that $\mathsf{Q}$ and all its consistent c.e. extensions are incomplete.
The incompleteness theorems sharpen this: they give an explicit undecided sentence, a $\Pi_1$ sentence built by the [[diagonal lemma|3PRW76471J]].
The complete theories that do exist in algebra and geometry are studied in model theory, where tests such as quantifier elimination prove completeness.
