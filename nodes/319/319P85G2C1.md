## Summary

The property of a logical system where every statement or its negation is provable within the system.
A [[theory|92SWXST1ZM]] $T$ is negation complete when, for every [[sentence|DKFGQTRYZE]] $\varphi$ of its language, $T \vdash \varphi$ or $T \vdash \neg \varphi$.
For [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]] theories this is the same as being a [[complete theory|655K8Y7VS8]] in the sense of model theory.

## Motivation

A theory is meant to settle the questions of its subject, and negation completeness says that it settles all of them.
Hilbert hoped for a negation complete and consistent axiom system for arithmetic.
[[Gödel's first incompleteness theorem|X9WYNYE29C]] shows that no consistent theory with a computably enumerable set of theorems that contains Robinson arithmetic is negation complete.

## Notation

A theory that is not negation complete is called incomplete, and a sentence $\varphi$ with $T \nvdash \varphi$ and $T \nvdash \neg \varphi$ is called independent of $T$, or undecided by $T$.
Many books say "complete" for "negation complete" in the context of incompleteness.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[True arithmetic is negation complete|9YQFEPHWJW]]: it contains every sentence true in $\N$ or its negation.
2. [[The theory of one-element structures is negation complete|8C3H1JGG40]]: the axiom $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x = y)$ decides every sentence of pure equality.
3. The theory of [[dense linear orders|2W4S2H4VYA]] without endpoints, [[Presburger arithmetic|4W0E9G7PPV]] and the theory of [[real closed fields|DDY3Q3PJ6W]] are negation complete.
4. Every inconsistent theory is negation complete, since it proves every sentence.

### Non-examples

1. [[Robinson arithmetic is not negation complete|F14YSZ3XKZ]]: it proves neither $\forall x \, (0 + x = x)$ nor its negation.
2. The theory of groups is not negation complete: it decides neither $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x \cdot y = y \cdot x)$ nor its negation.
3. Peano arithmetic is not negation complete, by the first incompleteness theorem.

### Basic properties

1. For a consistent theory $T$, negation completeness holds exactly when all models of $T$ satisfy the same sentences. This follows from the [[completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]]: $T \nvdash \varphi$ exactly when $T + \neg \varphi$ has a model.
2. The theory of a structure $M$, the set of sentences true in $M$, is negation complete.
3. A consistent, negation complete theory with a computably enumerable set of theorems is [[decidable|PPGD5KW8JC]]. To decide $\varphi$, list the theorems until $\varphi$ or $\neg \varphi$ appears; consistency makes the answer correct.
4. Every consistent theory has a consistent, negation complete extension in the same language, by Lindenbaum's lemma.
5. An extension of a negation complete theory in the same language is either the same set of theorems or inconsistent.

### History

The term distinguishes this syntactic property from the completeness of first-order logic that Gödel proved in his 1929 dissertation.
Gödel's 1931 incompleteness theorem is about negation completeness, not about the completeness of logic.

### Common errors

1. Confusing negation completeness of a theory with the completeness theorem: first-order logic is complete, yet $\mathsf{PA}$ is not negation complete.
2. Reading "$T$ is incomplete" as "some true sentence is unprovable": negation completeness does not mention truth, and an incomplete theory can also fail to prove false sentences.
3. Forgetting the language: a theory can be negation complete in one language and incomplete after new symbols are added.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A list of rules is negation complete if, for every yes-or-no question in its language, the rules prove the answer "yes" or the answer "no".
The rules "there is exactly one thing" are complete: every question about equality then has a fixed answer.
The rules of whole-number arithmetic that a computer can check are never complete, by Gödel's theorem.

### L2 human

1. To show that $T$ is incomplete, find two models of $T$ that disagree on one sentence.
2. For $\mathsf{Q}$, the models $\N$ and a model with two extra elements disagree on $\forall x \, (0 + x = x)$.
3. To show that $T$ is complete, show that all models satisfy the same sentences, for example by the [[Łoś-Vaught test|ZR7Y5C235P]] or by quantifier elimination.

The typical use is the first step of a decision procedure: a complete theory with c.e. theorems is decidable.
A trap: the inconsistent theory is negation complete, so incompleteness results must assume consistency.

### L3 human

Negation completeness is a syntactic property, and for consistent theories it is the same as completeness in model theory, by the completeness theorem.
The incompleteness theorems show that any consistent computably axiomatized theory that interprets $\mathsf{Q}$ has no consistent, negation complete c.e. extension.
Complete theories with c.e. theorems give decidable theories, so the undecidability of a theory such as $\mathsf{Q}$ also proves its incompleteness and that of its consistent c.e. extensions.
