## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 4 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ act on $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the dot product.
The matrix is [[symmetric|0N28QN4CYA]], so $x \mapsto Ax$ is [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]], and we find an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

1. The characteristic polynomial. Expand $\det(A - tI)$ along the first row. The middle entry is $0$, and the cofactor of the entry $-1$ in the corner is $0 \cdot (-1) - (4 - t)(-1) = 4 - t$:
$$\det(A - tI) = (4 - t)\big[(4 - t)(5 - t) - 1\big] - (4 - t) = (4 - t)\big[(4 - t)(5 - t) - 2\big].$$
2. Factor it. Since $(4 - t)(5 - t) - 2 = t^2 - 9t + 18 = (t - 3)(t - 6)$, the eigenvalues are $3$, $4$ and $6$. Check: their sum $13$ is the trace $4 + 4 + 5$.
3. The eigenvectors. Multiply out:
$$A \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 6 \\ -12 \end{pmatrix}.$$
So $(1, 1, 1)$, $(1, -1, 0)$ and $(1, 1, -2)$ are eigenvectors for $3$, $4$ and $6$.
4. Orthogonality. The three dot products are $1 - 1 + 0 = 0$, $1 + 1 - 2 = 0$ and $1 - 1 + 0 = 0$, so the vectors are pairwise orthogonal.
5. The orthogonal matrix. Their lengths are $\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{6}$. The unit vectors $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1, 1, 1)$, $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, -1, 0)$ and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1, 1, -2)$ are the columns of an [[orthogonal matrix|0QRBVFJ50X]] $Q$, and
$$Q^{\mathsf{T}} A Q = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}.$$

## Remarks

The three eigenvalues are distinct, so each eigenspace is a line, and step 4 needed no Gram-Schmidt step.
That is the content of [[orthogonality of eigenspaces|0JHGAV9H25]]: for a symmetric matrix, eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal.
The determinant gives a second check: $\det A = 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 = 72$, and the cofactor expansion along the first row gives $4 (20 - 1) - 4 = 72$.

The quadratic form is $x^{\mathsf{T}} A x = 4x_1^2 + 4x_2^2 + 5x_3^2 - 2x_1 x_3 - 2x_2 x_3$.
On the unit sphere, its largest value is $6$, at $\pm \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1, 1, -2)$, and its smallest value is $3$, at $\pm \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1, 1, 1)$, by the [[extreme values of the Rayleigh quotient|T7MFPSY009]].
For instance, at $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1, 1, 1)$ the form gives $\tfrac{1}{3}(4 + 4 + 5 - 2 - 2) = 3$.
All eigenvalues are positive, so $A$ is positive definite.

The orthogonal projection onto the eigenline of $3$ is $\tfrac{1}{3} J$, where $J$ is the $3 \times 3$ matrix of ones, because $\tfrac{1}{3}(1, 1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} (1, 1, 1) = \tfrac{1}{3} J$.
If you change the corner entries $-1$ to $-2$, the matrix stays symmetric and the theorem still applies, but the eigenvalues are no longer integers.

## Explanations

### L1 human

This symmetric matrix stretches space by the factors $3$, $4$ and $6$ along three perpendicular directions.
The direction $(1, 1, 1)$ gets the smallest stretch, and the direction $(1, 1, -2)$ gets the largest stretch.
If you use these three directions as new coordinate axes, the matrix becomes diagonal.

### L2 human

The recipe for a real symmetric matrix has three steps.
Find the roots of the characteristic polynomial, which are all real by the [[real spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]].
Find an eigenvector for each root; eigenvectors for different roots are orthogonal, so here no extra work is needed.
Normalize and use the vectors as the columns of $Q$, so that $Q^{-1} = Q^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $Q^{\mathsf{T}} A Q$ is diagonal.
Watch out: when an eigenvalue is repeated, two eigenvectors in its eigenspace need not be orthogonal, and you must apply the Gram-Schmidt process inside that eigenspace, as for the [[matrix I + J|PAJVDWFC9Q]].
