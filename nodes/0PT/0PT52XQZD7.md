## Summary

Let $G$ be a [[group|G5S1M9]], and let $H$ and $K$ be [[subgroups|0X1JXT47GA]] of $G$.
The product of $H$ and $K$ is the subset $HK = \{hk : h \in H, k \in K\}$ of $G$.
The set $HK$ contains $H$ and $K$, but it is not always a subgroup.
It is a subgroup if and only if $HK = KH$ as sets.
Products of subgroups are the first step from two known subgroups to a larger subgroup, and their sizes are given by the [[product formula|08PY4GNAPK]].

## Motivation

Two subgroups $H$ and $K$ of a group $G$ generate a subgroup $\langle H, K \rangle$.
In general, an element of $\langle H, K \rangle$ is a long word $h_1 k_1 h_2 k_2 \cdots h_r k_r$.
It is natural to ask when one block $hk$ is enough.
In the integers, $4\mathbb{Z} + 6\mathbb{Z}$ is already the subgroup $2\mathbb{Z}$, because $2 = 4 \cdot (-1) + 6 \cdot 1$.
In the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$, the product of two subgroups of order $2$ has $4$ elements, so it is not a subgroup.
The condition $HK = KH$ separates these two cases.

## Notation

The product is written $HK$, or $H + K$ when the operation of $G$ is addition.
The subgroup generated by $H \cup K$ is written $\langle H, K \rangle$, or $H \vee K$ in the [[lattice of subgroups|0B8BW4YWTF]].
When $HK$ is a subgroup, $HK = \langle H, K \rangle$.
Older books call a subset of a group a complex, and they call $HK$ the product of two complexes.

## Remarks

### Examples

- In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the product $4\mathbb{Z} + 6\mathbb{Z}$ is the subgroup $2\mathbb{Z}$, as [[this example|~2a781943]] shows.
- In $S_3$, the product of $A_3 = \{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$ and $\{e, (1\,2)\}$ is all of $S_3$, as [[this example|~0a686383]] shows. Here $A_3$ is normal in $S_3$.
- In $(\mathbb{R}^2, +)$, let $L_1$ and $L_2$ be two different lines through the origin. Then $L_1 + L_2 = \mathbb{R}^2$, because two vectors that are not parallel span the plane.
- If $K \subseteq H$, then $HK = H$. Each $hk$ is in $H$, and each $h = he$ is in $HK$.
- In an [[abelian group|082GHNPJ53]], $hk = kh$ for all $h$ and $k$. Thus $HK = KH$, and $HK$ is a subgroup.

### Non-examples

- In $S_3$, let $H = \{e, (1\,2)\}$ and $K = \{e, (1\,3)\}$. Then $HK$ has $4$ elements, and $4$ does not divide $6$. Thus $HK$ is not a subgroup, as [[this example|~f7dae73a]] shows.
- In the [[alternating group|0Z5905Q73D]] $A_4$, let $H = \langle (1\,2\,3) \rangle$ and $K = \langle (1\,2\,4) \rangle$. Then $H \cap K = \{e\}$, so $HK$ has $3 \cdot 3 / 1 = 9$ elements. The number $9$ does not divide $12$, so $HK$ is not a subgroup.

### Basic properties

- The set $HK$ is a subgroup if and only if $HK = KH$. The proof is below.
- If $H$ or $K$ is a [[normal subgroup|0CB0DX5BVV]] of $G$, then $HK = KH$, so [[$HK$ is a subgroup|0TXWV1JGG1]].
- If $HK$ is a subgroup, then $HK = \langle H, K \rangle$. Each subgroup that contains $H$ and $K$ contains each product $hk$, and $HK$ itself contains $H$ and $K$.
- For finite $H$ and $K$, the [[product formula|08PY4GNAPK]] gives $|HK| = |H| \, |K| / |H \cap K|$, also when $HK$ is not a subgroup.
- The set $HK$ is a union of left cosets of $K$, namely the cosets $hK$ with $h \in H$. It is also a union of right cosets of $H$.

Proof that $HK$ is a subgroup if and only if $HK = KH$.

1. Suppose that $HK$ is a subgroup. Then $HK$ is equal to the set of the inverses of its elements, because a subgroup contains inverses.
2. The inverse of $hk$ is $k^{-1}h^{-1}$, and $k^{-1}$ runs over $K$ and $h^{-1}$ runs over $H$. Thus the set of inverses of $HK$ is $KH$, and $HK = KH$.
3. Conversely, suppose that $HK = KH$. The identity $e = ee$ is in $HK$.
4. Take $h_1k_1$ and $h_2k_2$ in $HK$. The element $k_1h_2$ is in $KH = HK$, so $k_1h_2 = h_3k_3$ with $h_3 \in H$ and $k_3 \in K$.
5. Then $(h_1k_1)(h_2k_2) = (h_1h_3)(k_3k_2)$, and this element is in $HK$.
6. The inverse $(hk)^{-1} = k^{-1}h^{-1}$ is in $KH = HK$.
7. By steps 3 to 6, $HK$ contains $e$ and is closed under products and inverses, so it is a subgroup.

### History

The product of two subsets of a group is sometimes called the Frobenius product, but this name is not standard.
German texts call it the "Komplexprodukt", the product of two complexes.
The criterion $HK = KH$ and the product formula are standard results of a first course, as in Dummit and Foote, Section 3.2.

### Common errors

- The equation $HK = KH$ is an equation of sets. It does not say that $hk = kh$ for each $h \in H$ and $k \in K$.
- The product $HK$ is not the union $H \cup K$. The union of two subgroups is a subgroup only when one of them contains the other.
- A product $HK$ whose size divides $|G|$ need not be a subgroup. In $S_4$, the product of $\langle (1\,2) \rangle$ and $\langle (1\,3) \rangle$ has $4$ elements, and $4$ divides $24$, but it is not a subgroup.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take two clubs of a group, and form all products "one member of the first club times one member of the second club".
Sometimes the result is again a club, and sometimes it is not.
In the whole numbers, the multiples of $4$ plus the multiples of $6$ give exactly the even numbers.
The even numbers form a club under addition, so here the result is a club.
In a group where the order of products matters, the result can be a set that is not closed under the operation.

### L2 human

We check that $H = \{e, (1\,2)\}$ and $K = \{e, (1\,3)\}$ in $S_3$ have a product that is not a subgroup.
We compose permutations from right to left.

1. The four products $hk$ are $ee = e$, $e(1\,3) = (1\,3)$, $(1\,2)e = (1\,2)$ and $(1\,2)(1\,3) = (1\,3\,2)$.
2. Thus $HK = \{e, (1\,2), (1\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$.
3. The four products $kh$ are $e$, $(1\,2)$, $(1\,3)$ and $(1\,3)(1\,2) = (1\,2\,3)$.
4. Thus $KH = \{e, (1\,2), (1\,3), (1\,2\,3)\}$, and $KH \neq HK$.
5. By the criterion above, $HK$ is not a subgroup.
6. A direct check agrees: $(1\,3\,2) \in HK$, but its square $(1\,2\,3)$ is not in $HK$.

The typical use is to build a subgroup from two smaller ones.
If $N$ is normal and $H$ is any subgroup, then $HN$ is a subgroup, and the product formula gives its order.
This is the first step of the second isomorphism theorem.

A trap is to think that $HK$ is a subgroup because $H$ and $K$ are subgroups.
Check $HK = KH$, or find a normal factor, before you use $HK$ as a subgroup.

### L3 human

Two subgroups with $HK = KH$ are called permutable.
A subgroup that is permutable with each subgroup of $G$ is a quasinormal subgroup, and each normal subgroup is quasinormal.
The set $HK$ is the [[double coset|~d8f44326]] $HeK$.
The size of each double coset $HgK$ is the size of the product of $H$ and $gKg^{-1}$.
When $G = HK$ and $H \cap K = \{e\}$, each element of $G$ is a unique product $hk$.
This is the starting point of the [[semidirect product|0H3WZXCZNF]] when one factor is normal, and of the Zappa-Szép product in general.
