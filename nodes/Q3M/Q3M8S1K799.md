## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$.
Take rows $1$ and $2$ and columns $2$ and $1$, in this order, to form $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $\det B = 8 - 5 = 3$.
2. The [[minor|0SD528P0EK]] with rows $\set{1, 2}$ and columns $\set{1, 2}$ keeps the columns in increasing order: $\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = -3$.
3. $B$ arises from that submatrix by a swap of columns, so $\det B = -(-3) = 3$, by the [[effect of row and column operations|09YB3WQG38]].
4. The nine minors of order $2$ of $A$ are $-3, -6, -3, -6, -11, -4, -3, -2, 2$. None of them is $3$.

## Remarks

The definition of a minor keeps the chosen rows and columns in their original order.
With another order, the determinant is the minor times the sign of the reordering permutation.
For cofactor expansions this matters: the wrong order gives the wrong sign.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When you cut a small square out of a table to form a minor, do not shuffle its columns.
Shuffling two columns changes the sign of the determinant.

### L2 human

Here the shuffled piece has determinant $3$, while the correct minor is $-3$.
A trap: a sign error of this kind is invisible in the size of the answer, so check it by a second method.
