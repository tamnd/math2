## Summary

A linear equation says that a weighted sum of unknown numbers has a fixed value.
It is the simplest kind of equation that we can always solve, and all of linear algebra starts from it.
Over a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]] $F$, a linear equation in the unknowns $x_1, \dots, x_n$ has the form
$$a_1 x_1 + a_2 x_2 + \cdots + a_n x_n = b,$$
with fixed coefficients $a_1, \dots, a_n \in F$ and a fixed constant term $b \in F$.
A solution is a tuple $(s_1, \dots, s_n)$ in the [[coordinate space|0XM8BZD76G]] $F^n$ with $a_1 s_1 + \cdots + a_n s_n = b$, and the solution set is the set of all solutions.
The [[formal definition|0DNNT7DBFM]] states this with every condition.

## Motivation

Many conditions in geometry and in daily life say that a weighted total has a fixed value.
If you mix $x$ liters of one juice with $y$ liters of another to fill a 10-liter jug, then $x + y = 10$.
If pens cost 3 dollars and notebooks cost 2 dollars, and you spend 13 dollars, then $3p + 2n = 13$.
In geometry, a point $(x, y, z)$ lies on a given plane in space exactly when it satisfies one equation $ax + by + cz = d$.

What these equations share is that no unknown is squared and no two unknowns are multiplied.
This restriction makes the solution set flat: a line in the plane, a plane in space, or a flat set of higher dimension.
So a finite list of numbers describes all the solutions, and the methods for [[systems of linear equations|0GT4WWGWH2]] apply.
Most of the sub-area studies many linear equations at once, and the shape of one equation is the first piece of that picture.

## Notation

The unknowns are $x_1, \dots, x_n$, or $x, y, z$ when there are at most three of them.
With the coefficient vector $a = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$, books often write the equation as $a \cdot x = b$ or $a^{\mathsf{T}} x = b$.
The number $b$ goes by several names: the constant term, the right side or the inhomogeneous term.
The equation is called homogeneous when $b = 0$.

## Remarks

### A first equation and its solutions

Take $x + 2y - z = 4$ over $\mathbb{R}$.
The coefficient of $z$ is $-1$, which is not zero, so we can solve for $z$:
$$z = x + 2y - 4.$$
Now $x$ and $y$ can take any values $s$ and $t$, and each choice gives exactly one solution:
$$(x, y, z) = (s, t, s + 2t - 4) = (0, 0, -4) + s(1, 0, 1) + t(0, 1, 2).$$
Check: $s = 1$ and $t = 2$ give $(1, 2, 1)$, and $1 + 4 - 1 = 4$.
The solutions form a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$, worked out in full in [[the plane example|MT2XRG51GF]].

The same method works over every field.
Over the field $\mathbb{F}_3$ with three elements, the equation $2x + y + z = 1$ gives $y = 1 + x - z$, since $-2 = 1$ in $\mathbb{F}_3$.
Each of $x$ and $z$ has three possible values, so there are $3 \cdot 3 = 9$ solutions, and [[the example over the field with three elements|8MJP70KJ3M]] lists them.

### How many solutions there are

Suppose some coefficient $a_k$ is not zero.
For each choice of the other $n - 1$ unknowns, the equation fixes $x_k$ uniquely:
$$x_k = \frac{1}{a_k}\Big(b - \sum_{i \ne k} a_i x_i\Big).$$
So the equation always has a solution, for example $x_k = b / a_k$ with all other unknowns zero.
Over a finite field with $q$ elements this gives exactly $q^{n-1}$ solutions, which is $3^2 = 9$ in the example above.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ the solution set is a line when $n = 2$, a plane when $n = 3$, and a hyperplane in general.

If every coefficient is zero, the left side is always $0$.
Then every tuple is a solution when $b = 0$, and no tuple is a solution when $b \ne 0$.
The equation $0x_1 + 0x_2 = 5$ is a linear equation, and it has no solution at all.

### Solutions of the homogeneous equation

When $b = 0$, solutions can be added and scaled.
If $s$ and $t$ are solutions of $a_1 x_1 + \cdots + a_n x_n = 0$, then the left side at $s + t$ is the sum of the left sides at $s$ and at $t$, which is $0 + 0 = 0$.
In the same way, the left side at $cs$ is $c \cdot 0 = 0$.
This is the first sign of the [[structure of the solution set|PDH3HKXWAZ]] that the sub-area proves later.

### What is not linear

An equation is linear when, after you move all terms with unknowns to the left, each term is a constant times one unknown to the first power.
The equation $xy = 1$ fails because $xy$ is a product of two unknowns.
The equation $x^2 + y = 1$ fails because of the square, and $\sin x + y = 0$ fails because $x$ sits inside a function.
[[The non-examples|Z78WBAT2PA]] give a quick test: for a linear equation over $\mathbb{R}$, the midpoint of two solutions is again a solution, and for $xy = 1$ the midpoint of $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$ is $(0, 0)$, which is not.

Some equations only look nonlinear.
The constants may be any numbers, so $\sqrt{2}\, x - \pi y = 1$ is linear.
Unknowns on both sides are no problem either: $x + y = 3x - 1$ becomes $-2x + y = -1$.
Whether an equation is linear also depends on which letters are unknowns: $ax + y = 1$ is linear in $x, y$ when $a$ is a fixed number, but not in the three unknowns $a, x, y$.

### Watch out

A common mistake is to call $xy = 1$ linear because each letter appears only to the first power.
The test is about each term, and the term $xy$ has total degree $2$.
Another mistake is to think that a linear equation over $\mathbb{R}$ always describes a line.
In three unknowns, $x + 2y - z = 4$ describes a plane, and with all coefficients zero you get the empty set or the whole space.
Finally, before you divide by a coefficient, check that it is not zero.

### History

Problems that lead to linear equations in one or two unknowns appear in Babylonian clay tablets and in the Egyptian Rhind papyrus.
The Chinese book *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* solves systems of several linear equations with counting rods.
The use of letters for unknown and known quantities goes back to Viète in the late 16th century, and Descartes fixed the habit of $x, y, z$ for unknowns.
The word "linear" comes from the fact that the solutions in two real unknowns form a straight line.

### Try it

First, is $(x + 1)^2 = x^2 + y$ a linear equation in $x$ and $y$?
Yes: expanding gives $x^2 + 2x + 1 = x^2 + y$, which simplifies to $2x - y = -1$.

Second, how many solutions does $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4 = 0$ have over $\mathbb{F}_2$?
The coefficient of $x_1$ is not zero, so the count is $2^{4-1} = 8$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear equation adds up some unknown numbers, each times a fixed number, and says what the total is.
For example, "three pens and two notebooks cost 13 dollars" gives $3p + 2n = 13$.
The pair $p = 3$, $n = 2$ is a solution, because $9 + 4 = 13$.
The pair $p = 1$, $n = 5$ is another, because $3 + 10 = 13$.
If you mark all the solutions on graph paper, they lie on one straight line, and this is where the name "linear" comes from.

### L2 human

Here is a typical task: describe all real solutions of $2x - 4y + 6z = 8$.
The coefficient of $x$ is $2$, which is not zero, so we divide by it and keep the same solutions:
$$x - 2y + 3z = 4.$$
Then $x = 4 + 2y - 3z$, and $y$ and $z$ are free.
Write $y = s$ and $z = t$ to get every solution as
$$(x, y, z) = (4, 0, 0) + s(2, 1, 0) + t(-3, 0, 1).$$
Check: $s = t = 1$ gives $(3, 1, 1)$, and $6 - 4 + 6 = 8$.
The answer is a point plus all combinations of two directions, so the solution set is a plane.
This is how one linear equation describes a plane in space, as in [[the equation of a plane|07SDJ5DNE4]].

### L3 human

A linear equation over $F$ says that a [[linear form|07YQ1C78G0]] $\varphi \colon F^n \to F$ takes the value $b$.
When $\varphi \ne 0$, the solution set is a translate of the kernel of $\varphi$, and that kernel is a hyperplane, a subspace of codimension $1$.
So a nonzero linear equation describes an affine hyperplane, and a system describes an intersection of affine hyperplanes.
The same pattern returns in the [[structure of the solution set|PDH3HKXWAZ]] of a system, as a particular solution plus the solutions of the homogeneous system.
Over a ring that is not a field, such as $\mathbb{Z}$, linear equations become Diophantine equations, and solvability then depends on divisibility as well as on ranks.
