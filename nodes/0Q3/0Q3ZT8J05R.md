## Summary

Let $F$ be a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]] and let $n \ge 1$.
A linear equation over $F$ in the unknowns $x_1, \dots, x_n$ is an equation $a_1 x_1 + a_2 x_2 + \cdots + a_n x_n = b$ with fixed coefficients $a_1, \dots, a_n \in F$ and a fixed constant term $b \in F$.
A solution is a tuple $(s_1, \dots, s_n)$ in the [[coordinate space|0XM8BZD76G]] $F^n$ with $a_1 s_1 + \cdots + a_n s_n = b$, and the solution set is the set of all solutions.
The exact definition is in [[the definition node|0DNNT7DBFM]].
Linear equations matter because they are the simplest equations that we can always solve, and many problems of science reduce to them.

## Motivation

Many conditions in geometry and in applications say that a weighted sum of unknown quantities has a fixed value.
A mixture of $x$ litres of one liquid and $y$ litres of another, with a total of $10$ litres, gives $x + y = 10$.
A point $(x, y, z)$ in space lies on a plane through a given point with a given normal direction exactly when it satisfies one equation $ax + by + cz = d$.
In such equations no unknown is squared and no two unknowns are multiplied.
This restriction makes the solution set flat: a point, a line, a plane, or a higher-dimensional analogue.
So we can describe all the solutions by a finite list of numbers, and the methods of [[systems of linear equations|0GT4WWGWH2]] apply.

## Notation

1. The unknowns are $x_1, \dots, x_n$, or $x, y, z$ when $n \le 3$.
2. The coefficient vector is $a = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$, and the equation is often written $a \cdot x = b$ or $a^T x = b$.
3. Some books call $b$ the right-hand side, the constant, or the inhomogeneous term.
4. The equation is homogeneous when $b = 0$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The equation $x + 2y - z = 4$ over $\mathbb{R}$ is linear, and its solution set is a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$, see [[the plane example|MT2XRG51GF]].
2. The equation $2x + y + z = 1$ over the field $\mathbb{F}_3$ is linear, and it has exactly $9$ solutions, see [[the example over F3|8MJP70KJ3M]].
3. The equation $3x = 6$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is linear with $n = 1$, and its only solution is $x = 2$.
4. The equation $x + y = 3x - 1$ is linear: moving all unknowns to the left gives $-2x + y = -1$.
5. The equation $0x_1 + 0x_2 = 5$ is linear, with all coefficients zero, and it has no solution.
6. The equation $\sqrt{2}\, x - \pi y = 1$ over $\mathbb{R}$ is linear, because $\sqrt{2}$ and $\pi$ are constants.

### Non-examples

1. The equation $xy = 1$ is not linear, because it contains a product of two unknowns, see [[the non-examples|Z78WBAT2PA]].
2. The equation $x^2 + y = 1$ is not linear, because the unknown $x$ appears to the power $2$.
3. The equation $\sin x + y = 0$ is not linear, because $x$ appears inside a function that is not of the form $ax$.
4. The equation $ax + y = 1$ in the three unknowns $a, x, y$ is not linear, but it is linear in $x, y$ when $a$ is a fixed number.

### Basic properties

1. If some coefficient $a_k$ is nonzero, the equation has a solution: put $x_k = b / a_k$ and all other unknowns equal to $0$.
2. If all coefficients are zero, the equation has every tuple as a solution when $b = 0$, and no solution when $b \ne 0$.
3. If $a_k \ne 0$, then for each choice of the other $n - 1$ unknowns there is exactly one value of $x_k$ that gives a solution.
4. So over a finite field with $q$ elements, an equation with some $a_k \ne 0$ has exactly $q^{n-1}$ solutions.
5. Over $\mathbb{R}$ with some $a_k \ne 0$, the solution set is a line when $n = 2$, a plane when $n = 3$, and a hyperplane in general.
6. If $s$ and $t$ are solutions of the homogeneous equation $a_1 x_1 + \cdots + a_n x_n = 0$, then $s + t$ and $c s$ are solutions too, because the left side is additive and homogeneous.

### History

Linear equations in one and two unknowns appear in Babylonian and Egyptian problem texts, and in the Chinese text *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*.
The modern notation with letters for unknowns comes from Viète and Descartes.
The word "linear" refers to the fact that the solutions in two unknowns form a straight line.

### Common errors

1. Calling $xy = 1$ linear because "each unknown has degree $1$". The product $xy$ has total degree $2$, so the equation is not linear.
2. Forgetting that the coefficients may be zero. The equation $0x + 0y = 5$ is linear and has no solution.
3. Thinking that a linear equation over $\mathbb{R}$ always defines a line. In three unknowns it defines a plane, and with all coefficients zero it defines the empty set or the whole space.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear equation adds up some unknown numbers, each one multiplied by a fixed number, and says that the total is a fixed number.
For example, "three pens and two notebooks cost 13 euros" gives the equation $3p + 2n = 13$.
No unknown is squared, and no two unknowns are multiplied together.
The pair $p = 3$, $n = 2$ is a solution, because three times three plus two times two is thirteen.
The pair $p = 1$, $n = 5$ is another solution.
With two unknowns, all the solutions together draw a straight line on graph paper, and this gives the name "linear".

### L2 human

We describe all solutions of $2x - 4y + 6z = 8$ over $\mathbb{R}$.

1. The coefficient of $x$ is $2 \ne 0$, so we solve for $x$.
2. Dividing by $2$ gives $x - 2y + 3z = 4$, with the same solutions.
3. So $x = 4 + 2y - 3z$, where $y$ and $z$ can take any real values.
4. Write $y = s$ and $z = t$. The solutions are $(x, y, z) = (4, 0, 0) + s(2, 1, 0) + t(-3, 0, 1)$.
5. Check with $s = 1$, $t = 1$: the point is $(3, 1, 1)$, and $2 \cdot 3 - 4 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 1 = 8$.

The typical use of a single linear equation is to describe a line in the plane or a plane in space, as in [[the equation of a plane|07SDJ5DNE4]].
A trap: dividing by a coefficient is allowed only when that coefficient is nonzero, so first check which coefficients vanish.

### L3 human

A linear equation over $F$ says that a [[linear form|07YQ1C78G0]] $\varphi \colon F^n \to F$ takes the value $b$.
When $\varphi \ne 0$, the solution set is a translate of the kernel of $\varphi$, which is a hyperplane: a subspace of codimension $1$.
So the solution set of a nonzero linear equation is an affine hyperplane, and the solution set of a system is an intersection of affine hyperplanes.
The same pattern returns in the [[structure of the solution set|PDH3HKXWAZ]] of a system: a particular solution plus the solutions of the homogeneous system.
Over a ring that is not a field, such as $\mathbb{Z}$, linear equations become Diophantine equations, and solvability depends on divisibility, not only on ranks.
