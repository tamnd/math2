## Proof

We count squares to show that every binary diagonal form takes every value, and then we use the value $1$ to move all diagonal coefficients but the last to $1$.

Let $F$ be a [[finite field|0ZXZ82FTX2]] with $q$ elements, $q$ odd.
Then $2 \ne 0$ in $F$, so $1 \ne -1$.
For a quadratic form $Q$, we write $B$ for its associated symmetric bilinear form, with $B(v, v) = Q(v)$, as in [[the correspondence theorem|VN9AG8JKYT]].

**Part 1: the squares.**

1. The map $s(x) = x^2$ from $F^\times$ to $F^\times$ is a group homomorphism, because $(xy)^2 = x^2 y^2$.
2. Its kernel is the set of roots of $x^2 - 1$, and it contains the two distinct elements $1$ and $-1$.
3. A polynomial of degree $2$ over a field has [[at most two roots|0KJVQV3E0F]], so the kernel is exactly $\{ 1, -1 \}$.
4. Each fiber of $s$ over a point of its image is a coset of the kernel, so it has $2$ elements.
   Hence the image $F^{\times 2}$ has $(q - 1)/2$ elements, and $F^\times / F^{\times 2}$ has $2$ elements.

**Part 2: binary forms take every value.**

5. Let $a, b \in F^\times$ and $c \in F$, and let $S = \{ a x^2 : x \in F \}$ and $T = \{ c - b y^2 : y \in F \}$.
6. By part 1, the field $F$ has $(q - 1)/2 + 1 = (q + 1)/2$ squares, counting $0$.
7. The maps $t \mapsto at$ and $t \mapsto c - bt$ are injective, because $a \ne 0$ and $b \ne 0$.
   So $S$ and $T$ each have $(q + 1)/2$ elements.
8. Both sets lie in $F$, and $|S| + |T| = q + 1 > q$.
   By the [[pigeonhole principle|0HGVCWQGY3]], $S$ and $T$ have a common element.
9. So $a x^2 = c - b y^2$ for some $x, y \in F$, that is, $a x^2 + b y^2 = c$.

**Part 3: isotropy in dimension at least three.**

10. Let $Q$ be a quadratic form on $F^n$ with $n \ge 3$.
    By [[the diagonalization theorem|0Z3FEWZ8HA]], there is a basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ with $Q(\sum_i x_i e_i) = \sum_i a_i x_i^2$.
11. If some $a_i = 0$, then $Q(e_i) = 0$ with $e_i \ne 0$, so $Q$ is isotropic.
12. Otherwise $a_1$, $a_2$ and $a_3$ are nonzero.
    By part 2 with $c = -a_3$, there are $x, y \in F$ with $a_1 x^2 + a_2 y^2 = -a_3$.
13. The vector $v = x e_1 + y e_2 + e_3$ has $Q(v) = a_1 x^2 + a_2 y^2 + a_3 = 0$, and $v \ne 0$ because its coordinate at $e_3$ is $1$.

**Part 4: the normal form.**

14. Let $a, b \in F^\times$, and let $Q = a x^2 + b y^2$ on $F^2$.
    By part 2, there are $x, y \in F$ with $a x^2 + b y^2 = 1$.
15. Put $v_1 = (x, y)$ and $v_2 = (-by, ax)$.
    Then $Q(v_1) = 1$, and
    $$Q(v_2) = a b^2 y^2 + b a^2 x^2 = ab (a x^2 + b y^2) = ab.$$
16. The polar form gives $B(v_1, v_2) = a x (-by) + b y (ax) = 0$.
    The matrix with columns $v_1, v_2$ has determinant $a x^2 + b y^2 = 1 \ne 0$, so $v_1, v_2$ is a basis.
17. In this orthogonal basis $Q$ becomes $X^2 + ab Y^2$.
    In the bracket notation, $\langle a, b \rangle \cong \langle 1, ab \rangle$.
18. Let $Q$ be nondegenerate on $F^n$.
    By step 10, $Q \cong \langle a_1, \dots, a_n \rangle$, and every $a_i \ne 0$, because the diagonal matrix of a [[nondegenerate|01NHFZF2SK]] form is invertible.
19. If $n \ge 2$, step 17 applied to the first two coefficients gives $\langle a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots, a_n \rangle \cong \langle 1, a_1 a_2, a_3, \dots, a_n \rangle$.
20. Apply step 17 again to positions $2$ and $3$, then to positions $3$ and $4$, and so on.
    Each step keeps the product of all coefficients, so after $n - 1$ steps $Q \cong \langle 1, \dots, 1, a_1 a_2 \cdots a_n \rangle$.
    For $n = 1$ there is nothing to do.
21. Let $A$ be the matrix of $Q$, and let $P$ be the invertible matrix of the change of basis, so that $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P = \operatorname{diag}(a_1, \dots, a_n)$.
22. By [[multiplicativity of the determinant|04CPJ6AF00]] and [[the transpose rule|0ZZQ2E1MTE]],
    $$a_1 a_2 \cdots a_n = \det(P^{\mathsf{T}}) \det(A) \det(P) = \det(P)^2 d.$$
23. So $Q \cong \langle 1, \dots, 1, \det(P)^2 d \rangle$.
    Replacing the last basis vector $w$ by $\det(P)^{-1} w$ divides the last coefficient by $\det(P)^2$, so $Q \cong \langle 1, \dots, 1, d \rangle$.

**Part 5: the classification.**

24. Let $Q$ and $Q'$ be nondegenerate on $F^n$ with determinants $d$ and $d'$, and suppose that $d' = c^2 d$ with $c \in F^\times$.
25. By part 4, $Q \cong \langle 1, \dots, 1, d \rangle$ and $Q' \cong \langle 1, \dots, 1, c^2 d \rangle$.
    Replacing the last variable $x_n$ by $c x_n$ turns the first form into the second, so $Q \cong Q'$.
26. Conversely, suppose that $Q' = Q \circ T$ with $T$ invertible and of matrix $S$.
    Then the matrix of $Q'$ is $S^{\mathsf{T}} A S$.
27. By the rules of step 22, $d' = \det(S)^2 d$ with $\det S \ne 0$, so $d$ and $d'$ lie in the same square class.
28. By part 1 there are exactly two square classes.
    The forms $\langle 1, \dots, 1, 1 \rangle$ and $\langle 1, \dots, 1, g \rangle$, with $g$ a nonsquare, realize both, so there are exactly two classes for each $n \ge 1$.

## Idea

Half of the nonzero elements are squares, so the values of $a x^2$ and the values of $c - b y^2$ fill more than the whole field and must meet.
That makes every binary form $a x^2 + b y^2$ take every value, and isotropy in dimension $3$ follows at once.
The value $1$ gives a new orthogonal basis $v_1, v_2$ that turns the first coefficient into $1$ and keeps the product of the two.
Repeating this pushes the whole product into the last coefficient, and that product is the determinant up to a square.

## Variants

Part 1 also follows from the fact that $F^\times$ is cyclic of even order $q - 1$: the squares are exactly the even powers of a generator, and they form a subgroup of index $2$.

Part 3 follows from the [[Chevalley-Warning theorem|08895Q5W96]], which also covers characteristic $2$, but the counting proof above uses only part 2.

Part 2 can be sharpened to an exact count.
For $c \ne 0$, the equation $a x^2 + b y^2 = c$ has exactly $q - \chi(-ab)$ solutions, where $\chi$ is the quadratic character of $F$, equal to $1$ on nonzero squares and $-1$ on nonsquares.
For example, over $\mathbb{F}_3$ the equation $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ has $\chi(-1) = -1$ and so $4$ solutions, namely $(\pm 1, 0)$ and $(0, \pm 1)$.
