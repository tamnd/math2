## Statement

The subspaces of a quotient $V / U$ are exactly the images of the subspaces of $V$ that contain $U$, and each of them arises from only one such subspace.
Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$, let $U$ be a subspace of $V$, and let $\pi \colon V \to V / U$ be the [[quotient map|0J0SK5FF4M]].
Then the maps
$$X \mapsto \pi(X) = X / U, \qquad Y \mapsto \pi^{-1}(Y)$$
are inverse bijections between the set of [[subspaces|0EPTJA5QV4]] $X$ of $V$ with $U \subseteq X$ and the set of subspaces $Y$ of the [[quotient space|0SVR29SV1V]] $V / U$.
Both maps preserve inclusion.

## Remarks

### A first case

Let $U$ be the line $\R(1, 1, 1)$ in $V = \R^3$.
The map $T(x, y, z) = (x - y, \, y - z)$ is onto $\R^2$ with kernel $U$, so it identifies $V / U$ with $\R^2$.
The subspaces of $\R^2$ are $\{0\}$, the lines through $0$ and $\R^2$, and their preimages under $T$ are $U$, the planes that contain $U$, and $\R^3$.
For example, the preimage of the line $\R(1, 0)$ consists of the vectors with $y - z = 0$, which is the plane $y = z$.
This plane contains $(1, 1, 1)$, as the theorem predicts.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The subspaces of $V$ must contain $U$.
In $\R^2$ with $U$ the $x$-axis, the subspaces $\{0\}$ and $U$ both have image $\{0\}$ in $V / U$.
So $X \mapsto \pi(X)$ is not injective on all subspaces, and $\pi^{-1}(\pi(\{0\})) = U$ does not give back $\{0\}$.

The map must be the quotient map, or at least a surjective linear map with kernel $U$.
For a linear map $f$ that is not onto, $f(f^{-1}(Y))$ can be smaller than $Y$.
For the inclusion $f$ of the $x$-axis into $\R^2$ and $Y = \R^2$, we get $f(f^{-1}(Y))$ equal to the $x$-axis.

### What it gives you

The two maps also respect sums and intersections of subspaces that contain $U$:
$$(X_1 + X_2) / U = X_1 / U + X_2 / U, \qquad (X_1 \cap X_2) / U = X_1 / U \cap X_2 / U.$$
In finite dimension, $\dim (X / U) = \dim X - \dim U$, by the [[dimension of a quotient space|0SXDQ6TN00]].
With the [[third isomorphism theorem|07RB3EMBWW]], the quotients match as well: $(V / U) / (X / U) \cong V / X$ for each $X$ that contains $U$.
In particular, $Y$ is a hyperplane of $V / U$, a subspace of [[codimension|JJCGJ1EGCQ]] one, exactly when $\pi^{-1}(Y)$ is a hyperplane of $V$.

### Two maps, one bijection

Each of the two maps determines the other, since they are mutually inverse.
In practice one uses $\pi^{-1}$ to describe a subspace of $V / U$ by an equation in $V$, as with the plane $y = z$ above.
One uses $\pi$ to push a subspace of $V$ down into the quotient.

### Watch out

The image $\pi(X)$ of a subspace $X$ that does not contain $U$ is still a subspace of $V / U$, but it is the image of $X + U$ as well.
With $U$ the $z$-axis in $\R^3$, the $xy$-plane and all of $\R^3$ have the same image, the whole quotient.
So to list the subspaces of $V / U$, list only the subspaces of $V$ that contain $U$, and replace any other $X$ by $X + U$.

## History

For groups the analogous result, with normal subgroups, carries the same name, and some textbooks call it the fourth isomorphism theorem or the lattice isomorphism theorem.
The group version is the [[correspondence theorem|0S9ZVZ34MD]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A quotient $V / U$ is $V$ with $U$ squashed to a point.
Every subspace of the quotient is the squashed picture of exactly one subspace of $V$ that contains $U$.
If you squash the $z$-axis in space, the lines through the origin of the flat picture are the squashed pictures of the vertical planes through the $z$-axis.
Nothing new appears in the quotient, and no subspace that contains $U$ gets lost.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: find all subspaces $Y$ of $V / U$ for $V = \R^3$ and $U = \R(1, 1, 1)$ such that $Y$ contains the coset of $(1, 0, 0)$.
By the theorem, these are the images of the subspaces $X$ of $\R^3$ that contain both $U$ and $(1, 0, 0)$.
Such an $X$ contains the plane spanned by $(1, 1, 1)$ and $(1, 0, 0)$, so it is that plane or all of $\R^3$.
The plane consists of the vectors with $y = z$, since both spanning vectors satisfy $y = z$ and the plane has dimension $2$.
So there are exactly two such $Y$: the line $\pi(\{y = z\})$ and the whole quotient.
Check with the map $T$ of the first case: $T(1, 0, 0) = (1, 0)$, and the subspaces of $\R^2$ that contain $(1, 0)$ are the line $\R(1, 0)$ and $\R^2$.

### L3 human

The theorem says that the interval $[U, V]$ of the lattice of subspaces of $V$ is isomorphic, as a lattice, to the lattice of subspaces of $V / U$.
The same holds for submodules of a module over any ring, for normal subgroups of a group that contain a given normal subgroup, and for ideals of a ring that contain a given ideal.
The [[proof|X0XEJQ4TAA]] uses only that $\pi$ is surjective with kernel $U$.
