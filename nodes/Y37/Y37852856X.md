## Definition

Information theory is the part of [[probability theory|0RWE7CPJ32]] and applied mathematics whose objects are the following.

1. Sources, modelled as random variables or random processes whose values are symbols from an alphabet.
2. Measures of information of such random variables, such as the [[Shannon entropy|Y86E072V0V]], the [[mutual information|A83CYGDXFG]] and the [[Kullback–Leibler divergence|PQBW43X5RE]].
3. Codes, that is, maps from source symbols to strings and back, and [[communication channels|B9C5RBFATB]], modelled as conditional laws of an output given an input.

It studies the best rates of compression and of reliable transmission that codes can achieve.
It expresses these rates through the measures of information, as in the [[channel capacity|J7MZ2XQP9M]].

## Remarks

Example: the statement that a source of independent fair bits can not be compressed below one bit per symbol on average is a result of information theory.
It concerns a source, a code and the entropy of the source.

Non-example: the computation of the eigenvalues of a fixed symmetric matrix is not by itself a result of information theory.
It involves no source, no code and no measure of information.

Coding theory, the design of explicit error-correcting codes, and parts of cryptography are often counted as parts of information theory.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Information theory is the mathematics of messages.
It measures how much information a random message contains, and asks how short a code for it can be and how much can be sent over a noisy line.
