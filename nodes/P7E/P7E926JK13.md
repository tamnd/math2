## Summary

A *logical matrix* for a propositional language is a pair $M = \langle \mathbf{A}, D \rangle$, where $\mathbf{A}$ is an algebra with one operation for each connective and $D$ is a subset of its universe; see [[the definition|AJEJS6TFWP]].
The elements of $D$ are the designated values.
A valuation is a homomorphism from the formula algebra into $\mathbf{A}$, and $\Gamma \models_M \varphi$ means that every valuation that designates all of $\Gamma$ designates $\varphi$.
Logical matrices are the standard semantics of [[many-valued logics|DWYCKAKXZD]], and by [[Wójcicki's theorem|VWJVAS3VE3]] every structural logic is the logic of a class of matrices.

## Motivation

Truth tables compute the value of a formula from the values of its variables.
For three or more values one needs two pieces of data: the tables, which form an algebra, and the values that count as "true", which form the designated set.
The pair is the matrix, and matrix consequence generalizes "true premises give a true conclusion".
Matrices also give the standard method to prove that an axiom is independent: find a matrix that validates the other axioms and the rules but not this axiom.

## Notation

We write $\langle \mathbf{A}, D \rangle$, $\mathrm{Fm}$ for the formula algebra and $\models_M$ for matrix consequence.
For a class $\mathcal{K}$ of matrices, $\models_{\mathcal{K}}$ is the intersection of the relations $\models_M$ for $M \in \mathcal{K}$.
SEP prefers "substitution-invariant" to "structural", since "structural" has other meanings in proof theory.
"Logical matrix" also names a matrix with entries $0$ and $1$ in combinatorics; that meaning is unrelated.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The two-element matrix $\langle \mathbf{2}, \set{1} \rangle$ of classical logic.
2. [[Kleene's tables with one or two designated values|BJMXHXJ7QQ]] give $\mathsf{K}_3$ and the logic of paradox, two different logics on one algebra.
3. [[Lindenbaum matrices|ZV4GTXRRCV]]: the formula algebra with a closed theory as designated set.
4. $\langle [0, 1], \set{1} \rangle$ with the Łukasiewicz operations gives [[Łukasiewicz logic|NFCEM44XYH]].
5. [[A matrix used for an independence proof|SHCDQRD4ZK]] shows why the matrix must also respect the rules of inference.

### Non-examples

1. [[Probability is not truth-functional|EVW7FAWM68]]: no operation on $[0, 1]$ computes $P(\varphi \land \psi)$ from $P(\varphi)$ and $P(\psi)$, so probabilities are not valuations of a matrix.
2. [[Suszko's two-valued semantics|MAYHP6P8NC]] uses two values but not a matrix, because its valuations are not homomorphisms.
3. A Kripke model of intuitionistic logic assigns truth at worlds, not values in an algebra; it becomes a matrix only after passing to the Heyting algebra of up-sets.

### Basic properties

1. Every relation $\models_{\mathcal{K}}$ is a [[consequence relation|J78Y1M4GND]], and it is structural, since a valuation composed with a substitution is again a valuation.
2. [[Wójcicki's theorem|VWJVAS3VE3]]: every structural consequence relation equals $\models_{\mathcal{K}}$ for the class $\mathcal{K}$ of its Lindenbaum matrices.
3. If $\langle \mathbf{B}, E \rangle$ is a submatrix of $\langle \mathbf{A}, D \rangle$, with $E = D \cap B$, then every consequence of the larger matrix holds in the smaller one.
4. The consequence relation of a finite matrix is finitary, by a compactness argument over the finitely many values.

### History

SEP traces the notion of a logical matrix with designated values to Łukasiewicz and Tarski in 1930.
SEP attributes the characterization of substitution-invariant logics by classes of matrices to the book of Wójcicki of 1988, chapter 4.

### Common errors

1. Forgetting that valuations must be homomorphisms. A map that is not compositional is not a matrix valuation.
2. Reading "$\varphi$ is a tautology of $M$" as "$\varphi$ always has the top value". The condition is membership in $D$, and $D$ can contain several values.
3. Checking only the axioms in an independence proof. The matrix must also make each rule preserve designated values.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A logical matrix is a truth table system with a list of "good" values.
The tables tell how to compute the value of a compound sentence.
The good values tell which values count as true.
An argument is valid in the matrix when good premises always give a good conclusion.

### L2 human

We test modus ponens in the matrix $\langle \set{0, \tfrac12, 1}, \set{\tfrac12, 1} \rangle$ with $x \to y = \max(1 - x, y)$.

1. Take $p = \tfrac12$ and $q = 0$.
2. Then $p \to q = \max(\tfrac12, 0) = \tfrac12$, which is designated.
3. The premises $p$ and $p \to q$ are designated, and the conclusion $q = 0$ is not.

So modus ponens fails in this matrix.
The typical use of matrices is such a search for a counter-valuation.
A trap: the same tables with $D = \set{1}$ validate modus ponens, so the designated set matters.

### L3 human

In abstract algebraic logic, a matrix is a model of a logic, and the Leibniz congruence of a matrix identifies the values that no formula context can separate.
Reduced matrices, in which this congruence is the identity, give the algebraic counterpart of a logic, as studied in [[algebraic logic|QYA4H5Y7VR]].
Generalized matrices, with a family of designated sets, and non-deterministic matrices, with sets of possible values, extend the idea; SEP treats them under generalizations of logical matrices.
