## Example

Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 5 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}.$$
By the [[rule for triangular matrices|0K1KKMFHYH]], $\det A = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot (-1) \cdot 4 = -24$.
We find the [[cofactors|0W2QAVJQ1W]] and check that expansions along several lines give $-24$.

1. Each diagonal cofactor deletes row $i$ and column $i$, which leaves a triangular $3 \times 3$ matrix, and its sign is $+$.
   So each is the product of the other three diagonal entries:
$$C_{11} = 3 \cdot (-1) \cdot 4 = -12, \quad C_{22} = 2 \cdot (-1) \cdot 4 = -8, \quad C_{33} = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 24, \quad C_{44} = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot (-1) = -6.$$
2. Above the diagonal, take $C_{13}$ as a sample.
   Deleting row $1$ and column $3$ leaves the rows $(0, 3, 4)$, $(0, 0, 2)$ and $(0, 0, 4)$, whose first column is zero, so $C_{13} = 0$.
   In the same way, for $i < j$ the deleted matrix is upper triangular with a zero on its diagonal, so $C_{ij} = 0$.
3. Below the diagonal, take $C_{21}$ as a sample, with sign $(-1)^{2+1} = -1$.
   Deleting row $2$ and column $1$ leaves the rows $(1, 5, 3)$, $(0, -1, 2)$ and $(0, 0, 4)$, a triangular matrix with determinant $1 \cdot (-1) \cdot 4 = -4$.
   So $C_{21} = -(-4) = 4$.
4. Expand along row $2$ by the [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]]:
$$0 \cdot C_{21} + 3 \cdot C_{22} + 1 \cdot C_{23} + 4 \cdot C_{24} = 0 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot (-8) + 1 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 0 = -24.$$
5. Expand along row $4$ and along column $1$, which each have one nonzero entry:
$$4 \cdot C_{44} = 4 \cdot (-6) = -24, \qquad 2 \cdot C_{11} = 2 \cdot (-12) = -24.$$

All three expansions agree with the product of the diagonal.

## Remarks

The remaining cofactors are found in the same way, and the full cofactor matrix is lower triangular:
$$\operatorname{cof} A = \begin{pmatrix} -12 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & -8 & 0 & 0 \\ -56 & -8 & 24 & 0 \\ 33 & 12 & -12 & -6 \end{pmatrix}.$$
For example, $C_{31}$ deletes row $3$ and column $1$ and leaves the rows $(1, 5, 3)$, $(3, 1, 4)$ and $(0, 0, 4)$.
Expanding along the last row gives $4 (1 \cdot 1 - 5 \cdot 3) = -56$, and the sign $(-1)^{4}$ is $+$.
The transpose of $\operatorname{cof} A$, the adjugate, is upper triangular, and so is $A^{-1} = -\tfrac{1}{24} \operatorname{adj} A$, as the inverse of an invertible upper triangular matrix must be.
Check one entry of $A \operatorname{adj} A$: row $1$ of $A$ with column $4$ of the adjugate gives $2 \cdot 33 + 1 \cdot 12 + 5 \cdot (-12) + 3 \cdot (-6) = 0$.

If the entry $-1$ is replaced by $0$, the matrix becomes singular, and $C_{11}$, $C_{22}$ and $C_{44}$ all become $0$, while $C_{33} = 24$ does not change.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For a triangular table, many cofactors are zero.
Every row then gives the determinant with almost no work, because most of its terms vanish.
Here row $4$ has the single entry $4$, its cofactor is $-6$, and $4 \cdot (-6) = -24$.

### L2 human

The expansions along rows $2$ and $4$ and along column $1$ all give $-24$, which agrees with the product of the diagonal entries.
The example also shows why the triangular rule holds: expanding along the first column again and again peels off one diagonal entry at a time.
Watch out for the sign of position $(2, 1)$: it is minus, so the cofactor $4$ has the opposite sign to the minor $-4$.
