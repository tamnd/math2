## Statement

A subspace never has more independent directions than the space around it, and in finite dimension a subspace of full size is the whole space.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $W$ be a [[linear subspace|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$.

1. If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $W$ is finite-dimensional, and $\dim W \le \dim V$, where $\dim$ is the [[dimension|05G2G0P224]].
2. If $V$ is finite-dimensional and $\dim W = \dim V$, then $W = V$.
3. For every $V$, $\dim W \le \dim V$ as cardinal numbers.

## Remarks

### A subspace of four-space

Let $W$ be the set of vectors $(x, y, z, w)$ in $\mathbb{R}^4$ with $x + y + z + w = 0$.
Solve for $x$: every vector of $W$ is
$$(-y - z - w, \, y, \, z, \, w) = y(-1, 1, 0, 0) + z(-1, 0, 1, 0) + w(-1, 0, 0, 1).$$
The three vectors on the right span $W$, and they are independent, because their last three entries are the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
So $\dim W = 3$, which is at most $\dim \mathbb{R}^4 = 4$, as part 1 says.
The vector $(1, 0, 0, 0)$ is not in $W$, since its entries add to $1$, so $W \ne \mathbb{R}^4$.
This agrees with part 2: a proper subspace must have a smaller dimension.

### What it gives you

The subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$ have dimension $0$, $1$, $2$ or $3$, so they are the origin, the lines through the origin, the planes through the origin and $\mathbb{R}^3$ itself.
Every dimension from $0$ to $\dim V$ occurs, because the span of $k$ vectors of a basis has dimension $k$.

Part 2 is a standard way to prove that two subspaces are equal.
To show $U = W$ for finite-dimensional subspaces, show one inclusion $U \subseteq W$ and then $\dim U = \dim W$.
This is part 2 applied with $W$ in the role of $V$, and it has its own page: [[a subspace of full dimension is the whole space|0WGAXEK99E]].

The same bound holds for images.
For a linear map $T \colon V \to V'$, the images of a basis of $V$ span $T(V)$, so they contain a basis of $T(V)$, and $\dim T(V) \le \dim V$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Part 2 fails in infinite dimension.
In $F[x]$, the subspace $x F[x]$ of polynomials with constant term $0$ has the basis $x, x^2, x^3, \dots$, so its dimension is $\aleph_0 = \dim F[x]$.
But $1 \notin x F[x]$, so the subspace is proper; [[this example|DVEZQ8SP64]] gives the details.
Part 3 survives in infinite dimension, since it only claims an inequality.

The set $W$ must be a subspace.
The union of the two axes in $\mathbb{R}^2$ spans $\mathbb{R}^2$, but it is not closed under addition, since $(1, 0) + (0, 1)$ is on neither axis.
So it is not a vector space and has no dimension of its own.

The scalars must form a field.
In $\mathbb{Z}$, the subgroup $2\mathbb{Z}$ is free with basis $\{2\}$, so it has rank $1$, the same as $\mathbb{Z}$, but $2\mathbb{Z} \ne \mathbb{Z}$.

### Watch out

A common slip is to compare dimensions of sets that are not nested.
Two different lines through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$ both have dimension $1$, and they are not equal.
Part 2 needs $W \subseteq V$, not just equal dimensions.

### Try it

Is $W = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x = 2y\}$ all of $\mathbb{R}^3$?
No: $(1, 0, 0) \notin W$, so $\dim W \le 2$, and $(2, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 1)$ are independent in $W$, so $\dim W = 2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A plane in ordinary space is a smaller world inside a bigger one.
It has two independent directions, and space has three.
A subspace can never have more directions than the space it lives in.
And in finite dimension, if it has as many, it is the whole space.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: decide whether the vectors $u = (1, 2)$ and $v = (3, 5)$ span $\mathbb{R}^2$.
Let $W = \operatorname{span}(u, v)$.
The vectors are independent, because $a(1, 2) + b(3, 5) = 0$ gives $a + 3b = 0$ and $2a + 5b = 0$.
Subtracting twice the first equation from the second gives $-b = 0$, so $b = 0$ and $a = 0$.
So $\dim W = 2 = \dim \mathbb{R}^2$, and part 2 gives $W = \mathbb{R}^2$ without solving any system $au + bv = x$.
The trap is to use part 2 in infinite dimension, where the example $x F[x] \subsetneq F[x]$ shows that it fails.

### L3 human

Dimension is monotone on the lattice of subspaces, and in finite dimension it is strictly monotone, which makes it a rank function on that lattice.
The [[dimension formula for sums of subspaces|06HTJNCMPA]] refines this into an exact count for $U + W$ and $U \cap W$.
In infinite dimension, $\dim$ is still monotone but no longer strictly, because cardinal arithmetic absorbs: $\aleph_0 + 1 = \aleph_0$.
For free modules over $\mathbb{Z}$, rank is still monotone on free submodules, but part 2 fails already in rank $1$, as $2\mathbb{Z} \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}$ shows.
