## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $W$ be a [[linear subspace|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$.

1. If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $W$ is finite-dimensional, and $\dim W \le \dim V$, where $\dim$ is the [[dimension|05G2G0P224]].
2. If $V$ is finite-dimensional and $\dim W = \dim V$, then $W = V$.
3. For each $V$, $\dim W \le \dim V$ as cardinals.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Part 2 fails without finite dimension.
In $F[x]$, the subspace $x F[x]$ of polynomials with $p(0) = 0$ has dimension $\aleph_0 = \dim F[x]$, but $1 \notin x F[x]$; see [[this example|DVEZQ8SP64]].
The hypothesis that $W$ is a subspace is needed: the set $\set{(x, 0) : x \in \R} \cup \set{(0, y) : y \in \R}$ spans $\R^2$.
But it fails closure under addition, so it has no dimension of its own.

### Consequences

1. The subspaces of $\R^3$ have dimensions $0, 1, 2, 3$: the origin, lines and planes through $0$, and $\R^3$.
2. To show $W = V$ for finite-dimensional $V$, show $W \subseteq V$ and $\dim W \ge \dim V$.
3. For subspaces $U \subseteq W$ of finite dimension, $\dim U = \dim W$ implies $U = W$; this is part 2 with $V = W$.
4. Part 2 has a separate statement and proof: [[a subspace of full dimension is the whole space|0WGAXEK99E]].

### Converse

Part 1 has a converse: for each $k \le \dim V$, there is a subspace of dimension $k$, the span of $k$ vectors of a basis.
Part 2 has a trivial converse: $W = V$ gives $\dim W = \dim V$.

### Generalizations

For a linear map $T \colon V \to V'$, $\dim T(V) \le \dim V$, because the images of a basis of $V$ span $T(V)$ and so contain a basis of $T(V)$.
For modules over $\Z$, part 2 fails even in finite rank: $2 \Z \subsetneq \Z$, and both are free of rank $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A subspace cannot have more independent directions than the space around it.
A plane in ordinary space has dimension $2$, which is at most $3$.
In finite dimension, the only subspace with the same dimension is the whole space.

### L2 human

Example: let $W = \set{(x, y, z) \in \R^3 : x - y = 0}$.
It contains the independent vectors $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 1)$, so $\dim W \ge 2$.
It does not contain $(1, 0, 0)$, so $W \ne \R^3$, and by part 2, $\dim W \ne 3$.
So $\dim W = 2$, by part 1.
