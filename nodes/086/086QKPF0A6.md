## Summary

First-order logic is the formal language in which most of mathematics can be written: it has symbols for elements, operations and relations, and the words "for all" and "there is", which range over elements only.
Here is one sentence of it, in a language with one binary relation symbol $<$:
$$\forall x \, \exists y \, (x < y).$$
It says that no element is the largest.
It is true in $(\mathbb{N}, <)$, where $y = x + 1$ always works, and false in $(\{ 0, 1, 2 \}, <)$, where nothing is larger than $2$.

Exactly, a first-order language is fixed by a [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]] $\sigma$: a set of constant, function and relation symbols, each function and relation symbol with an arity.
The logical symbols are the same for every language: the variables $v_0, v_1, v_2, \dots$, the [[connectives|QS0XVAN9BG]] $\neg, \land, \lor, \to, \leftrightarrow$, the quantifiers $\forall$ and $\exists$, the equality symbol $=$, and parentheses.
From these symbols fixed rules build the [[terms|6D3WA7N2W4]], which name elements, and the [[formulas|HYJ10AF6RT]], which make statements.
A quantifier $\forall x$ or $\exists x$ always binds a variable $x$ that stands for one element of the domain, never for a set of elements or for a relation.
On top of this syntax, first-order logic has a semantics of [[structures|983GZBQYT4]] and [[satisfaction|2RBQPV02AS]], and a deductive calculus that proves exactly the sentences that the semantics makes valid.

## Motivation

[[Propositional logic|09K9MWD2QQ]] treats "every number has a larger number" as one atom $p$, and "$0$ has a larger number" as another atom $q$.
It cannot derive $q$ from $p$, because it does not see inside the two statements.
First-order logic breaks each statement into its parts: a [[predicate|0T3DPBG981]] $<$, variables, a constant $0$ and quantifiers.
Then the step from $\forall x \, \exists y \, (x < y)$ to $\exists y \, (0 < y)$ is a rule of logic: put $0$ in place of $x$.

Why quantify over elements only?
[[Second-order logic|G6X7SZSNC5]], which also quantifies over sets and relations, says more: it describes $\mathbb{N}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ up to isomorphism.
But it loses the good metatheory.
First-order logic has a complete proof system by [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]], it has the [[compactness theorem|1NR3HAWG9D]] and the [[Löwenheim-Skolem theorem|484M0TZ1S7]], and [[Lindström's theorem|FYZ3CJGZYV]] shows that no stronger logic keeps both compactness and the downward Löwenheim-Skolem property.
For these reasons first-order logic is the standard frame: group theory, field theory, Peano arithmetic and ZFC set theory are all first-order theories.

## Notation

Other names are predicate logic, predicate calculus, quantification theory and elementary logic, and "elementary" in "elementary equivalence" means "first-order".
Books write $\mathcal{L}$, $L$, $\tau$ or $\sigma$ for the language or its signature.
Books also differ on the primitive symbols: Enderton takes only $\neg$, $\to$ and $\forall$ and defines the rest, and other books take all five connectives and both quantifiers, as we do.
Older books write $(x)$ or $\bigwedge x$ for $\forall x$, $\supset$ for $\to$, and $\equiv$ for $\leftrightarrow$.
In Mathlib a signature is `FirstOrder.Language`, and formulas are `FirstOrder.Language.BoundedFormula`.

## Remarks

### Three parts of one logic

The syntax says which strings are terms and formulas, with no reference to meaning.
Its pieces are the [[signature|WDVVEYD6AT]], the [[terms|6D3WA7N2W4]], the [[atomic formulas|MCZRXR3SVB]], the [[formulas|HYJ10AF6RT]], [[free and bound variables|9CNGDRAMGC]], [[sentences|DKFGQTRYZE]] and [[substitution|NCDZ969V7X]], and they form the topic [[first-order languages and syntax|PSRDJXRW53]].
The semantics reads each symbol in a structure and says when a formula is true there; see [[structures, satisfaction and truth|NJDME6F845]].
The deduction side gives rules for proofs, as in [[proof systems for first-order logic|TTCRX3NFNY]].
The metatheory links the last two, and its main results are in [[completeness, compactness and interpolation|FBWW4J1XWG]].

### Mathematics in first-order form

Many definitions of analysis and algebra are first-order once the signature is right.
[[Continuity of a real function|YEJP7292A2]] at a point $a$ is a formula in the language of ordered fields with one more function symbol $f$, because $\varepsilon$, $\delta$ and $x$ all range over real numbers.
The statement that [[every positive number has a square root|QHDDWVK1NJ]] is the sentence $\forall x \, (0 < x \to \exists y \, (y \cdot y = x))$, true in $\mathbb{R}$ and false in $\mathbb{Q}$.
The group axioms are sentences in the signature $\{ \cdot, e, {}^{-1} \}$; for example, the identity axiom reads
$$\forall x \, (x \cdot e = x \land e \cdot x = x).$$
Together the axioms form a [[first-order theory|92SWXST1ZM]].
Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory is also a first-order theory, in the signature $\{ \in \}$, because its elements are the sets themselves.

### What first-order logic cannot say

The [[least upper bound property|3K03C8D8D8]] begins with "for every subset $A$", so it quantifies over subsets of the domain.
No set of first-order sentences has exactly the complete ordered fields as models, by the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem.
In the same way, the [[finiteness of a group is not first-order|3J825EJ81F]]: by the compactness theorem, a set of sentences with arbitrarily large finite models has an infinite model.
The induction axiom of the second-order [[Peano axioms|0QFT7Z76N5]] starts "for every set $P$ of numbers", so it is second-order.
An infinite disjunction such as $x = 0 \lor x = 1 \lor x = 2 \lor \cdots$ is not a formula either, since formulas are finite strings; it belongs to [[infinitary logic|PQG7VAZ1R2]].

### The first facts

Each formula is a finite string, and it can be read in only one way, by [[unique readability|3QPV4XW8MC]].
Over a countable signature there are only countably many formulas, because each formula is a finite string over a countable alphabet.
Soundness and Gödel's completeness theorem together say that a sentence is provable from a theory exactly when it is true in every model of the theory.
The compactness theorem says that a set of sentences has a model when each finite subset has one.
The Löwenheim-Skolem theorem says that a theory in a countable signature with an infinite model has a model of each infinite cardinality.
[[Church's theorem|VA6BNNY2YC]] says that validity is undecidable as soon as the signature has a relation symbol of arity at least $2$.

### Watch out

A restricted quantifier needs a different connective for $\forall$ and for $\exists$.
"For all $x > 0$, $\varphi$" is $\forall x \, (0 < x \to \varphi)$, with $\to$.
"There is an $x > 0$ with $\varphi$" is $\exists x \, (0 < x \land \varphi)$, with $\land$.
If you write $\exists x \, (0 < x \to x \cdot x = 2)$ for "some positive $x$ has square $2$", the result is true in $\mathbb{Q}$: take $x = -1$, so $0 < x$ is false and the implication holds.

A second slip is to think that first-order logic cannot talk about sets.
It can, as ZFC shows, when the sets are the elements of the domain.
What it cannot do is quantify over subsets of the domain of the structure at hand.

### History

Frege's Begriffsschrift of 1879 gave the first formal logic with quantifiers, but it also allowed quantification over concepts, so it was not restricted to first order.
Hilbert and Ackermann's textbook of 1928 isolated the first-order part as the "restricted functional calculus" and asked whether its proof system is complete.
Gödel answered yes in his dissertation of 1929.

### Try it

Write "between any two different elements there is a third" for an order $<$.
One answer is $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x < y \to \exists z \, (x < z \land z < y))$, true in $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$ and false in $(\mathbb{Z}, <)$.

Is "every nonempty set of natural numbers has a least element" a first-order sentence in the signature $\{ < \}$?
No, it quantifies over sets of elements, so it is second-order.

## Explanations

### L1 human

First-order logic is a precise language for statements about objects.
It has names for objects, such as $0$, and symbols for operations and relations, such as $+$ and $<$.
It has variables such as $x$ and $y$, and the phrases "for all $x$" and "there is an $x$".
For example, "for all $x$ there is a $y$ with $x < y$" says that no number is the largest.
This is true for the whole numbers $0, 1, 2, \dots$, because $y = x + 1$ always works.
The phrases "for all" and "there is" talk only about single objects, never about collections of objects.

### L2 human

We write "between two different real numbers there is a third" in first-order logic, and test it in two structures.
The signature has one binary relation symbol $<$.
"$z$ lies between $x$ and $y$" is the formula $x < z \land z < y$.
Now quantify: for all $x$ and $y$ with $x < y$, some $z$ lies between them.
$$\forall x \, \forall y \, \big( x < y \to \exists z \, (x < z \land z < y) \big)$$
The sentence has no free variables, so each ordered set makes it true or false.
In $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$ it is true: for $x < y$ take $z = (x + y)/2$.
Check: with $x = 0$ and $y = 1$ we get $z = \tfrac{1}{2}$, and $0 < \tfrac{1}{2} < 1$.
In $(\mathbb{Z}, <)$ it is false: for $x = 0$ and $y = 1$, no integer lies strictly between them.
This is the typical use of first-order logic: write the axioms of a class of structures, here the dense orders, and then study their models.

### L3 human

First-order logic sits between propositional logic and second-order logic.
Second-order logic can characterize $\mathbb{N}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ up to isomorphism, but it has no complete proof system and no compactness theorem.
Lindström's theorem explains the choice of first-order logic as the standard: no proper extension keeps both compactness and the downward Löwenheim-Skolem property.
The price is that a first-order theory with an infinite model has models of every infinite size, so it cannot pin down an infinite structure.
In model theory, the definable sets of a structure carry much of its geometry, as in o-minimality and stability theory.
Variants of the same syntax, such as [[first-order logic with equality|Q1494G7FBM]], [[many-sorted logic|7TDHDN8P96]] and [[Hilbert's epsilon calculus|TH5ZKCKN3W]], come later in the topic.
