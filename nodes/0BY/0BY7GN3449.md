## Statement

Let $A$ and $B$ be finite sets, let $f : A \to B$ be a function, and let $n$ be a natural number.
Assume that $n \cdot |B| < |A|$.
Then there is an element $b \in B$ whose fibre $f^{-1}(b) = \set{a \in A : f(a) = b}$ has more than $n$ elements.

The statement has two other standard forms.

- Ceiling form (equivalent). Let $N \geq 0$ objects go into $k \geq 1$ boxes. Then some box receives at least $\lceil N / k \rceil$ objects. Here $\lceil x \rceil$ is the [[ceiling|0EA4XFB0EY]] of $x$, the least integer $\geq x$.
- Weighted form (stronger). Let $q_1, \dots, q_k$ be positive integers. If $q_1 + q_2 + \cdots + q_k - k + 1$ objects go into boxes $1, \dots, k$, then for some $i$ the box $i$ receives at least $q_i$ objects.

The weighted form with $q_1 = \cdots = q_k = n + 1$ is the main form.
For $n = 1$ the main form is the [[pigeonhole principle|0HGVCWQGY3]].
The [[proof|V64DJESEA6]] proves all three forms.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The strict inequality $n \cdot |B| < |A|$ is needed. Let $A = B \times \set{1, \dots, n}$ and let $f(b, i) = b$. Then $|A| = n \cdot |B|$, and each fibre has exactly $n$ elements.
- In the ceiling form, $k \geq 1$ is needed for $N / k$ to make sense. With no boxes, no object can be placed.
- In the weighted form, the number $q_1 + \cdots + q_k - k$ of objects is not enough: put exactly $q_i - 1$ objects into box $i$ for each $i$.
- Each object must go into a box, that is, $f$ must be defined on all of $A$. Otherwise the unplaced objects do not count.

### Consequences

- Among $25$ people, at least $3$ share a birth month, because $2 \cdot 12 = 24 < 25$.
- In each colouring of the edges of the complete graph $K_6$ with two colours, a vertex has at least $\lceil 5/2 \rceil = 3$ edges of one colour. This is the first step of the [[theorem on friends and strangers|0NYM0ZGAK9]].
- The [[Erdős-Szekeres theorem|0SNJM8HSD3]] follows from the main form, applied to the lengths of the longest increasing subsequences that start at each term.
- For real numbers, the averaging form says that some term of a finite list is at least the mean of the list. The [[averaging argument|~f5987d03]] uses this form.

### Converse

The converse is true: if every function $f : A \to B$ has a fibre with more than $n$ elements, then $n \cdot |B| < |A|$.
Indeed, if $|A| \le n \cdot |B|$, we can deal the elements of $A$ to the elements of $B$ in turn, like cards to players.
Then each element of $B$ receives at most $n$ elements of $A$.
So the bound $\lceil N / k \rceil$ of the ceiling form is sharp: some distribution of $N$ objects into $k$ boxes has no box with more than $\lceil N / k \rceil$ objects.

### Generalizations

- The [[infinite pigeonhole principle|0FS3RHF6KE]] replaces "more than $n$" by "infinitely many".
- A version for weights: if real numbers $x_1, \dots, x_k$ have sum $s$, then $x_i \geq s / k$ for some $i$.
- [[Ramsey's theorem|00TQV3F5S4]] applies the weighted form many times, to the edges at one vertex of a complete graph.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If $12$ boxes hold at most $2$ objects each, they hold at most $24$ objects.
So if we put $25$ objects into $12$ boxes, some box must hold at least $3$ objects.
The general rule is to divide the number of objects by the number of boxes and to round up.
For $25$ objects and $12$ boxes, $25 / 12$ is about $2.08$, and rounding up gives $3$.

### L2 human

We find the least number of people in a room that forces $9$ people with the same birth month.

1. The boxes are the $12$ months, and each person goes into the box of the month of birth.
2. We want the least $N$ with $\lceil N / 12 \rceil \geq 9$. This means $N / 12 > 8$, that is, $N > 96$.
3. So $N = 97$ is enough: $8 \cdot 12 = 96 < 97$, and the main form with $n = 8$ gives a month with at least $9$ people.
4. The number $96$ is not enough: $8$ people in each month give $96$ people, and no month has $9$.
5. So the answer is $97$.

The typical use is a lower bound on the size of the largest class of a partition.
A trap is to round down instead of up: $\lfloor 97 / 12 \rfloor = 8$ is the wrong bound.
Another trap is to use the bound $N / k$ when $k$ does not divide $N$: no box can hold $97 / 12$ people, and the correct bound is the integer $\lceil 97 / 12 \rceil = 9$.

### L3 human

The main form is the statement that a function between finite sets has a large fibre, and the proof is a sum over the fibres.
In Mathlib it is `Fintype.exists_lt_card_fiber_of_mul_lt_card`, with variants for sums of weights in place of numbers of elements.
The weighted form with different thresholds $q_i$ is the shape that appears in the inductive proof of [[Ramsey's theorem|00TQV3F5S4]].

The averaging version for real numbers is the discrete case of the first moment method: a random variable takes a value at least its mean with positive probability.
This view connects the principle with the [[averaging argument|~f5987d03]] and with the [[probabilistic method|3B55HNS754]].
The [[infinite pigeonhole principle|0FS3RHF6KE]] is the limit form, and it leads to infinite Ramsey theory.
