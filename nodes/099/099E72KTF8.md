## Summary

Let $S$ be a set with a [[binary operation|0M418FE7BB]] $\ast$.
The operation $\ast$ is commutative when $a \ast b = b \ast a$ for all $a, b \in S$.
The law says that the order of the two inputs does not change the result.
A [[group|G5S1M9]] need not be commutative, and a group with a commutative operation is called an [[abelian group|082GHNPJ53]].

## Motivation

For addition and multiplication of numbers, the order of the inputs does not matter: $2 + 3 = 3 + 2$ and $2 \cdot 3 = 3 \cdot 2$.
Many operations in algebra do not have this property.
To put on socks and then shoes gives a different result from shoes and then socks.
In the same way, composition of functions and multiplication of matrices depend on the order.
The name commutativity marks the operations where the order does not matter, so that we can use the law when it holds and avoid it when it fails.

## Notation

The symbol $+$ is used by convention only for commutative operations.
For a general operation, books write $ab$ or $a \cdot b$, and the order of the factors must be kept.
Two elements $a$ and $b$ with $ab = ba$ are said to [[commute|YJRBCWQV6B]], also when the operation is not commutative.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. Addition and multiplication of [[integers|0C4JS9RF5M]], [[rational numbers|0NCJ5WXN6Z]] and [[real numbers|08J2M1JYAG]] are commutative.
2. [[Multiplication of complex numbers is commutative|G96XHQM1H6]], because $(a + bi)(c + di)$ and $(c + di)(a + bi)$ are both $(ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i$.
3. [[Rotations of the plane about the origin commute|AX0JXCRNJB]], because rotation by $\alpha$ and then by $\beta$ is rotation by $\alpha + \beta$ in both orders.
4. Addition on the [[integers modulo n|03VFZ2WETT]] is commutative, because it comes from addition of integers.
5. The operations $\max$ on $\mathbb{R}$, union of sets and intersection of sets are commutative.
6. The operation $a \ast b = a + b + ab$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ is commutative, because $a + b + ab = b + a + ba$.

### Non-examples

1. [[Composition of functions is not commutative|GH7V9FPCZC]]: for $f(x) = x + 1$ and $g(x) = 2x$ on $\mathbb{R}$, $(f \circ g)(x) = 2x + 1$, but $(g \circ f)(x) = 2x + 2$.
2. [[Multiplication of matrices is not commutative|0P506SPZP2]] for $n \times n$ matrices with $n \geq 2$.
3. Subtraction on $\mathbb{Z}$ is not commutative: $3 - 2 = 1$, but $2 - 3 = -1$.
4. The operation $a \ast b = a$ on a set with at least two elements is not commutative: for $a \neq b$, $a \ast b = a$, but $b \ast a = b$.

### Basic properties

1. On a finite set, $\ast$ is commutative exactly when its [[Cayley table|0YB2NRP0T5]] is symmetric about the main diagonal. So on a set with $n$ elements, only the $n(n - 1)/2$ pairs of different elements need a check.
2. Commutativity and [[associativity|0K8F7PVDMX]] are independent. The average $(a + b)/2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is commutative and not associative. Composition of functions is associative and not commutative.
3. When $\ast$ is associative and commutative, a product $a_1 \ast a_2 \ast \cdots \ast a_n$ does not depend on the order of the factors. The proof is an induction on $n$ that moves one factor at a time to its place by swaps of neighbours.
4. When $\ast$ is commutative, a left [[identity element|07WYQPB8E3]] is also a right identity, and a left [[inverse|0ZNZH0EXFX]] is also a right inverse. So for a commutative operation, the one-sided versions of these laws agree with the two-sided versions.
5. In a group, $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$ holds exactly when $ab = ba$. Thus the familiar rule $(ab)^n = a^n b^n$ of numbers can fail in a group that is not abelian.

### History

Euclid's Elements, Book VII, Proposition 16, proves that the product of two numbers does not depend on their order.
The word commutative was first used by François Servois in a memoir of 1814.
In 1843, William Rowan Hamilton found the quaternions, a number system whose multiplication is not commutative.

### Common errors

1. Commutativity is confused with associativity. Commutativity changes the order: $ab = ba$. Associativity changes the grouping: $(ab)c = a(bc)$.
2. A group is assumed to be commutative. The group axioms do not include commutativity, and many important groups, such as the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] on three or more points, are not abelian.
3. Rules of numbers are used in a group that is not abelian. In general, $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$, not $a^{-1}b^{-1}$, and $(ab)^2 = abab$, not $a^2b^2$.
4. One commuting pair is taken as a proof. Some pairs commute in each operation, for example an element and itself, so a few good pairs do not prove commutativity.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Commutativity says that the order does not matter.
For example, $3 + 5 = 8$ and $5 + 3 = 8$.
Also $3 \times 5 = 15$ and $5 \times 3 = 15$.
But $5 - 3 = 2$, while $3 - 5$ is $-2$.
So addition and multiplication are commutative, and subtraction is not.
In daily life, many actions do not commute: to open a door and walk through it is not the same as to walk and then open the door.

### L2 human

We show that in a group $G$ with identity $e$, the rule $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$ for all $a, b \in G$ implies that $G$ is abelian.
Let $a, b \in G$.

1. By the hypothesis and the meaning of a square, $abab = aabb$.
2. Multiply both sides on the left by $a^{-1}$. By associativity, $a^{-1}a = e$ and $eb = b$, we get $bab = abb$.
3. Multiply both sides on the right by $b^{-1}$. In the same way, $ba = ab$.
4. Since $a$ and $b$ were arbitrary, the operation of $G$ is commutative.

The typical use of commutativity is to reorder the factors of a product, for example to collect the powers of each element.
To show that an operation is commutative, take two arbitrary elements and show $a \ast b = b \ast a$.
To show that it is not, find one pair with $a \ast b \neq b \ast a$.
A trap is to choose a pair that commutes by chance, such as a matrix and the identity matrix.

### L3 human

In a group, $ab = ba$ holds exactly when the commutator $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ is the identity, so the commutators measure the failure of commutativity.
The elements that commute with all elements form the [[centre|0DZ93493VN]] of the group, and the group is abelian exactly when the centre is the whole group.
Commutativity divides algebra into two parts: abelian groups and [[commutative rings|0310REJJY8]] have a rich structure theory, and noncommutative groups and rings need other methods.
A deep source of commutativity is the [[Eckmann–Hilton argument|EJJYCPWHPA]]: two operations that each have an identity element and satisfy an interchange law are equal, and the operation is commutative.
This argument shows that the [[homotopy groups|P976WYEFMB]] $\pi_n$ with $n \geq 2$ are abelian.
In quantum mechanics, observables are operators that need not commute, and the failure of commutativity of position and momentum is the basis of the uncertainty principle.
