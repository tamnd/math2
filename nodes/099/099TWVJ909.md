## Summary

The geometric multiplicity of an eigenvalue counts its independent eigenvectors, and so it says how many basis vectors the eigenvalue can give to an eigenbasis.
Let $T$ be an endomorphism of a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ over a field $F$, and let $\lambda$ be an [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $T$.
The *geometric multiplicity* of $\lambda$ is the [[dimension|03GSZ8RTZJ]] of its [[eigenspace|0G0RFPG8HR]]:
$$g(\lambda) = \dim E_\lambda = \dim \ker(T - \lambda \operatorname{id}_V).$$
The [[formal definition|06YSJVR356]] states it.
For an $n \times n$ matrix, rank-nullity gives the working formula $g(\lambda) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$.

## Motivation

To diagonalize a map we need a basis of eigenvectors.
Each eigenvalue can give at most $g(\lambda)$ independent vectors to such a basis, and eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are [[independent of each other|0HNMFG8F8K]].
So an eigenbasis exists exactly when the numbers $g(\lambda)$ add up to $\dim V$.

The comparison with the [[algebraic multiplicity|0FY42N02DV]] shows where the eigenvectors are missing.
The two numbers satisfy $1 \le g(\lambda) \le a(\lambda)$, and an eigenvalue with $g(\lambda) < a(\lambda)$ is short of eigenvectors.
The sub-area on diagonalization turns this into the standard test, and the Jordan form explains what replaces the missing eigenvectors.

## Notation

Books write $g(\lambda)$, $\gamma_A(\lambda)$, $m_g(\lambda)$ or $\operatorname{gm}(\lambda)$.
An eigenvalue with $g(\lambda) < a(\lambda)$ is *defective*.
An eigenvalue with $a(\lambda) = 1$ is *simple*, and one with $g(\lambda) = a(\lambda)$ is *semisimple*.
Some books use "multiplicity" alone for the algebraic one, so check which count a text means.

## Remarks

### A full eigenspace

The [[3 by 3 matrix with 4 on the diagonal and 1 elsewhere|7XFGVVTZ2X]] has $p_A(t) = (t - 3)^2 (t - 6)$.
For the double eigenvalue $3$, the matrix $A - 3I$ has every entry $1$, so all its rows are equal and its rank is $1$.
Then
$$g(3) = 3 - \operatorname{rank}(A - 3I) = 3 - 1 = 2 = a(3).$$
The eigenspace is the plane $v_1 + v_2 + v_3 = 0$, spanned by $(1, -1, 0)$ and $(1, 0, -1)$.
So $3$ is semisimple but not simple, and with the eigenvector $(1, 1, 1)$ for $6$ we get an eigenbasis.

### Missing eigenvectors

The [[shear|HTAWRAC3QE]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has $a(1) = 2$.
Its matrix $A - I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has rank $1$, so $g(1) = 1$, and the eigenspace is the horizontal axis.
The eigenvalue $1$ is defective, and the shear has no eigenbasis.

The same happens in size $3$.
[[A 3 by 3 matrix with a defective eigenvalue|18XQY67HMS]] has $p_A(t) = (t - 1)(t - 2)^2$, and $A - 2I$ has rank $2$, so $g(2) = 1 < 2 = a(2)$.
Its eigenvectors span only a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

### The rules you can use

The bounds $1 \le g(\lambda) \le a(\lambda)$ hold for every eigenvalue, by the theorem that the [[dimension of an eigenspace is at most the algebraic multiplicity|FWY8A9M5HK]].
The lower bound holds because an eigenvalue has an eigenvector.
The eigenspaces form a direct sum, so $\sum_\lambda g(\lambda) \le \dim V$.
A simple eigenvalue is semisimple, since $1 \le g \le a = 1$.
Similar matrices have the same geometric multiplicities, because $v \mapsto S^{-1} v$ maps $\ker(A - \lambda I)$ onto $\ker(S^{-1} A S - \lambda I)$.
Finally, $T$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] if and only if $\sum_\lambda g(\lambda) = \dim V$, and this happens exactly when $p_T$ splits and $g(\lambda) = a(\lambda)$ for each eigenvalue.

### What it is not

[[Counting diagonal entries|BX4A1N2PFC]] of a triangular matrix gives the algebraic multiplicity, not the geometric one: the shear shows $1$ twice on its diagonal, but $g(1) = 1$.
The rank of $A - \lambda I$ is not $g(\lambda)$ either, since $g(\lambda) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$ is its complement.
For the $3 \times 3$ matrix with $4$ on the diagonal, $\operatorname{rank}(A - 3I) = 1$ while $g(3) = 2$.

### Watch out

The trace and determinant formulas use the algebraic multiplicity.
For the shear, the eigenvalue $1$ counted $g(1) = 1$ time gives the sum $1$, but the trace is $2 = 1 + 1$, the sum with algebraic multiplicity.
A second trap is to expect $g(\lambda) = a(\lambda)$ from every eigenvalue with $g(\lambda) \ge 2$.
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has $g(2) = 2$ but $a(2) = 3$.

### History

The two counts express two different sides of one eigenvalue: the algebraic count comes from the roots of a polynomial, and the geometric count comes from the solutions of a linear system.
The names "algebraic multiplicity" and "geometric multiplicity" follow this split.

### Try it

First, find $g(5)$ for $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
The matrix $A - 5I$ has the single nonzero row $(0, 0, 1)$, so its rank is $1$ and $g(5) = 2$, while $a(5) = 3$.

Second, show that $g(0) = 2$ for $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 3 & 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.
All rows are multiples of $(1, 1, 1)$, so the rank is $1$ and the kernel has dimension $3 - 1 = 2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For one eigenvalue, there can be one direction that the map stretches by it, or a whole plane of such directions, or more.
The geometric multiplicity counts the independent directions.
For a stretch of all of space by $2$, every direction works, and the count is $3$.
For a shear of the plane, only the horizontal direction works, and the count is $1$, even though the eigenvalue is a double root.
When the count falls short, the map has too few eigenvectors to make a full basis.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: decide whether $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is diagonalizable.
The matrix is triangular, so $p_A(t) = (t - 2)^3$ and $a(2) = 3$.
The matrix $A - 2I$ has the rows $(0, 1, 0)$, $(0, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 0)$, so its rank is $1$.
By rank-nullity, $g(2) = 3 - 1 = 2$, and $E_2$ is spanned by $e_1$ and $e_3$.
Check: $A e_1 = 2 e_1$ and $A e_3 = 2 e_3$, while $A e_2 = e_1 + 2 e_2$.
Since $2 < 3$, the eigenvalue $2$ is defective and $A$ is not diagonalizable.
Watch out: two independent eigenvectors are not enough here, because the eigenvalue asks for three.

### L3 human

Over $F[x]$, with $x$ acting as $T$, the eigenspace $E_\lambda$ is the set of vectors in the $(x - \lambda)$-primary part that are killed by $x - \lambda$.
In the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]], each Jordan block for $\lambda$ contributes exactly one eigenvector, so $g(\lambda)$ is the number of blocks, while $a(\lambda)$ is their total size.
The geometric multiplicity is not continuous in the matrix.
For $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \varepsilon \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ it is $1$ when $\varepsilon \ne 0$ and jumps to $2$ at $\varepsilon = 0$, because the rank of $A - I$ can only drop in a limit.
