## Summary

Let $T$ be an endomorphism of a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ over a field $F$, and let $\lambda$ be an [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $T$.
The geometric multiplicity of $\lambda$ is $\dim E_\lambda$, the [[dimension|03GSZ8RTZJ]] of the [[eigenspace|0G0RFPG8HR]] $\ker(T - \lambda \id_V)$.
It is the largest number of linearly independent eigenvectors for $\lambda$.
It satisfies $1 \le \dim E_\lambda \le a(\lambda)$, where $a(\lambda)$ is the [[algebraic multiplicity|0FY42N02DV]].

## Motivation

To diagonalise $T$, one needs a basis of eigenvectors.
The geometric multiplicity counts how many independent eigenvectors each eigenvalue contributes.
If these numbers add up to $\dim V$, then there is an eigenbasis; otherwise there is none.
Comparing with the algebraic multiplicity locates the eigenvalues where eigenvectors are missing.

## Notation

The formal definition is the [[definition of the geometric multiplicity|06YSJVR356]].
Common symbols are $g(\lambda)$, $\gamma_A(\lambda)$ and $\operatorname{gm}(\lambda)$.
An eigenvalue with $g(\lambda) < a(\lambda)$ is called defective.
An eigenvalue with $a(\lambda) = 1$ is called simple, and one with $g(\lambda) = a(\lambda)$ is called semisimple.

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The 3 by 3 matrix with 4 on the diagonal and 1 elsewhere|~29b91e21]]: the eigenvalue $3$ has $g = a = 2$, so it is semisimple and not simple.
- [[The shear of the plane|~919ae3c7]]: the eigenvalue $1$ has $g = 1$ and $a = 2$, so it is defective.
- [[A 3 by 3 matrix with a defective eigenvalue|~5db5cf19]]: the eigenvalue $2$ has $g = 1$ and $a = 2$, and the eigenvalue $1$ is simple.
- For $I_n$, the eigenvalue $1$ has $g = a = n$.

### Non-examples

- [[Counting diagonal entries gives the algebraic multiplicity|~6248b0a1]], not the geometric multiplicity, for a triangular matrix such as the shear.
- The rank of $A - \lambda I$ is not $g(\lambda)$. By [[rank-nullity|0W4FSW1TVB]], $g(\lambda) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$.

### Basic properties

- $1 \le g(\lambda) \le a(\lambda)$, by the theorem that the [[dimension of an eigenspace is at most the algebraic multiplicity|FWY8A9M5HK]].
- $g(\lambda) = n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$ for an $n \times n$ matrix $A$.
- $\sum_\lambda g(\lambda) \le \dim V$, because the sum of the eigenspaces is direct, by the theorem on [[eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues|0HNMFG8F8K]].
- $T$ is [[diagonalisable|07SQCNV233]] if and only if $\sum_\lambda g(\lambda) = \dim V$, if and only if $p_T$ splits and $g(\lambda) = a(\lambda)$ for each eigenvalue.
- A simple eigenvalue is semisimple, because $1 \le g \le a = 1$.
- Similar matrices have the same geometric multiplicities, because $S^{-1}$ maps $E_\lambda(A)$ isomorphically onto $E_\lambda(S^{-1}AS)$.

### History

The algebraic multiplicity comes from the count of roots of a polynomial, and the geometric multiplicity comes from the count of independent eigenvectors.
The two counts are equal for every eigenvalue exactly when the matrix is diagonalisable over a field that contains all its eigenvalues.

### Common errors

- Assuming that $g(\lambda) = a(\lambda)$, as for symmetric real matrices. For the shear it fails.
- Computing $\operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$ and reporting it as $g(\lambda)$.
- Using the geometric multiplicity in the formulas for the trace and the determinant. These use the algebraic multiplicity.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For one eigenvalue, there can be one direction or several independent directions that the map stretches by it.
The geometric multiplicity counts these directions.
It is never more than the algebraic multiplicity, the number of times the eigenvalue is a root.
When it is less, the map has too few eigenvectors to make a full basis.

### L2 human

We compute $g(2)$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $A - 2I$ has the rows $(0, 1, 0)$, $(0, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 0)$, so its rank is $1$.
2. By rank-nullity, $g(2) = 3 - 1 = 2$.
3. A basis of $E_2$ is $e_1, e_3$. Check: $A e_1 = 2 e_1$ and $A e_3 = 2 e_3$.
4. The algebraic multiplicity is $3$, from $p_A = (t - 2)^3$. So $2$ is defective, and $A$ is not diagonalisable.

The typical use is to decide diagonalisability: compute $g(\lambda)$ for each eigenvalue and compare with $a(\lambda)$.
A trap: $g(\lambda) \ge 2$ does not mean $g(\lambda) = a(\lambda)$, as this example shows.

### L3 human

The geometric multiplicity is the dimension of the socle of the $(x - \lambda)$-primary part of the $F[x]$-module $V$.
In the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]], it is the number of Jordan blocks for $\lambda$.
For a polynomial family of matrices, $g(\lambda)$ can jump up on a closed set, while the eigenvalues move continuously.
