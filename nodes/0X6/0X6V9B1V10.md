## Summary

The first question about any system of equations is whether it has a solution at all.
A [[system of linear equations|0GT4WWGWH2]] over a field $F$ is *consistent* when it has at least one solution in $F^n$, and *inconsistent* when it has none.
The [[formal definition|04JAAVHDDM]] says exactly this.
Row reduction answers the question: the system is inconsistent exactly when an echelon form of the augmented matrix has a row
$$(0, \dots, 0 \mid c) \qquad \text{with } c \ne 0.$$
In the language of rank, $Ax = b$ is consistent exactly when $\rank A = \rank\, [A \mid b]$, which is the [[Rouché-Capelli theorem|0HV1SKWPMS]].

## Motivation

A model of a real situation often gives more conditions than unknowns, and the conditions can contradict each other.
Measured data, for example, rarely fit a line exactly, so the equations for the line have no common solution.
Before we look for a formula for the solutions, we need to know whether any solution exists.

The geometry shows how a contradiction can hide.
Two parallel lines have no common point, and that is easy to see.
Three lines in the plane can fail to have a common point even though each two of them meet.
Elimination finds every such contradiction by a fixed procedure: it produces the equation $0 = c$ with $c \ne 0$.
The theorem on [[existence and uniqueness of solutions|BKA191KD1S]] turns this into the general test.

## Notation

Some books say "solvable" for consistent and "unsolvable" for inconsistent.
The word "consistent" also has a meaning in logic, where a set of axioms is consistent when it does not prove a contradiction.
For linear systems the two meanings fit together, because an inconsistent system is one from which elimination derives the false equation $0 = 1$.

## Remarks

### Spotting a contradiction

Take $x + 2y = 3$ and $2x + 4y = 7$ over $\mathbb{R}$.
Subtract twice the first equation from the second:
$$\left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 7 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_2 - 2R_1} \left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right].$$
The last row says $0x + 0y = 1$, and no pair of numbers satisfies it.
[[Row operations keep the solution set|K41GT10YM6]], so the original system has no solution either.
If the right side is $(3, 6)$ instead, the same step gives the row $(0, 0 \mid 0)$, and the system is consistent, with the solutions $(3 - 2t, t)$.

The contradiction need not be visible in any two equations.
In [[three lines without a common point|XJX2NN45ED]], the lines $x + y = 2$, $x - y = 0$ and $x + 2y = 4$ meet in pairs, but the three together give the row $(0, 0 \mid 1)$.

### Consistent systems you can see at once

Every homogeneous system $Ax = 0$ is consistent, because $x = 0$ is a solution.
A system can also be consistent for some right sides and not for others.
For example, $x + y + z = 1$, $x + 2y + 3z = 2$, $x + 3y + 5z = a$ is consistent exactly when $a = 3$, as [[a system with a parameter|2WB9Z90K4Y]] shows.
The system $Ax = b$ is consistent for every $b \in F^m$ exactly when $A$ has a pivot in every row, by the [[pivot criterion|HJTF7B36W9]].

### The field matters

Consistency is a question about solutions in a given field.
The equation $2x = 1$ is consistent over $\mathbb{Q}$, with $x = \tfrac12$, but over $\mathbb{F}_2$ it reads $0 = 1$, as [[an equation over two fields|WT7RS59FGD]] explains.
In the other direction, a system that is consistent over $F$ stays consistent over every larger field, since the same solution still works.

What does not count as inconsistency?
The equation $2x + 4y = 1$ has no solution in integers, because the left side is even, but over $\mathbb{Q}$ it has the solution $(\tfrac12, 0)$.
So it is a consistent system, and [[the integer example|MME3Y9VCHR]] explains the difference.
The equation $x^2 = -1$ has no real solution, but it is not linear, so the word does not apply in the sense of the sub-area.

### Watch out

A zero row in the coefficient part does not mean inconsistency.
The row $(0, 0 \mid 0)$ only says that one equation was redundant, and only a row $(0, \dots, 0 \mid c)$ with $c \ne 0$ shows a contradiction.
Do not decide consistency by counting equations either: $x = 1$, $2x = 2$ has more equations than unknowns and is consistent.
Finally, the [[reduced row echelon form|0WF5AQYTW6]] of $A$ alone cannot show inconsistency, so reduce $[A \mid b]$, as [[the trap example|HCYVBVXBF4]] shows.

### History

The test by ranks took shape in the 1870s to 1890s, in work of Rouché, Fontené, Frobenius and Capelli, and in the lectures of Kronecker.
That is why the [[Rouché-Capelli theorem|0HV1SKWPMS]] carries different names in different countries.

### Try it

First, is $x - y = 1$, $2x - 2y = 3$ consistent?
No: $R_2 - 2R_1$ gives $0 = 1$.

Second, for which $c$ is $x + y = 1$, $x - y = 3$, $2x = c$ consistent?
The first two equations give $x = 2$ and $y = -1$, so the third holds exactly when $c = 4$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A system is consistent when there is at least one way to make all the equations true together.
The equations $x + y = 1$ and $x + y = 2$ cannot both be true, because the same sum cannot be $1$ and $2$.
So that system is inconsistent, and in a picture the two lines are parallel and never meet.
Elimination shows the problem directly: subtracting the equations gives $0 = 1$.

### L2 human

A typical use is to test whether a vector is a combination of given vectors.
Is $b = (1, 2, 4)$ a combination of $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$?
We need $x(1, 0, 1) + y(0, 1, 1) = (1, 2, 4)$, which is the system $x = 1$, $y = 2$, $x + y = 4$.
Reduce the augmented matrix with $R_3 - R_1 - R_2$:
$$\left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 4 \end{array}\right] \to \left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right].$$
The last row is $(0, 0 \mid 1)$, so the system is inconsistent and $b$ is not such a combination.
With $b = (1, 2, 3)$ the last row would be $(0, 0 \mid 0)$, and $b = 1 \cdot (1, 0, 1) + 2 \cdot (0, 1, 1)$.

### L3 human

The system $Ax = b$ is consistent exactly when $b$ lies in the [[column space|03AJKXH1AV]] of $A$, by the theorem on [[linear systems as vector equations|E7SAHXQEVC]].
Dually, it is inconsistent exactly when some $y$ with $y^{\mathsf{T}} A = 0$ has $y^{\mathsf{T}} b \ne 0$, and this is the finite-dimensional [[Fredholm alternative|4HH8C9V8JQ]].
Such a $y$ is a certificate: it lists the multiples of the equations that add up to $0 = c$ with $c \ne 0$.
Over an ordered field, the analogue for inequalities is Farkas' lemma, which belongs to linear programming.
For inconsistent systems over $\mathbb{R}$, [[least squares|0BYTQ4X1HC]] gives the best approximate solution.
