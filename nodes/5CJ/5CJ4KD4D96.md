## Example

A figure is a helpful servant and a dangerous master, and the [[mathematical fallacies|S2BW0FDHV8]] of geometry take a fact from a figure that is drawn wrong.
Here is the classic false proof that every triangle is isosceles.
Draw it as you read, and look for the bad step.

1. Let $ABC$ be a triangle. Let $\ell$ be the bisector of the angle at $A$, and let $m$ be the [[perpendicular bisector|055VNYT0PB]] of $BC$, through the midpoint $D$ of $BC$. Let $O$ be the point where $\ell$ and $m$ meet, which the figure shows inside the triangle.
2. Let $R$ and $Q$ be the feet of the perpendiculars from $O$ to the lines $AB$ and $AC$.
3. The right triangles $ARO$ and $AQO$ share the side $AO$ and have equal angles at $A$, so they are [[congruent|093EDENVA1]]. So $AR = AQ$ and $OR = OQ$.
4. The point $O$ lies on $m$, so $OB = OC$.
5. The right triangles $ORB$ and $OQC$ have $OR = OQ$ and $OB = OC$, so they are congruent, and $RB = QC$.
6. The point $R$ lies between $A$ and $B$, so $AB = AR + RB$, and $Q$ lies between $A$ and $C$, so $AC = AQ + QC$.
7. By steps 3 and 5, $AB = AC$.

Steps 3, 4 and 5 are correct wherever the points lie.
The bad step is step 6, together with the figure of step 1.
When $AB \neq AC$, the point $O$ lies outside the triangle, on its circumcircle, and exactly one of $R$ and $Q$ falls outside its side.
We prove this.

1. The line $\ell$ meets the [[circumcircle|03VZJE6C8C]] of $ABC$ again at a point $M$. The [[inscribed angles|0GA1V8NN8C]] $BAM$ and $MAC$ are equal, so the chords $MB$ and $MC$ are equal, and $M$ lies on $m$.
2. The lines $\ell$ and $m$ are different, since $A$ on $m$ would give $AB = AC$. So they meet in one point, and $O = M$.
3. The point $M$ lies on the arc $BC$ that does not contain $A$, so $O$ lies on the other side of $BC$ from $A$, outside the triangle.
4. The quadrilateral $ABOC$ is inscribed in a circle, so its opposite angles add up to $180^\circ$: $\angle ABO + \angle ACO = 180^\circ$.
5. The two angles are not both right angles, since then the right triangles $ABO$ and $ACO$, with common hypotenuse $AO$ and $OB = OC$, would be congruent and $AB = AC$.
6. So one angle is obtuse, say $\angle ABO$. Then the foot $R$ lies on the line $AB$ beyond $B$, so $AB = AR - RB$.
7. The angle $\angle ACO$ is then acute, and $\angle OAC$ is half the angle at $A$, so $Q$ lies between $A$ and $C$, and $AC = AQ + QC$.

With steps 3 and 5 of the false proof, the correct version of step 6 gives
$$AC - AB = (AQ + QC) - (AR - RB) = 2\,RB,$$
which is not $0$.

## Remarks

### A triangle with numbers

Take $B = (0, 0)$, $C = (4, 0)$ and $A = (0, 3)$, so $AB = 3$ and $AC = 5$.
The perpendicular bisector of $BC$ is the line $x = 2$.
The unit vectors from $A$ toward $B$ and toward $C$ are $(0, -1)$ and $\left(\frac{4}{5}, -\frac{3}{5}\right)$, and their sum $\left(\frac{4}{5}, -\frac{8}{5}\right)$ points along the bisector at $A$.
The bisector is the set of points $(0, 3) + t\left(\frac{4}{5}, -\frac{8}{5}\right)$, and it meets $x = 2$ at $t = \frac{5}{2}$:
$$O = (0, 3) + \tfrac{5}{2}\left(\tfrac{4}{5}, -\tfrac{8}{5}\right) = (2, -1).$$
So $O$ lies below $BC$, outside the triangle.
The line $AB$ is the line $x = 0$, so $R = (0, -1)$ lies beyond $B$.
Check: the circumcircle has center $(2, \frac{3}{2})$, the midpoint of the hypotenuse $AC$, and radius $\frac{5}{2}$, and $O$ is at distance $\frac{5}{2}$ from that center.

### The missing square puzzle

Four pieces lie on a square grid: a right triangle with legs $8$ and $3$ (area $12$), a right triangle with legs $5$ and $2$ (area $5$), and two L-shaped pieces of $7$ and $8$ unit squares.
The total area is $12 + 5 + 7 + 8 = 32$.
In one arrangement they fill what looks like a right triangle with legs $13$ and $5$.
In a second arrangement the same pieces fill the same outline, except for one empty unit square.
Where did the square go?

The bad step is the belief that the long side is a straight line.
A true right triangle with legs $13$ and $5$ has area $\frac{13 \cdot 5}{2} = 32.5$, which is neither $32$ nor $33$, so neither figure is a triangle.
The two triangular pieces have slopes $\frac{3}{8} = 0.375$ and $\frac{2}{5} = 0.4$, and since these differ, the "hypotenuse" of each figure bends.

### Where the extra square comes from

Put the corner at $(0, 0)$ and the far end at $(13, 5)$.
In the first figure the bend is at $(8, 3)$, and the straight line has height $\frac{40}{13} \approx 3.08$ at $x = 8$, so the bend lies below it.
The thin sliver between them is the triangle with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(8, 3)$, $(13, 5)$, of area
$$\tfrac{1}{2}\left|8 \cdot 5 - 3 \cdot 13\right| = \tfrac{1}{2}.$$
So the first figure has area $32.5 - 0.5 = 32$.
In the second figure the bend is at $(5, 2)$, above the straight line, whose height there is $\frac{25}{13} \approx 1.92$, and the sliver again has area $\frac{1}{2}|5 \cdot 5 - 2 \cdot 13| = \frac{1}{2}$.
So the second outline has area $32.5 + 0.5 = 33$, and the extra unit is the "missing" square.
No area is lost; the two outlines are different shapes.

### Lessons on betweenness and figures

In both fallacies the figure supplies a fact that the argument never proves: that a point lies between two others, or that three points lie on one line.
Euclid's *Elements* used such facts from figures without proof, and a [[gap in Euclid's first construction|BRAE42513N]] is of the same kind.
The axioms of [[betweenness|CE8GX6ADZS]] and [[Pasch's axiom|KJT98G16Y4]] in [[Hilbert's axioms|KM1FB8XZ2R]] make these facts provable.
So a figure may suggest a proof, but every claim of the form "this point is inside" or "these points are on a line" needs a reason.
Watch out: a figure drawn with $AB$ almost equal to $AC$ puts $O$ just below $BC$, where it looks as if it were inside the triangle.

### Try it

In the isosceles fallacy, what happens when $AB = AC$?
Then $\ell$ and $m$ are the same line, the point $O$ is not determined, and the conclusion is true anyway.

Is the point $(8, 3)$ on the segment from $(0, 0)$ to $(13, 5)$?
No: points of that segment satisfy $13y = 5x$, and $13 \cdot 3 = 39 \neq 40 = 5 \cdot 8$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A picture can lie.
In a famous puzzle, two arrangements of the same four pieces seem to make the same triangle, but one has a hole.
The long side is not straight: it bends very slightly, inward in one picture and outward in the other.
The difference between the two pictures is exactly the area of the hole.
In geometry we prove each fact that we read from a picture, because a small error in the picture can change the answer.

### L2 human

We test the isosceles fallacy on a second triangle, $A = (0.3, 2)$, $B = (0, 0)$, $C = (4, 0)$, where $AB \approx 2.02$ and $AC \approx 4.21$.
The perpendicular bisector of $BC$ is again $x = 2$.
Adding the unit vectors from $A$ toward $B$ and toward $C$ and following that direction from $A$ until $x = 2$ gives $O \approx (2, -1.404)$.
So $O$ lies below $BC$, as the proof above predicts.
The foot $R$ of the perpendicular from $O$ to the line $AB$ lies past $B$, while the foot $Q$ on $AC$ lies between $A$ and $C$.

Check: the angle at $B$ in the quadrilateral $ABOC$ is obtuse, which matches step 6 of the correct proof.
The typical use of such a check is to test a figure on one concrete case before you trust it.
Watch out: a computer drawing settles the positions for one triangle; a proof must settle them for all triangles.
