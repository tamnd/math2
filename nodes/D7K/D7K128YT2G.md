## Definition

Let a closed system in equilibrium undergo a quasistatic process in which it receives the [[heat|CY4GJQGF06]] $\delta Q$ and its [[temperature|KK46Q2YV1D]] changes by $dT \ne 0$.
The *heat capacity* of the system along that process is $C = \delta Q / dT$.
For a simple system with [[internal energy|F6JVQ12X43]] $U(T, V)$ and [[enthalpy|722RZK72P5]] $H(T, p)$ the two standard heat capacities are:

1. the heat capacity at constant volume, along an [[isochoric process|YT8APTNYD5]]: $C_V = (\partial U / \partial T)_V$;
2. the heat capacity at constant pressure, along an [[isobaric process|WYAY33ZVYQ]]: $C_p = (\partial H / \partial T)_p$.

These are [[partial derivatives|05AXG3QRKR]].

## Remarks

The two formulas follow from the first law: at constant volume $\delta Q = dU$, and at constant pressure $\delta Q = dU + p\,dV = dH$.
Equivalently, with the entropy $S$, $C_V = T (\partial S / \partial T)_V$ and $C_p = T (\partial S / \partial T)_p$.
Heat capacity is extensive; the *specific* heat capacity is $C/m$ for the mass $m$, and the *molar* heat capacity is $C/n$ for the amount $n$.

Example: for $n$ moles of a monatomic ideal gas, $U = \tfrac{3}{2} nRT$ with the gas constant $R$.
Hence $C_V = \tfrac{3}{2} nR$, and since $H = U + pV = \tfrac{5}{2} nRT$, also $C_p = \tfrac{5}{2} nR$.

Non-example: during melting at fixed pressure, the system absorbs heat while $dT = 0$.
The quotient $\delta Q / dT$ is not defined there, and the heat absorbed is a latent heat, not a heat capacity.
