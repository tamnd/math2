## Statement

Let two point particles have masses $m_1, m_2 > 0$ and distinct positions $x_1, x_2 \in \R^3$.
Then particle $2$ exerts on particle $1$ the force
$$F_{12} = -\,G\, \frac{m_1 m_2}{\abs{x_1 - x_2}^3}\, (x_1 - x_2),$$
where $G > 0$ is a universal constant, the gravitational constant.

## Remarks

The force has size $G m_1 m_2 / r^2$ with $r = \abs{x_1 - x_2}$, points from particle $1$ towards particle $2$, and satisfies $F_{21} = -F_{12}$, as [[Newton's third law|KNBP19FG6K]] requires.
For fixed $x_2$, it is a [[central force|THX3FXH5C1]] with [[potential energy|5T5GAKQD3S]] $V = -G m_1 m_2 / r$.
In SI units, $G \approx 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\, m^2\, kg^{-2}}$.
A spherically symmetric body attracts outside points as if all its mass sat at its centre.
Example: near the surface of the Earth, with mass $M$ and radius $R$, the force on a mass $m$ is about $m g$.
Here $g = G M / R^2 \approx 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
The law is an empirical law of physics, published by Newton in the Principia in 1687; general relativity corrects it for strong fields and high speeds.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every two masses attract each other.
The pull grows with each mass and falls off with the square of the distance: twice as far means a quarter of the pull.
The same law explains falling apples, the tides and the orbits of the planets.

### L2 human

Combined with Newton's second law, the law gives the $N$-body problem $m_i \ddot x_i = \sum_{j \ne i} F_{ij}$.
For two bodies it reduces to a central-force problem whose bounded orbits are ellipses, which recovers Kepler's laws.
The inverse-square form makes the potential $-1/r$ harmonic away from the origin, which is the reason for the shell property of spherical bodies.
