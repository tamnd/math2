## Summary

Most errors in a first course of linear algebra come from one habit: students use a rule that is true for numbers on matrices or vector spaces, where it is false.
Five such errors appear in almost every class.
Students believe that matrix products commute, that the determinant of a sum is the sum of the determinants, that a spanning set is a basis, that row operations keep the column space, and that the zero vector can be an eigenvector.
Each error has a counterexample with $2 \times 2$ matrices, and the counterexample is the fastest way to replace the false rule by the true one.

## Motivation

Students bring the algebra of numbers into linear algebra.
Numbers commute, a product of nonzero numbers is nonzero, and many functions of a sum are sums of values.
Matrices and linear maps keep some of these rules and lose others.
The lost rules produce answers that look correct, so a teacher needs explicit counterexamples, and a student needs the habit of testing a rule on a small case before using it.
The topics behind the errors come back throughout the [[overview|Y7PBW8VD4Q]]: products in the sub-area on matrices, determinants in their own sub-area, and spans and bases in the sub-area on vector spaces.

## Notation

We write $I$ for the identity matrix and $\det$ for the determinant.
For a matrix $A$, $\operatorname{Col} A$ is the column space and $\operatorname{Nul} A$ is the null space.

## Remarks

### The order of a product matters

[[Matrix products|032Q9FB8MT]] do not commute in general.
Take
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Multiply in both orders:
$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad BA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
So the school formula $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + 2AB + B^2$ fails; the correct expansion is $A^2 + AB + BA + B^2$.
Some pairs do commute, for example $A$ with $I$, with $A^2$ or with $cI$, so one commuting pair proves nothing.
Diagonal matrices also commute with each other, which is why a rule checked only on diagonal matrices can mislead you.

### The determinant is not additive

Take $A = B = I_2$.
Then $\det(A + B) = \det(2 I_2) = 4$, but $\det A + \det B = 2$.
The [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] respects products instead: $\det(AB) = \det A \det B$.
For an $n \times n$ matrix, a scalar comes out with the power $n$, so $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$, and the example above is the case $c = 2$, $n = 2$.

### A spanning set need not be a basis

The vectors $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$ [[span|0DTY8B9HMS]] $\mathbb{R}^2$.
They are not a [[basis|0KCQ9Y3QKA]], because they are dependent: $(1, 1) = (1, 0) + (0, 1)$.
A basis must span and be independent, and then each vector has exactly one set of coordinates.
Here $(1, 1)$ has two: $1 \cdot (1, 1)$ and $1 \cdot (1, 0) + 1 \cdot (0, 1)$.

### Row operations change the column space

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and subtract twice the first row from the second.
The result is $R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
The [[column space|03AJKXH1AV]] of $A$ is the line through $(1, 2)$, but the column space of $R$ is the line through $(1, 0)$.
What row operations do keep is the [[null space|0PHDYNR24D]], the [[row space|069MCGX635]] and the linear relations among the columns.
So the pivot columns of the original $A$, not those of $R$, give a basis of $\operatorname{Col} A$; here that basis is $(1, 2)$.

### Eigenvectors are nonzero

The equation $A \cdot 0 = \lambda \cdot 0$ holds for every $\lambda$, so if $0$ counted as an [[eigenvector|0N6960W0BV]], every number would be an eigenvalue.
That is why the definition requires $v \neq 0$.
The eigenvalue $0$ is allowed, though:
$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = 0 \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

### Rules that do survive

Not every rule of numbers fails, and knowing which survive is half the cure.
The transpose of a sum is the sum of the transposes, $(A + B)^{\mathsf{T}} = A^{\mathsf{T}} + B^{\mathsf{T}}$, with no change of order.
The transpose and the inverse of a product reverse the order, $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}$, and this reversal is a true rule, not an error.
Cancellation survives only for invertible factors.
A product of nonzero matrices can be $0$, for example $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}^2 = 0$, so $AB = AC$ gives $B = C$ only when $A$ is invertible.

The recommendations of the Linear Algebra Curriculum Study Group in 1993, and the studies that Dorier collected in a volume of 2000, made errors like these a part of course design.
The essay on [[teaching linear algebra|9AXCDDT1Z1]] describes this work.

### Try it

Is $\det(-A) = -\det A$ for every $2 \times 2$ matrix $A$?
No: $\det(-A) = (-1)^2 \det A = \det A$, so the rule fails whenever $\det A \neq 0$, for example for $A = I_2$.

Do the columns of $R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ span the same line?
No: $\operatorname{Col} R$ is the line through $(1, 0)$ and $\operatorname{Col} A$ is the line through $(1, 2)$, although $R$ comes from $A$ by one row operation.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Numbers can be multiplied in either order: $2 \times 3 = 3 \times 2$.
Matrices are tables of numbers with their own product, and for them the order matters.
Several other habits from numbers also fail for matrices.
A good defense is to try each new rule on a small table of numbers before you trust it.

### L2 human

Here is a careful check of the column space error with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and its echelon form $R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Is $(1, 0)$ in $\operatorname{Col} A$?
We need numbers $x, y$ with $x (1, 2) + y (2, 4) = (1, 0)$, that is,
$$x + 2y = 1, \qquad 2x + 4y = 0.$$
Twice the first equation gives $2x + 4y = 2$, which contradicts the second, so $(1, 0)$ is not in $\operatorname{Col} A$.
But $(1, 0)$ is the first column of $R$, so it is in $\operatorname{Col} R$.

The correct use of $R$ is to find the pivot positions, here the first column, and then to take those columns of $A$.
Watch out: $A$ and $R$ have the same null space, so their column spaces have the same dimension, but the column spaces themselves are different lines.

### L3 human

Each error is a homomorphism that fails.
The determinant is a homomorphism for multiplication but not for addition, and matrix multiplication is associative but not commutative.
Row operations are left multiplications by invertible matrices $E$, so $\operatorname{Nul}(EA) = \operatorname{Nul} A$ while $\operatorname{Col}(EA) = E \operatorname{Col} A$, which is a different subspace in general.
The sub-areas on [[matrices|Y1ESNQ2V52]], [[systems|DXSXFVDMXQ]], [[determinants|DTBK11SR77]] and [[eigenvalues|6R1D6QPY3S]] treat each rule in full.
