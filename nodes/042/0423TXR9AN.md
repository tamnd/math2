## Statement

Let $G = \langle g \rangle$ be a [[cyclic group|0KVGH0J5B3]] of finite order $n$, with generator $g$.
For each positive [[divisor|0PG2H7WKGG]] $d$ of $n$, put $H_d = \langle g^{n/d} \rangle$.

1. (Existence and uniqueness.) Let $d$ be a positive divisor of $n$. The [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] $H_d$ has order $d$, and it is the only subgroup of $G$ of order $d$.
2. (All subgroups.) The order of every subgroup of $G$ divides $n$. So the subgroups of $G$ are exactly the groups $H_d$, and the number of subgroups is the number of positive divisors of $n$.
3. (Containment.) Let $d$ and $d'$ be positive divisors of $n$. Then $H_d \subseteq H_{d'}$ if and only if $d$ divides $d'$.
4. (Lattice.) Order the [[lattice of subgroups|0B8BW4YWTF]] of $G$ by inclusion, and the positive divisors of $n$ by divisibility. Then the map $H \mapsto \abs{H}$ is an [[order isomorphism|0D3WA36K2Q]] from the first set onto the second. For subgroups $H$ and $K$ of $G$, it sends $H \cap K$ to $\gcd(\abs{H}, \abs{K})$, and it sends the subgroup $\langle H \cup K \rangle$ to $\operatorname{lcm}(\abs{H}, \abs{K})$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

1. The group must be cyclic. The [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]] $V = \set{e, a, b, c}$ has order $4$ and three subgroups of order $2$: $\set{e, a}$, $\set{e, b}$ and $\set{e, c}$.
2. The number $d$ must divide $n$. By part 2, a cyclic group of order $12$ has no subgroup of order $5$, or of any other order that does not divide $12$.
3. The group must be finite for the statement in terms of orders. The infinite cyclic group $\Z$ has only one finite subgroup, $\set{0}$. For $\Z$, the right count uses the index: $\Z$ has exactly one subgroup of index $d$ for each $d \geq 1$, namely $d\Z$.

### Consequences

1. The group $\Z/12$ has exactly $6$ subgroups, one for each divisor of $12$. In additive notation they are $\langle 0 \rangle$, $\langle 6 \rangle$, $\langle 4 \rangle$, $\langle 3 \rangle$, $\langle 2 \rangle$ and $\langle 1 \rangle$, of orders $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$.
2. For a prime $p$, a cyclic group of order $p$ has only the two subgroups $\set{e}$ and $G$.
3. For a prime power $n = p^k$, the subgroups form a chain $H_1 \subseteq H_p \subseteq H_{p^2} \subseteq \cdots \subseteq H_{p^k}$, because each divisor of $p^k$ divides the next one.
4. Each subgroup of $G$ is determined by its order. So two subgroups of $G$ with the same order are equal.
5. The elements of order $d$ in $G$ all lie in $H_d$. This leads to the [[count of elements of each order|0XCYGCCX8V]].

### Converse

The converse of part 1 is true.
A finite group with at most one subgroup of each order is cyclic.
The reason: in such a group, the elements of order $d$ all generate the same subgroup of order $d$.
So there are $0$ or $\varphi(d)$ of them, where $\varphi$ is [[Euler's totient function|0DAMHCT17R]].
These counts add up to $n$, and $n = \sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d)$ by the [[sum of the totient over the divisors|09JXE6XMCC]].
So each count is $\varphi(d)$, and for $d = n$ this gives an element of order $n$.

### Generalizations

1. For the infinite cyclic group $\Z$, the subgroups other than $\set{0}$ are the groups $d\Z$ with $d \geq 1$, one for each index $d$. This is the [[classification of the subgroups of the integers|0SRATCFF1W]].
2. The lattice of subgroups of a cyclic group is distributive. By a theorem of Ore, a group has a distributive lattice of subgroups if and only if it is [[locally cyclic|RXWA8TF2RM]].
3. In a finite abelian group that is not cyclic, a subgroup is not determined by its order. The rule "one subgroup for each divisor" characterizes the cyclic groups among the finite groups, by the converse above.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a clock with $12$ hours, where we add hours.
Start at $0$ and move in steps of $3$ hours: we visit $0, 3, 6, 9$, which is a small group of $4$ hours.
Steps of $4$ hours give the $3$ hours $0, 4, 8$.
The theorem says that the clock has exactly one small group of each size that divides $12$.
The sizes are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6$ and $12$, so the clock has exactly $6$ such groups.
There is no small group of size $5$, because $5$ does not divide $12$.

### L2 human

We find all subgroups of $G = \Z/12$, with generator $g = 1$, and their containments.

1. The positive divisors of $12$ are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$.
2. For each divisor $d$, the generator of $H_d$ is $12/d$. This gives $H_1 = \set{0}$, $H_2 = \set{0, 6}$, $H_3 = \set{0, 4, 8}$, $H_4 = \set{0, 3, 6, 9}$, $H_6 = \set{0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10}$ and $H_{12} = G$.
3. By part 3, $H_2 \subseteq H_4$ because $2 \mid 4$, and $H_3 \subseteq H_6$ because $3 \mid 6$. We can check this in the lists.
4. The groups $H_3$ and $H_4$ are not contained in each other, because $3$ and $4$ do not divide each other.
5. By part 4, $H_4 \cap H_6$ has order $\gcd(4, 6) = 2$. In the lists, $H_4 \cap H_6 = \set{0, 6} = H_2$.

The typical use is a fast list of all subgroups of $\Z/n$: one for each divisor of $n$.
A trap: the subgroup of order $d$ is generated by $g^{n/d}$, not by $g^d$.
In $\Z/12$, the subgroup of order $4$ is $\langle 3 \rangle$, while $\langle 4 \rangle$ has order $3$.

### L3 human

The theorem says that the [[lattice|WH4SFAACAS]] of subgroups of $\Z/n$ is isomorphic to the lattice of positive divisors of $n$.
In the divisor lattice, $\gcd$ is the meet and $\operatorname{lcm}$ is the join.
By the [[correspondence theorem|0S9ZVZ34MD]], this is the part of the lattice of subgroups of $\Z$ above $n\Z$: the subgroups $d\Z$ with $d \mid n$.
Since $d\Z / n\Z$ has order $n/d$, the subgroup $d\Z / n\Z$ is $H_{n/d}$.

The divisor lattice is a product of chains, one chain for each prime factor of $n$.
So the lattice of subgroups of $\Z/n$ is distributive.
The [[criterion for products of cyclic groups|08FDFEF8D1]] gives the matching splitting of $\Z/n$ into cyclic groups of prime power order.
The same picture holds for the ideals of $\Z/n\Z$ as a ring, because the subgroups of $\Z/n\Z$ are its ideals.
