## Statement

The real spectral theorem says that the symmetric matrices are exactly the real matrices that become diagonal after a rotation of the coordinate axes.

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional inner product space, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be a linear operator.

1. Operator form. Let $V$ be real. Then $T$ is [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]] if and only if $V$ has an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of [[eigenvectors|0B5H4208Z9]] of $T$.
2. Matrix form. Let $A$ be a real $n \times n$ matrix. Then $A$ is [[symmetric|0N28QN4CYA]] if and only if there are a real [[orthogonal matrix|0QRBVFJ50X]] $Q$ and a real [[diagonal matrix|0Q9PGXD9E4]] $D$ with
$$A = Q D Q^{\mathsf{T}}.$$
3. Complex self-adjoint case. Let $V$ be complex and let $T$ be self-adjoint. Then $V$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $T$, and every eigenvalue of $T$ is real.

In part 2, the columns of $Q$ form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ of eigenvectors of $A$, and the diagonal entries of $D$ are the matching eigenvalues.

## Remarks

### A first diagonalization

Take the symmetric matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product.
Its characteristic polynomial factors, so the eigenvalues are $6$ and $1$:
$$(5 - t)(2 - t) - 4 = t^2 - 7t + 6 = (t - 6)(t - 1).$$
The vector $(2, 1)$ is an eigenvector for $6$, and $(1, -2)$ is an eigenvector for $1$.
Their dot product is $2 - 2 = 0$, as [[orthogonality of eigenspaces|0JHGAV9H25]] predicts.
Divide each vector by its length $\sqrt{5}$ and use them as columns:
$$Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q^{\mathsf{T}} A Q = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $A (2, 1) = (12, 6) = 6 \cdot (2, 1)$ and $A (1, -2) = (1, -2)$.
The [[worked example|MK4ET9TW4Q]] does every step, and a [[3 by 3 example|2P021RCC4Y]] does the same with three distinct eigenvalues.

### Why symmetry is needed

Real eigenvalues are not enough.
The [[matrix with rows (1, 1) and (0, 2)|5RQ7EC5RC6]] has the real eigenvalues $1$ and $2$ and a basis of eigenvectors, $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$.
But these two lines meet at $45$ degrees, so no orthonormal basis of eigenvectors exists, and the matrix is not symmetric.

Normality is not enough over $\mathbb{R}$ either.
The [[rotation by a right angle|1CF483TZZA]] commutes with its transpose, but it moves every line, so it has no real eigenvector.
Real normal operators need $2 \times 2$ rotation blocks, as the [[normal form of a real normal operator|G6T1FNE3JC]] shows.
Over $\mathbb{C}$ the condition is normality, and that is the [[complex spectral theorem|04CW1P44VD]].

Finite dimension is needed for eigenvectors to exist.
On the Hilbert space $L^2[0, 1]$, multiplication by $x$ is a bounded self-adjoint operator with no eigenvalue at all.

### What it gives you

Every real symmetric matrix is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] over $\mathbb{R}$, and its characteristic polynomial splits into real linear factors.
For a quadratic form $q(x) = x^{\mathsf{T}} A x$ with $A$ symmetric, the new coordinates $y = Q^{\mathsf{T}} x$ remove all mixed terms:
$$q(x) = \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \dots + \lambda_n y_n^2.$$
This is the [[principal axis theorem|0PR5P9Z4KM]].
It also shows that $A$ is [[positive definite|0KX66CZMBB]] exactly when every eigenvalue is positive.
If you group the eigenvectors by eigenvalue, you get the [[spectral decomposition|D5KVBA1K9R]] $T = \sum_i \lambda_i P_i$, and with it functions of $T$ such as square roots.

### The converse

The direction "if" is short.
In an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, the matrix of $T$ is a real diagonal matrix $D$.
By the [[matrix of the adjoint|0DBTE6HVBD]], the matrix of $T^*$ in that basis is $D^{\mathsf{T}} = D$, so $T^* = T$.
In matrix form, $(Q D Q^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}} = Q D^{\mathsf{T}} Q^{\mathsf{T}} = Q D Q^{\mathsf{T}}$.
In part 3 the same argument works when the eigenvalues are real, because then $\overline{D}^{\mathsf{T}} = D$.

### Three theorems with one name

Books use the name "spectral theorem" for three related results.
The real version is the one above, for symmetric matrices and self-adjoint operators on real spaces.
The [[spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices|0DGZBQWZWN]] is the matrix form of part 3, $A = U D U^*$ with $U$ unitary and $D$ real.
The [[complex spectral theorem|04CW1P44VD]] is about normal operators on complex spaces, and its eigenvalues can be any complex numbers.
Some books also call self-adjoint operators "symmetric" over $\mathbb{C}$, so read the field before you apply a statement.

### Watch out

The columns of $Q$ must have length $1$.
With the unnormalized columns $(2, 1)$ and $(1, -2)$, the matrix $P$ satisfies $P^{\mathsf{T}} P = 5I$, so $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P = \operatorname{diag}(30, 5)$ and not $\operatorname{diag}(6, 1)$.
A repeated eigenvalue needs care as well.
In the eigenspace of $1$ for the [[matrix I + J|PAJVDWFC9Q]], the eigenvectors $(1, -1, 0)$ and $(1, 0, -1)$ are not orthogonal, since their dot product is $1$.
Apply the [[Gram-Schmidt process|0VT6P0GNQR]] inside each eigenspace before you build $Q$.

### Try it

First, orthogonally diagonalize $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
The eigenvalues are $1$ and $-1$ with eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, so $Q = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $Q^{\mathsf{T}} B Q = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$.

Second, which of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ is orthogonally diagonalizable?
Only the first, because it is symmetric; the second has the real eigenvalues $1$ and $3$, but it is not symmetric.

## History

Cauchy proved in 1829 that the eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are real.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A symmetric matrix acts on the plane or on space as a stretch along perpendicular axes.
The axes are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factors are the eigenvalues.
For the matrix with rows $(5, 2)$ and $(2, 2)$, the direction $(2, 1)$ is stretched by $6$, and the perpendicular direction $(1, -2)$ stays as it is.
If you turn your coordinate axes to point along these two directions, the matrix becomes diagonal.
A matrix that is not symmetric can also have real stretch factors, but then its special directions are usually not perpendicular.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: what curve is $3x^2 + 2xy + 3y^2 = 8$?
The left side is $v^{\mathsf{T}} A v$ for $v = (x, y)$ and the symmetric matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.
Its eigenvalues are $4$ and $2$, with the orthogonal eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$.
In the coordinates $u = (x + y)/\sqrt{2}$ and $w = (x - y)/\sqrt{2}$ along these directions, the equation becomes
$$4u^2 + 2w^2 = 8, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad \frac{u^2}{2} + \frac{w^2}{4} = 1.$$
So the curve is an ellipse with semi-axis $\sqrt{2}$ along $(1, 1)$ and semi-axis $2$ along $(1, -1)$.
Check: the point $(1, 1)$ lies at distance $\sqrt{2}$ along the first axis, and $3 + 2 + 3 = 8$.
The point $(\sqrt{2}, -\sqrt{2})$ lies at distance $2$ along the second axis, and $6 - 4 + 6 = 8$.
The rotation is orthogonal, so it keeps lengths and angles, and the shape that we read off in the new coordinates is the true shape.

### L3 human

Two standard proofs exist.
The [[proof by induction|A81KPS16D7]] finds one eigenvector, passes to its orthogonal complement, and repeats.
The [[variational proof|GHGBEV51GZ]] finds the eigenvectors as successive maxima of $\ip{Tv}{v}$ on smaller and smaller unit spheres, which is the start of the [[Courant-Fischer min-max theorem|CJK2HS2508]].
In infinite dimension, a compact self-adjoint operator still has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, by the [[spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators|A1G25R99PY]].
A general self-adjoint operator needs a projection-valued measure in place of the eigenbasis, as in the [[spectral theorem for unbounded self-adjoint operators|18KPYRRPZY]].
In Mathlib, `LinearMap.IsSymmetric.eigenvectorBasis` gives an orthonormal eigenbasis for a symmetric operator over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$; it states only the direction "only if" of part 1, together with part 3.
