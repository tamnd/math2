## Statement

1. Operator form. Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real inner product space, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear. Then $T$ is [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]] if and only if $V$ has an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of [[eigenvectors|0B5H4208Z9]] of $T$.
2. Matrix form. Let $A$ be a real $n \times n$ matrix. Then $A$ is [[symmetric|0N28QN4CYA]] if and only if there are a real [[orthogonal matrix|0QRBVFJ50X]] $Q$ and a real [[diagonal matrix|0Q9PGXD9E4]] $D$ with
$$A = Q D Q^T.$$
3. Complex case. Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional complex inner product space, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be self-adjoint. Then $V$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $T$, and all eigenvalues of $T$ are real.

In part 2, the columns of $Q$ are an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ of eigenvectors of $A$, and the diagonal entries of $D$ are the eigenvalues.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Symmetry is needed, and real eigenvalues are not enough. [[The trap matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$|~3859cd16]] has the real eigenvalues $1$ and $2$ and a basis of eigenvectors. But the eigenvectors $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$ are not orthogonal, so no orthogonal $Q$ exists.
- Over $\mathbb{R}$, normality is not enough. [[The rotation by a right angle|~369bb802]] is normal, but it has no real eigenvalue. Real normal operators have [[a block form with $2 \times 2$ rotation blocks|~abc653f3]] instead.
- Finite dimension is needed. On the Hilbert space $L^2[0, 1]$, multiplication by $x$ is a bounded self-adjoint operator with no eigenvalue.

### Consequences

- Every real symmetric matrix is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] over $\mathbb{R}$, and its characteristic polynomial splits into real linear factors.
- For a real quadratic form $q(x) = x^T A x$ with $A$ symmetric, the substitution $y = Q^T x$ gives $q(x) = \sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2$. This is the [[principal axis theorem|0PR5P9Z4KM]].
- $A$ is positive definite if and only if all eigenvalues are positive, because $q(x) = \sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2$ in the new coordinates.
- Grouping the eigenvectors by eigenvalue gives [[the spectral decomposition|~e55cbc04]] $T = \sum_i \lambda_i P_i$.

### Converse

Part 1 and part 2 are equivalences, and the direction "if" is short.
In an orthonormal eigenbasis, the matrix of $T$ is a real diagonal matrix.
A diagonal matrix is symmetric, so $T$ is self-adjoint by [[the matrix of the adjoint|0DBTE6HVBD]].
In part 3 the converse also holds: an orthonormal eigenbasis with real eigenvalues makes $T$ self-adjoint, by the same argument.

### Three spectral theorems

The name "spectral theorem" is used for three related results.
This page is about real symmetric matrices and self-adjoint operators.
[[The spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices|0DGZBQWZWN]] is the complex matrix form of part 3.
[[The complex spectral theorem|04CW1P44VD]] is about normal operators on complex spaces.
Some books call self-adjoint operators "symmetric", also over $\mathbb{C}$.

### Common errors

- Students apply the theorem to every real matrix with real eigenvalues. The matrix must be symmetric, as the trap matrix shows.
- Students forget to normalize the eigenvectors. Orthogonal vectors of other lengths do not give an orthogonal matrix $Q$.
- For a repeated eigenvalue, students take any basis of the eigenspace. The basis must be orthonormal, for example after the Gram-Schmidt process.

### Generalizations

- A compact self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, in the sense of Hilbert spaces.
- Bounded and unbounded self-adjoint operators need [[a spectral theorem with projection-valued measures|18KPYRRPZY]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A symmetric matrix acts as a stretch along perpendicular axes.
The axes are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factors are the eigenvalues.
In coordinates along these axes, the matrix is diagonal.
A non-symmetric matrix can also have real stretch factors, but then its special directions are usually not perpendicular.

### L2 human

Worked example: [[the symmetric matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$|~a0fff7f9]].

1. The characteristic polynomial is $t^2 - 7t + 6 = (t - 6)(t - 1)$, so the eigenvalues are $6$ and $1$.
2. The vector $(2, 1)$ is an eigenvector for $6$, and $(1, -2)$ is an eigenvector for $1$. They are orthogonal, as [[orthogonality of eigenspaces|0JHGAV9H25]] predicts.
3. Normalize them and put them as columns: $Q = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. Then $Q^T Q = I$ and $A = Q \operatorname{diag}(6, 1) Q^T$.

The typical use is to rotate the coordinates so that a quadratic form has no mixed terms.
A trap: for a repeated eigenvalue, choose an orthonormal basis inside its eigenspace.

### L3 human

Two proofs are standard.
[[The induction proof|A81KPS16D7]] finds one eigenvector, passes to its orthogonal complement, and repeats.
[[The variational proof|~8046c4e7]] finds the eigenvectors as successive maxima of the quadratic form on spheres, and it gives the min-max characterization of eigenvalues.
In Lean, the decl `LinearMap.IsSymmetric.eigenvectorBasis` gives an orthonormal eigenbasis for a symmetric operator over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.
It states only the direction "only if" of part 1 and part 3.
