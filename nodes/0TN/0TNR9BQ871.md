## Summary

A diagonal matrix is easy to raise to a power: you raise each diagonal entry to that power.
Diagonalization carries this ease over to every [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] matrix, so that $A^{100}$ costs no more work than $A^2$.
Let $F$ be a field, and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$ with $A = P D P^{-1}$, where $P$ is [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] and $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ is [[diagonal|0Q9PGXD9E4]].
Then for every integer $k \ge 0$
$$A^k = P D^k P^{-1}, \qquad D^k = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k, \dots, \lambda_n^k).$$
When every $\lambda_i$ is nonzero, $A$ is invertible and the same formula holds for negative $k$.

## Motivation

Many processes repeat one linear step again and again.
A population model, a [[Markov chain|0G48PYM96Q]] and a linear recurrence such as the Fibonacci rule all have the form $x_{k+1} = A x_k$, so after $k$ steps the state is $A^k x_0$.
Multiplying $k$ matrices by hand is slow, and the product shows no pattern in $k$.
In a basis of [[eigenvectors|0B5H4208Z9]] the step is simple: each coordinate is multiplied by its own eigenvalue.
So $k$ steps multiply that coordinate by $\lambda_i^k$, and this gives a closed formula in $k$ together with the long-term behavior.

The same trick gives every polynomial in $A$, and over $\R$ or $\C$ it gives the [[matrix exponential|020M04FN1H]] that solves linear differential equations.
For matrices that are not diagonalizable, the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]] in the sub-area on canonical forms takes over.

## Notation

Write $A = P D P^{-1}$ with the eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ as the columns of $P$, in the same order as the eigenvalues in $D$.
If $x_0 = c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_n v_n$, then the coefficients are $c = P^{-1} x_0$, and
$$A^k x_0 = c_1 \lambda_1^k v_1 + \dots + c_n \lambda_n^k v_n.$$
With the [[eigendecomposition|D0AP2R7272]] $A = \sum_i \lambda_i E_i$, the same formula reads $A^k = \sum_i \lambda_i^k E_i$.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, the matrix of the [[2 × 2 diagonalization example|306FBDECMB]].
Its trace is $7$ and its determinant is $10$, so its eigenvalues are $5$ and $2$.
The vector $(1, 1)$ is an eigenvector for $5$, and $(1, -2)$ is an eigenvector for $2$, since $A(1, -2) = (2, -4)$.
So we take
$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad P^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Multiply out $P \operatorname{diag}(5^k, 2^k) P^{-1}$:
$$A^k = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \cdot 5^k + 2^k & 5^k - 2^k \\ 2 \cdot 5^k - 2 \cdot 2^k & 5^k + 2 \cdot 2^k \end{pmatrix}.$$
For $k = 1$ this gives $\tfrac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 12 & 3 \\ 6 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$, which is $A$.
For $k = 3$ it gives
$$A^3 = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 258 & 117 \\ 234 & 141 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 86 & 39 \\ 78 & 47 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 18 & 7 \\ 14 & 11 \end{pmatrix}$, and $A^2 A = \begin{pmatrix} 72 + 14 & 18 + 21 \\ 56 + 22 & 14 + 33 \end{pmatrix}$ is the same matrix.

### Why the formula holds

The inner factors cancel in pairs.
For $k = 2$,
$$A^2 = (P D P^{-1})(P D P^{-1}) = P D (P^{-1} P) D P^{-1} = P D^2 P^{-1}.$$
Induction on $k$ gives $A^{k+1} = A^k A = P D^k P^{-1} P D P^{-1} = P D^{k+1} P^{-1}$.
A diagonal matrix multiplies entry by entry on the diagonal, so $D^k$ has the entries $\lambda_i^k$.
Taking sums of powers, every polynomial $f$ gives $f(A) = P f(D) P^{-1}$ with $f(D) = \operatorname{diag}(f(\lambda_1), \dots, f(\lambda_n))$.

### Three typical uses

The [[Fibonacci matrix|AC3XJ3DC9X]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalues $\varphi = \tfrac{1 + \sqrt 5}{2}$ and $\psi = \tfrac{1 - \sqrt 5}{2}$.
Its powers hold the [[Fibonacci numbers|0GA9K57A39]], and the formula $A^k = P D^k P^{-1}$ turns into [[Binet's formula|0404D8TN7Y]].

The [[migration matrix|GP1NPA0Z0W]] $\begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalues $1$ and $0.7$.
Since $0.7^k \to 0$, its powers converge, and the population tends to the eigenvector for $1$.

The swap $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ with the eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$.
So $S^k = P \operatorname{diag}(1, (-1)^k) P^{-1}$, which is $I$ for even $k$ and $S$ for odd $k$.
In the same way a [[projection|033DA1WE8Y]] has $D$ with entries $0$ and $1$, so $D^k = D$ and the projection equals all its powers $k \ge 1$.

### Long-term behavior

The eigenvalue of largest absolute value wins in the long run.
Suppose $|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_i|$ for all $i \ge 2$ and $c_1 \ne 0$.
Divide the formula for $A^k x_0$ by $\lambda_1^k$:
$$\frac{A^k x_0}{\lambda_1^k} = c_1 v_1 + \sum_{i \ge 2} c_i \Big(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\Big)^k v_i \longrightarrow c_1 v_1.$$
So the direction of $A^k x_0$ tends to the line of $v_1$, which is the idea of [[power iteration|H4MQD841BR]].
When $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $|\lambda_i| < 1$ for $i \ge 2$, the vectors $A^k x_0$ themselves converge to $c_1 v_1$, a steady state.
The next largest eigenvalue sets the speed: if $|\lambda_2|$ is the largest of the $|\lambda_i|$ with $i \ge 2$, the error shrinks like $|\lambda_2 / \lambda_1|^k$.

### What it is not

The formula needs a diagonalization, and some matrices have none.
The [[shear|SBDF8APDF7]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has powers $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Its only eigenvalue is $1$, so a formula $P D^k P^{-1}$ would have $D^k = I$ and give $I$ for every $k$, and the entry $k$ shows that this is false.
A nonzero [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]] matrix $N$ has $N^k = 0$ for large $k$, yet it is a [[defective matrix|5F2AV5855Y]], and its powers come from the definition.
The [[rotation by a right angle|XS5H42KEP5]] has no diagonalization over $\R$, but over $\C$ it has $D = \operatorname{diag}(i, -i)$, and the formula works there.

### Watch out

Two slips are common.
The first is to raise the entries of $A$ to the $k$-th power, as if $A$ were diagonal.
For the matrix $A$ above, $a_{11}^3 = 64$, while the true entry of $A^3$ is $86$.
The second is the order of the factors.
With the eigenvectors as columns of $P$, the formula is $P D^k P^{-1}$, not $P^{-1} D^k P$.
For the swap and its symmetric $P$ the two orders happen to agree, so test the order on a matrix like $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.

### History

Linear recurrences were solved through their characteristic equations long before matrices existed.
The closed formula for the Fibonacci numbers carries the name of Binet, who published it in 1843, although de Moivre and Daniel Bernoulli knew it earlier.
The matrix method writes the same solution as $P D^k P^{-1}$.

### Try it

First, use the diagonalization above to find $A^{-1}$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.
Put $k = -1$ into the formula, so $5^{-1}$ and $2^{-1}$ replace $5^k$ and $2^k$: you get $\tfrac{1}{10} \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, which is the usual inverse with $\det A = 10$.

Second, what is the limit of $A^k / 5^k$?
The terms with $(2/5)^k$ vanish, so the limit is $\tfrac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, a matrix of rank one whose columns lie on the eigenvector $(1, 1)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Raising a matrix to a high power by hand is slow.
Some matrices only stretch a few special directions, and each direction has its own stretch factor.
In those directions one step multiplies each coordinate by its factor.
So $k$ steps multiply it by the factor $k$ times, and you change back to the usual coordinates at the end.
For a stretch by $5$ along one direction and by $2$ along another, ten steps stretch by $5^{10}$ and $2^{10}$.

### L2 human

Let us compute $A^k$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
The characteristic polynomial is
$$p_A(t) = (t - 1)^2 - 4 = (t - 3)(t + 1),$$
so the eigenvalues are $3$ and $-1$.
The eigenvectors are $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, since $A(1, 1) = (3, 3)$ and $A(1, -1) = (-1, 1)$.
So $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $P^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2} P$ because $P^2 = 2I$.
Multiply out $P \operatorname{diag}(3^k, (-1)^k) P^{-1}$:
$$A^k = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 3^k + (-1)^k & 3^k - (-1)^k \\ 3^k - (-1)^k & 3^k + (-1)^k \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check with $k = 2$: the formula gives $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, and direct multiplication gives $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 4 & 2 + 2 \\ 2 + 2 & 4 + 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the same matrix.
In the long run $A^k / 3^k \to \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so the eigenvalue $3$ decides the growth.
Watch out for the factor $(-1)^k$: it flips the sign of the small part at each step, and dropping it gives the wrong answer for odd $k$.

### L3 human

The formula is the case $f(t) = t^k$ of the functional calculus $f(A) = P f(D) P^{-1}$, which also gives $e^{A}$, and $\sqrt{A}$ when the eigenvalues have square roots.
For a matrix that is not diagonalizable, write $A = S + N$ by the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]].
Since $S$ and $N$ commute, the binomial formula gives $A^k = \sum_{j} \binom{k}{j} S^{k-j} N^j$, a finite sum because $N$ is nilpotent, and factors that are polynomials in $k$ appear.
The growth rate of $\|A^k\|$ is the [[spectral radius|9V7YQA85PC]], by Gelfand's formula $\lim_k \|A^k\|^{1/k} = \max_i |\lambda_i|$.
For linear recurrences of any order the same idea runs through the [[companion matrix|0ZCWPNZTQ3]] of the recurrence.
