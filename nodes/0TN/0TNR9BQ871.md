## Summary

Let $F$ be a field, and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$ with $A = P D P^{-1}$, where $P$ is [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] and $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$.
Then, for each integer $k \ge 0$,
$$A^k = P D^k P^{-1}, \qquad D^k = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k, \dots, \lambda_n^k).$$
If all $\lambda_i \ne 0$, the formula also holds for negative $k$.
So a power of a [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] matrix costs one diagonalization and $n$ scalar powers, and the long-term behavior of $x_{k+1} = A x_k$ can be read from the eigenvalues.

## Motivation

Many processes repeat the same linear step: population models, Markov chains and linear recurrences such as the Fibonacci recurrence.
After $k$ steps, the state is $A^k x_0$.
Multiplying $k$ matrices by hand is slow, and the product shows no pattern.
In a basis of eigenvectors, each coordinate is multiplied by its own eigenvalue at each step, which gives a closed formula.

## Notation

With the [[eigendecomposition|D0AP2R7272]] $A = \sum_i \lambda_i E_i$, the formula reads $A^k = \sum_i \lambda_i^k E_i$.
If $x_0 = c_1 v_1 + \cdots + c_n v_n$ in a basis of eigenvectors, then $A^k x_0 = c_1 \lambda_1^k v_1 + \cdots + c_n \lambda_n^k v_n$.
The coefficients are $c = P^{-1} x_0$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The [[Fibonacci matrix|AC3XJ3DC9X]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalues $\varphi$ and $\psi = -1/\varphi$. Its powers give [[Binet's formula|0404D8TN7Y]] for the [[Fibonacci numbers|0GA9K57A39]].
- The [[migration matrix|GP1NPA0Z0W]] $\begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalues $1$ and $0.7$. Its powers converge, and the population tends to a steady state.
- For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ in the [[2 × 2 example|306FBDECMB]], $A^k = 5^k E_1 + 2^k E_2$, and $k = 3$ gives $\begin{pmatrix} 86 & 39 \\ 78 & 47 \end{pmatrix}$.
- A [[projection|033DA1WE8Y]] has $D$ with entries in $\{0, 1\}$, so $D^k = D$ and $P^k = P$ for $k \ge 1$.
- The swap $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = P \operatorname{diag}(1, -1) P^{-1}$ has $D^k = \operatorname{diag}(1, (-1)^k)$, so its powers alternate between $I$ and itself.

### Non-examples

- The [[shear|SBDF8APDF7]] $S$ is not diagonalizable, and $S^k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. The entry $k$ grows linearly, which no formula $P D^k P^{-1}$ with $D^k = I$ can give.
- A nonzero [[nilpotent|063D5Z2C70]] matrix $N$ has $N^k = 0$ for large $k$, but it is not diagonalizable. Its powers come from the definition, not from $D^k$.
- The rotation by a right angle has no diagonalization over $\R$. Its powers are computed over $\C$ with $D = \operatorname{diag}(i, -i)$, as in the [[rotation example|XS5H42KEP5]].

### Basic properties

- The proof is a cancellation: $(P D P^{-1})^k = P D (P^{-1} P) D \cdots D P^{-1} = P D^k P^{-1}$, by induction on $k$.
- For each polynomial $f$, $f(A) = P f(D) P^{-1}$, with $f(D) = \operatorname{diag}(f(\lambda_1), \dots, f(\lambda_n))$.
- Over $\R$ or $\C$, the [[matrix exponential|020M04FN1H]] is $e^A = P e^D P^{-1}$.
- If $|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_i|$ for all $i \ge 2$ and $c_1 \ne 0$, then $A^k x_0 / \lambda_1^k \to c_1 v_1$. This is the idea of the power method.
- If $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $|\lambda_i| < 1$ for $i \ge 2$, then $A^k x_0 \to c_1 v_1$. This is the steady state of a [[Markov chain|0G48PYM96Q]] with a diagonalizable transition matrix.

### History

Linear recurrences were solved by characteristic equations long before matrices.
The closed formula for the Fibonacci numbers is named after Binet, who published it in 1843, but de Moivre and Daniel Bernoulli knew it earlier.
The matrix method writes the same solution as $P D^k P^{-1}$.

### Common errors

- Computing $A^k$ entry by entry as $a_{ij}^k$. Only $D^k$ is computed entry by entry.
- Writing $A^k = P^{-1} D^k P$ when the columns of $P$ are eigenvectors. The correct order is $P D^k P^{-1}$.
- Using the formula for a [[defective matrix|5F2AV5855Y]]. Then no such $P$ exists, and one needs the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Raising a matrix to a high power by hand is slow.
If the matrix only stretches some special directions, then we work in those directions.
There, each step multiplies each coordinate by a fixed number.
So $k$ steps multiply it by that number $k$ times, and we change back at the end.

### L2 human

Compute $A^k$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $p_A = (t - 1)^2 - 4 = (t - 3)(t + 1)$, so the eigenvalues are $3$ and $-1$.
2. Eigenvectors: $A(1, 1) = (3, 3)$ and $A(1, -1) = (-1, 1)$.
3. $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $P^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. $A^k = P \operatorname{diag}(3^k, (-1)^k) P^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 3^k + (-1)^k & 3^k - (-1)^k \\ 3^k - (-1)^k & 3^k + (-1)^k \end{pmatrix}$.
5. Check with $k = 2$: the formula gives $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, and $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 4 & 2 + 2 \\ 2 + 2 & 4 + 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is the same.

The typical use is the long-term behavior: here $A^k / 3^k \to \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
The trap is to forget the factor $(-1)^k$, which changes the sign of the small part at each step.

### L3 human

The formula is the special case $f(t) = t^k$ of the functional calculus $f(A) = P f(D) P^{-1}$.
For a matrix that is not diagonalizable, write $A = S + N$ by the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]].
Then $A^k = \sum_{j} \binom{k}{j} S^{k-j} N^j$ is a finite sum, since $N$ is nilpotent, and polynomial factors in $k$ appear.
The growth rate of $\|A^k\|$ is governed by the spectral radius, by Gelfand's formula.
