## Statement

The normal equations turn a least-squares problem into a square linear system, by asking that the residual be perpendicular to every column of $A$.

Let $A$ be a real $m \times n$ matrix with [[transpose|0W809K6AW0]] $A^{\mathsf{T}}$, and let $b \in \R^m$.
Then the following hold.

1. A vector $\hat x \in \R^n$ is a [[least-squares solution|0BYTQ4X1HC]] of $Ax = b$ if and only if it solves the *normal equations*
$$A^{\mathsf{T}} A \hat x = A^{\mathsf{T}} b.$$
2. Equivalently, $\hat x$ is a least-squares solution if and only if the residual $b - A \hat x$ is orthogonal to every column of $A$.
3. The normal equations always have a solution.
4. The [[null spaces|0PQ2GGY1QV]] agree, $\operatorname{Nul}(A^{\mathsf{T}} A) = \operatorname{Nul}(A)$, so $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ and $A$ have the same rank.
5. The $n \times n$ matrix $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] if and only if the columns of $A$ are [[linearly independent|0CRMGPAH83]], and in that case $\hat x = (A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}} b$ is the unique least-squares solution.

Over $\C$ the same holds with the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]] $A^*$ in place of $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
The [[proof|AVAKVED5NV]] turns the orthogonality condition of best approximation into a linear system.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The formula in part 5 needs independent columns.
For the matrix with columns $(1, 1, 1)$ and $(2, 2, 2)$, the product $A^{\mathsf{T}} A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 12 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $0$.
The normal equations then have a whole line of solutions, as in [[the example with dependent columns|FM9VEAKSA7]], and $(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{-1}$ does not exist.
Parts 1 to 4 need no hypothesis at all, and they still hold for this matrix.

Why multiply by the transpose and not by an inverse?
The matrix $A$ is usually not square, so $A^{-1} b$ has no meaning.
Multiplying $Ax = b$ on the left by $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ gives a square $n \times n$ system, and part 1 says that this particular square system has the right solutions.

### What it gives you

For a line $y = c + st$ through points $(t_i, y_i)$ with $i = 1, \dots, m$, the normal equations become two equations in $c$ and $s$:
$$m c + \Big(\sum t_i\Big) s = \sum y_i, \qquad \Big(\sum t_i\Big) c + \Big(\sum t_i^2\Big) s = \sum t_i y_i.$$
[[The four-point example|H2D0QA4AXV]] solves them by hand.
For independent columns, the projection of $b$ onto $\operatorname{Col}(A)$ is $A \hat x$, so the projection matrix is $A (A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}}$, as in the [[projection formulas|AK3JJ5HHG2]].

### The converse

Parts 1 and 5 are equivalences already.
A nearby claim is false: a solution of $A^{\mathsf{T}} A x = A^{\mathsf{T}} b$ need not solve $Ax = b$.
For $A = (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $b = (0, 2)$, the normal equation $2x = 2$ gives $x = 1$, but $Ax = (1, 1) \ne b$.

### Numerical trouble

The normal equations square the condition number.
For $A$ with independent columns, the 2-norm [[condition number|AB3S6MHJ8X]] is $\kappa_2(A) = \sigma_{\max} / \sigma_{\min}$, the ratio of the largest and smallest [[singular values|09VMDSRXWJ]].
With the [[singular value decomposition|000NPTMX6J]] $A = U \Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}}$ we get $A^{\mathsf{T}} A = V \Sigma^{\mathsf{T}} \Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}}$, so the singular values of $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ are the squares $\sigma_i^2$ and $\kappa_2(A^{\mathsf{T}} A) = \kappa_2(A)^2$.

Here is a small case where this matters.
Take $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$ and
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ \varepsilon & 0 \\ 0 & \varepsilon \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^{\mathsf{T}} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + \varepsilon^2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 + \varepsilon^2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The columns of $A$ are independent, but in double precision $1 + \varepsilon^2 = 1 + 10^{-16}$ rounds to $1$, so the computed $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ is the singular matrix of all ones.
For this reason the [[QR decomposition|0ERWG4NXQ9]], which works with $A$ itself, is preferred in computation; see [[numerical methods for least squares|AWQA2Y30N4]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The normal equations turn the question "which $x$ makes $Ax$ closest to $b$?" into an ordinary square linear system.
The word "normal" means "perpendicular" here.
The equations say that the error $b - Ax$ meets every column of $A$ at a right angle, just as the shortest path from a point to a floor meets the floor at a right angle.

### L2 human

For the four points $(0, 1)$, $(1, 2)$, $(2, 2)$, $(3, 4)$ and the line $y = c + st$, we first collect the sums.
$$m = 4, \quad \sum t_i = 6, \quad \sum t_i^2 = 14, \quad \sum y_i = 9, \quad \sum t_i y_i = 18.$$
The normal equations are $4c + 6s = 9$ and $6c + 14s = 18$.
Subtract $\tfrac{3}{2}$ times the first equation from the second to get $5s = 4.5$, so $s = 0.9$ and then $c = (9 - 5.4)/4 = 0.9$.
This is the typical use: a small fit by hand, or a fit whose columns are far from dependent.
The trap is to use the normal equations when the columns are nearly dependent, where the squared condition number destroys the accuracy.

### L3 human

The normal equations are the first-order condition for the convex quadratic $f(x) = \norm{Ax - b}^2$, whose gradient is $2 A^{\mathsf{T}} (Ax - b)$.
The matrix $A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ is the [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]] of the columns of $A$, and it is positive definite exactly when the columns are independent, because $x^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} A x = \norm{Ax}^2$.
In an abstract inner product space the same equations say $\ip{b - \sum_j x_j u_j}{u_i} = 0$ for a spanning family $u_1, \dots, u_n$ of the subspace.
In statistics, $(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)^{-1} A^{\mathsf{T}} b$ is the ordinary least-squares estimator, which belongs to [[linear regression|41FVC4P5JB]].
