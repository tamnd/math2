## Summary

A true "if A, then B" does not tell you that "if B, then A" is true.
"If a number ends in $0$, then it is even" is true, but "if a number is even, then it ends in $0$" is false, as $4$ shows.

Let $\varphi \to \psi$ be a [[material implication|0NMG7W7Q11]] with antecedent $\varphi$ and consequent $\psi$.
Its converse is $\psi \to \varphi$, in which the two parts change places.
Its inverse is $\neg \varphi \to \neg \psi$, in which both parts are [[negated|029JBBF693]] and keep their places.
Neither the converse nor the inverse is a [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]] of $\varphi \to \psi$.
The converse and the inverse are [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]] to each other, because the inverse is the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] of the converse.

After almost every theorem comes the question "does the converse hold?", and the answer needs its own proof each time.

## Motivation

A theorem "if $P$, then $Q$" gives information in one direction only.
For the [[Pythagorean theorem|0VP3A1290K]] the other direction holds: a triangle with sides $a$, $b$, $c$ and $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ has a right angle.
For [[Fermat's little theorem|0M25PRN3WQ]] the other direction fails: $341 = 11 \cdot 31$ is composite, but $2^{340} \equiv 1 \pmod{341}$.
So we need a name for the statement with the arrow reversed, and a rule for what it is equivalent to.

## Notation

For the implication $p \to q$, there are four related implications.

| Name | Formula | Equivalent to |
|---|---|---|
| implication | $p \to q$ | its contrapositive |
| converse | $q \to p$ | the inverse |
| inverse | $\neg p \to \neg q$ | the converse |
| contrapositive | $\neg q \to \neg p$ | the implication |

The converse $q \to p$ is sometimes written $p \leftarrow q$ and read "$p$ if $q$".
Negation is also written $\sim p$, $p'$ or $\overline{p}$, and the arrow is also written $\supset$ or $\Rightarrow$.
The words "converse" and "inverse" refer to an implication that is given first, since a formula on its own is not a converse.
The [[definition|0RVYE75E2M]] gives the formal version.

## Remarks

### The four columns side by side

Let $p$ be "the number ends in $0$" and $q$ be "the number is even".
The truth table of the four related implications shows which of them agree.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \to q$ | $q \to p$ | $\neg p \to \neg q$ | $\neg q \to \neg p$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | T | F |
| F | T | T | F | F | T |
| F | F | T | T | T | T |

In the row TF the implication is false and the converse is true, and in the row FT it is the other way round.
So neither of $p \to q$ and $q \to p$ follows from the other.
The number $4$ is in the row FT: it does not end in $0$ and it is even, so it refutes the converse and the inverse together.
The columns of the converse and the inverse are equal, and so are the columns of the implication and the contrapositive.

### Worked examples

The [[converse of the Pythagorean theorem is true|EFF30FNVZ0]], so the condition $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ characterizes right triangles.
For [["if 6 divides n, then 3 divides n"|1DVJWM421F]], the implication is true, and $n = 3$ refutes both its converse and its inverse.
The converse of "if $x = 2$, then $x^2 = 4$" is false for a real number $x$, because $x = -2$ satisfies $x^2 = 4$.
Wilson's theorem says that $(p - 1)! \equiv -1 \pmod p$ for each prime $p$, and [[its converse|06WRV8HFDY]] is also true.
[[Taking the converse of Fermat's little theorem as true|ZHMHBFQ2E4]] leads to a wrong primality test.

### What is not a converse or an inverse

The contrapositive $\neg q \to \neg p$ both swaps and negates the parts, so it is [[neither the converse nor the inverse|JADJM1SJ4E]].
The formula $q \land p$ is not the converse of $p \to q$, because a converse is an implication.
The formula $\neg(p \to q)$ is not the inverse either.
It is the negation of the implication, and it is equivalent to $p \land \neg q$.

### Where intuitionistic logic differs

In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], $p \to q$ still proves $\neg q \to \neg p$, but $\neg q \to \neg p$ no longer proves $p \to q$.
Apply this to the converse $q \to p$: it proves the inverse $\neg p \to \neg q$, but the inverse does not prove the converse.
So the converse and the inverse are equivalent classically, and only the converse is the stronger one intuitionistically.

### Other properties

The converse of the converse is the original implication.
The implication and its converse are both true exactly when the [[biconditional|0S6GMNJ62Y]] $p \leftrightarrow q$ is true.
For a universal statement $\forall x\, (P(x) \to Q(x))$, the converse is $\forall x\, (Q(x) \to P(x))$, with the quantifier outside and only the arrow reversed.

### Watch out

To prove the converse instead of the statement is the error of [[affirming the consequent|J7KFCFQRBT]], and [[checking a proof|4M98ZZNK0E]] must test for it.
To prove the inverse instead of the contrapositive proves the wrong statement, as in [[proving the inverse instead of the contrapositive|D0531MQ745]].
Examples do not prove a converse.
The statement "if $2^{n-1} \equiv 1 \pmod n$, then $n$ is prime" holds for every $n$ from $3$ to $340$, and it fails at $n = 341$.

### History

Euclid's Elements gives the Pythagorean theorem as Proposition 47 of Book I and its converse as Proposition 48.
So the habit of proving a converse as a separate theorem goes back to Greek geometry.
The modern table of implication, converse, inverse and contrapositive uses the material conditional, which Philo of Megara proposed.

### Try it

Write the converse and the inverse of "if a quadrilateral is a square, then its diagonals have equal length", and decide whether each is true.
Both are false: a rectangle with sides $1$ and $2$ is not a square, and its two diagonals both have length $\sqrt{5}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The converse of "if A, then B" is "if B, then A".
The inverse is "if not A, then not B".
Take "if a number ends in $0$, then it is even", which is true.
Its converse is "if a number is even, then it ends in $0$", and $4$ shows that it is false.
Its inverse is "if a number does not end in $0$, then it is not even", and $4$ shows again that it is false.
So a true sentence can have a false converse and a false inverse.

### L2 human

We find the converse and the inverse of "if a quadrilateral is a square, then its diagonals have equal length".
Let $p$ be "the quadrilateral is a square" and $q$ be "its diagonals have equal length".
The implication $p \to q$ is true, because both diagonals of a square with side $s$ have length $s\sqrt{2}$.
The converse $q \to p$ says that equal diagonals force a square.
A rectangle with sides $1$ and $2$ has two diagonals of length $\sqrt{5}$ and is not a square, so the converse is false.
The inverse $\neg p \to \neg q$ says that a non-square has diagonals of different lengths, and the same rectangle refutes it.
One rectangle refutes both, as the equivalence of converse and inverse predicts.
At the end of a theorem, ask whether the converse holds, and either prove it or find one object that refutes it.
The inverse sounds like a negated version of the theorem, but it does not follow from the theorem; only the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] does.

### L3 human

Read $p \to q$ as the order relation $p \le q$ in the [[two-element Boolean algebra|0WG43AC0EV]], or in the algebra of formulas up to equivalence.
Then the converse is the same relation read in the opposite order, and the implication with its converse is the equality $p = q$.
For predicates, $\forall x\, (P(x) \to Q(x))$ says that the truth set of $P$ is a [[subset|0BJR9QR08E]] of the truth set of $Q$, and the converse says the reverse inclusion.
Negation reverses this order in a Boolean algebra, so the inverse is the converse in disguise.

Many theorems of algebra come with a partial converse.
Lagrange's theorem says that the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group, and its converse fails: the alternating group $A_4$ has order $12$ and no subgroup of order $6$.
Cauchy's theorem and Sylow's theorems recover the converse for primes and for prime powers.
In reverse mathematics, a theorem is shown to imply the axioms used to prove it, which is a converse at the level of axiom systems.
