## Summary

Let $\varphi$ and $\psi$ be formulas, and let $\varphi \to \psi$ be the [[material implication|0NMG7W7Q11]] with antecedent $\varphi$ and consequent $\psi$.
The converse of $\varphi \to \psi$ is the implication $\psi \to \varphi$: the antecedent and the consequent change places.
The inverse of $\varphi \to \psi$ is the implication $\neg \varphi \to \neg \psi$: both parts are replaced by their [[negations|029JBBF693]], and they keep their places.
Neither the converse nor the inverse is a [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]] of $\varphi \to \psi$: each of them can be false while $\varphi \to \psi$ is true.
The converse and the inverse are [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]] to each other, because the inverse is the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] of the converse.
The idea matters because the question "does the converse hold?" follows almost every theorem, and its answer must be proved separately each time.

## Motivation

A theorem of the form "if $P$, then $Q$" gives information in one direction only.
After a theorem is proved, the next question is often whether the other direction also holds.
For the [[Pythagorean theorem|0VP3A1290K]], the other direction holds: a triangle with sides $a$, $b$, $c$ and $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ has a right angle.
For [[Fermat's little theorem|0M25PRN3WQ]], the other direction fails: $341 = 11 \cdot 31$ is composite, but $2^{340} \equiv 1 \pmod{341}$.
So we need a name for the statement with the arrow reversed, and a clear rule for what it is and is not equivalent to.

## Notation

For the implication $p \to q$, the four related implications are these.

| Name | Formula | Equivalent to |
|---|---|---|
| implication | $p \to q$ | its contrapositive |
| converse | $q \to p$ | the inverse |
| inverse | $\neg p \to \neg q$ | the converse |
| contrapositive | $\neg q \to \neg p$ | the implication |

The converse $q \to p$ is sometimes written $p \leftarrow q$, read "$p$ if $q$".
Negation is also written $\sim p$, $p'$ or $\overline{p}$, and the arrow is also written $\supset$ or $\Rightarrow$.
The words "converse" and "inverse" refer to an implication that is given first; a formula on its own is not a converse.
The [[definition node|0RVYE75E2M]] gives the formal definition.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The converse of the Pythagorean theorem is true|EFF30FNVZ0]]: the implication and its converse are both theorems, so the triangle condition is a characterization.
2. [[The converse and the inverse of "if 6 divides n, then 3 divides n"|1DVJWM421F]]: the implication is true, and its converse and inverse are both false for $n = 3$.
3. The converse of "if $x = 2$, then $x^2 = 4$" is "if $x^2 = 4$, then $x = 2$". For a real number $x$ the converse is false, because $x = -2$ satisfies $x^2 = 4$.
4. The inverse of "if $n$ is prime and $n > 2$, then $n$ is odd" is "if it is not the case that $n$ is prime and $n > 2$, then $n$ is even". It is false for $n = 9$, which is odd and not prime.
5. Wilson's theorem says that $(p - 1)! \equiv -1 \pmod p$ for each prime $p$. [[Its converse|06WRV8HFDY]] is also true: an integer $n > 1$ with $(n - 1)! \equiv -1 \pmod n$ is prime.
6. [[Taking the converse of Fermat's little theorem as true|ZHMHBFQ2E4]] shows how a false converse leads to a wrong primality test.

### Non-examples

1. [[The contrapositive of an implication is neither its converse nor its inverse|JADJM1SJ4E]]: in $\neg q \to \neg p$ the parts are both swapped and negated.
2. The formula $q \land p$ is not the converse of $p \to q$. A converse is an implication, and swapping the parts of a conjunction gives an equivalent formula.
3. The formula $\neg(p \to q)$ is not the inverse of $p \to q$. It is the [[negation|029JBBF693]] of the implication, and it is equivalent to $p \land \neg q$.

### Basic properties

1. The truth tables of the four related implications are these, with the rows in the standard order.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \to q$ | $q \to p$ | $\neg p \to \neg q$ | $\neg q \to \neg p$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | T | F |
| F | T | T | F | F | T |
| F | F | T | T | T | T |

2. In row TF the implication is false and the converse is true; in row FT the implication is true and the converse is false. So neither of $p \to q$ and $q \to p$ follows from the other.
3. The columns of the converse and of the inverse are equal, so $q \to p \equiv \neg p \to \neg q$. This is the law of the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] applied to the converse.
4. The converse of the converse is the original implication.
5. The implication and its converse are both true exactly when the [[biconditional|0S6GMNJ62Y]] $p \leftrightarrow q$ is true.
6. For a universal statement $\forall x\, (P(x) \to Q(x))$, the converse is $\forall x\, (Q(x) \to P(x))$: the quantifier stays outside, and only the arrow is reversed.

### History

Euclid's Elements gives the Pythagorean theorem as Proposition 47 of Book I and its converse as Proposition 48.
So the habit of stating and proving a converse as a separate theorem goes back to Greek geometry.
The modern four-way table of implication, converse, inverse and contrapositive uses the material conditional, which Philo of Megara proposed.

### Common errors

1. A student proves the converse instead of the statement. This is the error of [[affirming the consequent|J7KFCFQRBT]] in a proof, and [[checking a proof|4M98ZZNK0E]] must test for it.
2. A student treats the inverse as equivalent to the implication. The inverse is equivalent to the converse, so a "proof by contrapositive" that proves $\neg p \to \neg q$ proves the wrong statement, as in [[proving the inverse instead of the contrapositive|D0531MQ745]].
3. A student decides that the converse is true because it holds in several examples. The implication "if $2^{n-1} \equiv 1 \pmod n$, then $n$ is prime" holds for every $n$ from $3$ to $340$, and fails at $n = 341$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The converse of a sentence "if A, then B" is the sentence "if B, then A".
The inverse is "if not A, then not B".
Take "if a number ends in $0$, then it is even".
This is true.
Its converse is "if a number is even, then it ends in $0$", and $4$ shows that it is false.
Its inverse is "if a number does not end in $0$, then it is not even", and $4$ shows again that it is false.
So a true sentence can have a false converse and a false inverse.

### L2 human

We find the converse and the inverse of "if a quadrilateral is a square, then its diagonals have equal length", and we decide which are true.

1. Name the parts. Let $p$ be "the quadrilateral is a square", and let $q$ be "its diagonals have equal length".
2. The implication $p \to q$ is true: the diagonals of a square with side $s$ both have length $s\sqrt{2}$.
3. The converse $q \to p$ reads "if the diagonals have equal length, then the quadrilateral is a square". A rectangle with sides $1$ and $2$ has two diagonals of length $\sqrt{5}$ and is not a square, so the converse is false.
4. The inverse $\neg p \to \neg q$ reads "if the quadrilateral is not a square, then its diagonals do not have equal length". The same rectangle is not a square and has diagonals of equal length, so the inverse is false.
5. The same rectangle refutes the converse and the inverse, as the equivalence of the two predicts.

The typical use is at the end of a theorem: we ask whether the converse holds, and either prove it or find one object that refutes it.
A trap: the inverse sounds like a "negated version" of the theorem, so many readers believe that it follows from the theorem.
It does not; only the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] does.

### L3 human

Read $p \to q$ as the order relation $p \le q$ in the [[two-element Boolean algebra|0WG43AC0EV]], or in the algebra of formulas up to equivalence.
Then the converse is the same relation read in the opposite order, and the implication together with its converse is the equality $p = q$, which is the biconditional.
For predicates, $\forall x\, (P(x) \to Q(x))$ says that the truth set of $P$ is a [[subset|0BJR9QR08E]] of the truth set of $Q$, and its converse says the reverse inclusion.
Negation reverses this order in a Boolean algebra, which is why the inverse is the converse in disguise.

Many theorems of algebra and analysis come with a partial converse rather than a full one.
Lagrange's theorem says that the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group, and its converse fails: the alternating group $A_4$ has order $12$ and no subgroup of order $6$.
Sylow's theorems and Cauchy's theorem recover the converse for prime powers and for primes.
In reverse mathematics, a theorem is shown to imply the axioms used to prove it, which is a converse at the level of axiom systems.
