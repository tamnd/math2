## Definition

Fix Cartesian coordinates $(x, y, z)$ rotating with the Earth, with origin at its centre of mass and $z$-axis along its rotation axis.
Let $\rho$ be the mass density of the Earth, let $G$ be the gravitational constant, and let $\omega$ be the angular velocity of the Earth.
The gravitational potential is the [[Newtonian potential|DCMFQ01QGS]]
$$V(\mathbf{x}) = G \int \frac{\rho(\mathbf{y})}{\norm{\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}}} \, d\mathbf{y},$$
and the centrifugal potential is $\Phi(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\omega^2 (x^2 + y^2)$.
The *geopotential* is $W = V + \Phi$.
The gravity vector is its [[gradient|0ABK9V9Y64]], $\mathbf{g} = \nabla W$.

## Remarks

Example: for a non-rotating ball of mass $M$ with spherically symmetric density, $\omega = 0$ and $W(\mathbf{x}) = GM/\norm{\mathbf{x}}$ outside the ball; then $\nabla W$ has magnitude $GM/\norm{\mathbf{x}}^2$, as in [[Newton's law of universal gravitation|29WRN5J730]].
Non-example: the potential $V$ alone is not the geopotential of the rotating Earth, since it omits the [[centrifugal force|BSV03EAVYT]].
Geodesy uses the sign convention $W > 0$ and $\mathbf{g} = \nabla W$; physics often uses the opposite sign.
Outside the Earth $V$ is harmonic, but $\Delta W = 2\omega^2$, because of the centrifugal part.
The geopotential number of a point $P$ is $W_0 - W(P)$, where $W_0$ is the value on the geoid.
