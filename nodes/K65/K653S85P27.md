## Proof

We extend a basis of $U$ to a basis of $V$, and we take the span of the new vectors as the complement.

1. By the theorem that [[every vector space has a basis|00MS9VMQ9Z]], applied to $U$, there is a [[basis|0KCQ9Y3QKA]] $L$ of $U$.
   We view $L$ as a set of vectors of $U$.
2. The set $L$ is [[linearly independent|0CRMGPAH83]] in $U$, hence also in $V$, because a linear relation among vectors of $U$ is the same equation in both spaces.
3. By the [[extension of an independent set to a basis|0M4SXE5WW3]], there is a basis $B$ of $V$ with $L \subseteq B$.
4. Let $M = B \setminus L$, and let $W = \operatorname{span}(M)$ be its [[linear span|0DTY8B9HMS]].
5. $W$ is a [[subspace|00YC1D652K]] of $V$, since a span is a subspace.
6. We show that the [[sum|0JMTY8MF54]] $U + W$ is $V$.
   Let $v \in V$.
   Since $B$ spans $V$, the vector $v$ is a finite linear combination of vectors of $B$.
7. We group the terms of this combination.
   The terms with vectors in $L$ add up to a vector $u \in U$, and the terms with vectors in $M$ add up to a vector $w \in W$.
   So $v = u + w$, and $v \in U + W$.
8. So $V \subseteq U + W$.
   Also $U + W \subseteq V$, since $U$ and $W$ are subspaces of $V$.
   Hence $U + W = V$.
9. We show that $U \cap W = \{0\}$.
   Let $x \in U \cap W$.
10. Since $L$ spans $U$, we can write $x = a_1 \ell_1 + \cdots + a_p \ell_p$ with distinct $\ell_1, \dots, \ell_p \in L$ and scalars $a_i \in F$.
11. Since $x \in W$, we can also write $x = c_1 m_1 + \cdots + c_q m_q$ with distinct $m_1, \dots, m_q \in M$ and scalars $c_k \in F$.
12. Subtracting the two expressions gives
$$a_1 \ell_1 + \cdots + a_p \ell_p - c_1 m_1 - \cdots - c_q m_q = 0.$$
13. The sets $L$ and $M$ are disjoint, so the vectors $\ell_1, \dots, \ell_p, m_1, \dots, m_q$ are distinct elements of $B$.
14. The basis $B$ is linearly independent, so all $a_i$ and all $c_k$ are $0$.
    Hence $x = 0$.
15. By steps 8 and 14, $U + W = V$ and $U \cap W = \{0\}$.
    So $W$ is a [[complement|0M83V17KHX]] of $U$, and $V = U \oplus W$.

## Idea

A basis of $U$ can be enlarged to a basis of the whole space.
The vectors that the enlargement adds point in the directions that $U$ is missing.
Their span fills those directions, and it meets $U$ only in $0$ because the whole enlarged list is independent.
In finite dimension the enlargement is a finite process, and in infinite dimension it rests on Zorn's lemma.

## Variants

The [[proof by Zorn's lemma|P9V296DEAX]] avoids bases and applies Zorn's lemma to the subspaces that meet $U$ only in $0$.

In $F^n$ the proof becomes a computation.
Put a basis of $U$ as the first columns of a matrix, follow it by the standard basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$, and row reduce.
The pivot columns among the $e_j$ give the added vectors, and their span is a complement.
For $U = \R(1, 1)$ in $\R^2$, the columns $(1, 1), e_1, e_2$ have pivots in the first two columns, so $W = \R e_1$.
