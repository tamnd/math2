## Definition

Fix an observer at a point $O$ of space.
The *celestial sphere* of $O$ is the [[sphere|P9CM4GQ2T0]] $S$ of unit radius centred at $O$, used as the set of directions seen from $O$.
A celestial object at the point $X \ne O$ is represented by the point
$$\frac{X - O}{\norm{X - O}} \in S,$$
the unit vector from $O$ towards $X$.
The *angular distance* between two objects is the angle between their unit vectors, that is, the great-circle distance between their points on $S$.

## Remarks

Example: take stars in the directions of the unit vectors $(0, 0, 1)$ and $(1, 0, 0)$.
The dot product of these vectors is $0$, so the stars are at angular distance $\pi/2$.
Non-example: the position of the Moon as a point of space, about $384\,400$ km away, is not a point of the celestial sphere.
Only its direction is.
The radius of the sphere has no meaning; texts often say it is "infinitely large".
In practice the centre is often moved to the centre of the Earth (geocentric) or of the Sun (heliocentric).
The difference between these choices is parallax.
The Earth's axis gives the celestial poles and the celestial equator.
The local vertical gives the zenith and the horizon.
The apparent yearly path of the Sun gives the ecliptic.
