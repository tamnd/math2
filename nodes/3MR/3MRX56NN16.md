## Problem

A *squared square* is a square that is cut into finitely many smaller squares, with disjoint interiors, whose union is the whole square.
It is *perfect* if the smaller squares have pairwise different side lengths.
Its *order* is the number of smaller squares.
Decide whether a perfect squared square exists, and if so find the smallest possible order.

## Remarks

The problem is solved.
Roland Sprague published the first perfect squared square in 1939; it has order $55$ and side $4205$.
In 1978 A. J. W. Duijvestijn found by a computer search a perfect squared square of order $21$ and side $112$.
The order $21$ is the smallest possible.

A squared square is *simple* if no set of more than one of its squares forms a rectangle; otherwise it is *compound*.
Sprague's square is compound, and Duijvestijn's square is simple.
R. L. Brooks, C. A. B. Smith, A. H. Stone and W. T. Tutte studied the problem by turning a tiling into an [[electrical network|T7KRE7J3B4]].
Horizontal edges of the tiling become nodes, squares become wires, and side lengths become currents.

The analogue in three dimensions has a negative answer: no cube can be cut into finitely many smaller cubes of pairwise different sizes.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Can you tile a square with smaller squares that all have different sizes?
With only a few pieces it seems hopeless, and for a long time people thought it might be impossible.
It is possible: the smallest solution uses $21$ squares inside a square of side $112$.
In space the answer changes: a cube cannot be cut into finitely many smaller cubes that all have different sizes.
