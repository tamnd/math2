## Statement

The clauses $\{ p, q \}$, $\{ \neg p, q \}$ and $\{ \neg q \}$ are unsatisfiable, and resolution derives $\{ q \}$ and then $\square$ from them.
The theorem says that this always works: resolution finds a contradiction exactly when there is one.

Let $S$ be a set of [[clauses|T3SXVH3ZZC]] of propositional logic, each clause a finite set of literals.
A resolution derivation from $S$ is a finite list of clauses, each in $S$ or a resolvent of two earlier clauses, where resolvents are as defined under [[resolution|ZDW6N1PGJ7]].
A resolution refutation of $S$ is a resolution derivation from $S$ that ends with the empty clause $\square$.

Then the following hold.

1. Refutation soundness: if $S$ has a resolution refutation, then $S$ is not [[satisfiable|QN7M1JCGT9]].
2. Refutation completeness: if $S$ is not satisfiable, then $S$ has a resolution refutation.

So $S$ is unsatisfiable if and only if the empty clause can be derived from $S$ by resolution.

## Remarks

### Why the word "refutation" matters

Resolution is not complete for derivations of arbitrary consequences.
For example, $\{ p \} \models \{ p, q \}$, since every assignment that makes $p$ true makes $p \lor q$ true.
But no resolution derivation from $\{ \{ p \} \}$ produces $\{ p, q \}$, since $\{ p \}$ has no partner to resolve with.

To test $S \models C$, one refutes $S$ together with the unit clauses of the negated literals of $C$.
This works by the [[satisfiability|QN7M1JCGT9]] criterion: $S \models C$ if and only if $S \cup \{ \neg C \}$ is unsatisfiable.
A derivation of a nonempty clause $C$ from $S$ shows $S \models C$, but $S \models C$ does not give a derivation of $C$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Each clause must be finite.
A resolvent is then finite, and the satisfiability of a clause depends on finitely many variables, which the proof uses.

For an infinite set $S$ the completeness part needs the [[compactness theorem|4X9126SEMM]].
Infinitely many variables may occur in $S$, so the induction on the number of variables in the proof does not apply to $S$ directly.
Compactness gives a finite unsatisfiable subset, and the induction applies to it.

### Consequences

A propositional formula $\varphi$ is a [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]] if and only if a CNF of $\neg \varphi$ has a resolution refutation.
With the [[Tseitin transformation|S367PDDP61]], a satisfiability-equivalent CNF of linear size can be used instead.

Resolution is a sound and complete proof system for unsatisfiable CNFs, which makes it the base system of [[propositional proof complexity|CBAF7J051K]].

The Davis-Putnam procedure, which removes one variable at a time by taking all resolvents on it, is a decision procedure for satisfiability.
Its correctness is the inductive step of the proof.

### Generalizations

Robinson's resolution with unification is refutation complete for clauses of [[first-order logic|086QKPF0A6]].
Restricted forms keep completeness on special classes.
Unit resolution and SLD resolution are refutation complete for sets of [[Horn clauses|QR92EZDTKF]].

### Watch out

Completeness says only that a refutation exists.
It says nothing about its length, and some unsatisfiable sets need refutations of exponential length.

The rule cuts one variable at a time.
From $\{ p, q \}$ and $\{ \neg p, \neg q \}$ one may not derive $\square$, since $p = 1$, $q = 0$ satisfies both clauses.

### Try it

Show that $\{ \{ p \}, \{ \neg p, q \} \}$ has no refutation.
The only resolvent is $\{ q \}$, and it clashes with no clause, so the closure is $\{ \{ p \}, \{ \neg p, q \}, \{ q \} \}$ and does not contain $\square$.
By completeness the set is satisfiable, and indeed $p = q = 1$ satisfies it.

## History

Martin Davis and Hilary Putnam used resolution on ground clauses in their procedure of 1960.
John Alan Robinson introduced resolution with unification for first-order clauses in 1965, and proved it sound and refutation complete.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Write each condition as a clause, a list of options of which at least one must hold.
Resolution combines two clauses that disagree on one letter: from "$p$ or $q$" and "not $p$ or $r$" it gives "$q$ or $r$".
The new clause holds whenever both old ones hold.
If the process ever produces the empty clause, which offers no option, the conditions cannot all hold.
The theorem says that this test never lies, and that it always succeeds when the conditions are contradictory.

### L2 human

We refute $S = \{ \{ p, q \}, \{ \neg p, q \}, \{ p, \neg q \}, \{ \neg p, \neg q \} \}$.

1. Resolve $\{ p, q \}$ and $\{ \neg p, q \}$ on $p$.
   The resolvent is $\{ q \}$.
2. Resolve $\{ p, \neg q \}$ and $\{ \neg p, \neg q \}$ on $p$.
   The resolvent is $\{ \neg q \}$.
3. Resolve $\{ q \}$ and $\{ \neg q \}$ on $q$.
   The resolvent is $\square$.

By soundness, $S$ is unsatisfiable, and a truth table with four rows confirms it: each row falsifies one clause.
The typical use is to prove that a formula is valid: put its negation in CNF and refute it.

### L3 human

The completeness proof is a semantic argument, and it says nothing about the length of refutations.
Haken proved in 1985 that the pigeonhole formulas need refutations of exponential length, so resolution is not a polynomially bounded proof system, as discussed under [[propositional proof complexity|CBAF7J051K]].
The DPLL procedure and its modern descendants with clause learning give a resolution refutation from each unsatisfiable run.
This ties the practice of SAT solving to the proof theory of resolution.
In first-order logic the same pattern holds with unification, and [[Herbrand's theorem|VZ21F10WRS]] reduces the first-order case to the propositional one.
