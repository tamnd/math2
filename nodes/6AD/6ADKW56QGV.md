## Summary

An accretion disk is a flat, rotating disk of gas that orbits a central mass and slowly spirals into it.
The central mass can be a young star, a white dwarf, a neutron star or a black hole.
Gas in the disk moves on nearly circular orbits.
It can fall inward only if it loses [[angular momentum|7147M98XNR]], which internal stresses carry outward.
The gravitational energy released by the inflow heats the disk, which makes accretion disks some of the brightest sources in the universe.
Their theory combines celestial mechanics, [[viscosity|NM5Q4845Z4]] or turbulence, and radiative transfer.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Gas falling toward a star almost always has some sideways motion, so it cannot drop straight in.
Instead it settles into a spinning disk, like water swirling around a drain.
Inner parts of the disk spin faster than outer parts, and friction between neighbouring rings slows the inner ones and speeds up the outer ones.
So inner gas loses spin and sinks inward while a little outer gas moves out.
The friction also heats the gas, and the hot inner disk glows brightly, often in X-rays near black holes.
