## Proof

We take a basis of $W$, show that it is already a basis of $V$, and conclude $V \subseteq W$.

1. Let $n = \dim V$. By the theorem on the [[dimension of a subspace|0QW084460E]], $W$ is finite-dimensional, and by hypothesis $\dim W = n$.
2. By the definition of [[dimension|05G2G0P224]], $W$ has a [[basis|0VTQTPQGAV]] $w_1, \dots, w_n$ of $n$ vectors.
3. The vectors $w_1, \dots, w_n$ are [[linearly independent|0EVHJFTG9Z]] in $W$. A linear relation among them in $V$ is the same equation as a relation in $W$, so they are also independent in $V$.
4. By the [[right-size criterion|096V7H13H9]], $n$ independent vectors in the $n$-dimensional space $V$ form a basis of $V$. In particular they [[span|0BJKVF90Z6]] $V$.
5. Each $w_i$ lies in $W$, and $W$ is a [[linear subspace|0EPTJA5QV4]], so every linear combination of $w_1, \dots, w_n$ lies in $W$. By step 4, this gives $V \subseteq W$.
6. Since $W \subseteq V$ by hypothesis, the two inclusions give $W = V$.

## Idea

A basis of $W$ is an independent list in $V$ of length $\dim V$.
An independent list of that length cannot miss any direction of $V$, so it spans $V$.
But its span stays inside $W$, because $W$ is closed under linear combinations, so $W$ must be all of $V$.

## Variants

A proof by contradiction runs the other way.
If $W \ne V$, pick $v \in V$ outside $W$ and add it to a basis of $W$.
The longer list is still independent, because $v$ is outside the span of the basis, so $V$ contains $n + 1$ independent vectors.
That contradicts the bound of $n$ from the Steinitz exchange lemma.
