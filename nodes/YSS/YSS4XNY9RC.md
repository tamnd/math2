## Statement

Over a field, row replacements alone produce every matrix of determinant $1$, and one extra scaling produces every invertible matrix.
Let $F$ be a [[field|0QA8CHH8MG]] and let $n \ge 1$.
For $n \ge 2$, write $T_{ij}(c)$ for the [[transvection matrices|0NMN00FNW1]], and write $D_n(\delta)$ for the [[elementary matrix|0M79HRJCRR]] $\operatorname{diag}(1, \dots, 1, \delta)$.

1. Each $A \in \mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is a product $A = T \, D_n(\det A)$, where $T$ is a finite product of transvection matrices. For $n = 1$, $T$ is the empty product $I_1$.
2. The [[special linear group|0V9PMN0RBH]] $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$ is the set of finite products of transvection matrices. So the transvections generate $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$.
3. The [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is generated by the transvection matrices and the matrices $D_n(\delta)$ with $\delta \ne 0$.

Here $\det$ is the [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]].

## Remarks

### The theorem at work

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, with $\det A = 4 - 3 = 1$.
Subtract row 2 from row 1, then row 1 from row 2, then column 1 from column 2:
$$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{T_{12}(-1)\,\cdot} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{T_{21}(-1)\,\cdot} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{\cdot\,T_{12}(-1)} I_2.$$
So $T_{21}(-1)\,T_{12}(-1)\,A\,T_{12}(-1) = I_2$, and inverting each factor gives $A = T_{12}(1)\,T_{21}(1)\,T_{12}(1)$.
Check:
$$T_{12}(1)\,T_{21}(1) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = A.$$

### Watch out

The exchange matrix $P_{12}$ has determinant $-1$, so it is not a product of transvections, and part 2 does not contradict the need for exchanges in ordinary elimination.
What transvections can produce is an exchange with a sign: $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = T_{12}(1)\,T_{21}(-1)\,T_{12}(1)$, of determinant $1$.

### Why the hypotheses are needed

The proof divides by a nonzero entry, so $F$ must be a field.
Over a commutative ring $R$, the transvections generate a subgroup $E_n(R)$ of $\mathrm{SL}_n(R)$, and it can be smaller.
For $R = F[x, y]$ and $n = 2$, Cohn showed that $E_2(R) \ne \mathrm{SL}_2(R)$; the [[reduction to diagonal form|CFB41AZX87]] gives his matrix.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the Euclidean algorithm replaces division, and $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$ is again generated by transvections.
For $n = 1$ there are no transvections, and part 2 says only that $\mathrm{SL}_1(F) = \{(1)\}$.

### What it gives you

The theorem refines the result that an [[invertible matrix is a product of elementary matrices|1ZK55FS2BF]]: row exchanges are not needed, and one scaling at the end is enough.
It also turns questions about all of $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$ into questions about transvections.
For example, each $A \in \mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ can be joined to $I_n$ by a path inside $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$, because $t \mapsto T_{ij}(tc)$ for $0 \le t \le 1$ joins $I_n$ to $T_{ij}(c)$, and such paths can be multiplied.

### Converse and generalizations

The easy inclusions hold for every ring: a product of transvections has determinant $1$, and a product of transvections and matrices $D_n(\delta)$ with $\delta \ne 0$ is invertible.
So parts 2 and 3 are equalities of groups.
The [[lemma on reduction to diagonal form|CFB41AZX87]] treats all square matrices, invertible or not.
For $n \ge 3$, the transvections satisfy the Steinberg relations described in the concept of a [[transvection|0DTCBCKEJP]], and presentations of $\mathrm{SL}_n$ belong to group theory and algebraic K-theory.

## History

Dickson studied the linear groups over finite fields in his book *Linear groups*, published in 1901.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The moves "add a multiple of one row to another" can turn each matrix of determinant $1$ into the identity matrix.
Undo the moves, and you get the matrix as a product of these simple matrices.
For a general invertible matrix, you need one more move at the end: scale the last row by the determinant.

### L2 human

To write a given matrix of determinant $1$ as a product of transvections, row reduce it using only additions, bring a $1$ into the corner first, then clear its row and column, and continue with the smaller block.
Record each step, and at the end invert the recorded factors in reverse order, as in the computation above.
For an invertible matrix with $\det A = \delta \ne 1$, the same procedure ends at $D_n(\delta)$ instead of $I_n$.

### L3 human

When $n \ge 3$ or $|F| \ge 3$, each transvection is a commutator in $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$.
With part 2, this shows that $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$ is the commutator subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ in these cases.
The orders and the simplicity of these groups over finite fields belong to group theory.
