## Summary

Mathematics is written in a dialect of its own, and a reader who knows the logic can still be stopped by the dialect.
Take the sentence "for $n$ sufficiently large, $2^n > n^3$, and the bound is sharp in the sense that the threshold $n = 10$ cannot be lowered".
It uses a symbol whose meaning is fixed by agreement, a phrase that hides two quantifiers, and a word of praise with an exact meaning.
This topic, a part of [[reasoning and proof|SGSVYFDSYK]], teaches you to read such sentences and to write them so that a reader cannot take them in two ways.

The pages start with the written signs: letters, layout, the conventions that different books choose differently, and the shortcuts called abuse of notation.
Then they turn to the words: the abbreviations of proofs, the jargon of talks, and the words "trivial" and "obvious", which claim that a step needs no argument.
Next come phrases with a precise hidden meaning: "canonical", "almost all", "generic", "sufficiently large", "stronger", "sharp" and "elementary".
The topic ends with words that judge objects and proofs, such as "pathological", "deep" and "brute force", and with the marks that close a proof.
Where books disagree, each page shows both readings, says how to tell which one an author uses, and says which one this site uses.

## Remarks

### Reading order

1. [[Mathematical notation|QKEX4PD029]]: letter conventions, subscripts, the ellipsis and the layout of formulas.
2. [[Conflicting conventions|CSFEP56A7D]]: symbols that books define in two ways, such as $\mathbb{N}$, $\subset$ and $f^{-1}$, and the choice of this site.
3. [[Abuse of notation|JV68YRB5YZ]]: the standard shortcuts, and the check that keeps them safe.
4. [[Equality, definition and equivalence signs|BCGR3NPPXC]]: how to tell $=$, $:=$, $\equiv$, $\approx$, $\cong$ and $\sim$ apart.
5. [[Glossary of mathematical symbols|2PTCAGCWZE]]: a reference table of symbols with their readings.
6. [[Mathematical abbreviations|4ZNYMYRRY3]]: how to expand iff, wlog, TFAE, s.t., i.e. and a.e.
7. [[Mathematical jargon|4M1FGQ0HNZ]]: what "nice", "vanish" and "folklore" mean between mathematicians.
8. [[Trivial, obvious and clearly|Y0HXCJRB9B]]: what these words claim, and the gaps they can hide.
9. [[Canonical and natural|F8WNADZFMM]]: why $V \to V^{**}$ needs no choice and $V \cong V^*$ does.
10. [[Almost all, generic and in general|N1XSAXCQY3]]: the several meanings of "almost all".
11. [[Sufficiently large, eventually and frequently|FEQ7WATMHF]]: "for $n$ large enough" as quantifiers.
12. [[Stronger and weaker statements|KCK4AQ469Q]]: how to compare two results by implication.
13. [[Sharp and best possible results|KWQV0G0VJ3]]: how an equality case shows that a bound cannot be improved.
14. [[Elementary proof|84SZZBACP3]]: why "elementary" means "without certain tools", not "easy".
15. [[Pathological objects|WMBZPYPN2D]]: objects that break a natural guess.
16. [[Elegant and deep|STHWD1K9AN]]: the informal words that judge proofs and theorems.
17. [[Q.E.D. and end of proof marks|ET9J6R9T5C]]: the signs that close a proof, a claim or a construction.

### Prerequisites

The pages assume the basic language of [[sets|0BSJ7RQ9BW]] and [[functions|0EPAT51DXQ]].
They also assume the topic [[the language of mathematical statements|WMY13MXY3W]], because many phrases here are short forms of quantifiers and conditionals.
The topic [[definitions in mathematics|BP0WAF23HB]] explains how a defined word gets its meaning, and the conventions here are choices among such definitions.
The proofs that use this language are in the topic [[proof methods|G9JFJGY4TQ]].
The history of the symbols themselves is in the topic [[history of mathematical notation and language|F1P7ZSZ6EM]].
