## Statement

Two Hermitian forms can be brought to diagonal form by one change of variables, as long as one of them is positive definite: that one becomes the identity, and the other becomes diagonal.
Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ complex [[Hermitian|0EKZCX4K3P]] matrix, let $B$ be an $n \times n$ complex [[positive definite|0QQCG3JWJ4]] matrix, and let $^*$ be the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]].

1. Congruence. There are an invertible matrix $P$ and a real diagonal matrix $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ with
$$P^* B P = I \quad \text{and} \quad P^* A P = D.$$
2. Generalized eigenvectors. The columns $p_1, \dots, p_n$ of $P$ satisfy $A p_i = \lambda_i B p_i$ and $p_i^* B p_j = \delta_{ij}$.
3. Generalized eigenvalues. The numbers $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ are the roots of $\det(A - \lambda B) = 0$, repeated by multiplicity, and they are the [[eigenvalues|0ZND7NQ970]] of $B^{-1} A$.
4. Signs. $A$ is positive semidefinite if and only if all $\lambda_i \ge 0$, and positive definite if and only if all $\lambda_i > 0$.
5. Real case. If $A$ and $B$ are real, then $P$ can be chosen real, and then $P^{\mathsf{T}} B P = I$ and $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P = D$.

The equation $A p = \lambda B p$ is the generalized eigenvalue problem of the [[matrix pencil|KQ7V0J5CGG]] $A - \lambda B$.
The [[proof by Cholesky reduction|MB04D0QDWS]] proves all five parts.

## Remarks

### A first pair of forms

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, where $B$ is positive definite with leading minors $2$ and $1$.
The generalized eigenvalues solve
$$\det(A - \lambda B) = (3 - 2\lambda)(1 - \lambda) - (1 - \lambda)^2 = (1 - \lambda)(2 - \lambda) = 0,$$
so $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$.
The null vectors $p_1 = (0, 1)$ of $A - B$ and $p_2 = (1, -1)$ of $A - 2B$ already satisfy $p_i^{\mathsf{T}} B p_i = 1$.
With $P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ the two forms become $x^{\mathsf{T}} B x = y_1^2 + y_2^2$ and $x^{\mathsf{T}} A x = y_1^2 + 2 y_2^2$, where $x = Py$.
[[The worked example|V33RQ1T6D3]] multiplies out both congruences.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The matrix $B$ must be positive definite, not only Hermitian.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ we get $\det(A - \lambda B) = -1 - \lambda^2$, which has no real root.
A matrix $P$ as in part 1 would give $\det(A - \lambda B) = \prod_i (\lambda_i - \lambda) / \abs{\det P}^2$ with real $\lambda_i$, so no such $P$ exists.
Positivity is also necessary for $P^* B P = I$ alone, because that equation gives $B = (P^{-1})^* P^{-1}$, which is positive definite by the [[Gram matrix theorem|0AM050KCAW]].

The matrix $A$ must be Hermitian, because $P^* A P = D$ with $D$ real diagonal forces $A = (P^*)^{-1} D P^{-1}$, and that matrix equals its own conjugate transpose.

### What it gives you

The case $B = I$ is the [[spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices|0DGZBQWZWN]], with $P$ unitary.
In general, the generalized eigenvalues are real even though $B^{-1} A$ need not be Hermitian; in the first pair, $B^{-1} A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Part 4 gives a test of definiteness for $A$ that uses $B$ as a reference.
The substitution $x = Py$ turns $x^* B x$ into $\sum_i \abs{y_i}^2$ and $x^* A x$ into $\sum_i \lambda_i \abs{y_i}^2$.
So on the ellipsoid $x^* B x = 1$, the largest and smallest values of $x^* A x$ are the largest and smallest $\lambda_i$, and they are taken at the corresponding vectors $p_i$.

In mechanics, small vibrations of a system lead to $K p = \omega^2 M p$, with a symmetric stiffness matrix $K$ and a positive definite mass matrix $M$.
The theorem gives real squared frequencies $\omega^2$ and independent normal modes $p_i$.

### Watch out

The theorem is about congruence $P^* X P$, not about similarity $P^{-1} X P$, and $P$ is in general not unitary.
In the first pair, $p_1 \cdot p_2 = -1$, so the columns of $P$ are orthogonal only for the inner product $x^* B y$.
The generalized eigenvalues are also not the eigenvalues of $A$: there, $A$ has the eigenvalues $2 \pm \sqrt 2$, while the pencil has $1$ and $2$.

### Generalizations

Two Hermitian matrices that commute can be diagonalized by one unitary matrix, which is [[simultaneous diagonalization|0FK17N4DKA]] by similarity.
If some real combination $\alpha A + \beta B$ of two Hermitian matrices is positive definite, the theorem applies to $A$ and $\alpha A + \beta B$, and then $B$ is diagonal in the same coordinates too.
Three Hermitian matrices need not be diagonal in one congruence, even when one of them is positive definite: with $B = I$, the other two would have to commute.

### Try it

First, diagonalize $A = \operatorname{diag}(2, 6)$ against $B = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$.
The pencil gives $\lambda = 2$ and $\lambda = 3$, and $P = \operatorname{diag}(1, \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})$ gives $P^{\mathsf{T}} B P = I$ and $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P = \operatorname{diag}(2, 3)$.

Second, decide whether $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is definite, using $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
Here $\det(A - \lambda B) = (1 - \lambda)(-2\lambda) - \lambda^2 = \lambda(\lambda - 2)$, so the generalized eigenvalues are $0$ and $2$, and $A$ is semidefinite but not definite.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two quadratic forms can be simplified at the same time if one of them is positive.
We choose new coordinates in which the positive form becomes $y_1^2 + \dots + y_n^2$, a plain sum of squares.
Among all such coordinates, we can pick ones in which the other form also becomes a sum of squares, $\lambda_1 y_1^2 + \dots + \lambda_n y_n^2$.
For example, $2x_1^2 + 2x_1 x_2 + x_2^2$ and $3x_1^2 + 2x_1 x_2 + x_2^2$ become $y_1^2 + y_2^2$ and $y_1^2 + 2 y_2^2$.

### L2 human

To compute $P$, solve $\det(A - \lambda B) = 0$, find a vector $p_i$ with $(A - \lambda_i B) p_i = 0$, and scale it so that $p_i^* B p_i = 1$.
For distinct $\lambda_i$ and $\lambda_j$ the vectors are automatically $B$-orthogonal.
Indeed $\lambda_i \, p_j^* B p_i = p_j^* A p_i = (A p_j)^* p_i = \lambda_j \, p_j^* B p_i$, because $A$ is Hermitian and $\lambda_j$ is real, so $p_j^* B p_i = 0$.
For a repeated $\lambda$, run the Gram-Schmidt process inside its null space with the inner product $x^* B y$.
The trap is to normalize with the standard length $p_i^* p_i = 1$, which gives $P^* B P \ne I$.

### L3 human

A positive definite $B$ defines the inner product $\ip{x}{y}_B = y^* B x$ on $\C^n$.
The matrix $B^{-1} A$ is self-adjoint for this inner product, and the theorem is the spectral theorem in the space $(\C^n, \ip{\cdot}{\cdot}_B)$.
In the language of forms, pairs (Hermitian form, positive definite form) up to a common change of basis are classified by the generalized eigenvalues.
In numerical linear algebra, the reduction by the [[Cholesky factor|0WKG8CYY6A]] of $B$ is a standard method for the symmetric definite generalized eigenvalue problem.
