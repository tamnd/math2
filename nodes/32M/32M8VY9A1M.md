## Summary

A left eigenvector is a row vector that a matrix only rescales when the matrix acts on it from the right.
It has the same eigenvalues as an ordinary eigenvector, but it is usually a different vector, and the two kinds together give coordinates in an eigenbasis.

Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over a field $F$.
A *left eigenvector* of $A$ is a nonzero row vector $y \in F^{1 \times n}$ with
$$yA = \lambda y$$
for some $\lambda \in F$.
Taking transposes, $yA = \lambda y$ holds exactly when $A^{\mathsf{T}} y^{\mathsf{T}} = \lambda y^{\mathsf{T}}$.
So the left eigenvectors of $A$ are the transposes of the ordinary [[eigenvectors|0N6960W0BV]] of $A^{\mathsf{T}}$, and the scalars $\lambda$ that occur are the [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $A$.

## Motivation

Some problems put the vector on the left of the matrix.
A Markov chain with transition matrix $P$, whose rows sum to $1$, moves a probability row vector $\pi$ to $\pi P$ in one step.
A distribution that does not change is a row vector with $\pi P = \pi$, so it is a left eigenvector for the eigenvalue $1$.
The same idea ranks web pages, where the ranking is the stationary distribution of a random walk on links.

Left eigenvectors also answer a question about right eigenvectors.
If $v_1, \dots, v_n$ is a basis of right eigenvectors, how do we find the coordinates of a vector $x$ in that basis?
Each left eigenvector is orthogonal to the right eigenvectors of the other eigenvalues, so multiplying $x$ by a left eigenvector picks out one coordinate.
This returns in the sub-area on diagonalization, where the rows of $S^{-1}$ in $A = SDS^{-1}$ are exactly such left eigenvectors.

## Notation

The ordinary eigenvectors are called *right eigenvectors* when both kinds appear.
Over $\mathbb{C}$, some books use the conjugate transpose and write $y^* A = \lambda y^*$ with $y$ a column vector; then the eigenvalue that appears is $\lambda$, but $y$ is an eigenvector of $A^*$ for $\bar\lambda$.
In the language of linear maps, a left eigenvector of $A$ is an eigenvector of the [[dual map|0AZ5ARP287]] of $x \mapsto Ax$, acting on linear forms.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
It is triangular, so its eigenvalues are $1$ and $2$.
For $\lambda = 1$, a row $y = (y_1, y_2)$ must satisfy $y(A - I) = 0$:
$$(y_1, y_2) \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = (0, \; y_1 + y_2).$$
So $y_2 = -y_1$, and $y = (1, -1)$ is a left eigenvector.
Check: $(1, -1)A = (1, \; 1 - 2) = (1, -1)$.
For $\lambda = 2$ the same method gives $(y_1, y_2)\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = (-y_1, y_1)$, so $y = (0, 1)$, and indeed $(0, 1)A = (0, 2)$.
The right eigenvectors are $(1, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $1$ and $(1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $2$, so left and right eigenvectors differ; [[left and right eigenvectors of a triangular matrix|X7MB7BJTSZ]] works this case in full.

### The same eigenvalues

A matrix and its transpose have the same [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]], because
$$\det(tI - A^{\mathsf{T}}) = \det\big((tI - A)^{\mathsf{T}}\big) = \det(tI - A).$$
So left and right eigenvalues agree, with the same algebraic multiplicities.
The geometric multiplicities agree too.
The left eigenspace for $\lambda$ has dimension $n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)^{\mathsf{T}}$, the right one has dimension $n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$, and a matrix and its transpose have the same rank.

### Biorthogonality

Let $yA = \mu y$ and $Av = \lambda v$ with $\lambda \ne \mu$.
Compute $yAv$ in two ways:
$$\mu\,(yv) = (yA)v = y(Av) = \lambda\,(yv).$$
So $(\mu - \lambda)\, yv = 0$, and since $\mu \ne \lambda$, the number $yv$ is $0$.
In the example, $(1, -1)$ for $1$ and $(1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $2$ give $1 - 1 = 0$.
If $A = SDS^{-1}$ with $D$ diagonal, then $S^{-1}A = DS^{-1}$, so the rows of $S^{-1}$ are left eigenvectors, the columns of $S$ are right eigenvectors, and $S^{-1}S = I$ says that they pair to $1$ or $0$.

### Examples and a non-example

In [[a Markov chain with two states|N73KSZZ4V9]], the stationary distribution $(5/6, 1/6)$ is a left eigenvector for $1$, while the right eigenvector for $1$ is $(1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
For a real symmetric matrix, $A^{\mathsf{T}} = A$, so the left eigenvectors are just the transposes of the right ones.
For a nonsymmetric matrix this fails: [[a right eigenvector that is not a left eigenvector|RQ2FE7RVVZ]] shows that $(1, 0)$ works on the right of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ as a column but not on the left as a row.
The zero row is never a left eigenvector, although $0 \cdot A = \lambda \cdot 0$ for every $\lambda$, because the definition asks for $y \ne 0$.

### Watch out

Do not transpose a right eigenvector and expect a left eigenvector.
For the matrix above, $(1, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$ is a right eigenvector for $1$, but $(1, 0)A = (1, 1)$, which is not a multiple of $(1, 0)$.
The second trap is the system itself.
The equation $y(A - \lambda I) = 0$ is not $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$; written with columns it is $(A - \lambda I)^{\mathsf{T}} y^{\mathsf{T}} = 0$, so row reduce the transpose.

### History

The words "left" and "right" only record on which side of the matrix the vector stands, and both kinds are eigenvectors in the usual sense of a matrix or of its transpose.

### Try it

First, find a left eigenvector of $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ for the eigenvalue $5$.
We need $(y_1, y_2)\begin{pmatrix} -3 & 0 \\ 3 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = (-3y_1 + 3y_2, 0) = 0$, so $y = (1, 1)$, and $(1, 1)A = (5, 5)$.

Second, the matrix $A$ has the right eigenvector $(1, 2)^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $3$, and $y = (a, 1)$ is a left eigenvector of $A$ for $4$; find $a$.
Biorthogonality gives $a + 2 = 0$, so $a = -2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An ordinary eigenvector is a column that the matrix only stretches, when the matrix stands to its left.
A left eigenvector is a row that the matrix only stretches, when the matrix stands to its right.
The stretch factors are the same in both cases, but the vectors are usually different.
For the table of a two-state weather model, the row of long-run shares, such as $5/6$ sunny days and $1/6$ rainy days, is a left eigenvector with factor $1$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: write $x = (3, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ in the basis of right eigenvectors of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
The right eigenvectors are $v_1 = (1, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $1$ and $v_2 = (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $2$, and the left ones are $y_1 = (1, -1)$ and $y_2 = (0, 1)$.
They satisfy $y_1 v_1 = 1$, $y_2 v_2 = 1$, and $y_1 v_2 = y_2 v_1 = 0$.
So if $x = c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2$, multiplying by $y_1$ and by $y_2$ gives
$$c_1 = y_1 x = 3 - 1 = 2, \qquad c_2 = y_2 x = 1.$$
Check: $2(1, 0)^{\mathsf{T}} + 1 \cdot (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} = (3, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
Then $A^k x = 2 \cdot 1^k v_1 + 2^k v_2$ for every $k$, with no matrix powers computed.
Watch out: the coordinates come from the left eigenvectors scaled so that $y_i v_i = 1$; without that scaling, divide by $y_i v_i$.

### L3 human

A left eigenvector is an eigenvector of the dual map on $(F^n)^*$, so the theory of left eigenvectors is the theory of eigenvectors of the transpose.
For a simple eigenvalue $\lambda$ with right eigenvector $v$ and left eigenvector $y$, the number $yv$ is not zero, and $vy/(yv)$ is the projection onto the eigenline along the sum of the other generalized eigenspaces.
The same pair gives the first-order change of a simple eigenvalue under a perturbation $A(s)$: $\lambda'(0) = y A'(0) v / (yv)$.
For a nonnegative matrix the [[Perron-Frobenius theorem|62NS7PV1M9]] gives a left eigenvector with nonnegative entries, and that is the stationary distribution of a Markov chain.
