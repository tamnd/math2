## Summary

Group theory is the study of groups.
A group is a set with an operation that is associative, has an identity element, and has an inverse for each element.
Groups describe symmetry: the symmetries of an object form a group under composition.
The subject started with the work of Lagrange, Ruffini, Abel and Galois on the roots of polynomial equations, and it grew into a central part of algebra.
This topic collects the group theory pages of math2.
They start with the axioms and the first examples, and continue with subgroups, quotients, group actions and the Sylow theorems.
Then they treat the structure of finite groups and the classification of finite simple groups.
The last parts are on abelian groups, free groups, infinite and profinite groups, and the related structures: semigroups, monoids, quasigroups and loops.
Geometric group theory, Lie groups, representation theory and group cohomology have their own topics.

## Remarks

### What the area is about

A [[group|G5S1M9]] is a set with a [[binary operation|0M418FE7BB]] that is [[associative|0K8F7PVDMX]], has an [[identity element|07WYQPB8E3]], and gives each element an [[inverse|0ZNZH0EXFX]].
The symmetries of an object form a group under composition, and this is the main source of groups in mathematics and in science.
Group theory asks three kinds of questions.
The first question is about structure: what are the [[subgroups|0X1JXT47GA]], the [[normal subgroups|0CB0DX5BVV]] and the [[quotient groups|0KYX2N69J0]] of a given group?
The second question is about classification: which groups exist with a given order or a given property, up to [[isomorphism|08MG7H8JW5]]?
The third question is about actions: how does a group act on a set, and what does the action show about the group and about the set?

The main results of the first courses are [[Lagrange's theorem|3C53TD]], the [[Sylow theorems|FESTJ0K9TX]], the [[first isomorphism theorem|0CXXJNG5B6]], the [[class equation|08BPSC6YTE]] and [[Cayley's theorem|0151VYVX27]].
The [[Jordan-Hölder theorem|0E088ZP533]] shows that each finite group is built from [[simple groups|0YMYMNQ0QZ]] in a way that fixes the list of the simple pieces.
The [[classification of finite simple groups|06J6P03MG7]] lists these pieces: the cyclic groups of prime order, the [[alternating groups|0Z5905Q73D]] $A_n$ for $n \geq 5$, the [[groups of Lie type|NPEBABQ49M]], and $26$ [[sporadic groups|PZ49Z4CBFZ]].
The [[structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups|0RY0B72XAS]] gives a complete list of the finitely generated [[abelian groups|082GHNPJ53]].

The methods of the area are few, and they come back in each sub-area.
We count cosets and orbits, we use [[homomorphisms|0JYTG5K9Y4]] to pass to smaller groups, we let a group [[act|0WCY42EV9V]] on a set, and we use induction on the order of a finite group.
The area also holds the structures with fewer axioms than a group: [[semigroups|0XMEM406JM]], [[monoids|0N6R902FG1]], [[magmas|9689SFPVE9]], [[quasigroups|ZJD31SPPFP]] and loops.

### History

In 1770 and 1771 Joseph-Louis Lagrange studied how the permutations of the roots of a polynomial change some expressions in the roots.
Évariste Galois wrote around 1830 on the group of permutations of the roots of an equation, and he used the word "group" for this set of permutations.
His work was published by Joseph Liouville in 1846.
Augustin-Louis Cauchy developed the theory of permutation groups in papers of 1815 and of the years 1844 to 1846.
In 1854 Arthur Cayley defined a group by its operation, without a reference to permutations.
Camille Jordan collected the theory of permutation groups in his book "Traité des substitutions et des équations algébriques" of 1870.
Ludwig Sylow proved his theorems on subgroups of prime power order in 1872, and Otto Hölder completed the Jordan-Hölder theorem in 1889.
In the twentieth century the theory of finite groups moved to the study of simple groups.
Walter Feit and John Thompson proved in 1963 that each group of odd order is [[solvable|0FXJ7YFMMX]].
The classification of finite simple groups was completed with the work of Michael Aschbacher and Stephen Smith on quasithin groups, published in 2004.
The page [[history of group theory|ZWK8Q2N6YQ]] gives more detail.

### How to read the area

A reader who starts the subject reads the first five sub-areas in order.
[[Groups and the group axioms|BAZT8JJ70S]] gives the axioms and the first examples.
[[Subgroups and cyclic groups|BAVE5RN4EE]] and [[cosets and Lagrange's theorem|1VHQWXC64D]] give the first structure and the first counting argument.
[[Homomorphisms, normal subgroups and the isomorphism theorems|E1506A5A55]] and [[automorphisms, centres and commutators|6Q029S79W0]] complete the basic tools.

Then three sub-areas give the standard examples and the main method.
[[Symmetric and alternating groups|S6R7EBMMFD]] and [[standard examples and small groups|JSBSHAQ5ZK]] give the groups that the later theory uses as test cases.
[[Group actions|RTACMHG9AX]] gives orbits, stabilizers and the class equation.

The theory of finite groups continues with [[products and extensions|QM6AQMGC1N]] and the [[Cauchy and Sylow theorems|STDBRYKADM]].
After these, a reader can choose a path.
The path to the structure of finite groups goes through [[series, composition factors and simple groups|XYPQ68VXKM]], [[solvable groups|MDQS10ETQV]], [[nilpotent groups|C7V8M3V34M]] and [[finite p-groups|XCQPYBWG3X]].
Then it continues with [[transfer, fusion and local analysis|YC8E0SHESW]] and [[arithmetic and combinatorial properties of finite groups|G87AMJC5CF]].
The path to the simple groups goes through [[permutation groups|CJGYKWFYYV]], [[finite groups of Lie type|FSRPCBDTPY]], [[the classification of finite simple groups|RYY7E63B8A]] and the [[sporadic simple groups|4ZDDK4AQD2]].

The path of abelian and infinite groups starts with [[finite and finitely generated abelian groups|Q52TX6JZ6X]], and continues with [[infinite abelian groups|Q0G2WW6R77]].
It then reads [[free groups and presentations|N23MMDRZ91]], [[infinite groups and varieties of groups|FCY96QWFRM]] and [[profinite groups|FPGHSQYSYX]].
The path of the weaker structures reads [[semigroups and monoids|AWR73JMXW1]], [[the structure theory of semigroups|J9ZMP5PQ6X]], [[finite semigroups and automata|S0AKF2WGV6]] and [[magmas, quasigroups and loops|BYW6P3R65W]].
The last sub-area, [[probabilistic and statistical group theory|ZCJTWA08EF]], needs the theory of finite groups and some probability.

### Related areas

Geometric group theory studies infinite groups through their actions on spaces, for example through the [[Cayley graph|0GYQK9NDJF]], [[hyperbolic groups|JWJ8YPR7EB]] and the [[word problem|PBGTTAVMMT]].
Lie theory studies groups with a compatible topology or geometry, such as [[topological groups|92700QAB5Y]], [[Lie groups|7JBRGCVJRB]] and [[linear algebraic groups|GA81VVQHEP]].
Representation theory studies homomorphisms from a group to groups of invertible matrices, through [[group representations|WYGRXCG2RN]] and [[characters|RMWMYBY8TC]].
Homological algebra gives [[group cohomology|YXJXA0Q52R]], and its second cohomology group classifies the extensions of a group by an abelian group with a given action.
[[Galois theory|WNQDKQYZ2R]] attaches a [[Galois group|0XTY86BZKY]] to a field extension, and it was the first source of group theory.
Ring theory uses the [[group ring|4NMTSX0XDS]], and the theory of computation uses the [[syntactic monoid|D47RMDH8MJ]] of a language.
Combinatorics uses [[Burnside's lemma|0Z47M1B2VS]] to count objects up to symmetry.
