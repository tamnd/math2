## Statement

Let a source of light move with constant velocity of speed $v < c$ relative to an inertial frame $S$ of a receiver.
Let the source emit light of frequency $f_s$, measured in the rest frame of the source.
Let $\theta$ be the angle, measured in $S$, between the velocity of the source and the direction in which the received light travels.
Let $\beta = v / c$ and let $\gamma$ be the [[Lorentz factor|2E9AJVZS6E]] of $v$.
Then the frequency measured by the receiver is
$$f_r = \frac{f_s}{\gamma (1 - \beta \cos\theta)}.$$
In particular, for a source moving straight towards the receiver ($\theta = 0$) and straight away from it ($\theta = \pi$),
$$f_r = f_s \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}} \quad \text{and} \quad f_r = f_s \sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}}.$$

## Remarks

Proof idea: the wave vector and angular frequency form a [[four-vector|S8RKKKMSTG]] $(\omega / c, \mathbf{k})$, because the phase $\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega t$ is the same in all frames.
Apply the [[Lorentz transformation|WEVGN4C7PQ]] to the source frame: $\omega_s = \gamma (\omega_r - \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{k}_r) = \gamma \omega_r (1 - \beta \cos\theta)$, using $\abs{\mathbf{k}_r} = \omega_r / c$.
Example: at $\theta = \pi / 2$ in the receiver frame one gets $f_r = f_s / \gamma < f_s$, the transverse Doppler effect, which has no classical counterpart and is a form of time dilation.
For $\beta = 0.6$ and $\theta = 0$, $f_r = f_s \sqrt{1.6 / 0.4} = 2 f_s$.
To first order in $\beta$ the formula agrees with the classical Doppler formula $f_r \approx f_s (1 + \beta \cos\theta)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A light source moving towards you looks bluer, and one moving away looks redder.
In relativity the shift has two causes: the changing distance, as for sound, and the slowing of the moving source's clock.
Because of the second cause, even a source moving sideways looks slightly redder.

### L2 human

The cleanest derivation treats $(\omega / c, \mathbf{k})$ as a null four-vector and transforms it like any other four-vector.
The same transformation gives the aberration of light, the change of the direction $\theta$ between frames.
The longitudinal formula is used to measure the speeds of distant galaxies and of fast particles, and its square-root form makes it symmetric between source and receiver.
