## Example

We compute the [[unitary group|VW5H9XFNJB]] of degree $1$,
$$U(1) = \{ U \in M_1(\C) : U^*U = I_1 \}.$$

1. A complex $1 \times 1$ matrix is $U = (z)$ with $z \in \C$. Its conjugate transpose is $U^* = (\overline{z})$, where $\overline{z}$ is the [[complex conjugate|0BJV24C4SY]].
2. So the condition $U^*U = I_1$ reads $\overline{z} z = 1$. For $z = a + bi$ this is $a^2 + b^2 = 1$, that is, $\abs{z} = 1$.
3. Matrix multiplication of $1 \times 1$ matrices is multiplication of their entries. So $U(1)$ is the set $\{ z \in \C : \abs{z} = 1 \}$ with complex multiplication, which is the [[circle group|04EAFVFTF7]].
4. Each such $z$ is $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i \sin\theta$ for some $\theta \in \R$, and the product rule is $e^{i\theta} e^{i\varphi} = e^{i(\theta + \varphi)}$.
5. The map $w \mapsto e^{i\theta} w$ turns the complex plane about $0$ by the angle $\theta$.
6. The determinant of $(z)$ is $z$, so the special unitary group $SU(1)$ is the trivial group $\{(1)\}$.

Check: $z = \frac{3}{5} + \frac{4}{5} i$ has $\abs{z}^2 = \frac{9}{25} + \frac{16}{25} = 1$, so $(z) \in U(1)$, while $z = 1 + i$ has $\abs{z}^2 = 2$ and fails.

## Remarks

The example shows that $U(1)$ is a whole circle of elements.
Compare the real case: the real $1 \times 1$ matrices $(x)$ with $x^2 = 1$ are only $(1)$ and $(-1)$, and they form [[the orthogonal group of degree one|D9VHRJRMAS]].
So $O(1)$ has two points and two pieces, while $U(1)$ is connected.

$U(1)$ is commutative, because complex multiplication is commutative.
It is isomorphic to the rotation group $SO(2)$ of the plane, by [[the index-two theorem|XAVVA317M0]], which sends $a + bi$ with $a^2 + b^2 = 1$ to $\begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$.
For example, $i$ goes to $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the quarter turn.

$U(1)$ returns for every $n$, because the determinant of each unitary matrix lies in $U(1)$, and every point of $U(1)$ occurs as such a determinant.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A complex number of size $1$ is a point on the unit circle, such as $1$, $i$, $-1$ or $\frac{3}{5} + \frac{4}{5} i$.
Multiplication by such a number turns the complex plane about $0$ and keeps every distance.
These turns are exactly the unitary $1 \times 1$ matrices.

### L2 human

Here is the typical use: the determinant of every unitary matrix lies in $U(1)$.
For example, $\det \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = -i$, and $\abs{-i} = 1$.
Watch out: over the real numbers the same condition $x^2 = 1$ gives only $\pm 1$, so do not expect the determinant of a unitary matrix to be $\pm 1$.
