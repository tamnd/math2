## Proof

For part 1 we find a real polynomial that kills one vector, split it into real factors of degree $1$ and $2$, and turn a factor with a kernel into an invariant subspace.
For part 2 we use the intermediate value theorem on the characteristic polynomial.
Let $n = \dim V \ge 1$.

*Part 1: a polynomial that kills one vector.*

1. Choose $v \in V$ with $v \ne 0$.
   The $n + 1$ vectors $v, Tv, \dots, T^n v$ are dependent, by the [[Steinitz exchange lemma|08ZDRN6H41]].
2. So there is a nonzero $p \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ of degree at most $n$ with $p(T)v = 0$, by the definition of the [[value of a polynomial at an endomorphism|0BNEXSKNRV]].
3. The polynomial $p$ is not constant, because a constant $a_0 \ne 0$ with $a_0 v = 0$ would give $v = 0$.

*Part 1: real factors of degree 1 or 2.*

4. We claim that every nonconstant $f \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ has a monic factor $q \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ of degree $1$ or $2$.
   Steps 5 to 9 prove the claim.
5. By the [[fundamental theorem of algebra|0JP0W07Y9A]], $f$ has a root $z \in \mathbb{C}$.
6. If $z \in \mathbb{R}$, then $q = x - z$ divides $f$ in $\mathbb{R}[x]$, by the [[factor theorem|0K3MWA7DMC]].
7. If $z \notin \mathbb{R}$, then $f(\bar z) = \overline{f(z)} = 0$, because [[complex conjugation|0FTH2E9TAS]] keeps sums and products and fixes the real coefficients of $f$.
   Put
$$q = (x - z)(x - \bar z) = x^2 - (z + \bar z)x + z \bar z,$$
   whose coefficients $2 \operatorname{Re} z$ and $\lvert z \rvert^2$ are real.
8. By the [[division algorithm|0MES3KS0ZC]] in $\mathbb{R}[x]$, $f = qs + r$ with $r = \alpha x + \beta$ for some $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$.
9. Evaluating at $z$ gives $0 = f(z) = q(z)s(z) + \alpha z + \beta = \alpha z + \beta$.
   If $\alpha \ne 0$, then $z = -\beta/\alpha$ would be real, so $\alpha = 0$, then $\beta = 0$, and $q$ divides $f$.
10. Applying the claim to $p$ and then to each quotient, by induction on the degree, we get
$$p = c\, q_1 q_2 \cdots q_k$$
    with $c \in \mathbb{R}$, $c \ne 0$, and each $q_j$ monic of degree $1$ or $2$.

*Part 1: a factor with a kernel.*

11. Evaluation at $T$ keeps products, by the remarks on the [[value of a polynomial at an endomorphism|0BNEXSKNRV]], so $p(T) = c\, q_1(T) q_2(T) \cdots q_k(T)$.
12. If every $q_j(T)$ were injective, then $p(T)$ would be injective, as a composite of injective maps times $c \ne 0$.
    This contradicts $p(T)v = 0$ with $v \ne 0$.
13. So there are an index $j$ and a vector $w \ne 0$ with $q_j(T)w = 0$.
14. Case $\deg q_j = 1$.
    Then $q_j = x - r$ and $Tw = rw$, so the line $U = \mathbb{R} w$ is [[invariant|0ACX5QT8TE]], because $T(aw) = (ar)w \in U$, and $\dim U = 1$.
15. Case $\deg q_j = 2$.
    Then $q_j = x^2 + bx + d$, and $q_j(T)w = 0$ reads $T^2 w = -bTw - dw$.
16. Let $U = \operatorname{span}\{w, Tw\}$.
    Then $T(w) = Tw \in U$ and $T(Tw) = -bTw - dw \in U$, so $T(U) \subseteq U$ by linearity.
17. Since $w \ne 0$, $\dim U \ge 1$, and since $U$ is spanned by two vectors, $\dim U \le 2$.
    If $\dim U = 1$ the subspace is an invariant line, and otherwise it is an invariant plane, which proves part 1.

*Part 2: odd dimension.*

18. Let $n$ be odd, and let $p_T(t) = t^n + a_{n-1}t^{n-1} + \dots + a_0$ be the [[characteristic polynomial|0BZ81AJNWF]] of $T$, which has real coefficients.
19. Put $M = 1 + \lvert a_0 \rvert + \dots + \lvert a_{n-1} \rvert$.
    For $\lvert t \rvert \ge M$ we have $\lvert t \rvert \ge 1$, so $\lvert t \rvert^k \le \lvert t \rvert^{n-1}$ for $k \le n - 1$, and
$$\lvert a_{n-1}t^{n-1} + \dots + a_0 \rvert \le (M - 1)\lvert t \rvert^{n-1} < M \lvert t \rvert^{n-1} \le \lvert t \rvert^n.$$
20. So for $\lvert t \rvert \ge M$, $p_T(t)$ has the sign of $t^n$, which is the sign of $t$ because $n$ is odd.
    Thus $p_T(-M) < 0 < p_T(M)$.
21. A polynomial function is continuous, so the [[intermediate value theorem|00KAFBR32E]] gives $\lambda \in (-M, M)$ with $p_T(\lambda) = 0$.
22. By the theorem that [[eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial|053QVHF6N7]], $\lambda$ is a real [[eigenvalue|0ZND7NQ970]] of $T$.
    An eigenvector for $\lambda$ spans an invariant line, which proves part 2.

## Idea

As in [[Axler's proof|6TPH8WGRJJ]] over $\mathbb{C}$, a nonzero polynomial $p$ kills a fixed vector.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ we cannot split $p$ into linear factors, but we can split it into factors of degree $1$ and $2$, since the complex roots come in conjugate pairs.
One factor kills a nonzero vector $w$.
A linear factor makes $w$ an eigenvector, and a quadratic factor $x^2 + bx + d$ says that $T^2 w$ is a combination of $w$ and $Tw$, so the plane spanned by $w$ and $Tw$ is invariant.
For odd dimension, the characteristic polynomial has odd degree, so it is negative far to the left, positive far to the right, and zero somewhere in between.

## Variants

Complexification gives part 1 in another way.
Extend $T$ to $\mathbb{C} \otimes V$ and take an eigenvector $u + iw$ for an eigenvalue $\lambda = a + ib$.
If $b = 0$, then $u$ or $w$ is a real eigenvector.
If $b \ne 0$, comparing real and imaginary parts gives $Tu = au - bw$ and $Tw = bu + aw$, so $\operatorname{span}\{u, w\}$ is invariant.

Part 2 also follows from part 1 by induction on the odd dimension.
If there is no invariant line, take an invariant plane $W$.
The induced map on $V/W$ acts on a space of odd dimension $n - 2$, so by induction it has a real eigenvalue $\lambda$.
Its characteristic polynomial divides $p_T$, by the theorem on the [[characteristic polynomial of a block triangular matrix|C98TZ6ZAXC]], so $\lambda$ is a root of $p_T$ and an eigenvalue of $T$.
