## Definition

Let $P$ be a set of propositional variables.
A literal is a variable $p \in P$ or its [[negation|029JBBF693]] $\neg p$.
A [[propositional formula|~7139c138]] is in conjunctive normal form (CNF) when it has the form
$$C_1 \land C_2 \land \cdots \land C_m \quad (m \geq 1),$$
where each $C_i$ is a [[disjunction|0F72FK8EKB]] $l_{i,1} \lor \cdots \lor l_{i,k_i}$ of $k_i \geq 1$ literals.
Each $C_i$ is a clause of the formula.
The case $m = 1$ is allowed, and so is the case $k_i = 1$; in these cases the [[conjunction|025YZN3VJW]] or the disjunction has one part only.

## Notation

Brackets inside a clause and between clauses are dropped, because $\land$ and $\lor$ are associative.
A clause is often written as the set of its literals, $\set{l_1, \ldots, l_k}$, and a CNF as the finite set of its clauses.
In this set notation the empty clause $\square$ is false under every assignment, and the empty set of clauses is true under every assignment.
In circuit design a CNF is called a product of sums.

## Remarks

The definition is purely syntactic: it is about the shape of the formula, not about its truth values.
Negation occurs only directly in front of variables, and the only other connectives are $\land$ and $\lor$, with no $\land$ inside a $\lor$.
So $(p \lor \neg q) \land (q \lor r) \land \neg p$ is in CNF, but $\neg(p \lor q)$ and $p \lor (q \land r)$ are not.
A CNF is full when each clause contains each variable of the formula exactly once, as $p$ or as $\neg p$, and no two clauses are equal.
Every formula is logically equivalent to a formula in CNF; the [[concept page|0B3D4C8YF2]] proves this in two ways.
Another proof: put $\neg\varphi$ into [[disjunctive normal form|0GVDFAY4KW]], negate it, and push the negation inward with [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A formula in conjunctive normal form is an "and" of "or"s.
Each "or" part is a clause, a short list of conditions of the form "$p$ is true" or "$p$ is false".
The whole formula is true when each clause has at least one condition that holds.
For example, $(p \lor q) \land \neg r$ says "$p$ or $q$ holds, and $r$ does not hold".

### L2 human

We decide whether three formulas are in CNF.

1. $(\neg p \lor q) \land (p \lor \neg q \lor r)$: two clauses, each a disjunction of literals, so yes.
2. $\neg\neg p \lor q$: the part $\neg\neg p$ is not a literal, so no. After the double negation law it becomes the CNF $p \lor q$.
3. $(p \land q) \lor r$: the conjunction lies inside the disjunction, so no. By distributivity it is equivalent to the CNF $(p \lor r) \land (q \lor r)$.

A trap: a single literal such as $\neg p$ is in CNF, with one clause of one literal.

### L3 human

The CNFs over $n$ variables correspond to finite sets of clauses, and a formula in CNF describes the set of assignments that avoid each "forbidden pattern" given by the negation of a clause.
In this view CNF is the language of constraint satisfaction with Boolean variables.
The restriction to clauses of at most $k$ literals gives $k$-CNF; satisfiability of 2-CNF is decidable in polynomial time, and satisfiability of 3-CNF is NP-complete.
