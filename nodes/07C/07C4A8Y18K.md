## Definition

We define the shape of formula that is an "and" of "or"s of literals.

Let $P$ be a set of propositional variables.
A literal is a variable $p \in P$ or its [[negation|029JBBF693]] $\neg p$.
A [[propositional formula|TT7WT5HPN9]] is in conjunctive normal form (CNF) when it has the form
$$C_1 \land C_2 \land \cdots \land C_m \qquad (m \geq 1),$$
where each $C_i$ is a [[disjunction|0F72FK8EKB]] $l_{i,1} \lor \cdots \lor l_{i,k_i}$ of $k_i \geq 1$ literals.
Each $C_i$ is a clause of the formula.
The cases $m = 1$ and $k_i = 1$ are allowed, and then the [[conjunction|025YZN3VJW]] or the disjunction has only one part.
A CNF over the variables $p_1, \ldots, p_n$ is full when each clause contains each $p_i$ exactly once, as $p_i$ or as $\neg p_i$, and no two clauses are equal.

## Notation

We drop the brackets inside a clause and between clauses, because $\land$ and $\lor$ are associative.
A clause is often written as the set of its literals, $\{ l_1, \ldots, l_k \}$, and a CNF as the finite set of its clauses.
In set notation the empty clause $\square$ is false under every assignment, and the empty set of clauses is true under every assignment.
In circuit design a CNF is called a product of sums.

## Remarks

The definition is about the written shape of a formula, not about its truth table.
It has three conditions, and each one rules out a way that a formula can hide its structure.
Negation may stand only directly in front of a variable, so that every negation is visible at the bottom.
The only other connectives are $\land$ and $\lor$, so arrows and biconditionals must be rewritten first.
No $\land$ may lie inside a $\lor$, so that the formula splits at the top into simple alternatives.

For example, $(p \lor \neg q) \land (q \lor r) \land \neg p$ is in CNF, with three clauses.
The formula $p \lor (q \land r)$ fails the third condition, because a conjunction lies inside a disjunction.
The formula $\neg(p \lor q)$ fails the first condition, because the negation stands in front of $p \lor q$.
Every formula is equivalent to one in CNF, and the [[concept page|0B3D4C8YF2]] gives two proofs: one with the laws of logic and one from the truth table.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A formula in conjunctive normal form is an "and" of "or"s.
Each "or" part is a clause, a short list of conditions of the form "$p$ is true" or "$p$ is false".
The whole formula is true when each clause has at least one condition that holds.
For example, $(p \lor q) \land \neg r$ says "$p$ or $q$ holds, and $r$ does not hold".

### L2 human

We decide whether three formulas are in CNF.
The formula $(\neg p \lor q) \land (p \lor \neg q \lor r)$ is in CNF, since it has two clauses and each is a disjunction of literals.
The formula $\neg\neg p \lor q$ is not, because $\neg\neg p$ is not a literal; the double negation law turns it into the CNF $p \lor q$.
The formula $(p \land q) \lor r$ is not, because the conjunction lies inside the disjunction; distribution gives the CNF $(p \lor r) \land (q \lor r)$.
Watch out for the smallest case: a single literal such as $\neg p$ is in CNF, with one clause of one literal.

### L3 human

A CNF over $n$ variables is a finite set of clauses, and each clause forbids one pattern of values, namely the row or subcube where all its literals are false.
So CNF is the language of Boolean constraint satisfaction.
Restricting clauses to at most $k$ literals gives $k$-CNF.
Satisfiability of 2-CNF is decidable in polynomial time, while satisfiability of 3-CNF is NP-complete.
