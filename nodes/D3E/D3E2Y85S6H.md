## Statement

Congruence sorts the square matrices into classes, and inside one class the rank, the symmetry type and the square class of the determinant are the same.

Let $F$ be a field and let $n \ge 1$.

1. [[Congruence|NZJDNC6EP6]] of $n \times n$ matrices over $F$ is an [[equivalence relation|0SGTMBEWRF]].

Now let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$, let $P$ be an invertible $n \times n$ matrix over $F$, and put $A' = P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$.
Then the following hold.

2. $\operatorname{rank} A' = \operatorname{rank} A$.
3. If $A$ is [[symmetric|0VR671PHE5]], then $A'$ is symmetric, and if $A$ is [[skew-symmetric|0PBY4E9GQF]], then $A'$ is skew-symmetric.
4. If $x^{\mathsf{T}} A x = 0$ for all $x \in F^n$, then $x^{\mathsf{T}} A' x = 0$ for all $x \in F^n$.
5. $\det A' = (\det P)^2 \det A$.

The [[proof|2MT27F7W18]] uses the rules for transposes and products.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The matrix $P$ must be invertible.
For $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $A = I_2$ we get $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P = P$, of rank $1$.
So rank is lost, and the relation "$A' = P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$ for some $P$" is not symmetric, because no $Q$ gives $Q^{\mathsf{T}} P Q = I_2$ when $P$ has rank $1$.

The same $P$ must appear on both sides.
With $A' = Q_1 A Q_2$ for two unrelated invertible matrices, we get the weaker relation of matrix equivalence, and symmetry is not kept.
For example, $Q_1 = I_2$ and $Q_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ turn the symmetric $I_2$ into the matrix $Q_2$, which is not symmetric.

### What it gives you

Because congruent matrices are the matrices of one [[bilinear form|0SQZMXB6GP]] in different bases, each invariant in parts 2 to 5 is a property of the form itself.
So the rank of a form, and whether it is symmetric, skew-symmetric or alternating, do not depend on the basis.

The typical use is to prove that two matrices are not congruent.
For example, $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ have the same rank and the same determinant.
But $A$ is symmetric and $C$ is not, so part 3 shows that they are not congruent over any field.
Over $\mathbb{R}$, part 5 gives one more invariant: the sign of the determinant, since $(\det P)^2 > 0$.

### The converse

The converse of part 2 is false: matrices of equal rank need not be congruent.
Over $\mathbb{R}$, $I_2$ and $-I_2$ both have rank $2$ and are both symmetric, and even their determinants agree.
Still $x^{\mathsf{T}} P^{\mathsf{T}} P x = \lVert Px \rVert^2 \ge 0$ for every $P$, while $x^{\mathsf{T}} (-I_2) x < 0$ for $x \neq 0$, so they are not congruent.
Equal invariants from parts 2 to 5 are necessary for congruence, but they are not enough.

### Generalizations

The same proof works for $*$-congruence $A' = P^* A P$ over $\mathbb{C}$.
It keeps the rank and the property of being [[Hermitian|0P17NGQQMQ]], since $(P^* A P)^* = P^* A^* P$, and it multiplies the determinant by the positive real number $\lvert \det P \rvert^2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Congruence behaves like equality in three ways.
Each matrix is congruent to itself.
If a first matrix is congruent to a second, then the second is congruent to the first.
If a first is congruent to a second and the second to a third, then the first is congruent to the third.
Congruence also keeps the number of independent rows and the mirror symmetry of a matrix across its diagonal.

### L2 human

Here is transitivity on an example over $\mathbb{Q}$.
Start with $A_0 = I_2$ and $P = \operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$, which give $A_1 = P^{\mathsf{T}} A_0 P = \operatorname{diag}(4, 1)$.
Then $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ gives
$$A_1 Q = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A_2 = Q^{\mathsf{T}} A_1 Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The product $PQ = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ does both steps at once:
$$(PQ)^{\mathsf{T}} I_2 (PQ) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = A_2.$$
Check: all three matrices are symmetric of rank $2$, and their determinants $1$, $4$, $4$ differ by squares.
Watch out: equal determinants do not prove congruence, and over $\mathbb{Q}$ different determinants do not disprove it, as $1$ and $4$ show.

### L3 human

Part 1 holds because congruence is the orbit relation of the action $P \cdot A = (P^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} A P^{-1}$ of the group $GL_n(F)$ on $M_n(F)$, and every orbit relation of a group action is an equivalence relation.
Parts 2 to 5 say that the rank, the symmetric and skew-symmetric types, the alternating property and the square class of the determinant are invariants of this action.
The [[classification of alternating forms|V8KKHKXXB3]] shows that for alternating matrices the rank is a complete invariant, while for symmetric matrices the complete invariants depend on the field, as [[Sylvester's law of inertia|0WFED3K3DQ]] shows over $\mathbb{R}$.
