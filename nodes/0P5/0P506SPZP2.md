## Counterexample

For numbers $ab = ba$ always, but for matrices the order of the factors can change the product.
Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
be real $2 \times 2$ matrices.
We show that $AB \ne BA$ for the [[matrix product|076JGP6R3T]], and that two rules of school algebra fail with it.

1. Row 1 of $A$ is $(0, 1)$ and row 2 is $(0, 0)$. Multiply each row of $A$ with each column of $B$:
$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 & 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 0 \\ 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 & 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
2. In the other order, multiply each row of $B$ with each column of $A$:
$$BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \\ 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
3. The entries in position $(1, 1)$ are $1$ and $0$, so $AB \ne BA$.
4. The square of a sum is not $A^2 + 2AB + B^2$. The sum is $A + B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and multiplying it by itself gives $(A + B)^2 = I$.
5. Each of $A$ and $B$ has square $0$, for example $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = 0$. So
$$A^2 + 2AB + B^2 = 2AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \ne I.$$
6. The correct expansion keeps both mixed terms: $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2 = AB + BA = I$.
7. Cancellation fails. Let $C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Multiplying by $A$ on the left moves row 2 of $C$ into row 1 and puts zeros in row 2, so $AC = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = AB$.
8. But $C \ne B$, so $AB = AC$ does not imply $B = C$.

## Remarks

### What the example shows

The pair $A, B$ is a pair of [[matrix units|A4HTT7JFD1]], $A = E_{12}$ and $B = E_{21}$.
For every $n \ge 2$ and every ring $R$ with $1 \ne 0$, the matrix units $E_{12}$ and $E_{21}$ of order $n$ give $E_{12} E_{21} = E_{11}$ and $E_{21} E_{12} = E_{22}$.
So the ring $M_n(R)$ is not commutative for $n \ge 2$.
For $n = 1$ the situation is different, since $1 \times 1$ matrices multiply like their entries and $M_1(R)$ is commutative when $R$ is.

### A pair with a picture

The quarter turn $R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and the reflection $S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ in the horizontal axis give
$$RS = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad SR = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The first is the reflection in the line $y = x$, and the second is the reflection in the line $y = -x$.
Reflect then turn, or turn then reflect: the two orders give different mirrors.

### When the order is not even possible

For matrices that are not square, one of the two products may fail to exist.
A $2 \times 3$ matrix times a $3 \times 4$ matrix is a $2 \times 4$ matrix, but the reverse product of a $3 \times 4$ and a $2 \times 3$ matrix is not defined.

### Pairs that do commute

Some pairs commute, and they are the [[commuting matrices|8G3X8MPG7E]].
The [[scalar matrices|067NKSBEWT]] $cI$ commute with every matrix, and two diagonal matrices always commute with each other.
For commuting pairs the school formulas come back, as the [[binomial theorem for commuting matrices|DS9TS6AFMW]] shows, and [[a second binomial example|60W7PZXBVB]] tests the formula on a pair with no zero entries.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For numbers, $2 \cdot 3$ and $3 \cdot 2$ are both $6$.
For matrices, the order of the factors matters, and here $AB$ and $BA$ are different matrices.
Think of a matrix as an action on arrows in the plane.
Putting on socks and then shoes is not the same as shoes and then socks, and two actions done in the two orders can end in different places too.

### L2 human

Read the matrices as maps of the plane.
The matrix $A$ sends $e_2$ to $e_1$ and sends $e_1$ to $0$, and $B$ sends $e_1$ to $e_2$ and sends $e_2$ to $0$.
The product $AB$ applies $B$ first: $e_1$ goes to $e_2$ and then back to $e_1$, while $e_2$ goes to $0$.
So $AB$ keeps $e_1$ and kills $e_2$.
The product $BA$ applies $A$ first, and the same reasoning shows that it keeps $e_2$ and kills $e_1$.
The typical use of this picture is to predict a product without arithmetic.
Watch out: a test with diagonal matrices will never show the failure, because diagonal matrices commute with each other.
