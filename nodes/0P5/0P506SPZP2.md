## Counterexample

Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
be real $2 \times 2$ matrices.
We show that [[matrix multiplication|076JGP6R3T]] is not commutative, and that two rules of school algebra fail with it.

1. The entry $(1, 1)$ of $AB$ is $0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 = 1$, and the other entries of $AB$ are $0$. So $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. The entry $(2, 2)$ of $BA$ is $1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 = 1$, and the other entries of $BA$ are $0$. So $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. The entries $(1, 1)$ differ, so $AB \neq BA$.
4. The binomial rule fails. $A + B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and $(A + B)^2 = I$.
5. But $A^2 = 0$ and $B^2 = 0$, so $A^2 + 2AB + B^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \neq I$. The true expansion is $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2 = AB + BA = I$.
6. Cancellation fails. Let $C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Then $AC$ is the second row of $C$ moved to the first row: $AC = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = AB$.
7. But $C \neq B$, so $AB = AC$ does not imply $B = C$.

## Remarks

- For each $n \ge 2$, the matrices $E_{12}$ and $E_{21}$ of order $n$ give the same failure, so $M_n(R)$ is not commutative for $n \ge 2$ and any nonzero ring $R$.
- For $n = 1$, $M_1(R) \cong R$ is commutative when $R$ is.
- A geometric pair: the rotation $R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and the reflection $S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ give $RS = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $SR = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
- If one of $AB$ and $BA$ is defined, the other need not be: a $2 \times 3$ times a $3 \times 4$ matrix is defined, but not the reverse product.
- Some pairs do commute; these are the [[commuting matrices|8G3X8MPG7E]], and the [[scalar matrices|067NKSBEWT]] commute with all matrices.
- The binomial example of step 5 has a second form in the [[binomial counterexample|60W7PZXBVB]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

For numbers, $2 \cdot 3 = 3 \cdot 2$.
For matrices, the order of the factors matters: here $AB$ and $BA$ are different matrices.
So rules like $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ need care with matrices.

### L2 human

The matrix $A$ sends $e_2$ to $e_1$ and $e_1$ to $0$, and $B$ sends $e_1$ to $e_2$ and $e_2$ to $0$.
So $AB$ keeps $e_1$ and kills $e_2$, while $BA$ keeps $e_2$ and kills $e_1$.
Doing two operations in the other order changes the result.
