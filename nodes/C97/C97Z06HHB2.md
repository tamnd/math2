## Statement

Every linear map of the plane that keeps lengths is a turn about the origin or a mirror image in a line through the origin.
Two mirror images combine to a turn by twice the angle between their lines.

For $\theta, \alpha \in \R$, let
$$R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S(\alpha) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\alpha & \sin 2\alpha \\ \sin 2\alpha & -\cos 2\alpha \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. Every real $2 \times 2$ [[orthogonal matrix|0QRBVFJ50X]] is $R(\theta)$ for some $\theta$ or $S(\alpha)$ for some $\alpha$. The matrices $R(\theta)$ have [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]] $1$, and the matrices $S(\alpha)$ have determinant $-1$.
2. The matrix $S(\alpha)$ is the [[reflection|025158VEYZ]] in the line through $0$ and $(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)$.
3. For all $\theta, \varphi, \alpha, \beta \in \R$,
$$R(\theta) R(\varphi) = R(\theta + \varphi), \qquad S(\alpha) S(\beta) = R\big(2(\alpha - \beta)\big),$$
$$R(\theta) S(\alpha) = S\left(\alpha + \tfrac{\theta}{2}\right), \qquad S(\alpha) R(\theta) = S\left(\alpha - \tfrac{\theta}{2}\right).$$

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The matrix must be orthogonal.
The shape of $R(\theta)$ alone is not enough: $2R(\theta)$ has the same pattern of entries, but its columns have length $2$, so it is neither a rotation nor a reflection.

The size must be $2$.
In space, an orthogonal matrix with determinant $-1$ need not be a reflection.
The point reflection $-I_3$ has determinant $(-1)^3 = -1$, but it fixes only the origin, while a reflection fixes a whole plane.

The entries must be real.
The [[complex orthogonal matrix|JRPHE0FG7D]] $\begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{2} & i \\ -i & \sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}$ satisfies $Q^{\mathsf{T}} Q = I_2$, but its entries are not of the form $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ for a real $\theta$, since $\sqrt{2} > 1$.

### What it gives you

The product rule for reflections is the most used part.
Two reflections in lines at the angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ give the rotation by $2(\alpha - \beta)$, twice the angle between the lines.
For example, the [[reflections in y = x and in the x-axis|SRN03D73AJ]] compose to the quarter turn.
Read backward, every rotation is a product of two reflections, $R(\theta) = S(\theta/2) S(0)$, which is the case $n = 2$ of the [[Cartan-Dieudonné theorem|0VMWGYY35Q]].

Conjugation by a reflection reverses a rotation:
$$S(\alpha) R(\theta) S(\alpha)^{-1} = S\left(\alpha - \tfrac{\theta}{2}\right) S(\alpha) = R(-\theta).$$
So $O(2)$ is not commutative, unlike $SO(2)$.
In the same way, $S(\alpha) S(\beta) = R(2(\alpha - \beta))$ and $S(\beta) S(\alpha) = R(2(\beta - \alpha))$ agree only when $2(\alpha - \beta)$ is a multiple of $\pi$, that is, when the two lines are equal or perpendicular.

### The converse

Every $R(\theta)$ and every $S(\alpha)$ is orthogonal, because its columns are unit vectors with dot product $0$.
For example, the columns of $S(\alpha)$ give $\cos 2\alpha \sin 2\alpha - \sin 2\alpha \cos 2\alpha = 0$.
So the two families together are exactly $O(2)$, and the determinant tells them apart.

### Generalizations

In dimension $n$, every orthogonal matrix is block diagonal in a suitable orthonormal basis, with blocks $R(\theta)$, $1$ and $-1$, by [[the normal form|QTY3VMFGY1]].
In dimension $3$, every rotation turns about an axis, by [[Euler's rotation theorem|2ZVG43GSKA]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

In the plane, every map that keeps lengths and the origin is a turn or a mirror image.
A turn is fixed by its angle, and a mirror image is fixed by its mirror line.
If you reflect in one mirror and then in a second mirror, the picture is no longer reversed: it is turned.
The angle of the turn is twice the angle between the two mirror lines.

### L2 human

Here is a typical task: recognize $Q = \frac{1}{5} \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 4 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$.
The columns have length $\frac{\sqrt{9 + 16}}{5} = 1$ and dot product $\frac{12 - 12}{25} = 0$, so $Q$ is orthogonal.
Its determinant is
$$\det Q = \tfrac{1}{25}\big(3 \cdot (-3) - 4 \cdot 4\big) = -1,$$
so $Q = S(\alpha)$ with $\cos 2\alpha = \frac{3}{5}$ and $\sin 2\alpha = \frac{4}{5}$.
The mirror line is at the angle $\alpha$, and the half-angle formulas give $\cos\alpha = \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}$ and $\sin\alpha = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}$, so the mirror is the line through $(2, 1)$.
Check: $Q(2, 1) = \frac{1}{5}(6 + 4, 8 - 3) = (2, 1)$.
So the test is: compute the determinant, and then read the angle from the first column.
Watch out: $S(\alpha)$ contains the angle $2\alpha$, not $\alpha$, so the mirror line sits at half the angle of the first column.

### L3 human

The theorem says that $O(2)$ is the semidirect product $SO(2) \rtimes \{ I_2, S(0) \}$, where $S(0) = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ acts on $SO(2) \cong U(1)$ by inversion.
Its finite subgroups are the cyclic groups of rotations and the dihedral groups, which are the symmetry groups of regular polygons.
As a topological space, $O(2)$ is two circles: the rotations form one, and the reflections form the other, one for each mirror line.
In the language of complex numbers, $R(\theta)$ is $z \mapsto e^{i\theta} z$ and $S(\alpha)$ is $z \mapsto e^{2i\alpha} \overline{z}$.
