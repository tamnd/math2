## Summary

A *minor* of a [[matrix|0N1MY1T0W9]] is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]] of a square submatrix.
We get such a submatrix when we choose $k$ rows and $k$ columns and keep the entries where they cross, in their original order.
For a square matrix, the *first minor* $M_{ij}$ deletes row $i$ and column $j$.
Minors matter because they give the cofactor expansion, the adjugate and the inverse, and because they measure the rank.

## Motivation

The expansion of a $3 \times 3$ determinant along a row uses three $2 \times 2$ determinants, one for each entry of the row.
These smaller determinants are the first minors.
More generally, a matrix of any shape has square pieces, and their determinants record which sets of rows and columns are independent.

## Notation

For row indices $I = \set{i_1 < \dots < i_k}$ and column indices $J = \set{j_1 < \dots < j_k}$, the minor is written $\det A_{I,J}$, $[A]_{I,J}$ or $A^{I}_{J}$.
For a square matrix, $M_{ij}$ is the minor that deletes row $i$ and column $j$.
A *principal minor* has $I = J$, and the *leading principal minor* of order $k$ has $I = J = \set{1, \dots, k}$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The first minors and the leading principal minors of a $3 \times 3$ matrix; see the [[worked example|B2YDT3YNCW]].
- The three $2 \times 2$ minors of a $2 \times 3$ matrix, and the rank they give; see the [[rectangular example|8827Y0CH3H]].
- The minors of order $1$ are the entries, and the only minor of order $n$ of an $n \times n$ matrix is $\det A$.

### Non-examples

- The determinant of a submatrix with its columns put in another order is not in general a minor; it can differ by the sign. See the [[non-example|Q3M8S1K799]].
- A non-square submatrix has no determinant, so it gives no minor.

### Basic properties

- An $m \times n$ matrix has $\binom{m}{k} \binom{n}{k}$ minors of order $k$.
- The first minors with signs are the [[cofactors|0XPKR18APA]], and they give the [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]].
- Over a field, the [[rank|0ZC9YTXZRY]] of $A$ is the largest $k$ with a nonzero minor of order $k$; see the [[rank by minors|HVHQ50TJB1]].
- The minors of $AB$ are sums of products of minors of $A$ and of $B$, by the [[Cauchy-Binet formula|08WE2W7DYS]].
- A real symmetric matrix is positive definite if and only if all its leading principal minors are positive, by [[Sylvester's criterion|0WKEF78PMV]].

### History

Laplace gave the general expansion of a determinant in terms of its complementary minors in 1772.
Vandermonde had already given a special case.

### Common errors

- Forgetting the sign: a first minor $M_{ij}$ is not the cofactor $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}$.
- Changing the order of the chosen rows or columns, which can change the sign.
- Deleting row $i$ and column $j$ for $M_{ij}$, but then using row $j$ and column $i$ in a formula; the [[adjugate|07W9H8QF38]] is the transpose of the cofactor matrix for this reason.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A minor is the determinant of a smaller square piece of a matrix.
For a $3 \times 3$ matrix, delete one row and one column, and take the $2 \times 2$ determinant of what is left.
There are nine such minors, one for each entry.
Minors are the building blocks of the expansion of a determinant.

### L2 human

To compute a minor, choose the rows and columns, keep them in their original order, and take the determinant.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$, the first minor $M_{11}$ deletes row $1$ and column $1$ and is $5 \cdot 10 - 6 \cdot 8 = 2$.
The typical uses are the cofactor expansion, the formula for the inverse, and the test for rank.
A trap: the number of minors grows fast, so the rank by minors is a tool for proofs, not for computation.

### L3 human

The $k \times k$ minors of $A$ are the matrix entries of the $k$-th exterior power $\Lambda^k A$ in the bases of wedge products of basis vectors.
So the Cauchy-Binet formula is the functoriality $\Lambda^k(AB) = \Lambda^k A \, \Lambda^k B$.
The ideal generated by the $k \times k$ minors is the [[determinantal ideal|SXD7X0WQH1]] $I_k(A)$, which depends only on the module presented by $A$; its study belongs to commutative algebra.
