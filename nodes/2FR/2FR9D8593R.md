## Example

The *transverse Mercator projection* is the [[Mercator projection|DRQJ0R4HJQ]] turned by a right angle, so that a chosen meridian $\lambda_0$ plays the role of the equator.
On the sphere of radius $R$, with scale factor $k_0 > 0$ on the central meridian, set $B = \cos\varphi \sin(\lambda - \lambda_0)$ and
$$x = \frac{k_0 R}{2} \ln\frac{1 + B}{1 - B}, \qquad y = k_0 R \arctan\left(\frac{\tan\varphi}{\cos(\lambda - \lambda_0)}\right),$$
for points with $\abs{\lambda - \lambda_0} < \pi/2$.
It is a [[map projection|Y2C8D9VC7D]] on this hemisphere, and it is [[conformal|0YYT36R4YD]].
Check on the central meridian: there $\lambda = \lambda_0$, so $B = 0$, $x = 0$ and $y = k_0 R\varphi$, so distances along that meridian are multiplied by $k_0$.
The scale factor at a general point is $k_0/\sqrt{1 - B^2}$, which grows away from the central meridian.
The Universal Transverse Mercator system applies the ellipsoidal version in zones $6^\circ$ wide in longitude, with $k_0 = 0.9996$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The ordinary Mercator map is accurate near the equator and distorted far from it.
Turn the cylinder on its side so that it touches the globe along a north-south line instead.
Then the map is accurate in a narrow strip around that line, whatever the latitude.
Cutting the world into many such strips gives accurate maps of every region, which is what national grids and UTM do.
