## Example

A *de Laval nozzle* is a duct whose cross-section area $A(x)$ first decreases to a minimum $A^*$ at the throat and then increases.
Model the flow as steady, quasi-one-dimensional and [[isentropic|SH8FHGTMSE]], with velocity $u(x)$, density $\rho(x)$, pressure $p(x)$, local speed of sound $c(x)$ and [[Mach number|GFHJ858AN1]] $M = u/c$.
Conservation of mass gives $\rho u A = \text{const}$, so $\frac{d\rho}{\rho} + \frac{du}{u} + \frac{dA}{A} = 0$.
The momentum equation gives $u\, du = -dp/\rho = -c^2\, d\rho/\rho$, so $\frac{d\rho}{\rho} = -M^2 \frac{du}{u}$.
Together these give the area–velocity relation
$$\frac{dA}{A} = (M^2 - 1)\frac{du}{u}.$$
So for $M < 1$ the flow speeds up where the duct narrows, and for $M > 1$ it speeds up where the duct widens.
A smooth acceleration from rest to supersonic speed is therefore possible only if $M = 1$ exactly at the throat, where $dA = 0$.
For an ideal gas with ratio of specific heats $\gamma$ the area at Mach number $M$ is then given by
$$\frac{A}{A^*} = \frac{1}{M}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma + 1}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2} M^2\right)\right]^{(\gamma + 1)/(2(\gamma - 1))}.$$
Check for air, $\gamma = 1.4$, at $M = 2$: the bracket is $\frac{2}{2.4} \cdot 1.8 = 1.5$, the exponent is $3$, and $A / A^* = 1.5^3 / 2 = 1.6875$.
So a nozzle whose exit area is about $1.69$ times its throat area produces a Mach $2$ jet.

## Remarks

In this regime the throat is sonic and the flow is [[choked|83Z3E8HMQ1]]: the mass flow is fixed by the reservoir state and $A^*$.
If the outside pressure is too high for fully supersonic expansion, a [[shock wave|JSDMGGTG7G]] stands in the diverging part and the flow behind it is subsonic.
Each value $A/A^* > 1$ corresponds to exactly two Mach numbers, one subsonic and one supersonic.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A rocket engine nozzle first narrows and then widens, like an hourglass.
The hot gas speeds up in the narrow part until it reaches the speed of sound at the waist.
After that, surprisingly, it keeps speeding up as the nozzle widens, because in a supersonic gas the density drops faster than the speed rises, so it needs more room.
This shape is what lets rockets and supersonic wind tunnels produce gas jets several times faster than sound.
