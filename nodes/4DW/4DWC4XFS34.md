## Example

Let $S_3$ be the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] on $\{1, 2, 3\}$, of order $6$, with permutations composed from right to left.
Its elements are $e$, $(1\,2)$, $(1\,3)$, $(2\,3)$, $(1\,2\,3)$ and $(1\,3\,2)$.
Let $H = \{e, (1\,2)\}$.

1. The products that we need are $(1\,3)(1\,2) = (1\,2\,3)$, $(2\,3)(1\,2) = (1\,3\,2)$, $(1\,2)(1\,3) = (1\,3\,2)$ and $(1\,2)(2\,3) = (1\,2\,3)$. In each product the right factor acts first.
2. The [[left cosets|03DEH43F4H]] of $H$ are $H$, $(1\,3)H = \{(1\,3), (1\,2\,3)\}$ and $(2\,3)H = \{(2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$, by step 1.
3. The [[right cosets|0NE1DK7H4T]] of $H$ are $H$, $H(1\,3) = \{(1\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$ and $H(2\,3) = \{(2\,3), (1\,2\,3)\}$, by step 1.
4. Each partition has $3$ blocks of $2$ elements. So the [[index|0DTXBQPA2S]] is $[S_3 : H] = 3$, and $6 = 3 \cdot 2$, as [[Lagrange's theorem|3C53TD]] states.
5. The two partitions are different: $(1\,3)H \neq H(1\,3)$. So $H$ is not [[normal|0CB0DX5BVV]].
6. The element orders are $1$ for $e$, $2$ for the three transpositions, and $3$ for the two $3$-cycles. Each divides $6$, as the [[theorem on element orders|08V7JHS3QZ]] states.
7. The [[subgroups|0X1JXT47GA]] are $\{e\}$, $\{e, (1\,2)\}$, $\{e, (1\,3)\}$, $\{e, (2\,3)\}$, the [[alternating group|0Z5905Q73D]] $A_3 = \{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$, and $S_3$. Their orders $1$, $2$, $3$, $6$ all divide $6$.

## Remarks

### Why the left cosets of $H$ do not form a group

For a normal subgroup, the cosets form the [[quotient group|0KYX2N69J0]] with $(aH)(bH) = abH$.
For this $H$, the rule is not well defined.

1. The cosets $(1\,3)H$ and $(1\,2\,3)H$ are equal, because $(1\,2\,3) \in (1\,3)H$.
2. We multiply each representative by $(2\,3)$ on the right.
3. We get $(1\,3)(2\,3) = (1\,3\,2)$, which lies in $(2\,3)H$.
4. We get $(1\,2\,3)(2\,3) = (1\,2)$, which lies in $H$.
5. So the same pair of cosets gives two different results, $(2\,3)H$ and $H$.

The set product of the two cosets fails too.
The product $(1\,3)H \cdot (2\,3)H = \{ab : a \in (1\,3)H, b \in (2\,3)H\}$ is $\{e, (1\,2), (2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$.
This set has $4$ elements, so it is not a coset of $H$.

### How the example changes

If we replace $H$ by $A_3$, which has index $2$, then the left and right cosets are both $A_3$ and $\{(1\,2), (1\,3), (2\,3)\}$.
Then $A_3$ is normal, and the two cosets form a group of order $2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

There are $6$ ways to arrange three books on a shelf.
The moves "do nothing" and "swap books $1$ and $2$" form a small group $H$ of $2$ moves.
If we combine each move with $H$, the $6$ moves split into $3$ pairs.
So $6 = 3 \cdot 2$.
A group of $6$ moves can only contain small groups of size $1$, $2$, $3$ or $6$.

### L2 human

We use the example to find all subgroups of $S_3$ by Lagrange's theorem.

1. A subgroup has order $1$, $2$, $3$ or $6$, because these are the divisors of $6$.
2. A subgroup of order $2$ is $\{e, t\}$ with $t$ of order $2$. There are three, one for each transposition.
3. A subgroup of order $3$ is cyclic, because [[groups of prime order are cyclic|05MM4EK4SA]]. It is generated by an element of order $3$, so it is $A_3$.
4. The subgroups of order $1$ and $6$ are $\{e\}$ and $S_3$.

The typical use is this method of finding all subgroups of a small group.
A trap is to multiply cosets by their representatives when the subgroup is not normal, as the remark above shows.
