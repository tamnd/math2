## Statement

Invariant subspaces are submodules, and two matrices are similar exactly when their polynomial modules are isomorphic.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces over $F$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ and $S \colon W \to W$ be linear.
Let $V_T$ and $W_S$ be the [[modules over the polynomial ring|KGVTFBZX59]] defined by $T$ and $S$.

1. $V_T$ is an $F[x]$-module, it is [[finitely generated|05QY1VEBYD]], and it is torsion: the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] $m_T$ kills every element.
2. A subset $U \subseteq V$ is a [[submodule|05WDSN597C]] of $V_T$ if and only if $U$ is a [[$T$-invariant subspace|0ACX5QT8TE]].
3. A map $\varphi \colon V \to W$ is a homomorphism of $F[x]$-modules $V_T \to W_S$ if and only if $\varphi$ is $F$-linear and $\varphi T = S \varphi$. It is an isomorphism if and only if, in addition, it is bijective.
4. For $A, B \in M_n(F)$, the matrices are [[similar|027KDW2K72]] if and only if $F^n_A \cong F^n_B$ as $F[x]$-modules.
5. Every $F[x]$-module $M$ with $\dim_F M = n < \infty$ is isomorphic to $F^n_A$ for some $A \in M_n(F)$.

So $A \mapsto F^n_A$ induces a bijection from the similarity classes of $M_n(F)$ to the isomorphism classes of $F[x]$-modules of $F$-dimension $n$.

## Remarks

### A worked case

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.
A line $\mathbb{Q} v$ is a submodule exactly when $A v$ is a multiple of $v$, that is, when $v$ is an eigenvector.
The only eigenvalue is $1$, and the kernel of $A - I$ is $\mathbb{Q} e_1$, so by part 2 the submodules of $\mathbb{Q}^2_A$ are $0$, $\mathbb{Q} e_1$ and $\mathbb{Q}^2$.
Since the module has only one nonzero proper submodule, it is not a direct sum of two lines, and so $A$ is not similar to a diagonal matrix, by part 4.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Finite dimension gives part 1.
On an infinite-dimensional space a map can satisfy no polynomial: multiplication by $x$ on $F[x]$ is an example, and then the module is not torsion.
In part 4 both matrices must have the same size $n$, since an isomorphism is in particular a bijection of $F$-spaces.
In part 3 the condition $\varphi T = S \varphi$ cannot be dropped, because a linear bijection that does not intertwine $T$ and $S$ is not a module map.

### What it gives you

A decomposition $V_T = U_1 \oplus U_2$ of modules is the same as a decomposition of $V$ into two $T$-invariant subspaces.
Every isomorphism invariant of $F^n_A$, such as its list of invariant factors, is a similarity invariant of $A$.
With these translations, the [[cyclic decomposition theorem|R3Z20J19BR]] follows from the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain.

### The converse direction of part 1

Every finitely generated torsion $F[x]$-module has finite $F$-dimension.
If it is generated by $m_1, \dots, m_k$ and $a_i \ne 0$ kills $m_i$, then it is a quotient of $F[x]/(a_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus F[x]/(a_k)$, which has dimension $\deg a_1 + \cdots + \deg a_k$.
So by part 5 the modules $V_T$ are exactly the finitely generated torsion $F[x]$-modules.

### Generalizations

For a commutative ring $R$ and an $R$-module $V$ with an endomorphism $T$, the same rule makes $V$ an $R[x]$-module.
For several commuting endomorphisms $T_1, \dots, T_k$, the space $V$ becomes a module over $F[x_1, \dots, x_k]$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear map turns its space into a module over polynomials.
The theorem gives a dictionary between the two languages.
A subspace that the map keeps inside itself is a submodule.
A change of basis that turns one matrix into another is an isomorphism of modules.
So "these two matrices are similar" means "these two modules are the same up to renaming".

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: show that $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ are similar by writing down a module isomorphism.
Let $\varphi$ swap the two coordinates, with matrix $P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Then
$$PB = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = AP,$$
so by part 3, $\varphi$ is an isomorphism $\mathbb{Q}^2_B \to \mathbb{Q}^2_A$.
By part 4, $A$ and $B$ are similar, with $B = P^{-1} A P$.
Watch out: not every linear bijection is a module isomorphism, and the identity of $\mathbb{Q}^2$ is not a module map from $\mathbb{Q}^2_B$ to $\mathbb{Q}^2_A$, since $B \ne A$.

### L3 human

Parts 3 to 5 say that $(V, T) \mapsto V_T$ is an equivalence of categories between finite-dimensional spaces with an endomorphism and $F[x]$-modules of finite $F$-dimension.
Under this equivalence, $\operatorname{End}_{F[x]}(V_T)$ is the [[centralizer|GXZGWZSJ2J]] of $T$.
The classification of finitely generated torsion modules over the principal ideal domain $F[x]$ gives the [[rational canonical form|042JWAR3Q0]].
Over an algebraically closed field, the primary decomposition gives the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].
