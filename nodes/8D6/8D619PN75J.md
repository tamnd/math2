## Summary

The stress intensity factor measures the strength of the stress singularity at the tip of a crack in a linear elastic body.
Near the tip the stresses grow like $K / \sqrt{2 \pi r}$, where $r$ is the distance to the tip and $K$ is the stress intensity factor.
There is one factor $K_I$, $K_{II}$, $K_{III}$ for each of the [[modes of fracture|H1J9Q4TVJ6]].
These numbers carry all the dependence on the size of the body, the crack length and the loads.
The angular shape of the field is universal.
Comparing $K_I$ with the [[fracture toughness|BQ0AMNFJRA]] of the material decides whether the crack grows.

## Explanations

### L1 human

At the tip of a crack the stress in a linear elastic body becomes infinite, so the peak stress is useless as a measure of danger.
What is finite is the rate at which the stress blows up, and that rate is the stress intensity factor.
For a crack of length $2a$ in a large plate pulled with stress $\sigma$, it is $K_I = \sigma \sqrt{\pi a}$.
So a longer crack or a larger load gives a larger $K_I$, and the crack grows once $K_I$ reaches a critical value of the material.
