## Summary

A rule of inference with $n$ premises is a set of tuples $(\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n, \psi)$ of [[formulas|HYJ10AF6RT]]; each tuple is an instance of the rule, the $\varphi_i$ are its premises and $\psi$ is its conclusion.
In a [[formal system|~113060c3]] the rules are the only steps of a derivation, and it must be decidable whether a tuple is an instance.
Most rules are schematic: they are given by a pattern with metavariables, such as modus ponens, "from $\varphi$ and $\varphi \to \psi$ infer $\psi$", whose instances arise by putting formulas for $\varphi$ and $\psi$.
Some rules carry side conditions, for example that a variable does not occur free in the hypotheses.
A rule is sound for a semantics when every interpretation that makes all premises true makes the conclusion true.
Rules matter because, together with the axioms, they decide which formulas are theorems, and because their soundness gives the soundness of the whole system.

## Motivation

The argument "if it rains, the street is wet; it rains; so the street is wet" is correct because of its form, not because of facts about rain.
Every argument of the same form, with any two statements in place of "it rains" and "the street is wet", is also correct.
A rule of inference records such a form once, with letters for the parts that may vary.
To make proofs checkable by a machine, a formal system lists its rules exactly, and every step of a derivation must be an instance of one of them.
Two questions then arise: which rules are safe (sound), and which rules can be added without changing what the system proves (derivable and admissible rules).

## Notation

A rule is often written as a fraction, with the premises above the line and the conclusion below it:
$$\frac{\varphi \qquad \varphi \to \psi}{\psi} \; (\mathrm{MP}) \qquad \frac{\varphi}{\forall x\, \varphi} \; (\mathrm{Gen})$$
In running text we write "from $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n$ infer $\psi$" or $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n / \psi$.
In [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]] the rules have names such as ${\to}\mathrm{E}$ (implication elimination) and ${\forall}\mathrm{I}$ (universal introduction).
In a [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] the premises and the conclusion of a rule are sequents $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta$ instead of formulas.
The letters $\varphi, \psi$ are metavariables of the [[metalanguage|D9G8M42GKC]], not formulas.

## Remarks

### Schematic rules and side conditions

A schematic rule has infinitely many instances.
Modus ponens has the instance $p, \; p \to (q \wedge r) \,/\, q \wedge r$, with $\varphi = p$ and $\psi = q \wedge r$.
Rules of predicate logic often need side conditions, and each condition blocks a specific wrong inference.

- Universal instantiation: from $\forall x\, \varphi$ infer $\varphi(t/x)$, where the term $t$ is substitutable for $x$ in $\varphi$. Without the condition, $\forall x\, \exists y\, (x \neq y)$, which is true in the natural numbers, gives $\exists y\, (y \neq y)$, which is false.
- [[Universal generalization|BKQ3YD6SVZ]]: from $\varphi$ infer $\forall x\, \varphi$. In a derivation from hypotheses $\Gamma$, the variable $x$ must not occur free in $\Gamma$; the [[non-example|~f5529315]] shows what fails otherwise.
- [[Existential generalization|33ZNXRFVJJ]]: from $\varphi(t/x)$ infer $\exists x\, \varphi$, where $t$ is substitutable for $x$.
- [[Existential instantiation|WKPW4BAVQT]]: from $\exists x\, \varphi(x)$ infer $\varphi(c)$, where $c$ is a new constant that occurs in no earlier line and not in the final conclusion. Without the freshness condition, $\exists x\, \mathrm{Even}(x)$ and $\exists x\, \mathrm{Odd}(x)$ would give $\mathrm{Even}(c)$ and $\mathrm{Odd}(c)$, hence the false $\exists x\, (\mathrm{Even}(x) \wedge \mathrm{Odd}(x))$.

Existential instantiation does not preserve truth step by step, since $\varphi(c)$ need not be true for the given value of $c$.
It is correct in a weaker sense: a final conclusion that does not mention $c$ follows from the premises.
In natural deduction the same idea is the rule ${\exists}\mathrm{E}$, which discharges an assumption $\varphi(y)$ for a fresh variable $y$.

### Derivable and admissible rules

Let $S$ be a formal system and $R$ a rule.

- $R$ is derivable in $S$ when, for every instance, the conclusion has a derivation in $S$ from the premises used as hypotheses: $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n \vdash_S \psi$.
- $R$ is admissible in $S$ when, for every instance, the conclusion is a theorem of $S$ whenever all premises are theorems of $S$.

Admissibility has an equivalent form: $R$ is admissible exactly when adding $R$ to $S$ does not change the set of theorems.
If $R$ is admissible, an induction on derivations in $S + R$ shows that each line is a theorem of $S$.
Conversely, if $S + R$ has the same theorems as $S$, then one use of $R$ on theorems of $S$ gives a theorem of $S$.

Every derivable rule is admissible.
If $\vdash_S \varphi_i$ for each $i$ and $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n \vdash_S \psi$, we replace each hypothesis line $\varphi_i$ by a derivation of $\varphi_i$ and obtain a derivation of $\psi$.

The converse is false.
[[Uniform substitution is admissible but not derivable|~759059cd]] in a Hilbert system for propositional logic: it maps theorems to theorems, but $q$ cannot be derived from the hypothesis $p$.
In classical propositional logic every admissible rule that is given by a single schema is derivable; this property is called structural completeness.
Intuitionistic propositional logic is not structurally complete: the Kreisel-Putnam rule $\neg p \to (q \vee r) \,/\, (\neg p \to q) \vee (\neg p \to r)$ is admissible in it but not derivable.

### Soundness of a rule

There are two notions of soundness, and they differ for predicate logic.

- A rule preserves truth when, in every structure and under every assignment of the variables, the conclusion is true whenever all premises are true. Modus ponens preserves truth.
- A rule preserves validity when the conclusion is true in every structure under every assignment whenever all premises are. Generalization preserves validity but not truth: in the natural numbers, $x = 0$ is true when $x$ is assigned $0$, and $\forall x\, (x = 0)$ is false.

If all axioms are valid and all rules preserve validity, then every theorem is valid, by induction on derivations; this is the [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]] of the system.
For derivations from hypotheses, $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, soundness in the form $\Gamma \models \varphi$ needs rules that preserve truth, or side conditions such as the one on generalization.

### Examples

- [[Modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] preserves truth: if $\varphi \to \psi$ is true and $\varphi$ is true, the truth table of $\to$ makes $\psi$ true.
- [[Hypothetical syllogism is a derived rule of a Hilbert system|~a7ed0609]]: its conclusion has a derivation of seven lines from its premises.
- [[Uniform substitution is admissible but not derivable|~759059cd]] in a Hilbert system with axiom schemas.
- Generalization is a primitive rule of Hilbert systems for first-order logic. It preserves validity, and the [[deduction theorem|9X08XK5RV2]] for these systems needs a condition on it.
- The rules R1 to R4 of the [[MIU system|~9a65847b]] are rules of inference of a formal system without any semantics. Soundness is not defined for them.
- The cut rule of the sequent calculus is admissible in the cut-free calculus, by the [[cut-elimination theorem|AYRZ98Y0NW]].

### Non-examples

- [[Generalization on a variable free in a hypothesis|~f5529315]] leads from the true hypothesis $x = 0$, with $x$ assigned $0$, to the false $\forall x\, (x = 0)$. It fails soundness.
- [[Affirming the consequent|J7KFCFQRBT]], "from $\varphi \to \psi$ and $\psi$ infer $\varphi$", fails soundness. With $\varphi = p$ and $\psi = q$, the valuation $p$ false, $q$ true makes both premises true and the conclusion false.
- Universal instantiation without the substitutability condition fails soundness, by the instance with $\forall x\, \exists y\, (x \neq y)$ above.

### Basic properties

- Every derivable rule is admissible, and the converse fails (proof above).
- In the Hilbert system for propositional logic, a rule $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n / \psi$ is derivable exactly when $\vdash \varphi_1 \to (\varphi_2 \to \cdots \to (\varphi_n \to \psi) \cdots)$. One direction applies the [[deduction theorem|9X08XK5RV2]] $n$ times, and the other applies modus ponens $n$ times.
- A derivable rule may be used under hypotheses, as an abbreviation of its derivation. An admissible rule that is not derivable may be used only on theorems.
- If every rule of a system preserves validity and every axiom is valid, the system is sound.

### History

The Stoic logician Chrysippus listed five basic forms of argument, the indemonstrables, which include modus ponens and modus tollens.
Gottlob Frege's *Begriffsschrift* of 1879 used explicit rules of inference in a formal system.
Gerhard Gentzen introduced natural deduction and the sequent calculus in his paper of 1934 and 1935, where each connective has its own introduction and elimination rules.

### Common errors

- Using an admissible rule under hypotheses. Substitution turns the hypothesis $p$ into $q$, which does not follow from $p$.
- Forgetting a side condition. Generalization on a variable that is free in a hypothesis, or instantiation by a term that is not substitutable, gives false conclusions.
- Confusing a rule with an implication. Modus ponens is a rule of the system, while $\varphi \to \psi$ is a formula; the deduction theorem links them, but it is a metatheorem.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A rule of inference is a pattern for drawing a conclusion.
The pattern "if A then B; A; so B" works for any statements A and B.
For example, from "if 12 is divisible by 4, then 12 is even" and "12 is divisible by 4" we may conclude "12 is even".
A rule is sound when it never leads from true statements to a false one.
The pattern "if A then B; B; so A" is not sound: "if it is a square, it has four sides" and "it has four sides" do not show that it is a square.

### L2 human

We check that disjunctive syllogism, "from $\varphi \vee \psi$ and $\neg \varphi$ infer $\psi$", preserves truth.

1. Take a valuation $v$ that makes both premises true.
2. Since $\neg \varphi$ is true, $\varphi$ is false under $v$.
3. Since $\varphi \vee \psi$ is true and $\varphi$ is false, the truth table of $\vee$ makes $\psi$ true under $v$.
4. So every valuation that makes the premises true makes the conclusion true, and the rule is sound.

The typical use of such a check is to justify a new rule before we use it in proofs.
For a formal system we also ask whether the rule is derivable, so that it adds no new theorems and may be used under hypotheses.
A trap is to check only one instance: soundness must hold for every valuation and for every choice of $\varphi$ and $\psi$, and the argument above works for all of them.

### L3 human

In general a rule acts on judgments, not only on formulas: on sequents in sequent calculi, on derivations with open assumptions in natural deduction, and on typing judgments in type theory.
The structural rules (weakening, contraction, exchange, cut) do not mention any connective, and dropping some of them gives the [[substructural logics|98QF5J4W16]].
The relation "$\psi$ is derivable from $\Gamma$" generated by a set of rules is a [[consequence relation|~dc29e7c4]], and derivable rules are exactly the instances of this relation.

Admissible rules are a subtle object in non-classical logics.
In intuitionistic logic and in many modal logics the admissible rules strictly extend the derivable rules, and their description is a field of research in [[intuitionistic and intermediate logics|38EQ20GR7M]].
In proof-theoretic semantics the meaning of a connective is given by its rules, and the condition of [[harmony|XJSQVK0G71]] between introduction and elimination rules replaces truth tables.
