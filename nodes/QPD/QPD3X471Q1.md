## Summary

A rule of inference is a pattern that licenses one step of a proof, whatever the statements in it say.
From "if $12$ is divisible by $4$, then $12$ is even" and "$12$ is divisible by $4$" we may conclude "$12$ is even".
The step is correct because of its form, "from $\varphi$ and $\varphi \to \psi$ infer $\psi$", and every step of that form is correct.

Precisely, a rule of inference with $n$ premises is a set of tuples $(\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n, \psi)$ of [[formulas|HYJ10AF6RT]].
Each tuple is an instance of the rule, the $\varphi_i$ are its premises, and $\psi$ is its conclusion.
In a [[formal system|439M8B4DY2]] it must be decidable whether a tuple is an instance.
Most rules are schematic: they are given by a pattern with metavariables, and the instances come from putting formulas in place of the metavariables.
Some rules carry side conditions, such as "the variable $x$ does not occur free in the hypotheses".
A rule is sound for a semantics when every interpretation that makes all premises true also makes the conclusion true.

## Motivation

To make proofs checkable by a machine, a formal system lists its rules exactly, and every step of a derivation must be an instance of one of them.
Together with the axioms, the rules decide which formulas are theorems.
Two questions then arise.
Which rules are safe, in the sense that they never lead from truths to a falsehood?
And which rules can we add as shortcuts without changing what the system proves?
The first question leads to [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]], and the second to the distinction between derivable and admissible rules.
Both return later: soundness is half of [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]], and the admissibility of the cut rule is the cut-elimination theorem of proof theory.

## Notation

A rule is often written as a fraction, with the premises above the line and the conclusion below it:
$$\frac{\varphi \qquad \varphi \to \psi}{\psi} \; (\mathrm{MP}) \qquad \frac{\varphi}{\forall x\, \varphi} \; (\mathrm{Gen})$$
In running text we write "from $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n$ infer $\psi$" or $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n / \psi$.
In [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]] rules have names such as ${\to}\mathrm{E}$ (implication elimination) and ${\forall}\mathrm{I}$ (universal introduction).
In a [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] the premises and the conclusion are sequents $\Gamma \Rightarrow \Delta$ instead of formulas.
The letters $\varphi, \psi$ are metavariables of the [[metalanguage|D9G8M42GKC]], not formulas.

## Remarks

### Schemas and side conditions

A schematic rule has infinitely many instances.
Modus ponens has the instance $p, \; p \to (q \wedge r) \,/\, q \wedge r$, with $\varphi = p$ and $\psi = q \wedge r$.
Rules of predicate logic often need side conditions, and each condition blocks one specific wrong inference.

Universal instantiation, "from $\forall x\, \varphi$ infer $\varphi(t/x)$", needs the term $t$ to be substitutable for $x$.
Without the condition, the sentence $\forall x\, \exists y\, (x \neq y)$, true in $\mathbb{N}$, gives $\exists y\, (y \neq y)$ with $t = y$, which is false.
[[Universal generalization|BKQ3YD6SVZ]], "from $\varphi$ infer $\forall x\, \varphi$", needs $x$ not free in the hypotheses, as [[generalization on a variable free in a hypothesis|VT4RFH9GNK]] shows.
[[Existential instantiation|WKPW4BAVQT]], "from $\exists x\, \varphi(x)$ infer $\varphi(c)$", needs a new constant $c$.
Without that, $\exists x\, \mathrm{Even}(x)$ and $\exists x\, \mathrm{Odd}(x)$ would give $\mathrm{Even}(c)$ and $\mathrm{Odd}(c)$ for the same $c$, and so the false $\exists x\, (\mathrm{Even}(x) \wedge \mathrm{Odd}(x))$.
Existential instantiation is correct only in a weaker sense: a final conclusion that does not mention $c$ follows from the premises.
Natural deduction builds the same idea into the rule ${\exists}\mathrm{E}$.

### Derivable and admissible rules

Let $S$ be a formal system and $R$ a rule.
There are two ways in which $R$ can be harmless.

- $R$ is derivable in $S$ when, for every instance, the conclusion has a derivation from the premises used as hypotheses: $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n \vdash_S \psi$.
- $R$ is admissible in $S$ when, for every instance, the conclusion is a theorem of $S$ whenever all the premises are theorems of $S$.

Equivalently, $R$ is admissible exactly when adding $R$ to $S$ does not change the set of theorems; an induction on derivations in $S + R$ gives one direction, and one use of $R$ gives the other.
Every derivable rule is admissible: replace each hypothesis line $\varphi_i$ by a derivation of $\varphi_i$.
For example, hypothetical syllogism is [[derivable in a Hilbert system|RKGBHXM44V]], by a derivation of seven lines.

The converse fails.
[[Uniform substitution is admissible but not derivable|DE0GD8BM4M]] in a Hilbert system: it maps theorems to theorems, but $q$ does not follow from the hypothesis $p$.
A derivable rule may be used under hypotheses as an abbreviation of its derivation, while a rule that is only admissible may be applied only to theorems.

### Structural completeness

Classical propositional logic is structurally complete: every admissible schematic rule, that is, every rule closed under substitution, is derivable.
Uniform substitution is not a counterexample, because it is not a rule of that kind.
Intuitionistic propositional logic is not structurally complete.
The Kreisel-Putnam rule
$$\neg p \to (q \vee r) \,/\, (\neg p \to q) \vee (\neg p \to r)$$
is admissible there, but its implication is not an intuitionistic theorem, so it is not derivable.
In the classical Hilbert system there is also a simple test for derivability: $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n / \psi$ is derivable exactly when $\vdash \varphi_1 \to (\varphi_2 \to \cdots \to (\varphi_n \to \psi) \cdots)$, by the [[deduction theorem|9X08XK5RV2]] in one direction and modus ponens in the other.

### Two kinds of soundness

For predicate logic, "sound rule" has two readings.
A rule preserves truth when, in every structure and under every assignment of the variables, the conclusion is true whenever all premises are true.
A rule preserves validity when the conclusion is true under every assignment in every structure whenever all premises are.
Modus ponens preserves truth, since the truth table of $\to$ makes $\psi$ true when $\varphi$ and $\varphi \to \psi$ are true.
Generalization preserves validity but not truth: in $\mathbb{N}$, $x = 0$ is true when $x$ is assigned $0$, and $\forall x\, (x = 0)$ is false.

If all axioms are valid and all rules preserve validity, then every theorem is valid, by induction on derivations; this is the soundness of the system.
For derivations from hypotheses, soundness in the form "$\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ implies $\Gamma \models \varphi$" needs rules that preserve truth, or side conditions like the one on generalization.

### Rules that fail

Affirming the consequent, "from $\varphi \to \psi$ and $\psi$ infer $\varphi$", is unsound.
With $\varphi = p$ and $\psi = q$, the valuation with $p$ false and $q$ true makes both premises true and the conclusion false.
Universal instantiation without the substitutability condition is unsound, by the instance above.
The rules of the [[MIU system|PMN32E37BW]] are rules of a formal system with no semantics at all, so soundness is not even defined for them.

### Watch out

Do not use an admissible rule under hypotheses: substitution would turn the hypothesis $p$ into $q$, which does not follow from $p$.
Do not confuse a rule with an implication.
Modus ponens is a rule of the system, while $\varphi \to \psi$ is a formula; the deduction theorem links them, but it is a metatheorem.

### History

The Stoic logician Chrysippus listed five basic forms of argument, the indemonstrables, which include modus ponens and modus tollens.
Frege's *Begriffsschrift* of 1879 used explicit rules of inference in a formal system.
Gentzen introduced natural deduction and the sequent calculus in a paper published in 1934 and 1935, with an introduction and an elimination rule for each connective.

### Try it

Is "from $\varphi \wedge \psi$ infer $\varphi$" sound?
Yes: every valuation that makes $\varphi \wedge \psi$ true makes $\varphi$ true, by the truth table of $\wedge$.

Is "from $\varphi \vee \psi$ infer $\varphi$" sound?
No: with $\varphi = p$ and $\psi = q$, the valuation with $p$ false and $q$ true makes the premise true and the conclusion false.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A rule of inference is a pattern for drawing a conclusion.
The pattern "if A then B; A; so B" works for any statements A and B.
A rule is sound when it never leads from true statements to a false one.
The pattern "if A then B; B; so A" is not sound.
For example, "if it is a square, it has four sides" and "it has four sides" do not show that the figure is a square, since a rectangle that is not a square also has four sides.

### L2 human

Here is how you check a new rule before you use it: take disjunctive syllogism, "from $\varphi \vee \psi$ and $\neg \varphi$ infer $\psi$".
Take any valuation $v$ that makes both premises true.
Since $\neg \varphi$ is true, $\varphi$ is false under $v$.
Since $\varphi \vee \psi$ is true and $\varphi$ is false, the truth table of $\vee$ makes $\psi$ true under $v$.
So every valuation that makes the premises true makes the conclusion true, and the rule is sound.
For a formal system we also ask whether the rule is derivable, so that it adds no new theorems and may be used under hypotheses.
Watch out: one instance proves nothing, and soundness must hold for every valuation and every choice of $\varphi$ and $\psi$, as the argument above does.

### L3 human

In general a rule acts on judgments, not only on formulas: on sequents in sequent calculi, on derivations with open assumptions in natural deduction, and on typing judgments in type theory.
The structural rules (weakening, contraction, exchange, cut) mention no connective, and dropping some of them gives the [[substructural logics|98QF5J4W16]].
The relation "$\psi$ is derivable from $\Gamma$" that a set of rules generates is a [[consequence relation|J78Y1M4GND]].

Admissible rules are a subtle object in non-classical logics.
In intuitionistic logic and in many modal logics the admissible rules strictly extend the derivable ones, and [[intuitionistic and intermediate logics|38EQ20GR7M]] studies them.
In proof-theoretic semantics the meaning of a connective is given by its rules, and the condition of [[harmony|XJSQVK0G71]] between introduction and elimination rules takes the place of truth tables.
