## Definition

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $U$ and $W$ be [[subspaces|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$.
The subspaces $U$ and $W$ are *complementary* when $V = U \oplus W$ is a [[direct sum|041JTZCK9Q]].
Equivalently, the [[sum|0RMKABS2S5]] is $U + W = V$ and the [[intersection|0V33SPH7RR]] is $U \cap W = \{0\}$.
Then $W$ is called a *complement* of $U$ in $V$.

## Notation

Mathlib states this as `IsCompl U W` in the lattice of submodules, which means `U ⊓ W = ⊥` and `U ⊔ W = ⊤`.

## Remarks

A complement is not unique: in $\R^2$, each line through $0$ other than the $x$-axis is a complement of the $x$-axis.
If $V$ is finite-dimensional and $W$ is a complement of $U$, then $\dim W = \dim V - \dim U$.
A complement $W$ of $U$ is isomorphic to the [[quotient space|0BR3FY5Q8M]] $V / U$.
In an inner product space, the [[orthogonal complement|0XJXTBCB2T]] is one particular complement.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two subspaces are complementary when together they fill the space and they share only the zero vector.
Each vector then splits in a unique way into a part in each subspace.
A line and a plane through the origin in space are complementary when the line does not lie in the plane.

### L2 human

The relation is symmetric: if $W$ is a complement of $U$, then $U$ is a complement of $W$.
To check it in $\R^n$, put a basis of $U$ and a basis of $W$ side by side as the columns of a matrix.
The subspaces are complementary exactly when this matrix is square and invertible.
