## Definition

Two subspaces are complementary when together they split the whole space with no overlap.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $U$ and $W$ be [[subspaces|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$.
The subspaces $U$ and $W$ are *complementary* when $V = U \oplus W$ is a [[direct sum|041JTZCK9Q]].
Equivalently, the [[sum|0RMKABS2S5]] is $U + W = V$ and the [[intersection|0V33SPH7RR]] is $U \cap W = \{0\}$.
We then call $W$ a *complement* of $U$ in $V$.

## Notation

Mathlib states the condition as `IsCompl U W` in the lattice of submodules, which means `U ⊓ W = ⊥` and `U ⊔ W = ⊤`.

## Remarks

The definition has two conditions, and each one does a separate job.
The condition $U + W = V$ says that every vector of $V$ is some $u + w$, so the two subspaces leave nothing out.
The condition $U \cap W = \{0\}$ says that the expression $u + w$ is unique, so the two subspaces do not overlap.
Both are needed: the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis in $\R^3$ meet only in $0$ but do not fill $\R^3$, while the $xy$-plane and the $yz$-plane fill $\R^3$ but share the $y$-axis.

For a small example that passes, take $U = \R(1, 0)$ and $W = \R(1, 1)$ in $\R^2$.
The decomposition $(x, y) = (x - y)(1, 0) + y(1, 1)$ shows $U + W = \R^2$, and $t(1, 1)$ lies on the $x$-axis only for $t = 0$.
So $W$ is a complement of $U$, and so is every other line through $0$ except $U$ itself.

The definition does not say "the" complement, because there are usually many.
All of them are isomorphic to the [[quotient space|0BR3FY5Q8M]] $V / U$, and in finite dimension each has dimension $\dim V - \dim U$.
In an inner product space, the [[orthogonal complement|0XJXTBCB2T]] is one particular complement.
The concept page on the [[complementary subspace|0M83V17KHX]] has more examples and the main properties.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two subspaces are complementary when together they fill the space and share only the zero vector.
Each vector then splits in exactly one way into a part in each subspace.
In space, a line and a plane through the origin are complementary when the line does not lie in the plane.

### L2 human

The relation is symmetric: if $W$ is a complement of $U$, then $U$ is a complement of $W$.
To test it in $\R^n$, put a basis of $U$ and a basis of $W$ side by side as the columns of one matrix.
The subspaces are complementary exactly when this matrix is square and invertible, since then the columns form a basis of $\R^n$.
For $U = \R(1, 0)$ and $W = \R(1, 1)$ the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $1$, so the test passes.
