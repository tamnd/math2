## Statement

The determinant of a product of square matrices is the product of their determinants, so the determinant turns matrix multiplication into multiplication of numbers.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A$ and $B$ be $n \times n$ matrices over $K$.
Then
$$\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B,$$
where $AB$ is the [[matrix product|076JGP6R3T]] and $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].

## Remarks

### A check with numbers

Take
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix},$$
so $\det A = 4 - 6 = -2$ and $\det B = 6 - 0 = 6$.
Multiply the matrices in both orders:
$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 10 & 12 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad BA = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 10 & 14 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Then $\det(AB) = 48 - 60 = -12$ and $\det(BA) = 28 - 40 = -12$.
Both equal $\det A \det B = -2 \cdot 6 = -12$, although $AB \ne BA$.
There are two proofs: one [[by uniqueness of alternating forms|86J5B0YFNK]], valid over every commutative ring, and one [[by elementary matrices|3WPWGE036Q]], over a field.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Both matrices must be square of the same size.
For a $2 \times 3$ matrix $A$ and a $3 \times 2$ matrix $B$, the product $AB$ is square and has a determinant, but $\det A$ and $\det B$ do not exist.
The [[Cauchy-Binet formula|08WE2W7DYS]] handles that case with a sum over minors.
The ring must be commutative, because both proofs reorder products of entries, and without commutativity the determinant itself is not well defined.

### What it gives you

The rule has many consequences that are used every day.
Since $\det A \det B = \det B \det A$, we get $\det(AB) = \det(BA)$ for all square $A$ and $B$.
By induction, $\det(A^k) = (\det A)^k$ for $k \ge 0$.
If $A$ is invertible, then $\det A$ is a unit and $\det(A^{-1}) = (\det A)^{-1}$, the [[determinant of the inverse|0783S9D0ZH]].
Similar matrices have the same determinant, so the [[determinant of an endomorphism|0X0HQYSZ0K]] does not depend on the basis.

Here is a quick use.
No real $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$ satisfies $A^2 = -I_3$, because that would give $(\det A)^2 = \det(-I_3) = (-1)^3 = -1$, and no real square is negative.
Over a field $F$, the rule also says that $\det$ is a group homomorphism from the [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ onto $F^{\times}$, with kernel $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$.

### The converse

The product rule almost characterizes $\det$.
Over a field $F$, the commutator subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$, except when $n = 2$ and $F$ is the field with two elements.
So, apart from that case, every group homomorphism from $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ to a commutative group is a homomorphism of $F^{\times}$ composed with $\det$.
The proof of the statement on commutators uses the [[generation of SL and GL by elementary matrices|YSS4XNY9RC]].

### Generalizations

The [[Cauchy-Binet formula|08WE2W7DYS]] gives $\det(AB)$ for $A$ of size $k \times n$ and $B$ of size $n \times k$.
For linear maps of a finite-dimensional space to itself, $\det(f \circ g) = \det f \cdot \det g$.
For block matrices, the [[Schur complement rule|0KA7A4ENVJ]] plays a similar role.

### Watch out

There is no such rule for sums.
For $A = B = I_2$ we have $\det(A + B) = \det(2 I_2) = 4$, while $\det A + \det B = 2$; the [[counterexample on sums|D5VBC7621G]] gives more.

## History

In 1812 Cauchy and Binet each proved the product rule, in the more general form now called the Cauchy-Binet formula.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Over the real numbers, a matrix $A$ scales volumes by the factor $\lvert \det A \rvert$, and $B$ scales them by $\lvert \det B \rvert$.
Doing $B$ first and then $A$ scales volumes by the product of the two factors.
For example, if $B$ doubles every area and $A$ triples it, then $AB$ makes it six times as big.
The product rule says the same thing with signs, and it holds over every commutative ring.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: compute the determinant of a matrix that comes as a product of simple factors.
Take
$$L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 & 5 \\ 0 & -2 & 7 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Both are triangular, so their determinants are the products of the diagonal entries: $\det L = 1$ and $\det U = 3 \cdot (-2) \cdot 4 = -24$.
So $\det(LU) = 1 \cdot (-24) = -24$, without ever forming the product $LU$.
This is how computers find determinants: they factor $A = LU$ by elimination and multiply the diagonal of $U$, with a sign for each row swap.
Watch out: the rule needs a product, and it says nothing about $\det(A + B)$.

### L3 human

Multiplicativity says that $\det \colon \mathrm{GL}_n \to \mathbb{G}_m$ is a morphism of group schemes over $\mathbb{Z}$.
In terms of exterior powers it is the functoriality $\Lambda^n(f \circ g) = \Lambda^n f \circ \Lambda^n g$ on the line $\Lambda^n V$, where every endomorphism is multiplication by a scalar.
The rule returns in the theory of eigenvalues, where it shows that similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial.
