## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring and let $A$ and $B$ be $n \times n$ matrices over $K$.
Then
$$\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B,$$
where $AB$ is the [[matrix product|076JGP6R3T]] and $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Both matrices must be square of the same size.
For a $2 \times 3$ matrix $A$ and a $3 \times 2$ matrix $B$, $\det A$ and $\det B$ are not defined, although $\det(AB)$ is; the [[Cauchy-Binet formula|08WE2W7DYS]] treats this case.
The ring must be commutative: the proofs reorder products of entries.

### Consequences

- $\det(AB) = \det(BA)$, although $AB \neq BA$ in general.
- $\det(A^k) = (\det A)^k$ for $k \ge 0$.
- If $A$ is invertible, then $\det A$ is a unit and $\det A^{-1} = (\det A)^{-1}$; see the [[determinant of the inverse|0783S9D0ZH]].
- Similar matrices have the same determinant, so the [[determinant of an endomorphism|0X0HQYSZ0K]] is well defined.
- Over a field $F$, $\det$ is a group homomorphism from the [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ onto $F^{\times}$, with kernel $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$.

### Converse

The product rule nearly characterizes $\det$ among maps to commutative groups.
Over a field $F$, the commutator subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$, except for $n = 2$ and $F = \mathbb{F}_2$.
So every group homomorphism from $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ to a commutative group factors through $\det$, apart from that case.
The proof uses the [[generation of SL and GL by elementary matrices|YSS4XNY9RC]].

### Generalizations

The [[Cauchy-Binet formula|08WE2W7DYS]] gives $\det(AB)$ for $A$ of size $k \times n$ and $B$ of size $n \times k$.
For linear maps, $\det(f \circ g) = \det f \det g$.
For block matrices with commuting blocks, see the [[Schur complement rule|0KA7A4ENVJ]].

## History

Binet stated the multiplication theorem in 1811 and 1812, in the general form now called the Cauchy-Binet formula.
On the same day, 30 November 1812, Cauchy presented a paper with a proof more satisfactory than that of Binet.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The determinant of a product of two square matrices is the product of their determinants.
In the picture of volume, $A$ scales volumes by $\abs{\det A}$ and $B$ by $\abs{\det B}$.
Doing $B$ first and then $A$ scales volumes by the product.

### L2 human

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $\det A = 4 - 6 = -2$ and $\det B = 6 - 0 = 6$.
2. $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 10 & 12 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\det(AB) = 48 - 60 = -12$.
3. This agrees with $\det A \det B = -2 \cdot 6 = -12$.
4. $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 10 & 14 \end{pmatrix}$, with $\det(BA) = 28 - 40 = -12$, although $BA \neq AB$.

The typical use is to compute $\det$ of a factored matrix, as in the LU decomposition $A = LU$.
A trap: there is no similar rule for sums; see the [[counterexample on sums|~cbff1373]].

### L3 human

Multiplicativity says that $\det \colon \mathrm{GL}_n \to \mathbb{G}_m$ is a morphism of group schemes over $\mathbb{Z}$.
In invariant terms it is functoriality of the top exterior power: $\Lambda^n(fg) = \Lambda^n f \circ \Lambda^n g$.
