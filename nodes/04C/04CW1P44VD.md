## Statement

1. Operator form. Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional complex inner product space, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear. Then $T$ is [[normal|0ENZN5G4CV]] if and only if $V$ has an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of [[eigenvectors|0B5H4208Z9]] of $T$.
2. Matrix form. Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ complex matrix. Then $A$ is [[normal|0XYKE8BE7X]] if and only if there are a [[unitary matrix|0D6RMJ10M9]] $U$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ with
$$A = U D U^*.$$

In part 2, the columns of $U$ are an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^n$ of eigenvectors of $A$, and the diagonal entries of $D$ are the corresponding eigenvalues.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The field must be $\mathbb{C}$. [[The rotation by a right angle|1CF483TZZA]] on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is normal, but it has no real eigenvalue, so $\mathbb{R}^2$ has no basis of real eigenvectors.
- Normality is needed. [[The shear|BF9MKKHH4Z]] is not normal, and it has no basis of eigenvectors at all.
- Normality is needed even for diagonalizable matrices. [[The trap matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$|5RQ7EC5RC6]] has a basis of eigenvectors, but no orthonormal one.

### Consequences

- If $A$ is Hermitian, then $D$ is real. This is [[the spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices|0DGZBQWZWN]].
- If $A$ is unitary, then the diagonal entries of $D$ have absolute value $1$. If $A$ is skew-Hermitian, they are purely imaginary.
- A normal matrix with only real eigenvalues is Hermitian: $A^* = U \overline{D} U^* = U D U^* = A$.
- [[The spectral decomposition|D5KVBA1K9R]]: grouping the eigenvectors by eigenvalue gives $A = \sum \lambda_i P_i$ with orthogonal projections $P_i$.
- Commuting normal matrices are diagonal in one common orthonormal basis, by [[simultaneous unitary diagonalization|BFQQDA489F]].

### Converse

The theorem is an equivalence.
The direction "orthonormal eigenbasis implies normal" holds over $\mathbb{R}$ too.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ it even gives a symmetric matrix, as in [[the real spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]].

### Three spectral theorems

The name "spectral theorem" is used for three related results in finite dimension.
The real version is about symmetric matrices, the Hermitian version is about [[Hermitian matrices|0DGZBQWZWN]], and the complex version on this page is about normal matrices.
The hypotheses grow from real symmetric to Hermitian to normal matrices.
The conclusions differ: the real version gives a real orthogonal matrix, the Hermitian version gives real eigenvalues, and the normal version allows complex eigenvalues.

### Generalizations

- A compact normal operator on a Hilbert space has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, in the sense of Hilbert spaces.
- A bounded normal operator on a Hilbert space need not have eigenvectors. The [[spectral theorem for bounded normal operators|X71XRPJR7D]] uses a projection-valued measure instead.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A complex matrix is normal exactly when it acts in some perpendicular complex directions by plain multiplication.
In these directions, the matrix is a list of numbers on a diagonal.
For example, a quarter turn of the plane acts on two complex directions by $i$ and by $-i$.
A shear has only one special direction, so it is not normal.

### L2 human

Worked example: a normal matrix that is not Hermitian.

1. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Then $A A^* = 2I = A^* A$, so $A$ is normal.
2. The vector $(1, 1)$ is an eigenvector for $1 + i$, and $(1, -1)$ is an eigenvector for $1 - i$.
3. The two vectors are orthogonal. After normalization, $U = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ is unitary.
4. Then $U^* A U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + i & 0 \\ 0 & 1 - i \end{pmatrix}$, so $A = U D U^*$ with $D = \operatorname{diag}(1 + i, 1 - i)$.

The typical use: compute a function of a normal matrix, for example $A^k = U D^k U^*$.
A trap: a matrix with a basis of eigenvectors need not be normal, and then the eigenvectors cannot be chosen orthonormal.

### L3 human

The theorem says that, up to unitary equivalence, normal operators are the multiplication operators on a finite set with counting measure.
For bounded normal operators on a Hilbert space, the same statement holds with a multiplication operator on an $L^2$ space, and this is the form of the general spectral theorem.
The proof through [[the Schur decomposition|V1JG4C7R7B]] shows that the departure from normality is measured by the part above the diagonal of a Schur form.
Commutative $C^*$-algebras generalize the commuting family $\set{T, T^*}$, and Gelfand theory gives the general spectral theorem from this view.
