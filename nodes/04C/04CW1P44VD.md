## Statement

Over the complex numbers, the operators that are diagonal in some orthonormal basis are exactly the normal ones.

1. Operator form. Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional complex inner product space, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear. Then $T$ is [[normal|0ENZN5G4CV]] if and only if $V$ has an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of [[eigenvectors|0B5H4208Z9]] of $T$.
2. Matrix form. Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ complex matrix. Then $A$ is [[normal|0XYKE8BE7X]] if and only if there are a [[unitary matrix|0D6RMJ10M9]] $U$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ with
$$A = U D U^*.$$

In part 2, the columns of $U$ form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^n$ of eigenvectors of $A$, and the diagonal entries of $D$ are the matching eigenvalues.

## Remarks

### A first case

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which has $A A^* = 2I = A^* A$ and so is normal, but is not Hermitian.
Apply $A$ to two simple vectors:
$$A \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + i \\ 1 + i \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - i \\ i - 1 \end{pmatrix} = (1 - i) \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
So $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ are eigenvectors for $1 + i$ and $1 - i$, and they are orthogonal.
With $U = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ we get $A = U \operatorname{diag}(1 + i, 1 - i) U^*$.
Check: the trace of $D$ is $2$ and its determinant is $(1 + i)(1 - i) = 2$, and $A$ has trace $2$ and determinant $1 - i^2 = 2$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The field must be $\mathbb{C}$.
[[The rotation by a right angle|1CF483TZZA]] on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is normal, but it has no real eigenvalue, so $\mathbb{R}^2$ has no basis of real eigenvectors.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ the right theorem is [[the real spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]], which needs self-adjoint, not just normal.

Normality is needed.
[[The shear|BF9MKKHH4Z]] is not normal, and it has no basis of eigenvectors at all.
Normality is needed even for diagonalizable matrices.
[[The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$|5RQ7EC5RC6]] has the eigenvectors $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$, whose dot product is $1$, and the eigenspaces are lines, so no choice makes them orthogonal.

Finite dimension is needed for the conclusion as stated, since a bounded normal operator on a Hilbert space can have no eigenvectors.

### What it gives you

Special classes of normal matrices give special diagonals.
If $A$ is Hermitian, then $D$ is real, which is [[the spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices|0DGZBQWZWN]].
If $A$ is unitary, the diagonal entries of $D$ have absolute value $1$, and if $A$ is skew-Hermitian, they are purely imaginary.
Conversely, a normal matrix with only real eigenvalues is Hermitian, since $A^* = U \overline{D} U^* = U D U^* = A$.

Grouping the eigenvectors by eigenvalue gives [[the spectral decomposition|D5KVBA1K9R]] $A = \sum \lambda_i P_i$ with orthogonal projections $P_i$.
Commuting normal matrices are diagonal in one common orthonormal basis, by [[simultaneous unitary diagonalization|BFQQDA489F]].
The theorem has two standard proofs: [[by the Schur decomposition|A5M6ZY42WR]] and [[by deflation|P48CFDFAV9]].

### The converse

The theorem is an equivalence, and the direction from an orthonormal eigenbasis to normality is the easy one.
In that basis $T$ has a diagonal matrix $D$ and $T^*$ has the matrix $\overline{D}$, and diagonal matrices commute.
This direction holds over $\mathbb{R}$ too, and there it even gives a symmetric matrix, because a real orthonormal eigenbasis comes with real eigenvalues.

### Three spectral theorems

Books use the name "spectral theorem" for three related results in finite dimension.
The real version is about real symmetric matrices, the Hermitian version is about Hermitian matrices, and the complex version is about normal matrices.
Each class contains the one before it, and the conclusions get weaker in the same order.
The real version gives a real orthogonal matrix and real eigenvalues, the Hermitian version gives a unitary matrix and real eigenvalues, and the normal version gives a unitary matrix and complex eigenvalues.
Some books call self-adjoint operators "symmetric" even over $\mathbb{C}$, so check which condition a book means.

### Watch out

A matrix with a basis of eigenvectors need not be normal, and then its eigenvectors cannot be chosen orthonormal.
A second trap is to test $A A^{\mathsf{T}} = A^{\mathsf{T}} A$ for a complex matrix.
The matrix $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ is complex symmetric, so it passes that test, but $B^2 = 0$ and $B \neq 0$, so it is not diagonalizable at all.

## History

Schur published the unitary triangularization in 1909, and it gives the normal case at once, since a normal triangular matrix is diagonal.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A complex matrix is normal exactly when it acts on some perpendicular complex directions by plain multiplication.
In those directions, the matrix is just a list of numbers on a diagonal.
For example, a quarter turn of the plane acts on two complex directions by $i$ and by $-i$.
A shear has only one special direction, so it is not normal.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: compute $A^{8}$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
The first case above gives $A = U D U^*$ with $D = \operatorname{diag}(1 + i, 1 - i)$ and $U^* U = I$, so $A^8 = U D^8 U^*$.
Since $(1 + i)^2 = 2i$, we get $(1 + i)^8 = (2i)^4 = 16$, and in the same way $(1 - i)^8 = (-2i)^4 = 16$.
So $D^8 = 16 I$, and
$$A^8 = U (16 I) U^* = 16 I.$$
Check: $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2i \\ 2i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so $A^4 = (A^2)^2 = -4I$ and $A^8 = 16 I$.
The spectral theorem turned eight matrix products into two powers of complex numbers.
Watch out: the method needs $U^{-1} = U^*$, so the eigenvectors must be normalized.

### L3 human

The theorem says that, up to unitary equivalence, a normal operator is a multiplication operator on a finite set with counting measure.
For bounded normal operators on a Hilbert space, the same statement holds with a multiplication operator on an $L^2$ space, and this is [[the spectral theorem for bounded normal operators|X71XRPJR7D]].
The proof through [[the Schur decomposition|V1JG4C7R7B]] shows that the departure from normality is measured by the part of a Schur form above the diagonal.
From the view of $C^*$-algebras, $T$ and $T^*$ generate a commutative algebra, and Gelfand theory turns that commutative algebra into functions on the spectrum.
