## Summary

Gauss-Jordan elimination is the variant of [[Gaussian elimination|0JVJ2WCXZH]] that continues until the matrix is in [[reduced row echelon form|0WF5AQYTW6]].
After the forward phase gives a row echelon form, it scales each pivot row to make the [[pivot|0J0BBCMJ5W]] equal to $1$.
Then it adds multiples of each pivot row to the rows above it, to make every other entry in each pivot column zero.
Applied to the [[augmented matrix|0T6MWGZSXA]] of a system, the result shows the solutions directly, with no [[back substitution|0ATFVE08MW]].
It matters because the reduced form is unique, so it gives a standard answer, and because the same steps compute inverses.

## Motivation

After Gaussian elimination, back substitution still needs arithmetic with equations.
Gauss-Jordan elimination does this work with [[row operations|04XWH28452]] on the matrix instead.
Then each nonzero row reads "pivot variable $=$ constant $-$ combination of free variables", and we read the [[parametric form|~dc297165]] at once.

## Notation

1. Some books, for example Lay, call the whole process "row reduction" and the two parts the forward phase and the backward phase.
2. Other books use "Gaussian elimination" for the whole process; the name of the method does not fix the end form, so check the book's definition.
3. A variant does both parts in one pass: at each pivot it clears the entries above and below the pivot.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[A system with a free variable solved by Gauss-Jordan elimination|~68d3db07]] ends with the RREF and reads off a line of solutions.
2. [[Inverting a 3 by 3 matrix by Gauss-Jordan elimination|~3f9cc605]] reduces $[A \mid I]$ to $[I \mid A^{-1}]$.
3. [[A matrix that row reduction shows is not invertible|~ec1852b0]] gives a zero row in the left block.
4. The examples of [[reduced row echelon form|0WF5AQYTW6]] are all outputs of this method.

### Non-examples

1. Gaussian elimination that stops at a non-reduced echelon form, such as in [[the three planes example|~382fc66c]], is not Gauss-Jordan elimination.
2. Column operations change the solution set of a system, so a reduction that uses them is not Gauss-Jordan elimination.

### Basic properties

1. The result does not depend on the choices in the algorithm, because the reduced row echelon form of a matrix is unique, see [[the uniqueness of the RREF|BF9SMZMJZE]].
2. Applied to $[A \mid I]$ for an invertible $n \times n$ matrix $A$, it gives $[I \mid A^{-1}]$, see [[computing the inverse by row reduction|0Z83DHFMYM]].
3. If the left block of the result has a zero row, then $A$ is not invertible.
4. Operation count, with one operation for each addition, subtraction, multiplication or division: the forward phase on an $n \times n$ matrix costs $\tfrac23 n^3$ to leading order.
5. The backward phase, done from the bottom row up, changes only the pivot column and the right-hand side in each row, so it costs only order $n^2$ operations for one right-hand side.
6. The one-pass variant updates all $n - 1$ other rows at each pivot, and its leading term is $n^3$, so it is about $1.5$ times as expensive.
7. Inverting by $[A \mid I]$ costs about $2n^3$ operations, if the known zeros of the right block are skipped.

### History

The method is named after Gauss and after the geodesist Wilhelm Jordan, who described it in 1888 in a book on surveying.
Clasen published the method in the same year, and the two found it probably independently.
The Jordan of this method is not Camille Jordan, who gave the Jordan normal form.

### Common errors

1. Clearing the entries above a pivot before it is scaled to $1$, and then forgetting to scale it.
2. Using a row operation that brings back a nonzero entry in a column that is already cleared; work from the bottom pivot up to avoid this.
3. Thinking that the RREF of $[A \mid b]$ and the RREF of $A$ are the same object; the first has one more column, and a pivot in that column means that the system has no solution.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Gauss-Jordan elimination is Gaussian elimination with extra cleaning.
Each pivot becomes $1$, and the numbers above and below each pivot become $0$.
At the end each equation gives one unknown directly.
For example, the rows $(1, 0 \mid 2)$ and $(0, 1 \mid 3)$ say $x = 2$ and $y = 3$.

### L2 human

We reduce $\left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 1 & 3 & 5 \end{array}\right]$.

1. $R_1 \leftarrow \tfrac12 R_1$ gives $(1, 2 \mid 3)$.
2. $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1$ gives $(0, 1 \mid 2)$.
3. $R_1 \leftarrow R_1 - 2R_2$ gives $(1, 0 \mid -1)$.
4. So $x = -1$ and $y = 2$.
5. Check: $2(-1) + 4 \cdot 2 = 6$ and $-1 + 3 \cdot 2 = 5$.

The typical use is to find the general solution of a system with free variables, or to compute an inverse by hand.
A trap: for one square system with a unique solution, back substitution after Gaussian elimination is cheaper than the full reduction.

### L3 human

The RREF is a canonical form for row equivalence: two matrices of the same size are row equivalent if and only if their RREFs are equal.
So Gauss-Jordan elimination decides row equivalence, and it computes a canonical basis of the [[row space|069MCGX635]].
The nonzero rows of the RREF of $A$ are this basis, and the pivot columns of $A$ form a basis of its [[column space|03AJKXH1AV]].
In numerical work the one-pass method is rarely used, because LU decomposition with back substitution is cheaper.
