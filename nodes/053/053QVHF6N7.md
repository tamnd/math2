## Statement

To find the eigenvalues of a linear map on a finite-dimensional space, it is enough to find the roots of one polynomial of degree $n$.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ with $n = \dim V$ and $1 \le n < \infty$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be a linear map.
Let $p_T$ be the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] of $T$, and let $\lambda \in F$.

1. The scalar $\lambda$ is an [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $T$ if and only if $p_T(\lambda) = 0$.
2. The map $T$ has at most $n$ distinct eigenvalues.

For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over $F$, the same holds with $p_A(t) = \det(tI - A)$, applied to the map $x \mapsto Ax$ on $F^n$.

## Remarks

### A first use

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{R}$.
Its characteristic polynomial is
$$p_A(t) = \det \begin{pmatrix} t & -1 \\ 2 & t - 3 \end{pmatrix} = t(t - 3) + 2 = t^2 - 3t + 2 = (t - 1)(t - 2).$$
Part 1 says that the eigenvalues are exactly $1$ and $2$, and part 2 says that a $2 \times 2$ matrix could not have had more.
Check: $A(1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} = (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$, and $A(1, 2)^{\mathsf{T}} = (2, -2 + 6)^{\mathsf{T}} = 2 (1, 2)^{\mathsf{T}}$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The space must have finite dimension, since $p_T$ is defined only then.
In infinite dimension no polynomial could do the job: over $\mathbb{R}$ the [[left shift on sequences|HBVWZ0FXWC]] has every real number as an eigenvalue, while a nonzero polynomial has finitely many roots.

The scalars must form a field, or at least an integral domain.
Over the ring $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ take the $1 \times 1$ matrix $(1)$, with $p(t) = t - 1$.
For every odd $\lambda$ we have $4 \lambda \equiv 4 \pmod 8$, so $v = 4 \ne 0$ solves $1 \cdot v = \lambda v$ for $\lambda = 1, 3, 5, 7$.
So four scalars have an eigenvector, but only $\lambda = 1$ is a root of $p$, and part 2 fails too.

The roots must lie in $F$.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ the polynomial $t^2 + 1$ of the [[rotation by a right angle|5AE98D7959]] has no root, and the rotation has no real eigenvalue, even though the polynomial has the complex roots $\pm i$.

### What it gives you

The [[spectrum|0XPX5NC67E]] of a matrix is the set of roots of $p_A$ in $F$, and it has at most $n$ elements.
Similar matrices have the same eigenvalues, because they have the same characteristic polynomial.
Over an algebraically closed field such as $\mathbb{C}$, every nonconstant polynomial has a root, so every map has an eigenvalue; the theorem on the [[existence of an eigenvalue|08CJB2NQ92]] gives this and a proof without determinants.
The multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of $p_T$ is the [[algebraic multiplicity|0FY42N02DV]] of $\lambda$.

### The converse and sharpness

Part 1 is an equivalence, so both directions hold: each root in $F$ is an eigenvalue, and each eigenvalue is a root.
The bound in part 2 is sharp.
The diagonal matrix with entries $1, 2, \dots, n$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ has exactly $n$ eigenvalues.

### Generalizations

The [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] has the same roots as $p_T$, by the theorem that [[eigenvalues are the roots of the minimal polynomial|0QF4ZEFG52]].
For a bounded operator on a Banach space, the eigenvalues are only one part of the [[spectrum of the operator|XXXSST2DR5]], which collects the $\lambda$ for which $T - \lambda$ is not invertible.

## History

Cauchy called the eigenvalues "racines caractéristiques", characteristic roots, and his term survives in the name "characteristic equation" for $\det(\lambda I - A) = 0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To find the eigenvalues of a matrix, compute its characteristic polynomial and find its roots.
Each root is an eigenvalue, and each eigenvalue is a root.
A polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots, so an $n \times n$ matrix has at most $n$ eigenvalues.
For the matrix with rows $(0, 1)$ and $(-2, 3)$ the polynomial is $(t - 1)(t - 2)$, and the eigenvalues are $1$ and $2$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: find the eigenvalues of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -5 \\ 2 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{R}$.
The trace is $1$ and the determinant is $-12 + 10 = -2$, so
$$p_A(t) = t^2 - t - 2 = (t - 2)(t + 1).$$
By part 1 the eigenvalues are $2$ and $-1$.
To find an eigenvector for $2$, solve $(A - 2I)x = 0$: the first row $(2, -5)$ gives $x = (5, 2)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
Check: $A (5, 2)^{\mathsf{T}} = (20 - 10, 10 - 6)^{\mathsf{T}} = (10, 4)^{\mathsf{T}} = 2 (5, 2)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
This is the standard hand method, roots first and eigenvectors second, which [[computing eigenvalues and eigenvectors by hand|7JJE96MZQY]] develops.
Watch out: a root of $p_A$ outside $F$, for example a complex root of a real matrix, is not an eigenvalue over $F$.

### L3 human

The value $p_T(\lambda)$ is $\det(\lambda - T)$, and over a field a determinant vanishes exactly at the maps that are not invertible.
So the theorem is the finite-dimensional case of the rule that the eigenvalues are the points where $\lambda - T$ fails to be injective, combined with the fact that injective and invertible agree for endomorphisms of a finite-dimensional space.
Over a commutative ring $K$, the matrix $\lambda I - A$ is invertible exactly when $p_A(\lambda)$ is a unit, and a nonzero value need not be a unit.
In the $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ example, $p(3) = 2$ is nonzero, yet $2 \cdot 4 = 0$, so $3 I - A$ kills the vector $4$.
