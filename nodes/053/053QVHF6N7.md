## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ with $n = \dim V \ge 1$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be a linear map.
Let $p_T$ be the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] of $T$, and let $\lambda \in F$.

1. $\lambda$ is an [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $T$ if and only if $p_T(\lambda) = 0$.
2. $T$ has at most $n$ distinct eigenvalues.

For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over $F$, the same holds with $p_A(t) = \det(tI - A)$, applied to the map $x \mapsto Ax$ on $F^n$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $V$ must be finite-dimensional, because $p_T$ is defined only then. The left shift on all sequences has every scalar as an eigenvalue, so no polynomial can have its eigenvalues as roots; see [[the shift example|~56ecd655]].
- $F$ must be a field, or at least an integral domain. Over the ring $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$, take the $1 \times 1$ matrix $(1)$, with $p(t) = t - 1$.
  The equation $1 \cdot v = \lambda v$ has the solution $v = 4 \ne 0$ for $\lambda = 1, 3, 5, 7$, because $4 \lambda \equiv 4$ for odd $\lambda$.
  So there are four such scalars for $n = 1$, and only $\lambda = 1$ is a root of $p$.
- The roots must lie in $F$. Over $\R$, $t^2 + 1$ has no root, and the [[rotation by 90 degrees|~805e3c17]] has no real eigenvalue.

### Consequences

- The [[spectrum|0XPX5NC67E]] of a matrix is the set of roots of $p_A$ in $F$, and it has at most $n$ elements.
- Similar matrices have the same eigenvalues.
- Over an algebraically closed field, every endomorphism of a nonzero finite-dimensional space has an eigenvalue; see the theorem on the [[existence of eigenvalues|08CJB2NQ92]].
- The multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of $p_T$ is the [[algebraic multiplicity|0FY42N02DV]] of $\lambda$.

### Converse

Part 1 is an equivalence, so both directions hold.
The bound in part 2 is sharp: the diagonal matrix with entries $1, 2, \dots, n$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ has $n$ eigenvalues.

### Generalizations

- The [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] has the same roots as $p_T$, by the theorem that [[eigenvalues are the roots of the minimal polynomial|0QF4ZEFG52]].
- For a bounded operator on a Banach space, the eigenvalues form a part of the [[spectrum|XXXSST2DR5]], which can be infinite.

## History

Cauchy introduced the term "racine caractéristique", characteristic root, for what is now called an eigenvalue.
The name "characteristic equation" for $\det(\lambda I - A) = 0$ keeps his term.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To find the eigenvalues of a matrix, compute its characteristic polynomial and find its roots.
Each root is an eigenvalue, and each eigenvalue is a root.
A polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots, so an $n \times n$ matrix has at most $n$ eigenvalues.
Then, for each eigenvalue $\lambda$, solve $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$ to find the eigenvectors.

### L2 human

We find the eigenvalues of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $p_A(t) = \det \begin{pmatrix} t & -1 \\ 2 & t - 3 \end{pmatrix} = t(t - 3) + 2 = t^2 - 3t + 2$.
2. Second method: $\operatorname{tr} A = 3$ and $\det A = 0 + 2 = 2$, so $p_A(t) = t^2 - 3t + 2$.
3. $p_A(t) = (t - 1)(t - 2)$, so the eigenvalues are $1$ and $2$.
4. Check: $A (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} = (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$, and $A (1, 2)^{\mathsf{T}} = (2, -2 + 6)^{\mathsf{T}} = (2, 4)^{\mathsf{T}} = 2 (1, 2)^{\mathsf{T}}$.

The typical use is the standard hand method: find the roots of $p_A$, then the eigenvectors; see [[computing eigenvalues and eigenvectors by hand|~7d332ef3]].
A trap: a root of $p_A$ outside $F$, for example a complex root of a real matrix, is not an eigenvalue over $F$.

### L3 human

The evaluation of $p_T$ at $\lambda$ is $\det(\lambda - T)$, and a determinant over a field vanishes exactly at non-invertible maps.
So the theorem is the finite-dimensional case of "eigenvalues are the points where $\lambda - T$ is not invertible".
Over a commutative ring $K$, $p_A(\lambda)$ is a unit exactly when $\lambda I - A$ is invertible, but non-invertible does not mean non-injective.
