## Example

Statement: there are no integers $x$ and $y$ with $x^2 - 3y^2 = 2$.
We give a [[proof of impossibility|6F55216R53]] by a remainder obstruction.

1. Suppose, for a [[contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]], that $x$ and $y$ are integers with $x^2 - 3y^2 = 2$.
2. Then $x^2 = 3y^2 + 2$, so $x^2$ leaves remainder $2$ on division by $3$.
3. But the square of an integer leaves remainder $0$ or $1$ on division by $3$; see [[the square of an integer leaves remainder 0 or 1 on division by 3|N70XWKTVMC]].
4. Steps 2 and 3 contradict each other. So no integers $x$, $y$ satisfy the equation.

## Remarks

The obstruction is arithmetic, not a matter of size.
Over the real numbers the equation has many solutions, for example $x = 2$ and $y = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}$.
So the proof must use the fact that $x$ is an integer, and step 3 is where it does.

Changing the right side changes the answer.
The equation $x^2 - 3y^2 = 1$ has the integer solution $x = 2$, $y = 1$, and $x^2 - 3y^2 = -2$ has the solution $x = 1$, $y = 1$.
For these right sides the test modulo $3$ gives no obstruction, since $1$ and $-2 \equiv 1$ are possible remainders of a square.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Divide square numbers by $3$: $1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36$ leave the remainders $1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0$.
A remainder of $2$ never occurs.
If $x^2 - 3y^2$ were $2$, then $x^2$ would be $2$ more than a multiple of $3$, so it would leave remainder $2$.
That cannot happen, so the equation has no solution in whole numbers, and no search is needed.

### L2 human

We use the same method for the equation $x^2 + y^2 = 4z + 3$, with integers $x$, $y$, $z$.

1. A square leaves remainder $0$ or $1$ on division by $4$: $(2k)^2 = 4k^2$ and $(2k + 1)^2 = 4(k^2 + k) + 1$.
2. So $x^2 + y^2$ leaves remainder $0$, $1$ or $2$ on division by $4$.
3. The right side $4z + 3$ leaves remainder $3$.
4. So the equation has no integer solution.

The typical use: reduce both sides modulo a small number, and compare the possible remainders.

A trap: the modulus must be chosen with care.
Modulo $2$, the equation $x^2 - 3y^2 = 2$ becomes $x^2 + y^2 \equiv 0$, which is possible, so the test modulo $2$ fails to prove anything.
