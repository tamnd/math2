## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite dimension $n$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear.
If $T$ has $n$ pairwise distinct [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]] in $F$, then $T$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] over $F$.
In particular, an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$ whose characteristic polynomial has $n$ distinct roots in $F$ is diagonalizable over $F$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The eigenvalues must be distinct. The [[shear|~58e14c85]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalue $1$ twice and is not diagonalizable.
- The eigenvalues must lie in $F$. The rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has the distinct eigenvalues $\pm i$ in $\C$, but none in $\R$, and it is not diagonalizable over $\R$.

### Consequences

- Each eigenspace is then a line, and the matrix $P$ of eigenvectors is unique up to the order and the scaling of its columns.
- Generic complex matrices are diagonalizable. The [[discriminant|0VVK64Q4JK]] of $p_A$ is a polynomial in the entries of $A$, and it is nonzero exactly when $p_A$ has $n$ distinct roots.
- This polynomial is not the zero polynomial, since it is nonzero at $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2, \dots, n)$. So the matrices with $n$ distinct eigenvalues form a dense open subset of $M_n(\C)$, and diagonalizable matrices are dense.
- Over $\R$, diagonalizable matrices are not dense: matrices near the rotation by a right angle still have non-real eigenvalues.

### Converse

The converse is false.
The identity matrix $I_n$ with $n \ge 2$ is diagonal, but it has only one eigenvalue.
The [[3 × 3 matrix with a repeated eigenvalue|~e0a600d5]] is diagonalizable with only two distinct eigenvalues.
The exact condition is the [[test by multiplicities|~1ac265ba]].

### Generalizations

If $T$ has distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ and $\dim E_{\lambda_1} + \cdots + \dim E_{\lambda_k} = n$, then $T$ is diagonalizable, by the [[characterization by eigenvectors|056SW1E0AC]].
The statement with $k = n$ is the case where each eigenspace has dimension $1$.

## History

This sufficient condition appears in all standard textbooks, for example in Lay, Lay and McDonald, Section 5.3.
We do not know of a first source.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If an $n \times n$ matrix has $n$ different stretch factors, then each factor has its own special direction.
Directions with different stretch factors never lie on a common line or plane in a way that wastes a dimension.
So we get $n$ independent directions, which is enough to make the matrix diagonal.

### L2 human

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $A$ is triangular, so its eigenvalues are $1$ and $3$, which are distinct.
2. By the statement, $A$ is diagonalizable.
3. Eigenvectors: $(1, 0)$ for $1$, and $(1, 1)$ for $3$, since $A(1, 1) = (3, 3)$.
4. With $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, we get $P^{-1} A P = \operatorname{diag}(1, 3)$.

The typical use is a quick test: compute the characteristic polynomial and check that its roots are distinct.
The trap is to use the test backwards and conclude that a matrix with a repeated eigenvalue is not diagonalizable.

### L3 human

The set of matrices with a repeated eigenvalue is the zero set of the discriminant, a hypersurface in the space of matrices.
So in the Zariski topology, and over $\C$ also in the usual topology, diagonalizable matrices with distinct eigenvalues are generic.
This is why many identities for matrices, such as the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]] and $\det e^A = e^{\tr A}$, can be proved first for diagonalizable matrices and then extended by continuity or by a Zariski density argument.
