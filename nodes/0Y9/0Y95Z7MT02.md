## Statement

A map with as many distinct eigenvalues as the dimension is always diagonalizable.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$ of finite dimension $n$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear.
If $T$ has $n$ pairwise distinct [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]] in $F$, then $T$ is [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] over $F$.
In particular, an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$ whose characteristic polynomial has $n$ distinct roots in $F$ is diagonalizable over $F$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The eigenvalues must be distinct.
The [[shear|SBDF8APDF7]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalue $1$ twice, so it has $n = 2$ eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, and it is not diagonalizable.

The eigenvalues must lie in $F$.
The rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has the two distinct eigenvalues $i$ and $-i$ in $\C$, but none in $\R$.
So it is diagonalizable over $\C$ and not over $\R$.

### What it gives you

The test is the quickest one in practice: compute the characteristic polynomial and check that its roots are distinct and lie in $F$.
For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ is triangular with the distinct diagonal entries $1$ and $3$, so it is diagonalizable without any further work.
When the test applies, each eigenspace is a line, so the matrix $P$ of eigenvectors is unique up to the order and the scaling of its columns.

The test also shows that almost every complex matrix is diagonalizable.
The [[discriminant|0VVK64Q4JK]] of the characteristic polynomial $p_A$ is a polynomial in the entries of $A$, and it is nonzero exactly when $p_A$ has $n$ distinct roots.
It is not the zero polynomial, since it is nonzero at $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2, \dots, n)$.
So the matrices with $n$ distinct eigenvalues form a dense open subset of $M_n(\C)$, and the diagonalizable matrices are dense.
Over $\R$ this fails: every real matrix close to the rotation by a right angle still has two non-real eigenvalues, so it is not diagonalizable over $\R$.

### The converse is false

A diagonalizable matrix can have repeated eigenvalues.
The identity matrix $I_n$ with $n \ge 2$ is diagonal and has the single eigenvalue $1$.
The [[3 × 3 matrix with a repeated eigenvalue|K5NXSFJV50]] is diagonalizable with only two distinct eigenvalues.
The exact condition is the [[test by multiplicities|CDB8KCNNG9]].

### Generalizations

If $T$ has the distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$ and $\dim E_{\lambda_1} + \cdots + \dim E_{\lambda_k} = n$, then $T$ is diagonalizable, by the [[characterization by eigenvectors|056SW1E0AC]].
The corollary is the case $k = n$, in which each eigenspace has dimension $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose that an $n \times n$ matrix has $n$ different stretch factors.
Then each factor has its own special direction.
Directions with different stretch factors can never be combined to cancel each other, so they are independent.
So we get $n$ independent directions, which is enough to use as axes and make the matrix diagonal.

### L2 human

Let us diagonalize $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\R$.
The matrix is triangular, so its eigenvalues are $1$ and $3$, which are distinct, and the corollary says that $A$ is diagonalizable.
An eigenvector for $1$ is $(1, 0)$.
For $3$, the matrix $A - 3I = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ gives the eigenvector $(1, 1)$, and indeed $A(1, 1) = (3, 3)$.
With $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ we multiply out:
$$P^{-1} A P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix},$$
where the middle factor is $A P$.
Watch out: the test only works in one direction, and a repeated eigenvalue does not prove that a matrix is not diagonalizable.

### L3 human

The matrices with a repeated eigenvalue form the zero set of the discriminant, a hypersurface in the space of all matrices.
So, in the Zariski topology over any infinite field, and in the usual topology over $\C$, matrices with distinct eigenvalues are generic.
This is why many identities for matrices, such as the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]] and $\det e^A = e^{\tr A}$, can be proved first for diagonalizable matrices and then extended to all matrices by continuity or by a density argument.
