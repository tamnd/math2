## Statement

Let linearly [[polarized|PJ8ZSJ0QDW]] light of intensity $I_0$ fall on an ideal linear polarizer.
Let $\theta$ be the angle between the direction of polarization of the light and the transmission axis of the polarizer.
Then the transmitted light is polarized along the transmission axis, and its intensity is
$$I = I_0 \cos^2 \theta.$$

## Remarks

The ideal polarizer keeps the component of the electric field along its axis and removes the orthogonal component.
The kept amplitude is $E_0 \cos\theta$, and the intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude.
At $\theta = 0$ all light passes; at $\theta = 90^\circ$ none passes, which is the case of crossed polarizers.
With three polarizers at $0^\circ$, $45^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, light polarized at $0^\circ$ leaves with intensity $I_0 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = I_0/4$, although the first and last are crossed.
The hypothesis of linear polarization matters: for unpolarized light, the average of $\cos^2\theta$ over all angles gives $I_0/2$ for every orientation.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A polarizer is a filter that lets through only light vibrating in one direction.
If the light already vibrates in that direction, all of it passes.
If it vibrates at an angle, only the part along the filter passes, and the intensity drops like the square of the cosine of the angle.
This is why two polarizing filters can be turned to block nearly all light.

### L2 human

Malus's law is the simplest case of [[Mueller calculus|X17R7F1QH5]] and of [[Jones calculus|D3XTQ5Z2QA]]: it is the projection of a Jones vector onto the axis of the polarizer.
It is a quick way to measure the angle of linear polarization and is the basis of polarimetry.
The same cosine-squared rule appears in quantum mechanics as the probability that a single photon passes the polarizer.
