## Statement

Over the complex numbers, and over every other algebraically closed field, a linear map of a nonzero finite-dimensional space always has an eigenvalue.

Let $F$ be an [[algebraically closed field|02SJG1HC32]], let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$ with $V \ne 0$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be a linear map.
Then $T$ has at least one [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] $\lambda \in F$.
In particular, every endomorphism of a nonzero finite-dimensional complex vector space has an eigenvalue, and so does every complex $n \times n$ matrix with $n \ge 1$.

## Remarks

### Two ways to prove it

The short route goes through determinants.
The [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]] of $T$ has degree $\dim V \ge 1$, so it has a root in $F$, and every root is an eigenvalue; the [[proof by the characteristic polynomial|JZQEV913ZV]] writes this out.
For $F = \mathbb{C}$, the root comes from the [[fundamental theorem of algebra|0JP0W07Y9A]].

The second route uses no determinant at all.
Take one nonzero vector $v$ and look at $v, Tv, T^2 v, \dots$.
These vectors become dependent after at most $\dim V + 1$ steps, so some nonzero polynomial $p$ kills $v$.
Split $p$ into linear factors, and one factor $T - rI$ must kill a nonzero vector.
This is [[Axler's proof without determinants|6TPH8WGRJJ]].

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The field must be algebraically closed.
Over $\mathbb{R}$, the rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has the characteristic polynomial $t^2 + 1$, which has no real root, so the [[rotation of the plane|5AE98D7959]] has no real eigenvalue.
What survives over $\mathbb{R}$ is weaker: by the theorem on [[invariant subspaces of real operators|KAV44BRV77]], there is always an invariant line or an invariant plane.

The space must be nonzero.
On $V = 0$ there is no nonzero vector at all, so nothing can be an eigenvector.

The space must be finite-dimensional.
On $\mathbb{C}[x]$, the map $p(x) \mapsto x\, p(x)$ has no eigenvalue, because $x p = \lambda p$ with $p \ne 0$ would give $\deg p + 1 = \deg p$.
The [[right shift on sequences|HBVWZ0FXWC]] fails in the same way.

### What it gives you

The theorem starts many inductions on the dimension.
[[Triangularization|0363JMQ42Y]] picks an eigenvector, makes it the first basis vector, and repeats on a smaller space, so every complex square matrix is similar to an upper triangular one.
In the same way, every endomorphism of a nonzero finite-dimensional complex space has a one-dimensional [[invariant subspace|0ACX5QT8TE]], namely the line through an eigenvector.
A finite family of commuting complex matrices even has a common eigenvector, because each matrix maps the eigenspaces of the others into themselves.

### The converse

The converse is true.
Suppose that every endomorphism of every nonzero finite-dimensional space over $F$ has an eigenvalue.
A monic polynomial $f$ of degree $n \ge 1$ is the characteristic polynomial of its [[companion matrix|0ZCWPNZTQ3]], so an eigenvalue of that matrix is a root of $f$.
So every nonconstant polynomial over $F$ has a root, and $F$ is algebraically closed.

### Generalizations

In infinite dimension, eigenvalues may fail to exist, and the right replacement is the [[spectrum of an operator|XXXSST2DR5]].
A bounded operator on a nonzero complex Banach space always has a nonempty spectrum, but it can have no eigenvalue, as the right shift shows.
A compact self-adjoint operator on a nonzero Hilbert space does have an eigenvalue, and this fact starts the spectral theorem for compact operators.

## History

For complex matrices the result has long been read off from the fundamental theorem of algebra, applied to the characteristic polynomial.
The determinant-free proof was published by Sheldon Axler in his article "Down with Determinants!" in the American Mathematical Monthly, and it is the route of his book "Linear Algebra Done Right".

## Explanations

### L1 human

An eigenvector is a direction that a matrix only stretches.
Over the real numbers such a direction can fail to exist: a quarter turn of the plane moves every line.
Over the complex numbers this never happens.
The reason is that the eigenvalues are the roots of a polynomial, and every complex polynomial of degree at least $1$ has a root.
For the quarter turn the polynomial is $t^2 + 1$, and its complex roots $i$ and $-i$ are the eigenvalues.

### L2 human

Here is a typical computation: find a complex eigenvector of the real rotation $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
The characteristic polynomial is
$$\det(tI - A) = \det \begin{pmatrix} t & 1 \\ -1 & t \end{pmatrix} = t^2 + 1 = (t - i)(t + i).$$
So $i$ is an eigenvalue over $\mathbb{C}$.
The first row of $A - iI$ is $(-i, -1)$, so an eigenvector $v = (v_1, v_2)$ needs $-i v_1 - v_2 = 0$, that is, $v_2 = -i v_1$.
Take $v = (1, -i)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
Check:
$$Av = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 1 + (-1)(-i) \\ 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot (-i) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} i \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = i \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{pmatrix}.$$
Watch out: the matrix is real, but its eigenvector has entries that are not real, and no real eigenvector exists.
In proofs the theorem is used in this way: we pick one eigenvector and then work on a smaller space.

### L3 human

Make $V$ into a module over $F[x]$, with $x$ acting as $T$.
The theorem says that a nonzero finite-dimensional module of this kind contains a simple submodule $F[x]/(x - \lambda)$, because the simple modules over $F[x]$ are the quotients by irreducible polynomials, and over an algebraically closed field these are linear.
Axler's proof is exactly this argument: the annihilator of one vector is a nonzero ideal, and a linear factor of its generator acts with a kernel.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ the irreducible polynomials have degree $1$ or $2$, and the same reasoning gives the [[invariant planes of real operators|KAV44BRV77]].
