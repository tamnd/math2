## Statement

Let $F$ be an [[algebraically closed field|02SJG1HC32]], let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$ with $V \ne 0$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be a linear map.
Then $T$ has at least one [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] in $F$.
In particular, every endomorphism of a nonzero finite-dimensional complex vector space, and every complex square matrix of size $n \ge 1$, has an eigenvalue.

## Remarks

Two proofs are given: the [[proof by the characteristic polynomial|JZQEV913ZV]], and [[Axler's proof without determinants|6TPH8WGRJJ]].
For $F = \C$, the field is algebraically closed by the [[fundamental theorem of algebra|0JP0W07Y9A]].

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $F$ must be algebraically closed. Over $\R$, the [[rotation of the plane by 90 degrees|5AE98D7959]] has no real eigenvalue. Over $\R$ there is a weaker result, the theorem on [[invariant subspaces of real operators|KAV44BRV77]].
- $V$ must be nonzero. On $V = 0$ there is no nonzero vector, so there is no eigenvector.
- $V$ must be finite-dimensional. The map $p(x) \mapsto x\, p(x)$ on $\C[x]$ has no eigenvalue, because $x p = \lambda p$ with $p \ne 0$ compares degrees $\deg p + 1 = \deg p$. The [[right shift on sequences|HBVWZ0FXWC]] also has no eigenvalue.

### Consequences

- Every complex square matrix is similar to an upper triangular matrix, by [[triangularisation|0363JMQ42Y]], which uses this theorem in each step of an induction.
- Every endomorphism of a nonzero finite-dimensional complex space has a one-dimensional [[invariant subspace|0ACX5QT8TE]].
- Commuting complex matrices have a common eigenvector.

### Converse

If every endomorphism of every nonzero finite-dimensional $F$-space has an eigenvalue, then $F$ is algebraically closed.
The reason is that a monic polynomial $f$ of degree $n \ge 1$ is the characteristic polynomial of its [[companion matrix|0ZCWPNZTQ3]], so an eigenvalue of that matrix is a root of $f$.

### Generalizations

- A compact self-adjoint operator on a nonzero Hilbert space has an eigenvalue. This is part of the spectral theorem for compact operators.
- A bounded operator on a nonzero complex Banach space has nonempty [[spectrum|XXXSST2DR5]], but it can have no eigenvalue.

## History

For complex matrices, the result follows from the fundamental theorem of algebra, applied to the characteristic polynomial.
The determinant-free proof is due to Axler, in his paper "Down with Determinants!" in the American Mathematical Monthly and in his book "Linear Algebra Done Right".

## Explanations

### L1 human

Over the complex numbers, every square matrix has at least one eigenvector.
The reason is that every nonconstant complex polynomial has a root.
Over the real numbers, a rotation can move every line, so no real eigenvector exists.

### L2 human

We find an eigenvalue of the real rotation $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\C$.

1. $p_A(t) = t^2 + 1$, which has no real root.
2. Over $\C$, $t^2 + 1 = (t - i)(t + i)$, so $i$ is an eigenvalue.
3. Solve $(A - iI)v = 0$: the first row $(-i, -1)$ gives $v_2 = -i v_1$, so $v = (1, -i)^{\mathsf{T}}$.
4. Check: $Av = (i, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $iv = (i, -i^2)^{\mathsf{T}} = (i, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$.

The typical use is to start an induction on the dimension, as in the proof of triangularisation and of the spectral theorem.
A trap: the eigenvector can have nonreal entries even when the matrix is real.

### L3 human

The theorem says that a nonzero finite-dimensional $\C[x]$-module has a simple submodule $\C[x]/(x - \lambda)$.
Axler's proof shows this directly: the annihilator of a vector is a nonzero ideal, its generator splits into linear factors, and one factor fails to act injectively.
