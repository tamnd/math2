## Example

Three towns stand on a road, and from each town you see that town and the next one.
This picture is a reflexive frame that is not transitive, and it refutes axiom $\mathrm{4}$.
We use it to show that the logics $\mathsf{T}$ and $\mathsf{K4}$ of [[modal logics T, D and K4|XVYMEZKH7R]] are incomparable.
The model is this:
$$W = \{ 1, 2, 3 \}, \quad R = \{ (1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3), (1, 2), (2, 3) \}, \quad V(p) = \{ 1, 2 \}.$$

1. The frame is [[reflexive|0X8NMST0J2]], so it validates $\mathsf{T}$, by the [[common modal axiom schemata|FDFQFWPM4X]] and [[soundness|E87G6ZTMTN]].
2. The frame is not [[transitive|0GJZW9JFZQ]], because $1 R 2$ and $2 R 3$ hold and $1 R 3$ fails.
3. The successor sets are $R[1] = \{ 1, 2 \}$, $R[2] = \{ 2, 3 \}$ and $R[3] = \{ 3 \}$.
4. $\square p$ is true at $1$, because both $1$ and $2$ have $p$.
5. $\square p$ is false at $2$, because the successor $3$ lacks $p$.
6. $\square\square p$ is false at $1$, because the successor $2$ makes $\square p$ false.
7. So $\square p \to \square\square p$ fails at $1$, and $\mathsf{T}$ does not prove $\mathrm{4}$.

For the other direction, a single world is enough.

8. Take one world $w$ with the empty relation and $V(p) = \varnothing$.
   The empty relation is transitive, so the frame validates $\mathsf{K4}$.
9. At $w$, $\square p$ is true, since $w$ has no successors, and $p$ is false.
   So $\mathsf{K4}$ does not prove $\mathrm{T}$.
10. Neither logic contains the other.

## Remarks

The example shows that reflexivity and transitivity are independent, and so are the axioms $\mathrm{T}$ and $\mathrm{4}$.
The smallest normal logic that contains both is the [[modal logic S4|N60RVXSA5W]], the logic of reflexive transitive frames.
Steps 7 and 9 are the witnesses for $\mathsf{T} \subsetneq \mathsf{S4}$ and $\mathsf{K4} \subsetneq \mathsf{S4}$.

What changes if you change the frame?
If you add the pair $(1, 3)$, the frame becomes a preorder, $3$ becomes a successor of $1$, and $\square p$ becomes false at $1$, so this valuation no longer refutes $\mathrm{4}$.
The world $3$ is needed, because on the worlds $1$ and $2$ alone the relation is already a preorder.

The same frame refutes the dual form $\lozenge\lozenge p \to \lozenge p$ at $1$, with $V(p) = \{ 3 \}$.
At $2$ the successor $3$ has $p$, so $\lozenge p$ is true at $2$ and $\lozenge\lozenge p$ is true at $1$.
The successors $1$ and $2$ of $1$ lack $p$, so $\lozenge p$ is false at $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Picture three towns on a road, where from each town you see that town and the next one.
From town 1 you see towns 1 and 2, and from town 2 you see towns 2 and 3.
If it is sunny in towns 1 and 2, then in town 1 "everywhere I see, it is sunny" is true.
In town 2 the same sentence is false, since town 3 is cloudy.
So town 1 cannot say "everywhere I see, everyone sees only sun", and axiom 4 fails there.

### L2 human

The frame is the smallest reflexive frame that is not transitive, so it is the first thing to try when you want to separate $\mathsf{T}$ from a logic with $\mathrm{4}$.
Reflexivity does give one law that looks like $\mathrm{4}$.
The instance $\square\square p \to \square p$ of $\mathrm{T}$, with $\square p$ in place of $p$, is a theorem of $\mathsf{T}$.
Check it at $1$ in the model above: $\square\square p$ is false at $1$, so the implication holds there.
Watch out: this is the converse of $\mathrm{4}$, not $\mathrm{4}$ itself, so reflexivity buys you $\square\square p \to \square p$ and nothing in the other direction.
