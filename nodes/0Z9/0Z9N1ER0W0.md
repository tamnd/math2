## Statement

Let $F$ be a field and let $V$ and $W$ be [[finite-dimensional|018W3V3TCT]] vector spaces over $F$.
Then the [[space of linear maps|0VPCJWGMP9]] $\Hom_F(V, W)$ is finite-dimensional, and
$$\dim \Hom_F(V, W) = \dim V \cdot \dim W,$$
where $\dim$ is the [[dimension|05G2G0P224]].
If $(b_j)$ and $(c_i)$ are bases of $V$ and $W$, the maps $E_{ij}$ with $E_{ij}(b_k) = \delta_{jk} c_i$ form a basis of $\Hom_F(V, W)$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- Both spaces must be finite-dimensional for the product formula in natural numbers.
- If $V$ has an infinite basis indexed by $I$, then $\Hom(V, F) \cong F^I$, the space of all families $(a_i)_{i \in I}$. Its dimension is strictly larger than $\dim V$.
- If $W = \{0\}$, then $\Hom(V, W) = \{0\}$, which agrees with $\dim V \cdot 0 = 0$ for every finite-dimensional $V$.

### Consequences

- With $n = \dim V$ and $m = \dim W$, $\Hom_F(V, W) \cong M_{m \times n}(F)$, by the [[classification by dimension|0VJBSK15SN]]. The [[matrix of a linear map|0YQSGNYE3K]] gives one such isomorphism.
- The [[dual space|07JBSZE0V4]] $V^*$ has dimension $\dim V$.
- $\End(V)$ has dimension $n^2$. So for each $T \in \End(V)$, the $n^2 + 1$ maps $\id, T, T^2, \dots, T^{n^2}$ are linearly dependent, and $T$ satisfies a nonzero polynomial.

### Converse

The formula does not determine $V$ and $W$ separately.
For example, $\Hom(F^2, F^3)$ and $\Hom(F^6, F)$ both have dimension $6$, so they are isomorphic.

### Generalizations

- For finitely generated free modules over a commutative ring, $\Hom_R(R^n, R^m)$ is free of rank $mn$, with the same basis $E_{ij}$.
- In finite dimension, $\Hom(V, W) \cong V^* \otimes W$, and the formula becomes $\dim(V^* \otimes W) = \dim V \cdot \dim W$.

## History

Cayley introduced matrix notation in 1857, and an $m \times n$ matrix has $mn$ entries.
Peano gave the modern definition of vector spaces and linear maps in 1888.
The theorem is the basis-free form of the count of matrix entries.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear map from an $n$-dimensional space to an $m$-dimensional space is described by an $m \times n$ matrix.
A matrix of this size has $mn$ entries, and each entry can be chosen freely.
So the space of these maps has dimension $mn$.

### L2 human

The maps from $\R^3$ to $\R^2$ form a space of dimension $3 \cdot 2 = 6$.
A basis is given by the six maps $E_{ij}$, whose matrices have one entry $1$ and all other entries $0$.
The trap is to add the dimensions instead of multiplying them.

### L3 human

The basis $(E_{ij})$ depends on the bases of $V$ and $W$, but the dimension does not.
In finite dimension, the canonical map $V^* \otimes W \to \Hom(V, W)$ is an isomorphism, and the $E_{ij}$ correspond to $b_j^* \otimes c_i$.
For infinite-dimensional $V$, this map is injective with image the maps of finite rank.
