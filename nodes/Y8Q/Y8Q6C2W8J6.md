## Statement

Let $S$ be a smooth oriented surface in $\R^3$, fixed in time, whose boundary $\partial S$ is a closed curve oriented compatibly with $S$.
Let $E$ be the [[electric field|YZ7Q9DP8RT]] and $B$ the [[magnetic field|XRED3C547P]], both $C^1$ in space and time.
Then the electromotive force around $\partial S$ equals minus the rate of change of the magnetic [[flux|026J1MG2NW]] through $S$:
$$\oint_{\partial S} E \cdot d\ell = - \frac{d}{dt} \int_S B \cdot n \, dA.$$

## Remarks

This is a law of physics, found by experiment, and it is one of [[Maxwell's equations|9BWWD4QK8Y]].
By [[Stokes' theorem|0AKRKVHW94]] the left side is the flux of $\nabla \times E$ through $S$, where $\nabla \times$ is the [[curl|0NF6GQNXMY]].
Since $S$ is arbitrary, the law is equivalent to the differential form $\nabla \times E = - \partial B / \partial t$.
The minus sign is Lenz's law: the induced current makes a field that opposes the change of flux.

For a circuit that moves or deforms, the electromotive force is the [[line integral|0H5MR9B6GT]] of $E + v \times B$, where $v$ is the velocity of the wire.
With this definition the flux rule $\mathcal{E} = -d\Phi/dt$ holds also for moving circuits, as long as the circuit is a well-defined closed curve moving smoothly.

Example: let a flat loop of area $a$ rotate with angular speed $\omega$ in a uniform field of size $B_0$.
If the axis lies in the plane of the loop and is perpendicular to the field, the flux is $\Phi(t) = B_0 a \cos \omega t$.
So the induced electromotive force is $B_0 a \omega \sin \omega t$, which is how an alternating current generator works.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Move a magnet near a loop of wire and a current flows in the loop.
The faster the amount of magnetic field through the loop changes, the stronger the push on the current.
It does not matter whether the magnet moves, the loop moves, or the field is switched on and off; only the change of the field through the loop counts.
Generators, transformers and electric guitar pickups all work this way.

### L2 human

In Maxwell's theory Faraday's law says that a time-varying magnetic field produces an electric field with nonzero curl.
So in electrodynamics $E$ is no longer the gradient of a scalar potential, and one writes $E = -\nabla \phi - \partial_t A$ with the [[magnetic vector potential|8PEMR2J7EF]] $A$.
Combined with the Ampère–Maxwell law it gives the electromagnetic wave equation.
With [[Gauss's law for magnetism|2K5D5HH89T]] it forms the homogeneous half of Maxwell's equations, which in the covariant formulation becomes the closedness of the field strength $2$-form.
