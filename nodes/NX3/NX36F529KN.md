## Definition

Consider an [[auction|86ZWXGHNGB]] of one object with $n \ge 2$ bidders.
The object has a common value $V$, a [[random variable|0PNRKMRC55]] with finite [[expectation|0WQNRCNY3M]].
Bidder $i$ observes a signal $X_i = V + \varepsilon_i$.
The errors $\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n$ are independent of $V$, independent of each other, identically distributed, not almost surely constant, and have mean $0$.
Let $W$ be the bidder with the highest signal, so that $X_W = \max_j X_j$.
Each signal is an unbiased estimate of the value: $\mathbb{E}[X_i - V] = 0$.
The *winner's curse* is the fact that the highest signal is biased upward:
$$\mathbb{E}[X_W - V] = \mathbb{E}\left[\max_j \varepsilon_j\right] > 0.$$
So a bidder who treats her signal as the value, and wins, pays on average more than the value of the object.

## Remarks

Example: let $n = 2$ and let $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2$ be uniform on $[-1, 1]$.
The maximum of two such variables has mean $1/3$.
So if both bidders bid their signals in a first-price auction, the winner pays $V + 1/3$ on average and loses $1/3$.

The positivity holds because $\max_j \varepsilon_j \ge \varepsilon_1$, with strict inequality on an event of positive probability, and $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_1] = 0$.
A rational bidder avoids the curse by bidding with the [[conditional expectation|0TTDGTEFMN]] of $V$ given her signal and given that her signal is the highest.

Non-example: in a private value auction each bidder knows her own value exactly, so the errors are all $0$.
The errors are then constant, the bias is $0$, and there is no winner's curse.

The term is also used for the empirical observation that real bidders in common value auctions often lose money.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In an auction for something whose worth is uncertain, the highest bidder is usually the most optimistic one.
The winner's curse names the gap between what the winner expected and what the object turns out to be worth.
A careful bidder asks: "how much is it worth, given that I won?"
