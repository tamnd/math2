## Example

Let
$$R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
so that $Re_1 = e_2$, $Re_2 = e_3$ and $Re_3 = e_1$.
We find the axis and the angle of $R$ with [[the classification of O(3)|2ZVG43GSKA]].

1. The columns of $R$ are $e_2$, $e_3$ and $e_1$, which are orthonormal, so $R$ is orthogonal.
2. Expand along the first row, whose only nonzero entry is the $1$ in position $(1, 3)$ with sign $(-1)^{1 + 3} = 1$:
$$\det R = 1 \cdot \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1.$$
So $R \in SO(3)$, and $R$ is a rotation about some axis.
3. The [[trace|0XC1RDBY7A]] is $\tr R = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$. The theorem gives $\tr R = 1 + 2\cos\theta$, so $\cos\theta = -\frac{1}{2}$ and $\theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}$.
4. For the direction of the axis, subtract the transpose:
$$R - R^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
5. The theorem says that the entries in positions $(3, 2)$, $(1, 3)$ and $(2, 1)$ are $2\sin\theta$ times the coordinates $u_1, u_2, u_3$ of the unit axis vector $u$. Here these entries are $1$, $1$ and $1$.
6. Since $2\sin\frac{2\pi}{3} = 2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \sqrt{3}$, we get $u = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1, 1, 1)$.
7. So $R$ is the rotation by $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ about the axis through $(1, 1, 1)$, counterclockwise when $u$ points toward the viewer.

Check: $R(1, 1, 1) = (1, 1, 1)$, so the axis is fixed, and $\norm{u} = \sqrt{3 \cdot \frac{1}{3}} = 1$.

## Remarks

The example uses three parts of [[the classification of O(3)|2ZVG43GSKA]]: the determinant decides that $R$ is a rotation, the trace gives the angle, and $R - R^{\mathsf{T}}$ gives the axis with its direction.

The angle fits the powers of $R$.
Each application moves $e_1 \mapsto e_2 \mapsto e_3 \mapsto e_1$, so $R^3 = I_3$, and three turns by $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ make one full turn.
$R$ permutes the coordinates, so it maps the cube with vertices $(\pm 1, \pm 1, \pm 1)$ to itself, and its axis passes through the opposite vertices $(1, 1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1, -1)$.

The same matrix appears elsewhere.
It is the product $R_z(\pi/2) R_x(\pi/2)$ of [[two basic rotations|F1KZ5GE4TC]], and it is the rotation of the unit quaternion $\frac{1}{2}(1 + i + j + k)$, as [[the quaternion example|Z7AEM725B9]] shows.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix moves the $x$-axis to the $y$-axis, the $y$-axis to the $z$-axis, and the $z$-axis back to the $x$-axis.
Look at a cube along its long diagonal, through the corner $(1, 1, 1)$.
The three axes then look like three spokes of a wheel, $120$ degrees apart.
The matrix turns the wheel by one third of a full turn.

### L2 human

Here is the typical use: these steps find the axis and the angle of any rotation matrix with $0 < \theta < \pi$.
Read the angle from the trace, and read the axis from the entries of $R - R^{\mathsf{T}}$.
For $\theta = \pi$ the matrix $R - R^{\mathsf{T}}$ is zero, and then the axis is the kernel of $R - I$.
Watch out: the transpose $R^{\mathsf{T}}$ has the same trace and the opposite axis vector $-u$, so it is the rotation by $-\frac{2\pi}{3}$ about $u$, not the same rotation.
