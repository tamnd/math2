## Summary

Generalization replaces a problem by a wider one that contains it, in the hope that the wider problem is easier.
The usual move is to turn a fixed number into a parameter, because a parameter can be varied, differentiated or used in an induction.

Here is a problem that resists every direct attack:
$$I = \int_0^1 \frac{x - 1}{\ln x} \, dx.$$
Substitution and integration by parts lead nowhere, since the integrand has no antiderivative in elementary functions.
The helping move is to notice that the $1$ in the exponent of $x = x^1$ is just one value of a parameter $t$.
So we study the whole family
$$I(t) = \int_0^1 \frac{x^t - 1}{\ln x} \, dx, \qquad t \ge 0.$$
The given integral is $I(1)$, and $I(0) = 0$ since the integrand is then $0$.

The parameter gives us a new tool.
The derivative of $x^t$ in $t$ is $x^t \ln x$, so differentiating under the integral sign cancels the awkward $\ln x$:
$$I'(t) = \int_0^1 \frac{x^t \ln x}{\ln x} \, dx = \int_0^1 x^t \, dx = \frac{1}{t + 1}.$$
With $I(0) = 0$ this gives $I(t) = \ln(t + 1)$, and so
$$I = I(1) = \ln 2 \approx 0.693.$$
The step is justified by [[differentiation under the integral sign|0AR06W1E1B]]: for $0 < x < 1$ and $t \ge 0$ the derivative $x^t$ lies between $0$ and $1$, and this bound lets the derivative pass inside the integral.

In general, generalization as a heuristic solves a more general problem and then specializes back to the given case.
It is advice, not a procedure: some generalizations are easier than the given problem, and some are false or hopeless.

## Motivation

A problem with a fixed number such as $\frac{1}{2}$ or $100$ gives you nothing to vary.
Replace the number by a parameter, and the problem becomes a function of the parameter, so the tools for functions become available.
The given problem is then one value of that function, as $I$ was the value $I(1)$.

The general problem is a stronger statement, so it may seem harder.
Often it is easier, because the parameter shows the structure, and this effect is the [[inventor's paradox|FVRKDXBJF6]].
The same move runs through mathematics: a sum becomes a power series, a number becomes $n$ in an induction, and a theorem about one space becomes a theorem about a class of spaces.
The broader form of the move, for definitions and whole theories, is [[abstraction and generalization|976RZZCT1H]].

## Notation

Pólya's term is "generalization", and he described the passage to a wider class of objects that contains the given one.
Differentiation with respect to a parameter inside an integral is also called the Leibniz integral rule, and physicists often call it Feynman's trick.
Write the parameter so that the given problem is visible as one value, as in $I = I(1)$, so that the reader sees where you will specialize.

## Remarks

### Three ways to bring in a parameter

The exponent in an integral can become a parameter.
In [[the integral of x times ln x squared by a parameter|3C8ZS1TPAC]], two derivatives of $\int_0^1 x^t \, dx = \frac{1}{t + 1}$ give $\int_0^1 x (\ln x)^2 \, dx = \frac{1}{4}$.

A base in a series can become a variable.
In [[the sum of k over 2 to the k by a power series|QZQT0EN6K3]], the number $\frac{1}{2}$ becomes $x$, and the derivative of the geometric series gives $\sum k x^k = \frac{x}{(1 - x)^2}$, so the sum is $2$.

A specific number can become $n$.
To show that $3$ divides $2^{100} - 1$, prove that $3$ divides $4^n - 1$ for every $n$:
$$4^n - 1 = (4 - 1)(4^{n-1} + 4^{n-2} + \cdots + 1),$$
and then take $n = 50$, since $4^{50} = 2^{100}$.

### Choosing the right generalization

The right generalization keeps the features that the method uses and drops the rest.
Too narrow a generalization gives no new tool.
Too wide a generalization is false or hopeless: "find $\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(k)$ for every function $f$" has no answer, while "find $\sum_{k=1}^{n} k$" has the answer $\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}$.
In induction, the right generalization is often a sharper bound, as in [[strengthening the induction hypothesis|F94KJC2RXP]], where $\sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{k^2} \le 2 - \frac{1}{n}$ is easier to prove than the weaker "$< 2$".

### When generalization misleads

A pattern in a parameter may not continue.
In [[the quintic has no formula like the quadratic formula|QYK67FE6MY]], formulas for degrees $2$, $3$ and $4$ suggest a formula for every degree, and there is none for degree $5$.

A generalization can also lose the property that the proof uses.
Every bounded sequence in $\mathbb{R}^n$ has a convergent subsequence.
In an infinite-dimensional normed space this fails: the unit sphere contains a sequence with no convergent subsequence.
When a general claim turns out false, the given special case may still be true, and the lesson is only that the generalization was a poor choice.

### Watch out

The common error is to specialize too early.
To find $\sum_{k=0}^{n} k \binom{n}{k}$, a student may put $x = 1$ in $(1 + x)^n$ at once and get the number $2^n$, which cannot be differentiated usefully.
The correct order is to differentiate first and specialize last, as in the L2 explanation below.

A second error is to use the new tool without its conditions.
Differentiation under the integral sign needs a bound on the derivative, and without it the exchange of derivative and integral can fail.

### History

Pólya's heuristic dictionary in *How to Solve It* (1945) has entries on generalization and on the inventor's paradox.
Leibniz used differentiation with respect to a parameter, and Richard Feynman made the method popular among physicists.
In algebra, the search for a formula in radicals for every degree ended with the theorem of Ruffini (1799) and Abel (1824) that none exists for degree $5$.

### Try it

First, compute $\int_0^1 \frac{x^2 - 1}{\ln x} \, dx$.
The family above gives $I(2) = \ln 3$.

Second, find $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k}{3^k}$.
The formula $\sum k x^k = \frac{x}{(1 - x)^2}$ at $x = \frac{1}{3}$ gives $\frac{1/3}{4/9} = \frac{3}{4}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Sometimes a question about one number is easier as a question about all numbers.
What is $1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + 100$?
Ask instead for $1 + 2 + \cdots + n$, and write the sum twice, once forward and once backward.
Each column, such as $1 + n$ or $2 + (n - 1)$, adds to $n + 1$, and there are $n$ columns, so twice the sum is $n(n + 1)$.
For $n = 100$ the sum is $\frac{100 \cdot 101}{2} = 5050$.
The letter $n$ made the pattern easy to see.

### L2 human

We find $\sum_{k=0}^{n} k \binom{n}{k}$, which has no parameter to work with.
Put a variable $x$ into the binomial theorem:
$$(1 + x)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^k.$$
Differentiate both sides in $x$:
$$n(1 + x)^{n-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} k \binom{n}{k} x^{k-1}.$$
Now specialize to $x = 1$, which gives $\sum_{k=0}^{n} k \binom{n}{k} = n 2^{n-1}$.
Check with $n = 3$: $0 + 3 + 2 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 1 = 12$ and $3 \cdot 2^2 = 12$.

This is the typical use: the variable $x$ made differentiation available, and a factor $k$ in the terms is the trace of a derivative of $x^k$.
Watch out: the variable must stay until the tool has been used, and only then do you put $x = 1$.

### L3 human

In research, generalization is a main way to find the right setting for a theorem.
A result about the real line becomes a result about metric spaces, and the proof shows which hypotheses matter.
Grothendieck's theory of schemes and étale cohomology gave the setting in which Deligne proved the Weil conjectures in 1974.

The opposite case also occurs, where the special case is true and the general statement is false or out of reach, as in [[Fermat's Last Theorem for n = 4|672544QRJC]] against the general theorem.
Pólya advised trying generalization and [[specialization|FTBJX4G51S]] in turn, and judging a generalization by the tool it gives.
[[Analogy|7KDT0R1HF2]] often comes first, when two analogous problems are seen as cases of one, and [[variation of the problem|KT5SASGTQ5]] counts generalization as one of its moves.
