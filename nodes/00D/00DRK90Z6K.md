## Statement

Let $G$ be a [[group|G5S1M9]] with identity element $e$, and let $a \in G$.
For an integer $n$, define the power $a^n$ as follows: $a^0 = e$, $a^{n+1} = a^n a$ for $n \geq 0$, and $a^{-n} = (a^{-1})^n$ for $n > 0$.
Then the following laws hold for all integers $m$ and $n$.

1. $a^m a^n = a^{m+n}$.
2. $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$.
3. $(a^n)^{-1} = a^{-n}$, that is, the [[inverse|0ZNZH0EXFX]] of $a^n$ is $a^{-n}$.
4. If $b \in G$ and $ab = ba$, then $(ab)^n = a^n b^n$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Law 4 needs the hypothesis $ab = ba$.
In the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$, with composition from right to left, let $a = (1\,2)$ and $b = (1\,3)$.
Then $ab = (1\,3\,2)$, so $(ab)^2 = (1\,3\,2)(1\,3\,2) = (1\,2\,3)$.
But $a^2 = e$ and $b^2 = e$, so $a^2 b^2 = e \neq (ab)^2$.
For $n = -1$, law 4 would say $(ab)^{-1} = a^{-1} b^{-1}$, and this is also false in $S_3$, by [[uniqueness of identity and inverses|0D9NDGE9KP]].

### Consequences

1. Two powers of the same element commute: $a^m a^n = a^{m+n} = a^n a^m$.
2. The set $\{a^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ of all powers of $a$ is closed under products and inverses, by laws 1 and 3. It is the cyclic [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] generated by $a$.
3. Law 1 says that the map $\mathbb{Z} \to G$, $n \mapsto a^n$, is a [[group homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]] from $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ to $G$.
4. In an [[abelian group|082GHNPJ53]], law 4 holds for all $a$ and $b$.
5. In additive notation the power $a^n$ is the multiple $na$, and the laws are $ma + na = (m + n)a$, $n(ma) = (nm)a$, $-(na) = (-n)a$ and $n(a + b) = na + nb$.

### Converse

For $n = 2$, the converse of law 4 holds: if $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$, then $ab = ba$.
Indeed, $abab = aabb$, and cancellation of $a$ on the left and $b$ on the right gives $ba = ab$, by the [[cancellation laws|0VSNX4P2Q0]].
So a group in which $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$ for all $a$ and $b$ is abelian.

### Generalizations

In a [[monoid|0N6R902FG1]], the powers $a^n$ are defined for $n \geq 0$, and laws 1, 2 and 4 hold for $m, n \geq 0$ with the same proof.
In a [[semigroup|0XMEM406JM]], they hold for $m, n \geq 1$.
Law 4 for [[commuting elements|YJRBCWQV6B]] is the reason that the binomial theorem holds for commuting elements of a ring.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The powers of an element of a group follow the rules that you know for the powers of a nonzero number.
The power $a^3$ means $a a a$, and $a^0$ is the identity.
A negative power means a power of the inverse, so $a^{-2} = a^{-1} a^{-1}$.
For example, $a^3 a^{-1} = a a a a^{-1} = a a = a^2$.
One rule needs care: $(ab)^2 = abab$, and this is $aabb$ only when $ab = ba$.

### L2 human

The typical use is to compute a high power or a negative power with less work.
Let $a = (1\,2\,3)$ in $S_3$.

1. We have $a^2 = (1\,3\,2)$ and $a^3 = e$.
2. By law 1, $a^{10} = a^{9} a$, and by law 2, $a^9 = (a^3)^3 = e^3 = e$. So $a^{10} = a$.
3. By law 3, $a^{-5} = (a^5)^{-1}$. By law 1, $a^5 = a^3 a^2 = a^2$.
4. Since $a^2 a = a^3 = e$, the inverse of $a^2$ is $a$. So $a^{-5} = a$.
5. Check with law 1: $a^{-5} a^5 = a^0 = e$, and indeed $a \cdot a^2 = e$.

A trap is to use law 4 without checking that $a$ and $b$ commute.
A second trap is to apply the rules for real numbers that use addition, such as $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$: a group has only one operation.

### L3 human

When $G$ is abelian, laws 1, 2 and 4 make $G$ a module over the ring $\mathbb{Z}$, with $n \cdot a = a^n$.
For a general group, law 1 says that each element $a$ gives a unique homomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to G$ with $1 \mapsto a$, so $\mathbb{Z}$ is the [[free group|0KTW26C80J]] on one generator.
The kernel of this homomorphism is $k\mathbb{Z}$, where $k$ is the [[order of the element|0WE2NHZJ7B]] $a$, or $k = 0$ when this order is infinite.
Law 4 fails for general groups, and the failure is measured by the commutator $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$, which later sub-areas study.
