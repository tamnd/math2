## Statement

Kleene's negation, conjunction and disjunction cannot express Łukasiewicz's implication, even with the constants $0$ and $1$, while Łukasiewicz's connectives can express Kleene's.

On $V = \{0, \tfrac12, 1\}$ let $\neg x = 1 - x$, $x \land y = \min(x, y)$ and $x \lor y = \max(x, y)$, and let $x \to_{Ł} y = \min(1, 1 - x + y)$.
A [[formula|TFDYXS3K1N]] $\varphi(p, q)$ *defines* a function $f \colon V^2 \to V$ when $\varphi$ takes the value $f(x, y)$ at $p = x$, $q = y$, for all $x, y \in V$.

**Theorem.**

1. No formula built from $p$ and $q$ with $\neg$, $\land$ and $\lor$ defines $\to_{Ł}$.
2. This stays true when the constants $0$ and $1$ may also be used.
3. Conversely, $\land$ and $\lor$ are definable from $\neg$ and $\to_{Ł}$, by $x \lor y = (x \to_{Ł} y) \to_{Ł} y$ and $x \land y = \neg(\neg x \lor \neg y)$.

The proof is in [[the proof by an invariant|GWPJHN5CNB]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The third value is essential.
On $\{0, 1\}$ the implication is definable as $\neg x \lor y$, so the theorem says something only because of the value $\tfrac12$.
The particular implication matters too.
Kleene's own implication $\max(1 - x, y)$ is defined by $\neg p \lor q$, and the theorem is about the single entry $\tfrac12 \to_{Ł} \tfrac12 = 1$, where the two implications differ.
Allowing $0$ and $1$ as constants does not rescue definability, because the obstacle in item 2 is an order on the values that the constants also respect.

### What it gives you

The language of [[Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic|1CE2TGM6MQ]] is strictly more expressive than that of [[strong Kleene logic|SZHA10A9X7]] on the same values.
This explains the difference in their laws.
$\mathsf{K}_3$ has no tautologies, while $\mathrm{Ł}_3$ has $p \to_{Ł} p$, and item 1 shows that no Kleene formula can take over its role.

### The converse

Item 3 is the converse direction: the Kleene connectives are definable in $\mathrm{Ł}_3$.
So the Kleene language is a fragment of the Łukasiewicz language, and not the other way round.

### Generalizations

The proof of item 1 shows more: no function $f$ with $f(\tfrac12, \dots, \tfrac12) \neq \tfrac12$ is definable from $\neg$, $\land$ and $\lor$ without constants.
With the constants $0$ and $1$, every definable function is monotone for the order of information, in which $\tfrac12$ lies below $0$ and below $1$.
Every definable function also maps classical arguments to classical values.
So the constant function $\tfrac12$ is monotone but still not definable, and monotonicity alone does not characterize the definable functions.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Kleene's "not", "and" and "or" turn the middle value into the middle value.
A formula built from them alone therefore gives the middle value when all its letters have the middle value.
Łukasiewicz's "if $p$ then $p$" gives full truth there.
So it cannot be written with Kleene's connectives.

### L2 human

Try the obvious candidate $\neg p \lor q$ and evaluate it at $p = q = \tfrac12$.
The result is $\max(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) = \tfrac12$, while $\tfrac12 \to_{Ł} \tfrac12 = 1$, so this candidate fails.
The proof shows that every candidate fails at the same point.
The typical use of such results is to compare the expressive power of two sets of connectives on the same values.
Watch out: adding the constants $0$ and $1$ does not help, because of item 2.
