## Summary

A martensitic transformation is a solid-to-solid phase change in which the crystal lattice changes its shape by a coordinated shift of atoms, without diffusion.
A high-symmetry phase, the austenite, changes into a low-symmetry phase, the martensite, which exists in several symmetry-related variants.
The variants form fine twinned microstructures that fit together with the austenite, and these patterns are studied as energy-minimizing sequences in nonlinear elasticity.
The transformation is behind the hardening of steel by quenching and behind the shape-memory effect of alloys such as nickel-titanium.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When hot steel is plunged into cold water, its iron atoms have no time to wander and rearrange.
Instead whole blocks of the crystal shear into a new shape at once, like a deck of cards pushed sideways.
The new phase, called martensite, can form in several orientations, and these orientations fit together in fine stripes, called twins.
In some alloys, heating reverses the change, and the metal snaps back into its original shape.
