## Example

Let
$$U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
over the real numbers.
Each has one feature of a nilpotent matrix, and neither is [[nilpotent|0419GJC84P]].

1. The matrix $U$ is upper triangular, like a strictly upper triangular matrix, but its diagonal entries are $1$ and $1$.
2. We claim $U^k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ for all $k \ge 1$. It holds for $k = 1$, and if it holds for $k$, then
$$U^{k+1} = U^k U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k + 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
3. So every power of $U$ has the entry $1$ in position $(1, 1)$, and no power of $U$ is $0$.
4. The matrix $S$ has zero diagonal and trace $0$, but its square is
$$S^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 & 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 \\ 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = I.$$
5. Hence $S^{2j} = I$ and $S^{2j+1} = S$ for all $j \ge 0$, and no power of $S$ is $0$.
6. So neither $U$ nor $S$ is nilpotent.

## Remarks

The two failures point to two different missing conditions.
For a triangular matrix, the diagonal must be zero as well; $U = I + E_{12}$ is the identity plus a nilpotent matrix, which is called a *unipotent* matrix.
For a matrix with zero diagonal, the shape must also be triangular, or some other reason must kill the powers; $S = E_{12} + E_{21}$ is a sum of two nilpotent matrices that do not commute.

Both matrices are [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]], with $\det U = 1$ and $\det S = -1$, and an invertible matrix is never nilpotent.
If we replace the diagonal of $U$ by $0, 0$, we get $E_{12}$, which is nilpotent.
If we replace the lower $1$ of $S$ by $0$, we again get $E_{12}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A triangle of numbers with zeros below the diagonal is not enough to make a matrix vanish under powers: the diagonal must be zero too.
A zero diagonal is not enough either.
The swap matrix $S$ exchanges two coordinates, and swapping twice gives back what we started with, never zero.

### L2 human

The determinant gives a quick negative test: a nilpotent matrix has determinant $0$, since $(\det N)^k = \det N^k = 0$.
Here $\det U = 1$ and $\det S = -1$, so neither can be nilpotent.
Watch out: the test only works one way, since $\operatorname{diag}(1, 0)$ has determinant $0$ and is not nilpotent.
