## Example

Let $U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $U$ is upper triangular, like a strictly upper triangular matrix, but its diagonal is $1, 1$.
2. By induction, $U^k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, since $U^{k+1} = U^k U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k + 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. No power of $U$ is $0$.
3. $S$ has zero diagonal and trace $0$, but $S^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = I$.
4. So $S^{2j} = I$ and $S^{2j+1} = S$, and no power of $S$ is $0$.
5. Neither matrix is [[nilpotent|0419GJC84P]].

## Remarks

$U = I + E_{12}$ is $I$ plus a nilpotent matrix, a *unipotent* matrix.
$S = E_{12} + E_{21}$ is a sum of two nilpotent matrices that do not commute.
Both matrices are [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]], and an invertible matrix is never nilpotent.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A triangular shape is not enough: the diagonal must be zero too.
A zero diagonal is not enough either.

### L2 human

The determinant of a nilpotent matrix is $0$, and here $\det U = 1$ and $\det S = -1$.
This test is quick, but a determinant $0$ does not prove nilpotency.
