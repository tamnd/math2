## Summary

An involutory matrix is a matrix that undoes itself: applying it twice brings every vector back to where it started.
Reflections are the model, since a mirror image of a mirror image is the original.
Exactly, a [[square matrix|0JXZKX526R]] $A$ is *involutory* when
$$A^2 = I,$$
so that $A$ is invertible and $A^{-1} = A$; the [[formal definition|1GVCRBXQT8]] states this over any ring.
Outside characteristic $2$, $A$ is involutory exactly when $P = \tfrac12 (I + A)$ is [[idempotent|0KCE8CG86P]], so involutions and projections come in matching pairs.

## Motivation

Many natural operations are their own inverses: changing the sign of a coordinate, exchanging two coordinates, reflecting in a line or a plane.
Their matrices satisfy $A^2 = I$, and this one equation captures the geometry.
For example, a reflection fixes a mirror and reverses the directions across it, and the equation $A^2 = I$ forces exactly this split into a $+1$ part and a $-1$ part.

The link with projections makes involutions easy to build and to understand.
A projection $P$ keeps one part of a vector and erases the other, and $2P - I$ keeps the first part and reverses the second.
In numerical linear algebra the Householder reflections $I - 2 u u^{\mathsf{T}}$, for a unit vector $u$, are involutory, and they are the basic tool of the QR factorization.
Later, the sub-area on eigenvalues shows that an involutory matrix is diagonalizable with eigenvalues $\pm 1$ when $2 \ne 0$.

## Notation

An involutory matrix is also called an *involution*, especially when we think of the map rather than the matrix.
There is no special symbol; books write $A^2 = I$ or $A = A^{-1}$.
In Mathlib the condition is written `A * A = 1`.
Do not confuse *involutory* ($A^2 = I$) with *idempotent* ($A^2 = A$) or with *nilpotent* ($A^k = 0$); the three words name three different equations on powers.

## Remarks

### Three reflections

The matrix $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ sends $(x, y)$ to $(y, x)$.
Its square is
$$S^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 & 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 \\ 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = I,$$
so [[the matrix that exchanges two coordinates is involutory|28TDNCN9Z8]]; it is the reflection in the line $y = x$.

The diagonal matrix $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ sends $(x, y)$ to $(x, -y)$, and $A^2 = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1) = I$.
It is [[the reflection in the x-axis, with its projection (I + A)/2|8XT0ZBM0BN]].

A reflection need not be orthogonal.
For each real $c$, the matrix $A_c = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ c & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ has
$$A_c^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ c - c & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I.$$
So the equation $A^2 = I$ has infinitely many solutions, as [[a family of involutory 2 × 2 matrices with a free parameter|D20HHJ9CD6]] works out, while among real numbers only $\pm 1$ solve $x^2 = 1$.

### What it is not

The quarter turn $R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is invertible, but $R^2 = -I$, so it is not involutory; [[the rotation by a quarter turn is not involutory|HH1JX5YNJ1]] gives the details.
The projection $\operatorname{diag}(1, 0)$ is not involutory either: its square is itself, not $I$, and it is not even invertible.
A [[nilpotent matrix|07BSPZPSMF]] is never involutory, since it is not invertible.

### The rules you can use

Let $A$ be involutory over a field $F$.
Then $A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]] with $A^{-1} = A$.
The matrices $-A$, $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $Q^{-1} A Q$, for invertible $Q$, are involutory, since $(A^{\mathsf{T}})^2 = (A^2)^{\mathsf{T}} = I$ and $(Q^{-1} A Q)^2 = Q^{-1} A^2 Q = I$.
Each eigenvalue is $\pm 1$: from $A v = \lambda v$ with $v \ne 0$ we get $v = A^2 v = \lambda^2 v$, so $\lambda^2 = 1$.
For two involutory matrices $A$ and $B$, the product $AB$ is involutory if and only if $AB = BA$, because $ABAB = I$ means $AB = B^{-1} A^{-1} = BA$.

### Involutions and projections

Suppose $2 \ne 0$ in $F$.
If $A^2 = I$, then $P = \tfrac12 (I + A)$ satisfies
$$P^2 = \tfrac14 (I + 2A + A^2) = \tfrac14 (2I + 2A) = P.$$
Conversely, if $P^2 = P$, then $A = 2P - I$ satisfies $A^2 = 4P^2 - 4P + I = I$.
So every vector splits as $v = \tfrac12 (v + Av) + \tfrac12 (v - Av)$, a part fixed by $A$ plus a part that $A$ reverses.

### Watch out

The correspondence needs $2$ to be invertible.
Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ we have $-1 = 1$, so $\operatorname{diag}(1, -1) = I$, and $\tfrac12 (I + A)$ makes no sense.
In characteristic $2$ the involutory matrices are exactly the matrices $I + N$ with $N^2 = 0$, since $(I + N)^2 = I + 2N + N^2 = I + N^2$ there.
For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is involutory over $\mathbb{F}_2$, but it is not diagonalizable.

### Try it

First, for which real $a$ is $\begin{pmatrix} a & 1 \\ 0 & -a \end{pmatrix}$ involutory?
Its square is $\begin{pmatrix} a^2 & a - a \\ 0 & a^2 \end{pmatrix} = a^2 I$, so exactly for $a = 1$ and $a = -1$.

Second, if $A$ is involutory, what is $(I + A)(I - A)$?
It is $I - A + A - A^2 = I - I = 0$, so the two projections $\tfrac12 (I \pm A)$ multiply to zero.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a light switch.
Pressing it once changes the state, and pressing it again brings back the old state.
An involutory matrix is a switch for vectors: the swap $(3, 5) \mapsto (5, 3)$ applied twice gives $(3, 5)$ again.
Mirrors work the same way, which is why reflections are the main examples.

### L2 human

Find the reflection $H$ of the plane in the line through $(1, 2)$.
A normal vector of the line is $n = (2, -1)$, with $n^{\mathsf{T}} n = 5$, and the reflection is $H = I - \tfrac{2}{5} n n^{\mathsf{T}}$:
$$H = I - \frac25 \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac15 \begin{pmatrix} -3 & 4 \\ 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check $H^2 = I$: the diagonal entries are $\tfrac{9 + 16}{25} = 1$, and the off-diagonal entries are $\tfrac{-12 + 12}{25} = 0$.
Also $H (1, 2) = \tfrac15 (-3 + 8, 4 + 6) = (1, 2)$ and $H (2, -1) = \tfrac15 (-6 - 4, 8 - 3) = (-2, 1)$.
So the line is fixed and its normal is reversed, and $\tfrac12 (I + H) = \tfrac15 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ is the projection onto the line.

### L3 human

The involutory matrices in $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ are its elements of order $1$ or $2$.
When $2 \ne 0$, each is diagonalizable with eigenvalues $\pm 1$, so its similarity class is fixed by the dimension $p$ of the $+1$ space.
Over a field of characteristic $0$ the trace $p - (n - p) = 2p - n$ determines $p$, so two involutory matrices of the same size are similar exactly when they have the same trace.
In characteristic $p > 2$ the trace is known only modulo $p$, and the test fails: over $\mathbb{F}_3$, the $3 \times 3$ matrices $I$ and $-I$ both have trace $0$.
In group theory, the conjugacy classes of involutions are a basic tool for studying the classical groups.
