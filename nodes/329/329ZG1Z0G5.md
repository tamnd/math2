## Definition

We define the symbols of formal arithmetic, the names of the numbers, and the structure in which the symbols have their usual meaning.

1. The language of arithmetic $L_A$ is the first-order [[signature|PWHJZYBNEY]] with these non-logical symbols: the constant symbol $0$, the unary function symbol $S$, the binary function symbols $+$ and $\cdot$, and the binary relation symbol $<$.
   The logical symbols include equality $=$.
2. The terms and formulas of $L_A$ are the [[terms|BQQ0FPFZ67]] and [[well-formed formulas|2CHENV4DCC]] of first-order logic over this signature.
   We write $s + t$, $s \cdot t$ and $s < t$ in infix form.
3. For $n \in \mathbb{N}$, the numeral $\overline{n}$ is the closed term defined by $\overline{0} = 0$ and $\overline{n + 1} = S\,\overline{n}$.
4. The abbreviation $s \le t$ stands for $s < t \lor s = t$.
5. Let $t$ be a term and $x$ a variable that does not occur in $t$.
   The bounded quantifiers are the abbreviations $\forall x < t\, \varphi$ for $\forall x\,(x < t \to \varphi)$ and $\exists x < t\, \varphi$ for $\exists x\,(x < t \land \varphi)$.
   The forms $\forall x \le t\, \varphi$ and $\exists x \le t\, \varphi$ are defined in the same way with $\le$.
6. The standard model $\mathfrak{N}$ is the $L_A$-[[structure|Y6C40MHZZC]] with domain $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$, with $0^{\mathfrak{N}} = 0$ and $S^{\mathfrak{N}}(n) = n + 1$.
   In it, $+^{\mathfrak{N}}$ and $\cdot^{\mathfrak{N}}$ are the usual addition and multiplication, and $<^{\mathfrak{N}} = \{(m, n) : m < n\}$.

## Notation

A sentence $\varphi$ of $L_A$ is called true when $\mathfrak{N} \models \varphi$.
A theory in $L_A$ that uses the symbol $<$ contains the defining axiom $\forall x\, \forall y\, (x < y \leftrightarrow \exists v\, (v + S x = y))$, unless it says otherwise.
Defined relations get names, for example $x \mid y$ for $\exists z \le y\, (z \cdot x = y)$.
These names are abbreviations, not new symbols.

## Remarks

Each clause has a job.
Clause 1 fixes the alphabet, and clause 2 says that the grammar is the usual grammar of first-order logic, so nothing new is needed there.
Clause 3 gives every number a name inside the language.
Its value in $\mathfrak{N}$ is the right number, by induction on $n$: $\overline{0}$ has the value $0$, and if $\overline{n}$ has the value $n$, then $S\,\overline{n}$ has the value $n + 1$.

The condition in clause 5 that $x$ does not occur in $t$ keeps the bound from depending on the quantified variable.
For example, $\forall x < x\, \varphi$ is not a bounded quantifier in this sense, because the bound $x$ is the variable being bound.
Clause 6 makes the defining axiom of $<$ true in $\mathfrak{N}$: $m < n$ holds exactly when $n = v + (m + 1)$ for some $v \in \mathbb{N}$, namely $v = n - m - 1$.
Check: for $m = 2$ and $n = 5$ we get $v = 2$, and $2 + 3 = 5$.

The [[concept of the language of arithmetic|D8SD54M5PN]] explains what the language can and cannot express, and lists the variants in other books.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The definition lists the five signs of the language: zero, "next", plus, times and "less than".
It also gives names to the terms that stand for the numbers: $\overline{2}$ is "next of next of zero".
Finally, it says what the signs mean in the ordinary numbers $0, 1, 2, \ldots$.

### L2 human

We compute the value in $\mathfrak{N}$ of the term $S(\overline{1} \cdot \overline{2}) + \overline{3}$, from the inside out.
The numerals have the values $1$, $2$ and $3$, by clause 3.
The product has the value $1 \cdot 2 = 2$ by clause 6, and its successor has the value $3$.
So the whole term has the value
$$3 + 3 = 6,$$
which is the value of $\overline{6}$.

The typical use of the definition is to fix the symbols before a theory such as [[Robinson arithmetic|3K1AKGXF8A]] gives axioms for them.
Watch out: the term in this computation is not the numeral $\overline{6}$.
The two are different strings with the same value, and a theory needs a proof to show that they are equal.
