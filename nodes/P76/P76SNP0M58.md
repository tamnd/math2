## Definition

Consider a [[plasticity|K8BBMTN0KV]] model at a material point with yield function $f(\sigma, \kappa)$.
Here $\sigma$ is the stress and $\kappa \ge 0$ is a hardening variable that measures accumulated plastic strain.
A common choice is $\kappa(t) = \int_0^t \sqrt{\tfrac23 \dot\varepsilon^p : \dot\varepsilon^p} \, ds$.
In isotropic hardening, $f(\sigma, \kappa) = \bar\sigma(\sigma) - \sigma_y(\kappa)$, where $\bar\sigma$ is an equivalent stress and $\sigma_y$ is the current yield stress.
The material shows *work hardening* if $\sigma_y$ is increasing in $\kappa$, so that the elastic domain $\set{\sigma : f(\sigma, \kappa) < 0}$ grows as plastic deformation accumulates.
More generally, it shows work hardening if continued plastic flow requires an increasing stress.
That is, the plastic modulus $H$ in $d\bar\sigma = H \, d\kappa$ during plastic loading is positive.

## Remarks

Example: linear isotropic hardening, $\sigma_y(\kappa) = \sigma_0 + H\kappa$ with $H > 0$; after plastic straining to $\kappa_1$, the [[yield surface|CWWCEXZ8BH]] has grown, and reloading stays elastic up to $\sigma_0 + H\kappa_1$.
Non-example: a perfectly plastic material, $\sigma_y(\kappa) = \sigma_0$, has $H = 0$, so its yield surface never changes.
In kinematic hardening the yield surface translates instead of growing: $f(\sigma, \alpha) = \bar\sigma(\sigma - \alpha)$ with a back stress $\alpha$ that evolves with plastic strain; this models the Bauschinger effect.
When $H < 0$ the material softens.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The stress needed to keep deforming plastically goes up as plastic deformation accumulates.
In the model, the yield limit grows with plastic strain.
