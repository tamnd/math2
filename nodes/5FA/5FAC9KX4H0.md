## Summary

The *Lambek calculus* $\mathsf{L}$ is the [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] with fusion $\cdot$ and the two implications $\backslash$ and $/$.
Its sequents $\Gamma \Rightarrow A$ have a nonempty list $\Gamma$ on the left and one formula on the right.
It has no structural rule; see [[the definition|46AN6CZMRD]].
Formulas are read as syntactic types, and a string of words is a sentence if the list of the types of its words derives the type $s$.
The calculus is the logic of categorial grammar, and it is a substructural logic: it has no [[exchange|YAFD18WYJ8]], no weakening and no contraction.

## Motivation

In categorial grammar, a word gets a type that says what it combines with.
A determiner such as "the" has type $np / n$: it takes a noun $n$ on its right and gives a noun phrase $np$.
An intransitive verb such as "sleeps" has type $np \backslash s$: it takes a noun phrase on its left and gives a sentence $s$.
Lambek saw that the rules of combination are the rules of a logic in which $\backslash$ and $/$ are implications, and in which the order of hypotheses is the order of words.

## Notation

We follow Lambek: $A \backslash B$ takes $A$ on the left, and $B / A$ takes $A$ on the right.
Some authors write $A \backslash B$ as $B \backslash A$ (result first) or use arrows, so check the convention of each source.
The variant $\mathsf{L}^*$ allows empty antecedents, and the variant $\mathsf{NL}$ (nonassociative Lambek calculus) uses trees of formulas in place of lists.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. "The cat sees a dog" is a sentence: $np / n, n, (np \backslash s) / np, np / n, n \Rightarrow s$ is derivable; see [[the parse|WE5HSA9BPQ]].
2. Type raising $np \Rightarrow s / (np \backslash s)$ is derivable; see [[type raising|DEAVBXZNX7]].
3. Composition $A / B, B / C \Rightarrow A / C$ is derivable, by ${/}\mathrm{L}$ twice and ${/}\mathrm{R}$.
4. Every context-free language without the empty word is generated by a Lambek grammar.

### Non-examples

1. "Dog the sleeps" is not a sentence: $n, np / n, np \backslash s \Rightarrow s$ is not derivable; see [[the non-example|GYJQE97XNA]].
2. Exchange fails: $p, q \Rightarrow p \cdot q$ is derivable, but $q, p \Rightarrow p \cdot q$ is not; see [[exchange fails in the Lambek calculus|7PY2HR7ND5]].
3. $\Rightarrow p \backslash p$ is not a sequent of $\mathsf{L}$, since the antecedent is empty; it is a sequent of $\mathsf{L}^*$.

### Basic properties

1. $\mathsf{L}$ has cut elimination and so the subformula property, by the proof for the [[full Lambek calculus|JSAH84Y9G2]].
2. Derivability in $\mathsf{L}$ is decidable.
   Pentus proved in 2006 that it is NP-complete.
3. Pentus proved in 1993 that every language generated by a Lambek grammar is context-free.
4. $\mathsf{L}$ is complete for residuated semigroups, by the Lindenbaum argument for the [[full Lambek calculus|Q47701D0E1]].

### History

Lambek introduced the calculus in 1958 in the American Mathematical Monthly, as a calculus of syntactic types.
Wikipedia reports that the term categorial grammar is due to Bar-Hillel in 1953.
The question whether Lambek grammars generate only context-free languages stayed open for about thirty years, until Pentus proved it, as Wikipedia reports.
Pentus also proved that derivability in the calculus is NP-complete, in a paper of 2006.

### Common errors

1. Using exchange in a Lambek derivation.
   The order of the antecedent is the order of words, and it cannot change.
2. Confusing $A \backslash B$ and $B / A$.
   Both have argument $A$ and result $B$, but the argument stands on different sides.
3. Thinking that Lambek grammars generate more than the context-free languages.
   Pentus's theorem says they do not.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Lambek calculus treats grammar as logic.
Each word has a type, like "noun" or "needs a noun on its right to make a noun phrase".
A string of words is a sentence when the types fit together, in their order, to give the type "sentence".
For "the cat sleeps", "the" plus "cat" gives a noun phrase, and the noun phrase plus "sleeps" gives a sentence.

### L2 human

We derive composition, $A / B, B / C \Rightarrow A / C$, which lets two functors combine before they get their argument.

1. $C \Rightarrow C$, $B \Rightarrow B$ and $A \Rightarrow A$ are axioms.
2. ${/}\mathrm{L}$ with $\Gamma = C$ gives $B / C, C \Rightarrow B$.
3. ${/}\mathrm{L}$ with the premises $B / C, C \Rightarrow B$ and $A \Rightarrow A$ gives $A / B, B / C, C \Rightarrow A$.
4. ${/}\mathrm{R}$ moves $C$ from the right end: $A / B, B / C \Rightarrow A / C$.

The typical use of composition is to parse phrases such as "the friend of" before the next noun arrives.
A trap: ${/}\mathrm{R}$ can remove only the rightmost formula of the antecedent, and the remaining antecedent must be nonempty.

### L3 human

The Lambek calculus has the rules of the [[full Lambek calculus|V5XVVN16GS]] for $\cdot$, $\backslash$ and $/$, restricted to nonempty antecedents.
Its algebras are residuated semigroups, and the variant $\mathsf{L}^*$ corresponds to residuated monoids.
By Pentus's theorem, Lambek grammars have the same weak generative power as context-free grammars, so the interest of the calculus for language lies in the structures it assigns, not in the set of strings.
Later type-logical grammars add modalities and other connectives to allow controlled reordering.
