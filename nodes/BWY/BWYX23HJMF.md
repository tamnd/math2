## Statement

Let $F$ be a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]] of characteristic different from $2$.
A Jordan polynomial in $x, y, z$ is an expression built from $x$, $y$, $z$ by sums, scalar multiples and the Jordan product.
It can be evaluated in every [[Jordan algebra|6C2MCVB8C7]] over $F$.
Let $p(x, y, z)$ be a Jordan polynomial in which every term has degree at most $1$ in $z$.
If $p(a, b, c) = 0$ for all elements $a, b, c$ of every [[special Jordan algebra|T4AWF1BRZB]] over $F$, then $p(a, b, c) = 0$ for all elements $a, b, c$ of every Jordan algebra over $F$.

## Remarks

Proof idea: the identities in question can be rewritten in terms of multiplication operators of $x$ and $y$ applied to $z$.
One studies the algebra generated by these operators in the free Jordan algebra and compares it with the corresponding operator algebra in a free associative algebra.
The key point is that two elements always behave as in a special algebra, a fact also expressed by the Shirshov–Cohn theorem.

Example: put $U_a b = 2a(ab) - (aa)b$.
The fundamental formula $U_{U_x y} z = U_x U_y U_x z$ is linear in $z$, and in a special algebra $A^+$ it reads $(xyx) z (xyx) = x(y(xzx)y)x$, which is true.
By the theorem it holds in every Jordan algebra over $F$.

The bound on the degree in $z$ is necessary.
Glennie found a Jordan identity of degree $8$ in three variables that holds in all special Jordan algebras but fails in the Albert algebra.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Special Jordan algebras come from associative algebras, so identities are easy to test in them: one expands the products and compares words.
Macdonald's theorem says that for identities in three letters, with the third letter used at most once in each term, this test is enough.
Such an identity then holds in all Jordan algebras, including the exceptional ones.

### L2 human

The theorem is the main tool for proving the standard identities of Jordan theory, such as the fundamental formula and its linearizations.
Without it, each identity would need a long direct calculation from the Jordan identity.
It extends the [[Shirshov–Cohn theorem|H4DT2XM8GJ]], which says that a Jordan algebra generated by two elements is special, so that identities in two variables can be tested in special algebras.
Glennie's identities show that one cannot go much further: some identities in three variables hold only in special Jordan algebras.
