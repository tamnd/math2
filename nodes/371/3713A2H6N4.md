## Example

A [[graph|00C0BR7D7H]] $G$ has the *extension property* when, for any two disjoint finite sets $U$ and $V$ of vertices, some vertex $z \notin U \cup V$ is adjacent to every vertex of $U$ and to no vertex of $V$.
Let $G$ and $H$ be countable graphs with the extension property.
We show by the [[back-and-forth method|6F2DB4HTJR]] that $G$ and $H$ are [[isomorphic|0F7QKGJS6B]].
Let $I$ be the set of finite partial isomorphisms from $G$ to $H$: injective maps between finite sets of vertices with $u \sim v$ if and only if $p(u) \sim p(v)$.

1. $I$ is not empty, because the empty map belongs to $I$.
2. Forth: let $p \in I$ map $g_1, \dots, g_k$ to $h_1, \dots, h_k$, and let $g$ be a vertex of $G$ outside the domain.
3. Let $U = \set{h_i : g_i \sim g}$ and $V = \set{h_i : g_i \not\sim g}$. These sets are disjoint, and their union is the range of $p$.
4. The extension property of $H$ gives a vertex $z$ outside the range of $p$, adjacent to every vertex of $U$ and to no vertex of $V$.
5. The map $q = p \cup \set{(g, z)}$ is injective, since $z$ is new. For each $i$, $g_i \sim g$ if and only if $h_i \in U$, if and only if $h_i \sim z$. So $q \in I$.
6. Back: for $p \in I$ and a vertex $h$ of $H$ outside the range, the same argument with the extension property of $G$ gives a preimage of $h$.
7. Both graphs are countable, so the method applies, and the union of the chain is an isomorphism from $G$ onto $H$.

## Remarks

The [[Rado graph|RMXR0FEF54]] has the extension property, so it is, up to isomorphism, the only countable graph with this property.
A countable [[random graph|TNT3DG0HRJ]] with independent edges of probability $\frac12$ has the property with probability $1$.
For fixed $U$ and $V$ with $m$ vertices in total, each other vertex fails to be a witness with probability $1 - 2^{-m}$, independently, so all of infinitely many vertices fail with probability $0$.
There are only countably many pairs $(U, V)$, so with probability $1$ every pair has a witness.

The same construction, started from any $p_0 \in I$ with $G = H$, shows that every isomorphism between finite induced subgraphs of the Rado graph extends to an automorphism.
If the extension property is weakened to "some vertex is adjacent to every vertex of $U$", the argument fails at step 4, because $V$ is ignored.
The infinite complete graph and the Rado graph both have the weak property, and they are not isomorphic, since only the Rado graph has two nonadjacent vertices.
No finite graph has the extension property, because $U$ = all vertices and $V = \emptyset$ leave no room for $z$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Imagine a huge network in which, for any finite group of people and any choice of who should be a friend, someone new exists with exactly those friends in the group.
If two networks both have this property, we can match their members one by one.
Each time we pick a new member on one side, we look at which matched members are friends of this person.
The property gives a new member on the other side with exactly the matching friends.
Going back and forth, we match everyone, and the two networks turn out to be the same.

### L2 human

We do two steps of the construction between two copies $G$ and $H$ of the Rado graph on $\N$, where $i < j$ are adjacent when the binary digit of $j$ at position $i$ is $1$.

1. Forth with $g = 0$: the domain is empty, so $U = V = \emptyset$, and we may choose $z = 0$. The map is $0 \mapsto 0$.
2. Back with $h = 1$: we need a vertex of $G$ adjacent to the image $0$ exactly when $1 \sim 0$ in $H$. In binary $1 = 1_2$, so $0 \sim 1$.
3. So we need $g' \ne 0$ in $G$ with $g' \sim 0$, and $g' = 1$ works. The map is now $0 \mapsto 0$, $1 \mapsto 1$.

The typical use of this example is to show that a theory, here the theory of graphs with the extension property, has only one countable model.
A trap is to forget that $z$ must avoid the whole range of $p$; otherwise the map is not injective.
