## Statement

Let IP be the class of languages that have an [[interactive proof system|7PFQZJF1DX]].
Let [[PSPACE|T9NTRY93ZT]] be the class of languages decided by deterministic Turing machines in polynomial space.
Then
$$\mathrm{IP} = \mathrm{PSPACE}.$$

## Remarks

The inclusion $\mathrm{IP} \subseteq \mathrm{PSPACE}$ holds because, for a fixed verifier, the largest acceptance probability over all provers can be computed by a recursion over the tree of messages, in polynomial space.
For $\mathrm{PSPACE} \subseteq \mathrm{IP}$ it suffices to give a protocol for the PSPACE-complete [[true quantified Boolean formula|WV2N01B4YV]] problem.
The formula is arithmetized: it becomes a polynomial expression over a finite field $\F_p$ for a large prime $p$, with $\exists$ and $\forall$ turned into sums and products over $\set{0,1}$.
The prover then strips the quantifiers one at a time, each time sending a univariate polynomial that the verifier checks at a random point, as in the sumcheck protocol.
Degree-reduction steps keep these polynomials of small degree, so that a false claim survives a random check only with small probability.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A verifier with polynomial time and random coins, talking to an all-powerful but untrusted prover, can check exactly the problems solvable with polynomial memory.
This includes deciding who wins many two-player games, which seem far beyond the reach of short certificates.
Randomness and interaction together make verification much more powerful than reading a written proof.

### L2 human

Adi Shamir proved the theorem in 1990, building on the protocol of Lund, Fortnow, Karloff and Nisan for the counting class $\#\mathrm{P}$ and on arithmetization.
The result does not relativize: there are oracles relative to which IP and PSPACE differ, so the proof avoids the [[relativization barrier|BST2RKE12N]].
Its methods, arithmetization and low-degree testing, led to the PCP theorem.
