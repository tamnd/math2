## Example

Claim: for every integer $n$, $n$ is even if and only if $n^2$ is even.
A student writes this argument.

1. $(\Rightarrow)$ If $n$ is even, then $n = 2k$ and $n^2 = 2(2k^2)$ is even.
2. $(\Leftarrow)$ If $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd, because if $n$ were even, $n^2$ would be even by part 1.
3. So both directions hold.

The claim is true, but the argument is not a valid [[proof of a biconditional|TFQ1845T84]].
We check the condition that fails.

1. A proof of "$P$ if and only if $Q$" must prove $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow P$.
   Here $P$ is "$n$ is even" and $Q$ is "$n^2$ is even".
2. Part 1 proves $P \Rightarrow Q$.
3. Part 2 proves "if not $Q$, then not $P$", which is the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] of $P \Rightarrow Q$.
   It is equivalent to part 1, and its proof does nothing but cite part 1.
4. The [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] $Q \Rightarrow P$, "if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even", is proved nowhere.
   Its contrapositive is "if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd", and that is what part 2 should have proved.

## Remarks

The error is easy to make, because the two parts look different on the page.
A quick test catches it: write each part as "if $X$, then $Y$" and form its contrapositive.
If the two parts agree up to contraposition, only one direction is proved.

The four implications built from $P$ and $Q$ fall into two pairs:
$$P \Rightarrow Q \ \equiv \ \neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P, \qquad Q \Rightarrow P \ \equiv \ \neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q.$$
A proof of the biconditional needs one implication from each pair.
The student took both from the first pair.
A correct proof is [[an integer is even if and only if its square is even|T7C1TEZBRD]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take the sentence "it rains if and only if the street is wet".
The student checked "if it rains, the street is wet".
Then the student checked "if the street is dry, it does not rain".
These are the same fact said twice.
The other half, "if the street is wet, it rains", was never checked, and in real life it is false: a water truck can wet the street.

### L2 human

Let us repair part 2.
The direction we need is "if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even", and its contrapositive is "if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd".
Assume $n$ is odd, so $n = 2k + 1$, and compute:
$$n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1.$$
So $n^2$ is odd, the converse holds, and together with part 1 the biconditional is proved.
Check: $n = 5$ gives $k = 2$ and $25 = 2 \cdot 12 + 1$.
The typical use of this check is to write out each direction before you prove it and to compare it with the directions you already have.
Watch out: a direction whose proof only cites the other direction can never be the missing direction.
