## Example

Claim: for every integer $n$, $n$ is even if and only if $n^2$ is even.
A student writes this argument.

1. $(\Rightarrow)$ If $n$ is even, then $n = 2k$ and $n^2 = 2(2k^2)$ is even.
2. $(\Leftarrow)$ If $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd, because if $n$ were even, $n^2$ would be even by part 1.
3. So both directions hold.

The claim is true, but this is not a valid [[proof of a biconditional|TFQ1845T84]].
We check the condition that fails.

1. A proof of "$P$ if and only if $Q$" must prove $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow P$. Here $P$ is "$n$ is even" and $Q$ is "$n^2$ is even".
2. Part 1 proves $P \Rightarrow Q$.
3. Part 2 proves "if not $Q$, then not $P$", the [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] of $P \Rightarrow Q$. It is logically equivalent to part 1, and indeed its proof just cites part 1.
4. The [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] $Q \Rightarrow P$, "if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even", is not proved anywhere. Its contrapositive is "if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd", and that is the statement part 2 should have proved.

## Remarks

The error is easy to make, because the two parts look different on the page.
A quick test: write each part as "if $X$, then $Y$" and compute its contrapositive.
If the two parts are equal up to contraposition, only one direction is proved.

The four statements built from an implication fall into two pairs.
$P \Rightarrow Q$ and $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ are equivalent to each other.
$Q \Rightarrow P$ and $\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q$ are equivalent to each other.
A proof of a biconditional needs one statement from each pair.
A correct proof of this claim is [[an integer is even if and only if its square is even|T7C1TEZBRD]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take the sentence "it rains if and only if the street is wet".
The student checked "if it rains, the street is wet".
Then the student checked "if the street is dry, it does not rain".
These are the same fact said twice.
The other half, "if the street is wet, it rains", was never checked, and in real life it is false: a water truck can wet the street.

### L2 human

We repair the proof of part 2.

1. The needed direction is: if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.
2. Its contrapositive is: if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd.
3. Assume $n$ is odd, so $n = 2k + 1$. Then $n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$ is odd.
4. So the converse holds, and together with part 1 the biconditional is proved.

The typical use: before proving a direction, write it out and compare it with the directions already proved.

A trap: part 2 of the student's argument cites part 1.
A direction that follows from the other direction alone is never the missing direction.
