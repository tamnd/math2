## Example

*Chladni figures* are the patterns that sand forms on a thin plate made to vibrate in one of its modes.
The sand is thrown off the moving parts and collects along the nodal lines, where the mode shape $W$ is zero.
So the figure is a picture of the nodal set of a mode of the [[vibration of plates|Z8F5VBZQ5M]].

A computable case is a simply supported square plate $0 < x, y < a$, whose modes include $W_{mn} = \sin(m\pi x/a) \sin(n\pi y/a)$.
The frequencies of $W_{12}$ and $W_{21}$ are equal, so every combination $W_{12} + c\,W_{21}$ is also a mode.
Using $\sin 2\theta = 2 \sin\theta \cos\theta$,
$$W_{12} - W_{21} = 2 \sin\frac{\pi x}{a} \sin\frac{\pi y}{a} \left( \cos\frac{\pi y}{a} - \cos\frac{\pi x}{a} \right),$$
which vanishes inside the square exactly on the diagonal $y = x$.
In the same way $W_{12} + W_{21}$ vanishes exactly on the other diagonal $x + y = a$.
So the sand gathers on a diagonal, although neither $W_{12}$ nor $W_{21}$ alone has a diagonal nodal line.

## Remarks

Chladni's own plates were usually held at one point and free along the edges, and their modes have no closed formula; the square above is an idealised case.
Equal frequencies of different modes, as on the square, are what produce the rich curved patterns seen in experiments.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Ernst Chladni sprinkled sand on a metal plate and stroked its edge with a violin bow.
The plate sang one note, and the sand ran to the lines that did not move, drawing a pattern.
Each note gives its own pattern, and higher notes give finer ones.
The figures show the still lines of the vibration modes of the plate.
