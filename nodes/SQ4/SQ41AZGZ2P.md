## Summary

A critical exponent is the power in the power law that a physical quantity follows near a critical point.
The standard exponents $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta, \nu, \eta$ describe the specific heat, the order parameter, the susceptibility, the critical isotherm, the correlation length and the decay of correlations.
They are the same for all systems of one [[universality class|GWXP0KSW9Y]].
They obey [[scaling relations|Z6W0AXDN9G]], so only two of them are independent in most cases.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Near a critical temperature $T_c$, many quantities behave like a power of the distance $\abs{T - T_c}$.
The critical exponent is that power.
For example, the magnetization of a magnet just below $T_c$ grows like $(T_c - T)^{\beta}$.
The exponent $\beta$ is about $0.33$ for three-dimensional magnets with one easy axis and exactly $1/8$ for the two-dimensional Ising model.
A larger exponent means a slower start of the growth.
The exponent ignores constant factors, so it captures the shape of the singularity, not its size.
