## Statement

Suppose that $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$, where P is the class [[P|GVT2NKN37K]] and NP is the class [[NP|QQRA9GBW51]].
Then there is a language $L \in \mathrm{NP}$ that is neither in $\mathrm{P}$ nor [[NP-complete|JGZWHWA8P8]].

## Remarks

Such languages are called NP-intermediate.
The proof is a delayed diagonalization due to Richard Ladner, published in 1975.
One builds $L$ from SAT by keeping the instances of some input lengths and removing those of the other lengths.
The pattern of lengths is chosen in stages, so that each polynomial-time machine fails to decide $L$ and each polynomial-time reduction fails to reduce SAT to $L$.
The language obtained is in NP, and it is not complete for NP under [[polynomial-time reductions|V0K79AD81V]].

The theorem gives an artificial language.
Natural candidates for NP-intermediate problems are graph isomorphism and the decision version of integer factorization, but none is proved to be NP-intermediate, since that would imply $\mathrm{P} \ne \mathrm{NP}$.
The hypothesis cannot be dropped: if $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$, then every language in NP other than $\emptyset$ and $\Sigma^*$ is NP-complete.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If the hard problems in NP really are hard, then NP is not split cleanly into easy problems and hardest problems.
There are problems strictly in between.
So showing that a problem is not NP-complete does not show that it is easy.

### L2 human

The theorem is the reason that problems such as graph isomorphism can be studied as candidates for an intermediate status.
Its proof generalizes: the same method shows that between any two languages $A <_p B$ with $B$ decidable there is a third, strictly between them.
It contrasts with dichotomy theorems for restricted families, such as Schaefer's theorem for Boolean constraint problems, where no intermediate case occurs.
