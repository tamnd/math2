## Definition

The orthogonal complement of a subspace is the set of all vectors that are orthogonal to every vector of the subspace.
Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{K} = \R$ or $\C$ with an [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}$, and let $U$ be a [[subspace|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$.
The *orthogonal complement* of $U$ is the set
$$U^\perp = \{ v \in V : \ip{v}{u} = 0 \text{ for all } u \in U \}.$$
So $v \in U^\perp$ exactly when $v$ is [[orthogonal|0TS7VZJBWD]] to every vector of $U$.

## Notation

The same formula defines $S^\perp$ for any subset $S$ of $V$, and then $S^\perp = (\operatorname{span} S)^\perp$.
The order of the two arguments does not matter, because $\ip{u}{v} = \overline{\ip{v}{u}}$ and a complex number is $0$ exactly when its conjugate is $0$.
In Mathlib, `Submodule.orthogonal U` is written `Uᗮ`.

## Remarks

The definition has two ingredients, and each one is there for a reason.
The inner product supplies the meaning of "orthogonal", so a different inner product on the same space gives a different complement.
The condition must hold for every $u \in U$, not for some, and in practice this means for every vector of a spanning set of $U$.
The definition does not ask $U^\perp$ to be a subspace; that it is one, and that $U \cap U^\perp = \{0\}$, are [[basic properties|T5Q51M5BAK]] with proofs.

For a small example, take $U = \R(1, 2, 0)$ in $\R^3$ with the dot product and $v = (2, -1, 3)$.
One dot product decides the question:
$$(2, -1, 3) \cdot (1, 2, 0) = 2 - 2 + 0 = 0.$$
Every vector of $U$ is $t(1, 2, 0)$, and $v \cdot t(1, 2, 0) = t \cdot 0 = 0$, so $v \in U^\perp$.
The vector $w = (1, 0, 0)$ fails the condition, since $w \cdot (1, 2, 0) = 1$, so $w \notin U^\perp$.

The [[concept page|0XJXTBCB2T]] gives more examples, and [[the plane spanned by (1, 1, 0) and (0, 1, 1)|38S0WYCSA3]] computes a complement as a null space.
For a real matrix $A$, the null space of $A$ is the complement of the row space, by the [[orthogonality of the fundamental subspaces|GQF2KG1R1N]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The orthogonal complement collects all the vectors that are perpendicular to every vector of a subspace.
In space, the complement of a line through the origin is the plane through the origin at a right angle to it.
The complement of a plane through the origin is its normal line.

### L2 human

To test whether $v = (1, 1, -1)$ lies in the complement of the plane $U$ spanned by $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$, compute two dot products:
$$(1, 1, -1) \cdot (1, 0, 1) = 1 - 1 = 0, \qquad (1, 1, -1) \cdot (0, 1, 1) = 1 - 1 = 0.$$
Both are zero, and every vector of $U$ is a combination of the two spanning vectors, so $v \in U^\perp$.
Watch out: a single test against $(1, 0, 1)$ would not be enough, since $(0, 1, 0)$ also passes that test but has dot product $1$ with $(0, 1, 1)$.
