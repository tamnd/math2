## Definition

Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{K} = \R$ or $\C$ with an [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}$, and let $U$ be a [[subspace|0EPTJA5QV4]] of $V$.
The *orthogonal complement* of $U$ is the set
$$U^\perp = \set{v \in V : \ip{v}{u} = 0 \text{ for all } u \in U}.$$
So $v \in U^\perp$ exactly when $v$ is [[orthogonal|0TS7VZJBWD]] to every vector of $U$.

## Notation

The same formula defines $S^\perp$ for any subset $S$ of $V$, and then $S^\perp = (\operatorname{span} S)^\perp$.
The order of the arguments does not matter: $\ip{v}{u} = 0$ if and only if $\ip{u}{v} = \overline{\ip{v}{u}} = 0$.
In Mathlib, `Submodule.orthogonal U` is written `Uᗮ`.

## Remarks

The definition only asks for orthogonality; the facts that $U^\perp$ is a subspace and that $U \cap U^\perp = \set{0}$ are [[basic properties|~2c397fab]] with proofs.
In $\R^3$ with the dot product, the orthogonal complement of a plane through $0$ is its normal line, as in [[the plane spanned by (1, 1, 0) and (0, 1, 1)|~f9f46f63]].
For a real matrix $A$, the null space of $A$ is the orthogonal complement of the row space of $A$, by the [[orthogonality of the fundamental subspaces|~b3a6e0d4]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The orthogonal complement collects all vectors that are perpendicular to every vector of a subspace.
In space, the complement of a line through the origin is a plane, and the complement of a plane through the origin is a line.

### L2 human

To test whether $v = (2, -1, 3)$ lies in the complement of $U = \R(1, 2, 0)$ in $\R^3$, compute one dot product.
Here $(2, -1, 3) \cdot (1, 2, 0) = 2 - 2 + 0 = 0$, so $v \in U^\perp$.
One test is enough because each vector of $U$ is $t(1, 2, 0)$, and $v \cdot t(1, 2, 0) = t \cdot 0 = 0$.
The trap is to test against vectors that do not span $U$, which can give a false yes.
