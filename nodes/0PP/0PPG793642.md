## Statement

If we hold all rows of a square matrix fixed except one, the determinant becomes a linear function of that one row.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $1 \le i \le n$.
Let $A$, $B$ and $C$ be $n \times n$ matrices over $K$ that have the same rows outside row $i$.
Let $a$, $b$ and $c$ be row $i$ of $A$, $B$ and $C$, and assume that $c = sa + tb$ with $s, t \in K$.
Then
$$\det C = s \det A + t \det B,$$
where $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].

## Remarks

### Splitting one row

Take $C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$ and write its second row as a sum, $(4, 7) = (3, 4) + (1, 3)$.
Keep the first row and use each piece as the second row:
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Their determinants are $\det A = 4 - 6 = -2$ and $\det B = 3 - 2 = 1$.
With $s = t = 1$ the statement gives $\det C = -2 + 1 = -1$.
Check: directly, $\det C = 1 \cdot 7 - 2 \cdot 4 = -1$.

A factor of one row comes out in the same way, with $t = 0$.
For example, the first row of $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ is $2 (1, 2)$, so
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = 2 \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = 2 (5 - 6) = -2.$$
Check: $2 \cdot 5 - 4 \cdot 3 = -2$.

### Why the other rows must stay fixed

The hypothesis that $A$, $B$ and $C$ agree outside row $i$ carries the whole statement.
Without it, the rule turns into the false claim that $\det$ is additive on matrices.
The [[counterexample on sums|D5VBC7621G]] takes $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, 0)$ and $B = \operatorname{diag}(0, 1)$.
Both have determinant $0$, but $A + B = I_2$ has determinant $1$.
Here $A$ and $B$ differ in both rows, so the statement does not apply to them.

### What it gives you

Several rules for computing follow at once.
A [[zero row gives determinant 0|0K4VQB7HWR]], because the zero row is $0$ times itself, so $\det$ is $0$ times a number.
Scaling one row by $s$ scales the determinant by $s$, which is one of the [[effects of row operations|09YB3WQG38]].
Scaling all $n$ rows by $c$ uses the rule $n$ times, so $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$.
By the [[determinant of the transpose|0ZZQ2E1MTE]], the determinant is also linear in each column.

The rule also expands a sum of matrices correctly.
Every row of $A + B$ is a sum of two rows, so splitting each row in turn gives $2^n$ determinants, one for each choice of rows from $A$ or from $B$.
For $n = 2$ there are four terms, and the [[counterexample on sums|D5VBC7621G]] works them out.

### Watch out

Linearity in each row is not linearity in the matrix.
A common wrong move is $\det(2A) = 2 \det A$.
For $A = I_2$ this would give $2$, but
$$\det(2 I_2) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 4 = 2^2 \det I_2.$$
The correct move is to take one factor $c$ out of each of the $n$ rows.

### The converse

Linearity in each row does not single out the determinant.
The [[permanent|X9B1XETBPF]], the Leibniz sum without the signs, is also linear in each row.
It is not the determinant, since the permanent of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is $1 + 1 = 2$ while the determinant is $0$.
The [[existence and uniqueness theorem|1C8RRWHD45]] adds the alternating property and the value $\det I_n = 1$, and then only the determinant is left.

### Try it

First, use the rule to compute $\det \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ by taking the factor $3$ out of the first row.
The answer is $3 \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = 3 (5 - 2) = 9$, and $15 - 6 = 9$ agrees.

Second, a $3 \times 3$ matrix has determinant $5$; what is the determinant of $-A$?
Each of the three rows gives a factor $-1$, so $\det(-A) = (-1)^3 \cdot 5 = -5$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a square table of numbers and fix every row but one.
Then the determinant reacts to the free row in the simplest possible way, by proportion.
If you double that row, the determinant doubles.
If you write that row as a sum of two rows, the determinant is the sum of the two determinants you get.
For example, with first row $(1, 2)$, the second rows $(3, 4)$ and $(1, 3)$ give $-2$ and $1$, and their sum $(4, 7)$ gives $-1$.
Adding two whole tables is different, and the determinants do not add then.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: compute
$$D = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 10 & 20 & 31 \end{pmatrix}$$
by splitting the last row.
Write $(10, 20, 31) = 10 (1, 2, 3) + (0, 0, 1)$.
The statement gives $D = 10\, D_1 + D_2$, where $D_1$ and $D_2$ have last rows $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(0, 0, 1)$.
For $D_1$, the last row equals the second row minus the first row, so the rows are dependent and $D_1 = 0$.
For $D_2$, expand along the last row: $D_2 = 1 \cdot (1 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 2) = 1$.
So $D = 10 \cdot 0 + 1 = 1$.
Check by the rule of Sarrus: $93 + 40 + 40 - 30 - 80 - 62 = 1$.
The problem shows the typical use: split a row so that most pieces vanish or become simple.
Watch out: the rule splits one row at a time, never the whole matrix.

### L3 human

Linearity in each row says that $\det$ is a [[multilinear map|0443XNARDV]] $(K^n)^n \to K$, so it factors through the tensor power $(K^n)^{\otimes n}$.
The alternating property lets it factor further through the top exterior power $\Lambda^n K^n$, which is free of rank $1$; that is the structural reason for uniqueness.
For a matrix $A(t)$ whose entries depend on $t$, multilinearity gives the derivative of $\det A(t)$ as a sum of $n$ determinants, each with one row differentiated.
This is the starting point of [[Jacobi's formula|357FEAZ0Q4]].
