## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $1 \le i \le n$.
Let $A$, $B$, $C$ be $n \times n$ matrices over $K$ that agree outside row $i$.
Let $a$, $b$, $c$ be row $i$ of $A$, $B$, $C$, and assume $c = sa + tb$ with $s, t \in K$.
Then
$$\det C = s \det A + t \det B,$$
where $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].
In words: with the other rows fixed, $\det$ is a linear function of row $i$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The matrices must agree outside row $i$.
If they differ in two rows, the rule fails: $\det(A + B) \neq \det A + \det B$ in general, as the [[counterexample on sums|D5VBC7621G]] shows with $\det \operatorname{diag}(1, 0) = \det \operatorname{diag}(0, 1) = 0$ and $\det I_2 = 1$.

### Consequences

- A common factor of one row comes out of the determinant: scaling row $i$ by $s$ scales $\det$ by $s$. See the [[effect of row operations|09YB3WQG38]].
- A zero row gives determinant $0$, since the zero row is $0$ times itself; see [[equal rows or a zero row|0K4VQB7HWR]].
- Scaling all $n$ rows by $c$ gives $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$, not $c \det A$.
- The determinant is also multilinear in the columns, by the [[determinant of the transpose|0ZZQ2E1MTE]].

### Converse

Multilinearity alone does not characterize $\det$: the [[permanent|X9B1XETBPF]] is also multilinear in the rows.
The [[existence and uniqueness theorem|1C8RRWHD45]] needs the alternating property as well.

### Generalizations

Each [[multilinear map|0443XNARDV]] has the same property in each argument.
For a matrix whose row $i$ is a sum of $k$ rows, the determinant is the sum of $k$ determinants.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Fix all rows of a square table except one.
Then the determinant behaves like a simple linear rule in the remaining row.
If that row doubles, the determinant doubles.
If that row is a sum of two rows, the determinant is the sum of two determinants.
But adding two whole tables does not add their determinants.

### L2 human

We split row $2$ of $C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$ as $(4, 7) = (3, 4) + (1, 3)$.

1. $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\det A = 4 - 6 = -2$.
2. $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\det B = 3 - 2 = 1$.
3. The rule gives $\det C = -2 + 1 = -1$.
4. Directly, $\det C = 7 - 8 = -1$.

The typical use is to split a determinant into simpler pieces, or to pull a factor out of one row.
A trap: the rule is about one row at a time; $\det(A + B)$ splits into $2^n$ determinants, not two.

### L3 human

Multilinearity says that $\det$ factors through the tensor product $(K^n)^{\otimes n}$, and the alternating property says that it factors further through $\Lambda^n K^n$.
For a matrix that depends on parameters, multilinearity gives the derivative of $\det$ as a sum over rows; this is [[Jacobi's formula|357FEAZ0Q4]].
