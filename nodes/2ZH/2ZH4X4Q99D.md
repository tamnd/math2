## Statement

The inverse, the transpose, nonzero scalar multiples and powers of an invertible matrix are again invertible, with inverses given by explicit formulas.
Let $R$ be a commutative ring and let $A$ be an [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] $n \times n$ matrix over $R$.

1. The matrix $A^{-1}$ is invertible, and $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$.
2. The [[transpose|09DXWYRD43]] $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ is invertible, and $(A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1} = (A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}}$.
3. If $c$ is a unit of $R$, then $cA$ is invertible, and $(cA)^{-1} = c^{-1} A^{-1}$.
4. For each integer $k \ge 0$, the power $A^k$ is invertible, and $(A^k)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^k$.

## Remarks

### Seeing the rules on one matrix

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, whose inverse is $A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
Part 3 with $c = 3$ gives $(3A)^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{3} A^{-1}$, and the check is $(3A)(\tfrac{1}{3} A^{-1}) = AA^{-1} = I_2$.
For part 4 with $k = 2$, square both matrices:
$$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (A^{-1})^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Their product is
$$\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -3 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 - 9 & -15 + 15 \\ 6 - 6 & -9 + 10 \end{pmatrix} = I_2,$$
as part 4 predicts.
Since $A$ is symmetric, part 2 says that $A^{-1}$ is symmetric too, and indeed both off-diagonal entries of $A^{-1}$ are $-1$.

### Why the scalar must be a unit

In part 3 the scalar $c$ must have an inverse in $R$.
For $c = 0$ the matrix $cA$ is the zero matrix, which is singular.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the matrix $2 I_2$ is singular, because its only candidate inverse $\tfrac{1}{2} I_2$ has entries outside $\mathbb{Z}$.
Over a field every $c \ne 0$ is a unit, so part 3 holds for each nonzero scalar.

Watch out for the place of the reciprocal.
The inverse of $cA$ is $c^{-1} A^{-1}$, not $c A^{-1}$: with $A = I_1$ and $c = 2$, the inverse of $(2)$ is $(\tfrac{1}{2})$, not $(2)$.

### What it gives you

Part 4 makes the notation $A^{-k} = (A^{-1})^k = (A^k)^{-1}$ unambiguous.
With it, the law $A^j A^k = A^{j+k}$ holds for all integers $j$ and $k$, as for nonzero numbers.
Part 2 shows that the inverse of a symmetric invertible matrix is symmetric: if $A^{\mathsf{T}} = A$, then $(A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} = (A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1} = A^{-1}$.

### The converses

Part 2 has a converse: if $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ is invertible, then so is $A = (A^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}$, by part 2 applied to $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
Part 4 has a converse for $k \ge 1$: if $A^k$ is invertible, then $A$ is invertible, by the [[inverse of a product|0S4WTRE9GG]], since $A^k = A \cdot A^{k-1}$ is a product of square factors.

### Generalizations

Parts 1 and 4 hold in any monoid, because their proofs use only associativity and the identity.
Part 2 holds for any operation that reverses products and fixes the identity.
For example, the conjugate transpose of a complex matrix satisfies $(A^{*})^{-1} = (A^{-1})^{*}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Undoing the undo gives back the original matrix.
Flipping a matrix over its diagonal and then inverting gives the same result as inverting first and then flipping.
Scaling a matrix by $3$ is undone by the inverse matrix scaled by $\tfrac{1}{3}$.

### L2 human

The typical use is to move between related inverses without new elimination.
If you know $A^{-1}$, then you know the inverses of $A^{\mathsf{T}}$, of $5A$ and of $A^3$ at once: they are $(A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}}$, $\tfrac{1}{5} A^{-1}$ and $(A^{-1})^3$.
Each rule is proved by the same move: name the candidate, multiply, and get the identity.
Watch out: the rule for the transpose needs the transpose of a product, $(XY)^{\mathsf{T}} = Y^{\mathsf{T}} X^{\mathsf{T}}$, with the order reversed.

### L3 human

Parts 1, 3 and 4 say that the invertible matrices are closed under the group operations, and that the scalar matrices $cI_n$ with $c$ a unit lie among them.
These scalar matrices commute with every matrix, so they lie in the center of the [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]].
Part 2 says that $A \mapsto (A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}$ is a group homomorphism from $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ to itself, since the transpose and the inverse each reverse products.
Applying the map twice gives the identity map.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ with $n = 2$ it is not conjugation by a fixed matrix: conjugation keeps the trace, but $\operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$, with trace $3$, goes to $\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac{1}{2}, 1)$, with trace $\tfrac{3}{2}$.
