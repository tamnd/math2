## Example

Consider the sentence "there will be a sea battle tomorrow, or there will not be one".
Classical logic calls it true whatever happens, because it has the form $p \lor \neg p$.
[[Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic|1CE2TGM6MQ]] disagrees, and it is the standard example of a logic without the [[principle of bivalence|5DYNDW9526]].
Its truth values are $0$ (false), $\tfrac{1}{2}$ (undetermined) and $1$ (true), and its connectives are
$$\neg x = 1 - x, \qquad x \land y = \min(x, y), \qquad x \lor y = \max(x, y).$$
A formula is valid when it has the value $1$ under every assignment of the three values to its variables.

1. The value $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is neither $1$ nor $0$.
   So a proposition with value $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is neither true nor false, and bivalence fails.
2. Give $p$ the value $\tfrac{1}{2}$, as Łukasiewicz did for statements about future events such as the sea battle.
3. The [[negation|029JBBF693]] is $\neg p = 1 - \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.
4. The [[disjunction|0F72FK8EKB]] is $p \lor \neg p = \max(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}) = \tfrac{1}{2}$.
   So $p \lor \neg p$ is not valid, and the [[law of excluded middle|0RYYK8PG4S]] fails together with bivalence.
5. In the same way $p \land \neg p = \min(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so $\neg(p \land \neg p) = \tfrac{1}{2}$.
   The [[law of noncontradiction|MP4R7T546Z]] is not valid either.

## Remarks

The condition that fails is the size of the set of truth values: it has three elements, not two.
On the values $0$ and $1$ alone the connectives agree with classical logic, and for $p = 1$ or $p = 0$ the value of $p \lor \neg p$ is $1$.
So every failure of a classical law here comes from the third value.

Łukasiewicz's implication is $x \to y = \min(1, 1 - x + y)$.
For $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ it gives $p \to p = \min(1, 1) = 1$, so $p \to p$ is valid while $\neg p \lor p$ is not.
In classical logic $p \to q$ and $\neg p \lor q$ are equivalent, so this equivalence fails in the three-valued logic too.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Imagine a third answer besides "true" and "false", namely "not decided yet".
A claim about tomorrow's weather may get this third answer today.
Then the claim "it will rain tomorrow or it will not rain tomorrow" also gets the answer "not decided yet" in this logic.
So the rule that every claim is true or false is given up.

### L2 human

Compute the value of $p \lor (q \land \neg p)$ for $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $q = 1$.
First $\neg p = 1 - \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, and then
$$q \land \neg p = \min\left(1, \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \tfrac{1}{2}, \qquad p \lor (q \land \neg p) = \max\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \tfrac{1}{2}.$$
In classical logic, with $q$ true, the formula reduces to $p \lor \neg p$, which is always true, but here its value is $\tfrac{1}{2}$.
One assignment with a value below $1$ is enough to show that a classical law is not valid in a many-valued logic.
Watch out: the value $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is not a probability, since the value of the whole comes from $\min$, $\max$ and $1 - x$, and probabilities do not combine that way.
