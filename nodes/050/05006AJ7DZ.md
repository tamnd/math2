## Summary

A tautology is a formula of [[propositional logic|09K9MWD2QQ]] that is true for every assignment of truth values to its variables, see the [[definition|0D1AHYPM8N]].
A contradiction is a formula that is false for every assignment, and a contingency is a formula that is true for some assignments and false for others.
Every formula is of exactly one of these three kinds, and a [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]] tells which: all $\mathsf{T}$, all $\mathsf{F}$, or mixed.
If each variable of a tautology is replaced, at every occurrence, by a formula, the result is again a tautology.
Tautologies are the laws of propositional logic, such as $p \lor \neg p$, $\neg(p \land \neg p)$ and $(p \land (p \to q)) \to q$.

## Motivation

Some statements are true because of the facts they describe, and some are true because of their form alone.
"$7$ is prime or $7$ is not prime" is true whatever the number $7$ is like, because every statement of the form $p \lor \neg p$ is true.
A proof may use such a form at any step without further justification.
So it is useful to know exactly which forms are always true, and to have a method that finds them.
The tautologies are these forms, and the truth table is the method.

## Notation

The metalanguage writes $\models \varphi$ for "$\varphi$ is a tautology".
Some books call a tautology a valid formula, and write $\models \varphi$ in the same sense.
The constant $\top$ denotes a fixed tautology and $\bot$ a fixed contradiction.
In tables, $\mathsf{T}$ and $\mathsf{F}$ are also written $1$ and $0$; a tautology has a column of $1$s.
In Lean 4 with Mathlib, the law $p \lor \neg p$ is `Classical.em`, and the law $\neg\neg p \leftrightarrow p$ is `Classical.not_not`.
The everyday word "tautology", which means saying the same thing twice, is a different sense.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[$(p \land (p \to q)) \to q$ is a tautology|~6a06c275]]: the law behind [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]], checked by a $4$-row table.
2. [[A substitution instance of $p \to (q \to p)$|~81400630]]: the formula $(r \land s) \to (\neg r \to (r \land s))$ is a tautology because it has the form of a tautology.
3. The [[law of excluded middle|0RYYK8PG4S]] $p \lor \neg p$, proved in the [[proof of the law of excluded middle by a truth table|ZNQN1JW1V3]].
4. The [[law of noncontradiction|~f0f20560]] $\neg(p \land \neg p)$.
5. [[The truth table of $((p \to q) \land (q \to r)) \to (p \to r)$|~c9f3fceb]] and [[Peirce's law by the assume-false method|~e5d7cc0b]].

### Non-examples

1. [[$(p \to q) \to (q \to p)$ is not a tautology|~2962da46]]: it is a contingency, false in the row $p = \mathsf{F}$, $q = \mathsf{T}$.
2. The formula $p \land \neg p$ is a contradiction: its column is $\mathsf{F}, \mathsf{F}$.
3. The formula $p \to q$ is a contingency: its column is $\mathsf{T}, \mathsf{F}, \mathsf{T}, \mathsf{T}$.
4. The statement "$2 + 2 = 4$" is true, but it is not a tautology: its propositional form is a single variable $p$, which is a contingency.

### Basic properties

1. A formula $\varphi$ is a tautology exactly when $\neg \varphi$ is a contradiction, by the table of [[negation|0XR7A7RSXB]].
2. Substitution. If $\varphi$ is a tautology with variables $p_1, \dots, p_n$, and $\psi_1, \dots, \psi_n$ are any formulas, then replacing each $p_i$ by $\psi_i$ at every occurrence gives a tautology. The reason: any assignment gives values to $\psi_1, \dots, \psi_n$, and these values form a row of the table of $\varphi$.
3. The converse of property 2 fails: $p \to p$ is a tautology and an instance of $p \to q$, which is not a tautology.
4. Two formulas $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]] exactly when $\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi$ is a tautology.
5. An argument from $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_k$ to $\psi$ is valid exactly when $(\varphi_1 \land \dots \land \varphi_k) \to \psi$ is a tautology; this is [[tautological consequence|4N2HHE7PC1]].
6. If $\varphi$ and $\varphi \to \psi$ are tautologies, then $\psi$ is a tautology, by the table of [[material implication|0ZNX6DQ3EJ]].
7. The truth table decides in finitely many steps whether a formula is a tautology, a contradiction or a contingency.

### History

Ludwig Wittgenstein, in the Tractatus of 1921, called a proposition true in every row of its truth table a tautology.
He called one false in every row a contradiction.
In the same year, Emil Post proved that the tautologies are exactly the theorems of the propositional calculus of Principia Mathematica.

### Common errors

1. A student calls every true statement a tautology. A tautology is true by its form; "$2 + 2 = 4$" is true by its content.
2. A student replaces a variable at some occurrences and not at others. Substitution must replace every occurrence of a variable by the same formula.
3. A student concludes that a formula that is not a tautology is a contradiction. Most such formulas are contingencies, such as $p \to q$.
4. A student replaces a subformula that is not a variable, as in passing from $p \lor \neg p$ to $p \lor q$. Only variables may be replaced.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A tautology is a statement that is true because of its shape, whatever its parts mean.
"It is raining or it is not raining" is always true, so its shape "$p$ or not $p$" is a tautology.
A contradiction is always false: "it is raining and it is not raining".
Most statements, like "if it rains, the road is wet", are true in some cases and false in others; these are contingencies.
A truth table checks every case and tells which kind a statement is.

### L2 human

We classify three formulas by their truth tables, with rows $\mathsf{T}\mathsf{T}$, $\mathsf{T}\mathsf{F}$, $\mathsf{F}\mathsf{T}$, $\mathsf{F}\mathsf{F}$.

1. The formula $p \to (p \lor q)$ is false only if $p$ is $\mathsf{T}$ and $p \lor q$ is $\mathsf{F}$, which is impossible. So its column is $\mathsf{T}, \mathsf{T}, \mathsf{T}, \mathsf{T}$, and it is a tautology.
2. The formula $(p \lor q) \land \neg p \land \neg q$ needs $p$ and $q$ both false and $p \lor q$ true. This is impossible, so it is a contradiction.
3. The formula $p \to (p \land q)$ has column $\mathsf{T}, \mathsf{F}, \mathsf{T}, \mathsf{T}$. It is a contingency.

The typical use is to recognize a step of a proof as an instance of a tautology, which justifies the step.
A trap: a contingency can have a tautology as a substitution instance, as $p \to (p \land q)$ becomes $p \to (p \land p)$ when $q$ is replaced by $p$.

### L3 human

In the semantics of propositional logic, $\models \varphi$ means that every valuation $v$ of the variables gives $v(\varphi) = \mathsf{T}$.
By the soundness and completeness theorems for a standard Hilbert system, the tautologies are exactly the formulas provable from no assumptions.
The set of tautologies is decidable, but deciding it is complete for the class [[co-NP|NBSD5FG7NY]], the complement of [[satisfiability|QN7M1JCGT9]].
Indeed, $\varphi$ is a tautology exactly when $\neg \varphi$ is not satisfiable.
In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], some classical tautologies, such as $p \lor \neg p$ and Peirce's law $((p \to q) \to p) \to p$, are not provable.
For this reason Lean 4 derives excluded middle, as `Classical.em`, from the axiom of choice and not from its constructive core rules.
