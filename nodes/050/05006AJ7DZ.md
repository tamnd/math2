## Summary

A tautology is a statement that is true because of its form alone, whatever its parts say.
Take the sentence "$7$ is prime or $7$ is not prime".
With $p$ for "$7$ is prime", its form is $p \lor \neg p$, and its truth table has two rows.

| $p$ | $\neg p$ | $p \lor \neg p$ | $p \land \neg p$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| T | F | T | F |
| F | T | T | F |

The column of $p \lor \neg p$ is T in both rows, so the sentence is true even for a reader who knows nothing about the number $7$.
In general, a formula of [[propositional logic|09K9MWD2QQ]] is a tautology when it is true for every assignment of truth values to its variables, see the [[definition|0D1AHYPM8N]].
A contradiction, such as $p \land \neg p$ in the last column, is false for every assignment.
A contingency is true for some assignments and false for others.
Every formula is of exactly one of these three kinds, and a [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]] tells which: all T, all F, or mixed.

## Motivation

Some statements are true because of the facts they describe, and some are true because of their form alone.
"$7$ is prime" is true because of a fact about $7$, but "$7$ is prime or $7$ is not prime" is true because every statement of the form $p \lor \neg p$ is true.
A proof may use a step of such a form without further justification.
So it is useful to know exactly which forms are always true, and to have a method that finds them.
The tautologies are these forms, and the truth table is the method.

## Notation

The metalanguage writes $\models \varphi$ for "$\varphi$ is a tautology".
Some books call a tautology a valid formula, and write $\models \varphi$ in the same sense.
The constant $\top$ denotes a fixed tautology and $\bot$ a fixed contradiction.
In tables, T and F are also written $1$ and $0$, and then a tautology has a column of $1$s.
In Lean 4 with Mathlib, the law $p \lor \neg p$ is `Classical.em`, and the law $\neg\neg p \leftrightarrow p$ is `Classical.not_not`.
The everyday word "tautology", which means saying the same thing twice, is a different sense.

## Remarks

### Three kinds of formula

The formula $p \to q$ shows the third kind.
Its column in the rows TT, TF, FT, FF is T, F, T, T, so it is neither always true nor always false.
A formula $\varphi$ is a tautology exactly when $\neg \varphi$ is a contradiction, by the table of [[negation|0XR7A7RSXB]].
The truth table decides in finitely many steps which of the three kinds a formula is.

### Laws that proofs use

Most laws of logic that proofs use are tautologies.
[[$(p \land (p \to q)) \to q$ is a tautology|9SPM8WSVGS]] gives the law behind [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]], checked by a $4$-row table.
The [[law of excluded middle|0RYYK8PG4S]] $p \lor \neg p$ is checked in the [[proof of the law of excluded middle by a truth table|ZNQN1JW1V3]].
The [[law of noncontradiction|MP4R7T546Z]] is the tautology $\neg(p \land \neg p)$.
[[The truth table of $((p \to q) \land (q \to r)) \to (p \to r)$|VP6RN56Z5A]] proves the chain rule for implications.
[[Peirce's law by the assume-false method|FPDDSFR941]] proves $((p \to q) \to p) \to p$ without a full table.

### Formulas that are not tautologies

[[$(p \to q) \to (q \to p)$ is not a tautology|529N3DA95K]]: it is false in the row where $p$ is F and $q$ is T, so it is a contingency.
One false row is enough to stop a formula from being a tautology.
The sentence "$2 + 2 = 4$" is true, but it is not a tautology.
Its propositional form is a single variable $p$, and $p$ can be F.

### Substitution

If $\varphi$ is a tautology with variables $p_1, \dots, p_n$, and $\psi_1, \dots, \psi_n$ are any formulas, then replacing each $p_i$ by $\psi_i$ at every occurrence gives a tautology.
The reason is that any assignment gives values to $\psi_1, \dots, \psi_n$, and these values form a row of the table of $\varphi$, where the value is T.
[[A substitution instance of $p \to (q \to p)$|D6RT06GKC3]] uses this to show that $(r \land s) \to (\neg r \to (r \land s))$ is a tautology without a new table.
The converse fails: $p \to p$ is a tautology and an instance of $p \to q$, which is not a tautology.

### Equivalence and valid arguments

Two formulas $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]] exactly when $\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi$ is a tautology.
An argument from $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_k$ to $\psi$ is valid exactly when $(\varphi_1 \land \dots \land \varphi_k) \to \psi$ is a tautology, and then $\psi$ is a [[tautological consequence|4N2HHE7PC1]] of the premises.
If $\varphi$ and $\varphi \to \psi$ are tautologies, then $\psi$ is a tautology.
In each row both are T, and by the table of [[material implication|0ZNX6DQ3EJ]] this forces $\psi$ to be T.

### Watch out

A tautology is true by its form, not by its content, so not every true statement is a tautology.
A formula that is not a tautology is usually not a contradiction either: $p \to q$ is a contingency.
Substitution must replace every occurrence of a variable by the same formula, and it may replace only variables.
Passing from $p \lor \neg p$ to $p \lor q$ replaces $\neg p$, which is not a variable, and the result is not a tautology.

### History

Ludwig Wittgenstein, in the Tractatus of 1921, called a proposition true in every row of its truth table a tautology.
He called one false in every row a contradiction.
In the same year, Emil Post proved that the tautologies are exactly the theorems of the propositional calculus of Principia Mathematica.

### Try it

Is $((p \to q) \land \neg q) \to \neg p$ a tautology?
Assume a row makes it F.
Then $\neg p$ is F, so $p$ is T, and the antecedent is T, so $p \to q$ is T and $\neg q$ is T.
From $p$ T and $p \to q$ T we get $q$ T, but $\neg q$ T gives $q$ F, which is a contradiction.
So it is a tautology, the law behind modus tollens.

Is $(p \lor q) \to (p \land q)$ a tautology?
In the row TF it is $\mathrm{T} \to \mathrm{F} = \mathrm{F}$, and in the row TT it is T.
So it is a contingency.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A tautology is a statement that is true because of its shape, whatever its parts mean.
"It is raining or it is not raining" is always true, so its shape "$p$ or not $p$" is a tautology.
A contradiction is always false: "it is raining and it is not raining".
Most statements, like "if it rains, the road is wet", are true in some cases and false in others.
These are contingencies.
A truth table checks every case and tells which kind a statement is.

### L2 human

We classify three formulas by their truth tables, with the rows TT, TF, FT, FF.

1. The formula $p \to (p \lor q)$ is false only if $p$ is T and $p \lor q$ is F, which is impossible.
   So its column is T, T, T, T, and it is a tautology.
2. The formula $(p \lor q) \land \neg p \land \neg q$ needs $p$ and $q$ both false and $p \lor q$ true.
   This is impossible, so it is a contradiction.
3. The formula $p \to (p \land q)$ has the column T, F, T, T.
   It is a contingency.

In proofs, a step is justified when it is an instance of a tautology.
Watch out: a contingency can have a tautology as a substitution instance.
Replacing $q$ by $p$ turns $p \to (p \land q)$ into the tautology $p \to (p \land p)$.

### L3 human

In the semantics of propositional logic, $\models \varphi$ means that every valuation $v$ of the variables gives $v(\varphi) = \mathrm{T}$.
By the soundness and completeness theorems for a standard Hilbert system, the tautologies are exactly the formulas provable from no assumptions.
The set of tautologies is decidable, but deciding it is complete for the class [[co-NP|NBSD5FG7NY]], the complement of [[satisfiability|QN7M1JCGT9]].
Indeed, $\varphi$ is a tautology exactly when $\neg \varphi$ is not satisfiable.
In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], some classical tautologies, such as $p \lor \neg p$ and Peirce's law $((p \to q) \to p) \to p$, are not provable.
For this reason Lean 4 does not get excluded middle from its constructive core rules, and derives `Classical.em` from the axiom of choice.
