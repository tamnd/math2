## Summary

Hamiltonian optics describes light rays as the trajectories of a [[Hamiltonian system|0EDKGA2FAK]].
A ray is given by a position and an optical momentum, the direction of the ray times the [[refractive index|9VGK0SP6EQ]].
With a coordinate along the optical axis playing the role of time, the rays obey Hamilton's equations.
This brings the tools of classical mechanics into optics: phase space, invariants such as the etendue, and characteristic functions of an optical system.
See the [[definition|R3CDET6AT6]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A ray of light is fixed by where it crosses a plane and in which direction it goes.
Hamiltonian optics treats these as the position and the momentum of a particle.
As the ray moves along the axis, position and direction change by the same kind of rules that move planets.
In a uniform medium the "momentum" stays constant and the ray is a straight line.
In a medium whose index changes from place to place, such as warm air over a road, the rays bend.
