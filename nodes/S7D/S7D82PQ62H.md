## Statement

Let one indivisible object be sold to $n$ risk-neutral bidders.
Bidder $i$ has a private value $v_i$, and $v_1, \dots, v_n$ are independent random variables.
Each $v_i$ has a [[cumulative distribution function|0H8H7ZTHW2]] $F_i$ with a positive density on an interval $[\underline{v}_i, \overline{v}_i]$.
Consider two auctions together with a Bayes–Nash equilibrium of each, viewed as a [[Bayesian game|2XQ7VWT8KJ]].
Assume that:

1. for every profile of values, the object goes to the same bidder in both equilibria (for example, always to the bidder with the highest value);
2. in both equilibria, each bidder $i$ with the lowest value $\underline{v}_i$ has the same expected payoff (for example $0$).

Then, for every bidder $i$ and every value $v_i$, the [[expected|0WQNRCNY3M]] payment of bidder $i$ given $v_i$ is the same in both equilibria.
In particular the two auctions give the seller the same expected revenue.

## Remarks

By the revelation principle each equilibrium can be read as a direct mechanism that is [[incentive compatible|T1GTBBMHCW]].
Let $Q_i(v_i)$ be the probability that bidder $i$ wins given $v_i$, and $U_i(v_i)$ the expected payoff.
Incentive compatibility forces $U_i$ to be convex with derivative $Q_i$, so $U_i(v_i) = U_i(\underline{v}_i) + \int_{\underline{v}_i}^{v_i} Q_i(t)\, dt$.
The expected payment is $v_i Q_i(v_i) - U_i(v_i)$, which is then fixed by the allocation rule and by $U_i(\underline{v}_i)$.

Example: two bidders with values uniform on $[0, 1]$.
In the second-price auction each bidder bids truthfully and the revenue is $\mathbb{E}[\min(v_1, v_2)] = 1/3$.
In the first-price auction the equilibrium bid is $v/2$, and the revenue is $\mathbb{E}[\max(v_1, v_2)]/2 = (2/3)/2 = 1/3$.

The theorem fails when bidders are risk-averse: then the first-price auction gives more expected revenue than the second-price auction.
It also fails without independence of the values, and when the allocation rules differ, for example when one auction has a reserve price.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Many auction formats look very different: sealed bids, rising prices, falling prices, everyone pays.
The theorem says that under standard assumptions they all earn the seller the same money on average.
What matters is who gets the object, not the details of the payment rule.

### L2 human

The theorem reduces the study of revenue to the study of allocation rules.
It is the main tool for computing equilibrium bids: one finds the bid that gives the same expected payment as in the second-price auction.
Myerson's optimal auction extends the same payment formula to find the allocation rule that maximizes revenue.
