## Counterexample

The determinant is linear in each row, so it is tempting to think that it is linear in the whole matrix; two diagonal matrices show that it is not.
The claim to refute is that $\det(A + B) = \det A + \det B$ for all real $2 \times 2$ matrices, that is, that $\det \colon M_2(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$ is a [[linear map|0NRYHJPQG2]].
Take
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. By the [[2 × 2 formula|0ZN2J42SK7]], $\det A = 1 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 0 = 0$.
2. In the same way, $\det B = 0 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0 = 0$.
3. The sum is the identity matrix, $A + B = I_2$, and $\det I_2 = 1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0 = 1$.
4. So
$$\det(A + B) = 1 \ne 0 = \det A + \det B.$$

Homogeneity fails as well.
With $A = I_2$ and the factor $2$,
$$\det(2 I_2) = 2 \cdot 2 - 0 \cdot 0 = 4, \qquad \text{but} \qquad 2 \det I_2 = 2.$$
So $\det$ is neither additive nor homogeneous of degree $1$ on $M_2(\mathbb{R})$.

## Remarks

### What goes wrong

The determinant is linear in each row while the other rows stay fixed, by [[multilinearity in rows|0PPG793642]].
In $A + B$ both rows change at once, so that rule does not apply directly.
Applied once to each row, it gives four terms:
$$\det(A + B) = \det \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix} + \det \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ b_2 \end{pmatrix} + \det \begin{pmatrix} b_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix} + \det \begin{pmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{pmatrix},$$
where $a_1, a_2$ are the rows of $A$ and $b_1, b_2$ are the rows of $B$.
The first and last terms are $\det A$ and $\det B$.
In the example, the mixed term with rows $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ is $\det I_2 = 1$, and the other mixed term has a zero row and is $0$.
So the two mixed terms carry the whole difference.

### How it changes with the size

For $n \times n$ matrices, the factor rule gives $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$, so $\det$ is homogeneous of degree $n$.
For $n = 1$ the determinant of $(a)$ is $a$, and the map is linear; the failure starts at $n = 2$.
The same pair of diagonal matrices works in every size $n \ge 2$: split $I_n$ into $\operatorname{diag}(1, 0, \dots, 0)$ and $\operatorname{diag}(0, 1, \dots, 1)$, two matrices of determinant $0$.
For special sums there are correct formulas, such as the [[matrix determinant lemma|0BC3Q6K92H]] for a change of rank one.

## Explanations

### L1 human

You might hope that the determinant of a sum is the sum of the determinants.
Here are two tables of numbers, each with determinant $0$.
Their sum is the identity table, whose determinant is $1$.
Since $0 + 0$ is not $1$, the hope fails.
Doubling a $2 \times 2$ table also multiplies its determinant by $4$, not by $2$.

### L2 human

Here is how to expand $\det(A + B)$ correctly for
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Directly, $A + B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\det(A + B) = 4 - 6 = -2$.
The four terms of the expansion are $\det A = 1$, $\det B = 1$, and the two mixed terms
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1 - 6 = -5, \qquad \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1.$$
Check: $1 + 1 - 5 + 1 = -2$.
The naive sum $\det A + \det B = 2$ is wrong even in sign.
Watch out: in a proof, never replace $\det(A + B)$ by $\det A + \det B$ when $n \ge 2$.
