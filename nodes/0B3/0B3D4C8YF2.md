## Summary

Take $(p \to q) \land \neg q$.
Replace the arrow by the law $p \to q \equiv \neg p \lor q$, and the formula becomes $(\neg p \lor q) \land \neg q$.
Now it is an "and" of two "or" conditions on single letters, and you can read it at once: $q$ must be false, and then $p$ must be false.

A formula in conjunctive normal form is a list of simple conditions that must all hold, where each condition is a choice between a few literals.
Every formula can be rewritten in this shape, and that is why programs that test formulas take their input as such lists.

A literal is a propositional variable $p$ or its negation $\neg p$.
A [[propositional formula|TT7WT5HPN9]] is in conjunctive normal form (CNF) when it has the form
$$C_1 \land C_2 \land \cdots \land C_m \qquad (m \geq 1),$$
where each $C_i$ is a disjunction of one or more literals.
Each $C_i$ is a [[clause|T3SXVH3ZZC]], so a CNF is a conjunction of clauses.
Every formula is [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]] to a formula in CNF, and the [[formal definition|07C4A8Y18K]] states the shape exactly.

## Motivation

A formula such as $\neg((p \to q) \land \neg(r \lor \neg p))$ is hard to read.
To see when it is true, you must evaluate the nested parts one at a time.
A conjunction of clauses is easy to read: it is true exactly when each clause has at least one true literal.
So a CNF says "all of these conditions hold", and each condition is a short list of alternatives.

Many problems arrive in this shape from the start.
The rules of a timetable or of a Sudoku grid are conditions that must all hold, and each rule lists the allowed choices.
Programs that decide [[satisfiability|QN7M1JCGT9]] take CNF as their standard input, and the proof system of [[resolution|ZDW6N1PGJ7]] works on clauses only.
Later pages build on CNF: [[Horn clauses|QR92EZDTKF]] are a special kind of clause, and the [[Tseitin transformation|S367PDDP61]] produces a CNF of small size.

## Notation

The connectives in a CNF are $\neg$, $\land$ and $\lor$, as in the [[propositional calculus|09K9MWD2QQ]].
A clause $l_1 \lor \cdots \lor l_k$ is often written as the set $\{ l_1, \ldots, l_k \}$, and a CNF as a set of such sets.
In set notation the empty clause $\square$ has no literals and is false under every assignment, while the empty set of clauses is true under every assignment.
Other names are clausal normal form and, in circuit design, "product of sums", where $\land$ is written as a product and $\lor$ as a sum.
The dual shape, a disjunction of conjunctions of literals, is the [[disjunctive normal form|0GVDFAY4KW]].

## Remarks

### A first conversion

Put $(p \to q) \land \neg q$ into CNF.
The only part that is not allowed is the arrow, so rewrite it with the law $\alpha \to \beta \equiv \neg \alpha \lor \beta$:
$$(p \to q) \land \neg q \;\equiv\; (\neg p \lor q) \land \neg q.$$
This is a CNF with two clauses, $\{ \neg p, q \}$ and $\{ \neg q \}$.

Check with the truth table.
The clause $\neg q$ forces $q = 0$, and then $\neg p \lor q$ forces $p = 0$.
So the CNF is true only at $p = 0$, $q = 0$, and the original formula is true there too, since $0 \to 0$ is true and $\neg 0$ is true.
At the other three rows the original formula is false: $(1, 0)$ makes $p \to q$ false, and the rows with $q = 1$ make $\neg q$ false.

The same formula is also equivalent to $\neg p \land \neg q$, a CNF with two one-literal clauses.
So a formula has many CNFs, and the conversion gives one of them.
The equivalence $(p \to q) \land \neg q \equiv \neg p \land \neg q$ is the law behind modus tollens.

### The method in three stages

Every formula $\varphi$ can be brought to CNF by laws of logic, in a fixed order.

1. Replace $\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi$ by $(\varphi \to \psi) \land (\psi \to \varphi)$, and replace $\varphi \to \psi$ by $\neg \varphi \lor \psi$.
2. Push each negation inward with [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]], and remove double negations with the [[double negation law|0NFAGWH84K]].
   The result is built from literals with $\land$ and $\lor$ only, which is negation normal form.
3. Distribute $\lor$ over $\land$, with $\alpha \lor (\beta \land \gamma) \equiv (\alpha \lor \beta) \land (\alpha \lor \gamma)$, until no $\land$ lies below a $\lor$.

Each law is a logical equivalence, so each stage keeps the truth table.
Why does stage 3 stop with a CNF?
Use [[structural induction|12MQ2NJ29A]] on a formula built from literals with $\land$ and $\lor$.
A literal is a CNF with one clause.
If $\varphi \equiv A_1 \land \cdots \land A_m$ and $\psi \equiv B_1 \land \cdots \land B_n$ are CNFs, then $\varphi \land \psi$ is the CNF with all $m + n$ clauses.
For $\varphi \lor \psi$, distribution gives
$$\varphi \lor \psi \;\equiv\; \bigwedge_{i \leq m} \bigwedge_{j \leq n} (A_i \lor B_j),$$
and each $A_i \lor B_j$ is again a clause, so this is a CNF with $mn$ clauses.
[[The CNF of (p → q) → r|EE8XHVGJ0R]] runs all three stages on a small formula.

### Reading a CNF off the truth table

There is a second route that needs no laws at all.
Let $\varphi$ have the variables $p_1, \ldots, p_n$, and take a row $a = (a_1, \ldots, a_n)$ where $\varphi$ is false.
Build the clause $M_a = l_1 \lor \cdots \lor l_n$ with $l_i = p_i$ when $a_i = 0$ and $l_i = \neg p_i$ when $a_i = 1$.
Each literal of $M_a$ is false at $a$, so $M_a$ is false at $a$.
At any other row $b$, some $b_i \neq a_i$, and then $l_i$ is true, so $M_a$ is false at the row $a$ only.

Now let $\Phi$ be the conjunction of the clauses $M_a$ over all rows $a$ where $\varphi$ is false.
Then $\Phi$ is false exactly at those rows, so $\Phi \equiv \varphi$.
The clauses $M_a$ are called maxterms, and $\Phi$ is the full CNF of $\varphi$.
A tautology has no false rows, and we use the CNF $p_1 \lor \neg p_1$ for it.
[[The CNF of exclusive or|1CGFB0YM6F]] carries out this method.

### Validity is easy, satisfiability is hard

A clause is valid exactly when it contains a pair $p$ and $\neg p$.
With such a pair, one of the two literals is true at each row.
Without one, the row that makes each literal of the clause false is well defined, and the clause is false there.
A CNF is valid exactly when each clause is valid, so you can test the validity of a CNF in one pass over it.

Satisfiability of a CNF is a different matter.
The [[Boolean satisfiability problem|3QKF32ZAKN]] for CNF, even with three literals in each clause, is [[NP-complete|REKDJ8V504]] by the [[Cook-Levin theorem|CTK8AH98Z1]].
So no fast method turns every formula into an equivalent CNF, unless P = NP: otherwise you could test the validity of any $\varphi$ fast, and $\varphi$ is unsatisfiable exactly when $\neg \varphi$ is valid.
For [[disjunctive normal form|0GVDFAY4KW]] the roles are the other way round.

### Why a CNF can be huge

Take the formula
$$\varphi_n = (x_1 \land y_1) \lor (x_2 \land y_2) \lor \cdots \lor (x_n \land y_n),$$
whose length grows linearly with $n$.
Distribution gives one clause $s_1 \lor \cdots \lor s_n$ for each choice of $s_i \in \{ x_i, y_i \}$, so $2^n$ clauses.
In fact every CNF $\Phi$ equivalent to $\varphi_n$ has at least $2^n$ clauses.

Fix a choice $s = (s_1, \ldots, s_n)$, and let $t_i$ be the other variable of the pair $\{ x_i, y_i \}$.
Let $w_s$ make each $s_i$ false, each $t_i$ true, and every other variable of $\Phi$ false.
Each $x_i \land y_i$ is false at $w_s$, so $\varphi_n$ and $\Phi$ are false there.
Hence some clause $C_s$ of $\Phi$ is false at $w_s$, and every literal of $C_s$ is false at $w_s$.
In particular no $t_i$ occurs in $C_s$ as a positive literal.

### Why a CNF can be huge, continued

Next we show that each $s_i$ occurs in $C_s$.
Suppose it does not occur as $s_i$; it cannot occur as $\neg s_i$ either, because $\neg s_i$ is true at $w_s$.
Change $w_s$ only at $s_i$, to get a row $u$ with $u(s_i) = 1$.
The clause $C_s$ does not mention $s_i$, so it is still false at $u$, and $\Phi$ is false at $u$.
But $u(x_i) = u(y_i) = 1$, so $\varphi_n$ is true at $u$, which contradicts $\Phi \equiv \varphi_n$.

So the positive literals of $C_s$ among the $x_i$ and $y_i$ are exactly $s_1, \ldots, s_n$.
Different choices $s$ give different sets, so they give different clauses, and $\Phi$ has at least $2^n$ clauses.
The [[Tseitin transformation|S367PDDP61]] avoids this blow-up with new variables.
It gives a CNF of linear size that is [[equisatisfiable|B37TYWXS74]] with $\varphi_n$, but not equivalent to it.

### What is not a CNF

The formula $\neg(p \lor q) \land r$ is not in CNF, because the negation stands in front of a compound formula; [[this non-example|6T80N3RXDM]] gives the details and the equivalent CNF $\neg p \land \neg q \land r$.
The formula $p \lor (q \land r)$ is not in CNF, because a conjunction lies inside a disjunction.
Its CNF is $(p \lor q) \land (p \lor r)$.
The formula $p \to q$ is not in CNF either, because $\to$ is not an allowed connective, but it is equivalent to the one-clause CNF $\neg p \lor q$.
In the other direction, $p \land (q \lor r)$ is in CNF but [[not in disjunctive normal form|7THX92W0AQ]].

### Watch out

Students often think that every clause must contain every variable.
Only the full CNF has that property: $(\neg p \lor q) \land \neg q$ is a CNF although its second clause has one literal.

The second slip is in De Morgan's law.
The wrong step $\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \land \neg q$ fails at $p = 1$, $q = 0$, where the left side is true and the right side is false.
The correct law changes the connective as well as the signs: $\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q$.

A third slip is to mix up CNF and DNF.
In a CNF the top connective is $\land$ and the parts are disjunctions; in a DNF it is the other way round.

### History

George Boole, in *The Laws of Thought* (1854), expanded a logical function into a sum of terms, one for each combination of the variables and their complements; see [[Boole's expansion theorem|C1ETR2KJKY]].
Grigori Tseitin published the transformation that gives equisatisfiable CNFs of linear size in 1968.
Today CNF is the standard input format of SAT solvers.

### Try it

Put $\neg(p \to q)$ into CNF.
The answer is $p \land \neg q$: first $\neg(\neg p \lor q)$, then De Morgan's law and double negation.

Is $(p \lor \neg p \lor q) \land (q \lor \neg q)$ valid?
Yes, because each clause contains a complementary pair.

How many clauses does the full CNF of $p \land q$ have?
Three, one for each false row: $(p \lor q) \land (p \lor \neg q) \land (\neg p \lor q)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A formula in conjunctive normal form is a list of rules that must all hold.
Each rule offers a few choices, such as "$p$ is true, or $q$ is false".
For example, $(p \lor q) \land (\neg p \lor \neg q)$ has two rules: at least one of $p$ and $q$ is true, and at least one of them is false.
Together the two rules say that exactly one of $p$ and $q$ is true.
Every formula of propositional logic can be rewritten as such a list of rules.

### L2 human

We put $\varphi = \neg(p \land (q \to r))$ into CNF, one law at a time.
First remove the arrow with $q \to r \equiv \neg q \lor r$:
$$\varphi \equiv \neg(p \land (\neg q \lor r)).$$
Then push the negation inward with De Morgan's law, twice, and remove the double negation:
$$\varphi \equiv \neg p \lor \neg(\neg q \lor r) \equiv \neg p \lor (\neg\neg q \land \neg r) \equiv \neg p \lor (q \land \neg r).$$
Finally distribute $\neg p \lor {}$ over the conjunction:
$$\varphi \equiv (\neg p \lor q) \land (\neg p \lor \neg r).$$
Check at the row $p = 1$, $q = 1$, $r = 0$: here $q \to r$ is false, so $\varphi$ is true, and both clauses are true because $q = 1$ and $r = 0$.
A SAT solver would receive this formula as the two clauses $\{ \neg p, q \}$ and $\{ \neg p, \neg r \}$.
The example shows the typical use: a formula from an application is rewritten into clauses, and then a clause-based method takes over.

### L3 human

CNF and DNF are the two normal forms of the free [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]] on $n$ generators.
The full CNF writes an element as a meet of coatoms, and the full DNF writes it as a join of atoms.
In the [[Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra|QC9M3Q8B8Q]] of the propositional calculus on $n$ variables, these forms show that the algebra has exactly $2^{2^n}$ elements.
In first-order logic, a formula in [[prenex normal form|CS82V969DE]] with a CNF matrix, after Skolemization, gives the clause form that first-order [[resolution|ZDW6N1PGJ7]] uses.
In complexity theory the size of the smallest CNF of a Boolean function is one of its basic measures, and the formula $\varphi_n$ above is the simplest exponential gap between formula size and CNF size.
