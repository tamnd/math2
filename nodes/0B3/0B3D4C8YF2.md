## Summary

A literal is a propositional variable $p$ or its negation $\neg p$.
A [[propositional formula|TT7WT5HPN9]] is in conjunctive normal form (CNF) when it is a conjunction $C_1 \land C_2 \land \cdots \land C_m$ with $m \geq 1$, where each $C_i$ is a disjunction of one or more literals.
Each $C_i$ is a [[clause|T3SXVH3ZZC]], so a CNF is a conjunction of clauses.
Every formula is [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]] to a formula in CNF.
CNF matters because it turns a formula into a list of simple constraints, and it is the standard input form of programs that decide [[satisfiability|QN7M1JCGT9]].

## Motivation

A formula such as $\neg((p \to q) \land \neg(r \lor \neg p))$ is hard to read and hard to test.
To see when it is true, we must evaluate the nested parts one by one.
A conjunction of clauses is easy to read: it is true exactly when each clause has at least one true literal.
So a CNF says "these conditions must all hold", and each condition is a short list of alternatives.
Many problems come in this form from the start, for example scheduling rules or the rules of a puzzle such as Sudoku.
A normal form also gives a canonical way to store and compare formulas in a program.

## Notation

The connectives are $\neg$, $\land$, $\lor$, as in the [[propositional calculus|09K9MWD2QQ]].
A clause $l_1 \lor \cdots \lor l_k$ is often written as the set $\set{l_1, \ldots, l_k}$, and a CNF as a set of such sets.
In the set notation, the empty clause $\square$ has no literals and is false under every assignment, and the empty set of clauses is true under every assignment.
Some books allow these two empty cases in the definition of CNF; we use them in the set notation only.
Other names are clausal normal form and "product of sums", the name used in circuit design, where $\land$ is written as a product and $\lor$ as a sum.
The dual form, a disjunction of conjunctions of literals, is the [[disjunctive normal form|0GVDFAY4KW]].
The precise definition is the node [[conjunctive normal form (definition)|07C4A8Y18K]].

## Remarks

### Examples

- $(p \lor \neg q) \land (q \lor r) \land \neg p$ is in CNF with three clauses; the last clause has one literal.
- $p \lor q \lor \neg r$ is in CNF with one clause, since $m = 1$ is allowed.
- $p \land \neg q$ is in CNF with two clauses of one literal each. It is also in disjunctive normal form, with one term.
- The formula $(p \to q) \to r$ is equivalent to $(p \lor r) \land (\neg q \lor r)$; [[this example|EE8XHVGJ0R]] derives the CNF with the laws of logic.
- Exclusive or, $p \oplus q$, is equivalent to $(p \lor q) \land (\neg p \lor \neg q)$; [[this example|1CGFB0YM6F]] reads the CNF off the truth table.

### Non-examples

- $\neg(p \lor q) \land r$ is not in CNF: the negation applies to a compound formula, so $\neg(p \lor q)$ is not a literal. [[This non-example|6T80N3RXDM]] gives the details.
- $p \lor (q \land r)$ is not in CNF: a conjunction occurs inside a disjunction. Its CNF is $(p \lor q) \land (p \lor r)$.
- $p \to q$ is not in CNF, because $\to$ is not one of the allowed connectives. Its CNF is the single clause $\neg p \lor q$.

### Basic properties

**Existence by the laws of logic.**
Every formula $\varphi$ is logically equivalent to a formula in CNF.
We build it in three stages.

1. Replace $\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi$ by $(\varphi \to \psi) \land (\psi \to \varphi)$, and replace $\varphi \to \psi$ by $\neg \varphi \lor \psi$. Each replacement gives an equivalent formula, by a truth table.
2. Push each negation inward with [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]], $\neg(\varphi \land \psi) \equiv \neg\varphi \lor \neg\psi$ and $\neg(\varphi \lor \psi) \equiv \neg\varphi \land \neg\psi$, and remove double negations with the [[double negation law|0NFAGWH84K]]. The result is built from literals with $\land$ and $\lor$ only (negation normal form).
3. Distribute $\lor$ over $\land$ with $\varphi \lor (\psi \land \chi) \equiv (\varphi \lor \psi) \land (\varphi \lor \chi)$ until no $\land$ lies below a $\lor$.

Stage 3 works by [[structural induction|12MQ2NJ29A]] on a formula $\varphi$ built from literals with $\land$ and $\lor$.

1. A literal $l$ is a CNF with one clause.
2. If $\varphi \equiv A_1 \land \cdots \land A_m$ and $\psi \equiv B_1 \land \cdots \land B_n$ are in CNF, then $\varphi \land \psi \equiv A_1 \land \cdots \land A_m \land B_1 \land \cdots \land B_n$, which is a CNF.
3. With the same $\varphi$ and $\psi$, distributivity gives $\varphi \lor \psi \equiv \bigwedge_{i \leq m} \bigwedge_{j \leq n} (A_i \lor B_j)$. Each $A_i \lor B_j$ is a disjunction of literals, so this is a CNF with $mn$ clauses.

**Existence by the truth table.**
Let $\varphi$ have the variables $p_1, \ldots, p_n$.
For an assignment $a = (a_1, \ldots, a_n) \in \set{0,1}^n$, let $M_a = l_1 \lor \cdots \lor l_n$, where $l_i = \neg p_i$ if $a_i = 1$ and $l_i = p_i$ if $a_i = 0$.

1. Each literal of $M_a$ is false at $a$, so $M_a$ is false at $a$.
2. At any other assignment $b \neq a$, some $b_i \neq a_i$, and then $l_i$ is true at $b$. So $M_a$ is false only at $a$.
3. Let $\Phi$ be the conjunction of the clauses $M_a$ over all $a$ with $\varphi(a) = 0$.
4. $\Phi$ is false at $b$ exactly when some $M_a$ is false at $b$, that is, exactly when $b$ is one of these $a$, that is, exactly when $\varphi(b) = 0$.
5. So $\Phi \equiv \varphi$, whenever $\varphi$ is false at some row. A tautology is equivalent to the CNF $p_1 \lor \neg p_1$.

The clauses $M_a$ are called maxterms, and $\Phi$ is the full CNF of $\varphi$.
It is the dual of the full [[disjunctive normal form|0GVDFAY4KW]]: the full CNF of $\varphi$ is the negation of the full DNF of $\neg \varphi$, after De Morgan's laws.

**Validity of a CNF is easy to test.**
A clause is valid exactly when it contains a complementary pair $p$ and $\neg p$.
If it contains such a pair, one of the two literals is true under each assignment.
If it contains no such pair, the assignment that makes each of its literals false is well defined, and it makes the clause false.
A CNF is valid exactly when each of its clauses is valid, so validity of a CNF is decided in time linear in its length.

**Satisfiability of a CNF is hard.**
The [[Boolean satisfiability problem|3QKF32ZAKN]] for CNF formulas, even with three literals in each clause, is [[NP-complete|REKDJ8V504]] by the [[Cook-Levin theorem|CTK8AH98Z1]].
So the transformation of a formula into an equivalent CNF cannot be efficient in general, unless P = NP; the next property shows that it can be exponential.

**Exponential blow-up.**
Let $\varphi_n = (x_1 \land y_1) \lor (x_2 \land y_2) \lor \cdots \lor (x_n \land y_n)$, a formula of length linear in $n$.
Distribution gives a CNF with $2^n$ clauses: one clause $s_1 \lor \cdots \lor s_n$ for each choice of $s_i \in \set{x_i, y_i}$.
We show that every CNF $\Phi$ equivalent to $\varphi_n$ has at least $2^n$ different clauses.

1. Fix a choice $s = (s_1, \ldots, s_n)$ with $s_i \in \set{x_i, y_i}$, and let $t_i$ be the other variable of the pair $\set{x_i, y_i}$.
2. Let $w_s$ be the assignment with $w_s(s_i) = 0$ and $w_s(t_i) = 1$ for each $i$, and $w_s(z) = 0$ for each other variable $z$ of $\Phi$.
3. Each conjunction $x_i \land y_i$ is false at $w_s$, because $s_i$ is false. So $\varphi_n$ is false at $w_s$, and $\Phi$ is false at $w_s$, because $\Phi \equiv \varphi_n$.
4. A conjunction is false only when one of its parts is false, so some clause $C_s$ of $\Phi$ is false at $w_s$. Then each literal of $C_s$ is false at $w_s$.
5. No $t_i$ occurs in $C_s$ as a positive literal, because $w_s(t_i) = 1$.
6. Suppose that $s_i$ does not occur in $C_s$ as a positive literal, for some $i$. The literal $\neg s_i$ is true at $w_s$, so it does not occur in $C_s$ either.
7. Let $u$ agree with $w_s$ except that $u(s_i) = 1$. No literal of $C_s$ contains $s_i$, so $C_s$ is still false at $u$, and so $\Phi$ is false at $u$.
8. But $u(x_i) = u(y_i) = 1$, so $\varphi_n$ is true at $u$. This contradicts $\Phi \equiv \varphi_n$. So each $s_i$ occurs in $C_s$ as a positive literal.
9. By steps 5 and 8, the positive literals of $C_s$ among $x_1, y_1, \ldots, x_n, y_n$ are exactly $s_1, \ldots, s_n$.
10. Two different choices $s \neq s'$ give different sets in step 9, so $C_s \neq C_{s'}$. There are $2^n$ choices, so $\Phi$ has at least $2^n$ different clauses.

The [[Tseitin transformation|S367PDDP61]] avoids this blow-up by adding new variables: it gives a CNF of linear size that is [[equisatisfiable|B37TYWXS74]] with $\varphi$, but not equivalent to it.

### History

George Boole expanded logical functions into sums of products of variables and their complements in "The Laws of Thought" (1854); see [[Boole's expansion theorem|C1ETR2KJKY]].
In 1968 Grigori Tseitin published the transformation that gives equisatisfiable CNFs of linear size.
Today CNF is the input format of almost all SAT solvers.

### Common errors

- To think that every clause must contain every variable. Only the full CNF has this property; a CNF may have clauses of any length.
- To confuse CNF with DNF. In a CNF the top connective is $\land$ and the clauses are disjunctions; in a DNF it is the other way round.
- To think that a CNF is easy to test for satisfiability because it is easy to test for validity. Validity of a CNF is a linear-time check, but satisfiability of a CNF is NP-complete.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A formula in conjunctive normal form is a list of rules that must all hold.
Each rule offers a few choices, such as "$p$ is true, or $q$ is false".
For example, $(p \lor q) \land (\neg p \lor \neg q)$ has two rules: "at least one of $p$, $q$ is true" and "at least one of them is false".
Together the two rules say that exactly one of $p$ and $q$ is true.
Every formula of propositional logic can be rewritten as such a list of rules.

### L2 human

We put $\varphi = \neg(p \land (q \to r))$ into CNF.

1. Remove the implication: $q \to r \equiv \neg q \lor r$, so $\varphi \equiv \neg(p \land (\neg q \lor r))$.
2. Apply De Morgan's law to the outer negation: $\varphi \equiv \neg p \lor \neg(\neg q \lor r)$.
3. Apply De Morgan's law again: $\neg(\neg q \lor r) \equiv \neg\neg q \land \neg r$, so $\varphi \equiv \neg p \lor (\neg\neg q \land \neg r)$.
4. Remove the double negation: $\varphi \equiv \neg p \lor (q \land \neg r)$.
5. Distribute: $\varphi \equiv (\neg p \lor q) \land (\neg p \lor \neg r)$.
6. Check at one row: at $p = 1$, $q = 1$, $r = 0$, the formula $q \to r$ is false, so $\varphi$ is true; and both clauses are true, since $q = 1$ and $r = 0$.

The typical use is to prepare a problem for a SAT solver: the solver receives the clauses $\set{\neg p, q}$ and $\set{\neg p, \neg r}$.
A trap is step 3: De Morgan's law changes $\lor$ into $\land$, and students often forget to negate each part.

### L3 human

CNF and DNF are the two normal forms of the free [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]] on $n$ generators: the full CNF writes an element as a meet of coatoms, and the full DNF as a join of atoms.
In the [[Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra|QC9M3Q8B8Q]] of the propositional calculus, the existence of these forms says that this algebra is the finite Boolean algebra with $2^{2^n}$ elements when there are $n$ variables.
In first-order logic, a formula in [[prenex normal form|CS82V969DE]] whose matrix is in CNF, after Skolemization, gives the clause form used by [[resolution|ZDW6N1PGJ7]].
In complexity theory, the size of the smallest CNF of a Boolean function is its CNF complexity.
The blow-up above is the simplest example of an exponential gap between formula size and CNF size; circuit and proof complexity study many such gaps.
