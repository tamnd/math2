## Statement

Every linear operator on a finite-dimensional space splits the space into cyclic pieces, and if the annihilators of the pieces are made to divide each other, then the list of annihilators is unique.

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$ and let $T \colon V \to V$ be linear.
Then there are an integer $r \ge 0$ and nonzero vectors $v_1, \dots, v_r \in V$ with these two properties.

1. $V = Z(v_1; T) \oplus \cdots \oplus Z(v_r; T)$ is an [[internal direct sum|0XYYT5KNTX]] of [[$T$-cyclic subspaces|42MMQBTP0M]].
2. The $T$-annihilators $d_i = m_{v_i}$ are nonconstant monic polynomials with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$, where $\mid$ means [[divides|0PG2H7WKGG]].

The number $r$ and the polynomials $d_1, \dots, d_r$ are determined by $T$ alone: every choice of vectors with properties 1 and 2 gives the same $r$ and the same $d_i$.
In addition, $d_r = m_T$ is the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] of $T$, and $d_1 d_2 \cdots d_r = \chi_T$ is the [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] of $T$.
The polynomials $d_1, \dots, d_r$ are the [[invariant factors|F7GJPMVBXA]] of $T$.

## Remarks

### A first decomposition

Take $A = \operatorname{diag}(2, 2, 3)$ acting on $\mathbb{Q}^3$.
The vector $v_2 = e_1 + e_3$ has $A v_2 = 2 e_1 + 3 e_3$, and the two vectors $v_2, A v_2$ span the plane $\operatorname{span}\{e_1, e_3\}$, since
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = 1 \ne 0.$$
On that plane $A$ has the eigenvalues $2$ and $3$, so the annihilator of $v_2$ is $d_2 = (x - 2)(x - 3)$.
The vector $v_1 = e_2$ is an eigenvector, so $Z(v_1) = \mathbb{Q} e_2$ and $d_1 = x - 2$.
So $\mathbb{Q}^3 = Z(v_1) \oplus Z(v_2)$ with $d_1 \mid d_2$.
Check: $d_1 d_2 = (x - 2)^2 (x - 3) = \chi_A$, and $d_2$ is the minimal polynomial, because $A$ is diagonal with the two distinct entries $2$ and $3$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Finite dimension is needed.
The identity map of an infinite-dimensional space has only lines as cyclic subspaces, since $Z(v) = Fv$ for each $v$.
So the space is not a finite direct sum of cyclic subspaces.

The divisibility condition is what makes the list unique.
For $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ the axes give $F^2 = Fe_1 \oplus Fe_2$, with the annihilators $x - 1$ and $x - 2$.
The single vector $(1, 1)$ is also enough, since $(1, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$ are a basis, and then $F^2 = Z((1, 1))$ with annihilator $(x - 1)(x - 2)$.
Only the second decomposition meets the condition, and the theorem says that it gives the invariant factors.
The condition that the $d_i$ are nonconstant only rules out the useless summands $Z(0) = 0$.

### What it gives you

In the basis $v_i, T v_i, \dots, T^{\deg d_i - 1} v_i$ of each summand, $T$ acts by the companion matrix of $d_i$.
So $T$ has the matrix $C(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus C(d_r)$, and this is the [[rational canonical form|042JWAR3Q0]] of $T$.

Several facts can then be read off the list.
The operator $T$ is cyclic exactly when $r = 1$, that is, when $m_T = \chi_T$.
The minimal and the characteristic polynomial have the same irreducible factors, because each $d_i$ divides $d_r$.
The number $r$ is the least number of cyclic subspaces whose sum is $V$.
It also equals the largest of the numbers $\dim \ker(T - \lambda)$, for $\lambda$ in an algebraic closure of $F$, because a root of $d_1$ is a root of every $d_i$.

### The converse

Suppose that $V = Z(w_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus Z(w_t)$ with nonconstant monic annihilators $e_1 \mid \cdots \mid e_t$.
The uniqueness part says that then $t = r$ and $e_i = d_i$ for every $i$.
The vectors themselves are far from unique: in the example above, $v_2 = e_1 + e_3$ can be replaced by $e_1 + 5 e_3$, or by $e_1 + e_2 + e_3$ with $v_1 = e_2$ kept.

### Watch out

A decomposition into cyclic subspaces does not by itself give the invariant factors.
For $\operatorname{diag}(2, 2, 3)$ the three axes are cyclic subspaces with the annihilators $x - 2$, $x - 2$ and $x - 3$.
These do not divide each other in a chain, since $x - 2 \nmid x - 3$.
The correct move is to merge pieces with coprime annihilators, as $e_1$ and $e_3$ were merged above.

### Generalizations

The theorem is the structure theorem for finitely generated torsion modules over the principal ideal domain $F[x]$, applied to the [[module of $T$|KGVTFBZX59]].
Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the same theorem is the [[structure theorem for finite abelian groups|0RY0B72XAS]], with integers in place of polynomials.
Hoffman and Kunze prove a stronger form inside linear algebra: each proper $T$-admissible subspace $W_0$ has a complement of the form $Z(v_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus Z(v_r)$.
They list the annihilators in the opposite order, with $p_1 = m_T$ and $p_k \mid p_{k-1}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear map on a big space can look complicated.
The theorem cuts the space into pieces, and on each piece the map does the simplest thing possible.
It pushes one starting vector along a chain, $v$, then $Tv$, then $T^2 v$, until the chain closes up.
Each piece comes with a polynomial that says how the chain closes, and each polynomial divides the next.
These polynomials describe the map completely, up to a change of basis.

### L2 human

Here is a typical problem: find the invariant factors of $A = J_2(0) \oplus J_1(0)$, the $3 \times 3$ matrix with a single $1$ in position $(1, 2)$.
Then $A e_1 = 0$, $A e_2 = e_1$ and $A e_3 = 0$.

The vector $e_2$ gives the chain $e_2 \mapsto e_1 \mapsto 0$, so $Z(e_2) = \operatorname{span}\{e_1, e_2\}$ with annihilator $x^2$.
The vector $e_3$ is killed by $A$, so $Z(e_3) = F e_3$ with annihilator $x$.
The two pieces together give $F^3$, and $x \mid x^2$, so the invariant factors are $d_1 = x$ and $d_2 = x^2$.
Check: $d_1 d_2 = x^3 = \chi_A$, and $A^2 = 0 \ne A$, so $m_A = x^2 = d_2$.

The typical use is a step towards the rational canonical form, here $C(x) \oplus C(x^2)$.
The trap is to split $F^3$ into the three axes.
The line $F e_2$ is not invariant, because $A e_2 = e_1$ leaves it, so it is not a cyclic subspace of $A$.

### L3 human

For the module $V_T$ over the principal ideal domain $F[x]$, the theorem is the invariant factor form of the structure theorem, with no free part because $V$ has finite dimension.
The elementary divisor form splits each $Z(v_i)$ further into cyclic pieces whose annihilators are powers $p^e$ of irreducible polynomials.
Over an algebraically closed field these powers are $(x - \lambda)^e$, and the pieces give the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].
The same theorem classifies finite abelian groups, and the analogy between $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$ runs through the whole theory, up to the [[Smith normal form of the characteristic matrix|92VZF0QRM8]].
