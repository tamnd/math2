## Definition

Consider a hot expanding universe described by the [[Friedmann equations|PPHFS2P4G4]].
Its temperature $T$ falls as the inverse of the [[scale factor|J70F32P86W]].
*Big Bang nucleosynthesis* is the network of nuclear reactions that acts between temperatures of about $1$ MeV and $10$ keV.
It converts free protons and neutrons into the light nuclei ${}^2\mathrm{H}$, ${}^3\mathrm{He}$, ${}^4\mathrm{He}$ and ${}^7\mathrm{Li}$.
Its output is the set of primordial abundances of these nuclei, as functions of the baryon-to-photon ratio $\eta = n_b / n_\gamma$ and of the expansion rate.

## Remarks

The main steps are as follows.
While weak interactions are fast, the neutron-to-proton ratio follows the [[Boltzmann distribution|GYNMZ9201M]] $n/p = e^{-Q / k T}$, with $Q \approx 1.293$ MeV the neutron-proton mass difference.
The weak rates freeze out near $k T \approx 0.8$ MeV, leaving $n/p \approx 1/6$, which neutron decay lowers to about $1/7$.
Deuterium is destroyed by the many photons until $k T$ drops to about $0.1$ MeV; then nearly all neutrons are bound into ${}^4\mathrm{He}$.

Example: if all neutrons end in ${}^4\mathrm{He}$ and $n/p = 1/7$, the helium mass fraction is
$$Y_p = \frac{2 (n/p)}{1 + n/p} = \frac{2/7}{8/7} = 0.25.$$
Check: each helium nucleus has two neutrons and two protons, so the helium mass is about twice the neutron mass, in units of the nucleon mass.

Non-example: the formation of carbon and heavier elements does not belong to Big Bang nucleosynthesis.
There are no stable nuclei of mass $5$ or $8$, and the density falls too fast, so heavier elements are made later in stars.
