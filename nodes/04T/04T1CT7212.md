## Statement

"If it rains, then the street is wet" says the same as "if the street is not wet, then it does not rain".
The second sentence is the contrapositive of the first.

Let $\varphi$ and $\psi$ be formulas of propositional logic.
The contrapositive of the [[implication|0ZNX6DQ3EJ]] $\varphi \to \psi$ is the implication $\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi$, where $\neg$ is [[negation|0XR7A7RSXB]].
The law of contraposition says that an implication and its contrapositive are [[logically equivalent|06GYGS41JK]]:
$$\varphi \to \psi \;\equiv\; \neg \psi \to \neg \varphi.$$
Equivalently, the biconditional $(\varphi \to \psi) \leftrightarrow (\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi)$ is a [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]].

The law has two directions.
Direction (a) says that $\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi$ is a [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]] of $\varphi \to \psi$.
Direction (b) says that $\varphi \to \psi$ is a logical consequence of $\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi$.
Both directions hold in classical logic.
In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], direction (a) holds and direction (b) does not hold in general.

In Mathlib, the classical equivalence is `not_imp_not`, stated as `¬a → ¬b ↔ b → a`.
Direction (a) alone is `mt`, of type `(a → b) → ¬b → ¬a`, and its proof uses no classical axiom.
Here `¬a` is `Not a`, which Lean defines as `a → False`.

## Remarks

### Checking the law on the rain example

Let $p$ be "it rains" and $q$ be "the street is wet".
The [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]] compares $p \to q$ with $\neg q \to \neg p$.

| $p$ | $q$ | $\neg q$ | $\neg p$ | $p \to q$ | $\neg q \to \neg p$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | F | F | T | T |
| T | F | T | F | F | F |
| F | T | F | T | T | T |
| F | F | T | T | T | T |

Both sentences fail in the same single row: rain on a dry street.
In every other row both are true, so they are always true together or false together.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The statement puts no condition on $\varphi$ and $\psi$, but the form of the contrapositive and the logic in use both matter.
Both parts must be negated, since without the negations we get the [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] $\psi \to \varphi$.
Both parts must change places, since without the swap we get the [[inverse|VB7B8766D2]] $\neg \varphi \to \neg \psi$.
Each of these differs from $\varphi \to \psi$ in the rows where exactly one of $\varphi$ and $\psi$ is true.

Direction (b) needs classical logic, through the [[law of excluded middle|0RYYK8PG4S]] or [[double negation elimination|0NFAGWH84K]].
In intuitionistic logic take $\varphi = \neg\neg r$ and $\psi = r$ for a propositional variable $r$.
Then $\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi$ is $\neg r \to \neg\neg\neg r$, which holds intuitionistically.
But $\varphi \to \psi$ is $\neg\neg r \to r$, which does not.

### What it gives you

The proof method [[proof by contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]] proves $\varphi \to \psi$ by proving $\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi$.
The method uses direction (b), so it is a classical method.
The rule [[modus tollens|32AQPJQQAM]] infers $\neg \varphi$ from $\varphi \to \psi$ and $\neg \psi$, and it is direction (a) followed by [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]].
The converse and the inverse of an implication are equivalent, because the inverse is the contrapositive of the converse.

The law applies inside quantifiers: $\forall x\, (P(x) \to Q(x))$ is equivalent to $\forall x\, (\neg Q(x) \to \neg P(x))$.
For sets, $A \subseteq B$ holds exactly when $B^c \subseteq A^c$, where ${}^c$ is the [[complement|0RKF1ZT5JJ]] in a fixed universe.
A side hypothesis can stay in place: $(\varphi \land \chi) \to \psi$ is equivalent to $(\neg \psi \land \chi) \to \neg \varphi$.
Both formulas are false exactly when $\varphi$ and $\chi$ are true and $\psi$ is false.

### The converse

The law is a biconditional, so the question of its converse concerns each direction.
The converse of direction (a) is direction (b).
It is true in classical logic, by the [[truth table proof|SWH5EJP8PS]].
It is false in intuitionistic logic, by the example with $\neg\neg r$ above.
The [[proof by rules of inference|QB99VRZQ4Y]] shows the one step that uses excluded middle.

### Generalizations

In a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]], $a \le b$ holds exactly when $b' \le a'$, where $'$ is the complement.
Complementation reverses the order, and the law of contraposition is the case of the algebra of formulas.
In a [[Heyting algebra|NG943XNQWH]], $a \le b$ implies $\neg b \le \neg a$, but the converse can fail, which is the algebraic form of the intuitionistic status of the two directions.
With two hypotheses, $(\varphi_1 \land \varphi_2) \to \psi$ is equivalent to $\neg \psi \to (\neg \varphi_1 \lor \neg \varphi_2)$, by contraposition and [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]].

### Watch out

"If $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even" is not the contrapositive of "if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd".
It is the inverse, and it is equivalent to the converse "if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd".
That statement happens to be true, but proving it does not prove the original claim.

### Try it

Write the contrapositive of "if $x^2 \ne 1$, then $x \ne 1$" for a real number $x$.
It is "if $x = 1$, then $x^2 = 1$", which is true, so the original is true.

## History

Modus tollens, the rule of inference behind direction (a), is in the list of indemonstrable arguments of the Stoic logician Chrysippus.
The truth-table proof uses the method of truth tables, which Wittgenstein and Post used in 1921.
Brouwer rejected the law of excluded middle, and in the intuitionistic logic that grew from his work only direction (a) remains valid.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"If it rains, then the street is wet" says the same as "if the street is not wet, then it does not rain".
Both sentences are broken by the same single situation: rain on a dry street.
In every other situation both sentences are kept.
So a statement and its contrapositive are always true together or false together.

### L2 human

We use the law to show that for every integer $n$, if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd.
Let $\varphi$ be "$n^2$ is odd" and $\psi$ be "$n$ is odd".
The contrapositive $\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi$ reads "if $n$ is even, then $n^2$ is even".
Suppose $n$ is even, so $n = 2k$ for an integer $k$.
Then $n^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2)$, which is even.
So the contrapositive is true for every $n$, and the law of contraposition gives the original implication.
The contrapositive helps here because its hypothesis "$n = 2k$" is easier to use than "$n^2$ is odd".

### L3 human

In a Boolean algebra negation is an order-reversing involution, and contraposition says that it reverses the order in both directions.
In a Heyting algebra, $\neg a = a \to 0$ is only order reversing, and $\neg\neg a \ge a$ can be strict, so only direction (a) survives.
This is why Mathlib has the constructive `mt` and the classical `not_imp_not` as two separate results.

Contraposition also appears as duality in other places.
In a [[Galois connection|Q5JHEPRYQ6]] between two orders, an antitone pair of maps reverses order in the same way.
For a linear map $T$ between finite-dimensional spaces, $T$ is injective exactly when its [[transpose|09DXWYRD43]] is surjective.
In the classical [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]], negation moves a formula from one side of a sequent to the other.
This symmetry between the two sides is the structural reason for classical contraposition.
