## Summary

A written [[proof|0B94TKFQJX]] leaves out steps, and reading it actively means supplying them: finding the reason for each line, testing each line on an example, and seeing where each hypothesis is used.
Here is a three-line proof from a textbook.

> Let $n$ be an integer not divisible by $3$.
> Then $n = 3k \pm 1$ for some integer $k$, so $n^2 = 9k^2 \pm 6k + 1 = 3(3k^2 \pm 2k) + 1$.
> Hence $n^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$.

An active reader writes notes like these next to it.

| Line | Note in the margin |
|---|---|
| statement | hypothesis: $3 \nmid n$; conclusion: $n^2$ leaves remainder $1$ on division by $3$; test $n = 5$: $25 = 3 \cdot 8 + 1$ |
| "$n = 3k \pm 1$" | why? Division by $3$ leaves remainder $1$ or $2$, since $3 \nmid n$, and $3m + 2 = 3(m + 1) - 1$; the hypothesis is used here |
| the square | expand $(3k \pm 1)^2$; for $n = 5 = 3 \cdot 2 - 1$, $k = 2$ and $3(12 - 4) + 1 = 25$ |
| "hence" | $3k^2 \pm 2k$ is an integer, so this is the definition of the congruence |
| looking back | the hypothesis is needed, since $n = 6$ gives $36 \equiv 0$; idea: write $n$ near a multiple of $3$ and square |

The proof is correct, but the reason behind "$n = 3k \pm 1$" is not written in it, and a reader who does not supply it has not checked the proof.
Reading this way is slow, and it is how a reader comes to know why a result holds rather than only that it does.

## Motivation

A proof in a textbook or a paper is compressed, because the writer leaves out the steps that the intended reader can supply, as [[level of detail in proofs|VYGBV5D8Y4]] explains.
A reader who reads the lines as a story feels that each line is plausible and yet cannot say why each follows.
That feeling of understanding is often false: the same reader cannot rebuild the proof a day later or use its idea on a similar problem.
Passive reading also cannot detect an error, because a false step looks just like a true one.
Active reading replaces the question "does this look right?" with "what exactly justifies this line?".

## Remarks

### The method

Start with the statement.
Find its [[hypotheses and its conclusion|W48XN2MEM9]], [[unpack the definitions|41JZ39GE3F]] of its terms, and test it on one or two small examples, which then serve as test cases for the proof.

Read the proof once quickly for its shape: the [[proof method|G9JFJGY4TQ]], such as induction or contradiction, and the main claims.
Then read it line by line, and write next to each line its reason, whether a hypothesis, a definition, an earlier line or a known result.
When a line has no visible reason, fill the gap in writing, or mark it and return later if it is too large.
Mark where each hypothesis is used, and run the proof on an example with its numbers.

Finish by summarizing the proof in two to four sentences that name the main idea, and by looking back: is each hypothesis needed, does the converse hold, and what else does the method prove?

### Two readings in detail

[[Reading a proof that divisibility is transitive|1W6P12X54M]] writes the reason for each line, marks the use of both hypotheses, and runs the proof with $3$, $6$ and $24$.
The reading adds two reasons the text omits, that the multipliers are integers and that their product is an integer.

[[Self-explanation of a proof that a square is 0 or 1 modulo 4|8FG8SXKP0B]] explains each line through the definitions and earlier lines, and then tests both cases with $n = 6$ and $n = 7$.

### Paraphrase is not explanation

Repeating a line in other words, such as "so $n^2 = 4k^2$", tests nothing.
Explaining it, such as "since $n = 2k$ by the first line, squaring gives $n^2 = 4k^2$", tests the reason.
[[Paraphrase without checking|61PMPT3QQZ]] shows a reader who restates every line of a false proof, accepts "take square roots of both sides", and misses that the claim fails for $x = -3$.

Checking the statement on many examples is not reading the proof either.
The examples test the statement and say nothing about the argument, as [[forty prime values of a quadratic|H75AB4EJT5]] shows.

### Unused hypotheses

A hypothesis that the proof never uses means one of two things: the theorem holds without it, or the proof has an error.
The reader has to decide which.
A reader of a proof of the [[boundedness theorem|0ATVXC3NCC]] should mark where it uses that the interval $[a, b]$ is closed and bounded.
The function $f(x) = 1/x$ on $(0, 1]$ shows that the result fails without that hypothesis.

### Limits of the method

The method stalls when the reader lacks the background, because then most lines have no visible reason and the definitions and standard results must come first.
A very long proof read line by line from the start also defeats it.
There the reader first finds the structure, the lemmas and the main theorem, and reads each lemma as a separate proof.
A step checked by a computer program cannot be read line by line, and the reader can only check what the program computes and why its cases are complete.

### Watch out

A common error is to stop at the first unclear line.
A better habit is to mark the line, accept it for the moment, read on, and come back, since a later line often explains an earlier one.

Do not expect speed.
A first active reading of a ten-line proof can take half an hour, and that is normal.

### History

Pólya's "How to Solve It" (1945) made "looking back" the last of its four stages of solving a problem.
Halmos's memoir "I Want to Be a Mathematician" (1985) advises readers to fight a text rather than only read it, asking whether each hypothesis is needed and whether the converse holds.
In 2012 Juan Pablo Mejía-Ramos and four coauthors published a model for assessing proof comprehension in undergraduate mathematics.
In 2014 Mark Hodds, Lara Alcock and Matthew Inglis reported that a short training in self-explanation improved undergraduates' proof comprehension.

### Try it

In the opening proof, where would the reasoning break if the hypothesis were "$n$ is odd" instead of "$3 \nmid n$"?
At the line "$n = 3k \pm 1$": the odd number $n = 3$ is $3k$ with $k = 1$, and $9 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$.

A proof says "since $p$ is prime and $p \mid ab$, $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$".
What reason should the reader write in the margin?
[[Euclid's lemma|051VYKA1EX]], which needs $p$ to be prime.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Reading a proof is not like reading a story.
Each line of a proof must follow from the lines before it, and a good reader checks this for each line.
For example, a proof may say "the number $n$ is even, so $n^2$ is even".
The reader stops and asks why: $n = 2k$ for some whole number $k$, so $n^2 = 4k^2 = 2 \cdot 2k^2$, which is even.
The reader also tries the claim on a number, for example $n = 6$ gives $n^2 = 36$, which is even.

### L2 human

We read a proof that if $a$ is an odd integer, then $8$ divides $a^2 - 1$.

> Write $a = 2k + 1$.
> Then $a^2 - 1 = 4k(k + 1)$, and $k(k + 1)$ is even.
> Hence $8$ divides $a^2 - 1$.

The first line uses the hypothesis through the definition of an odd integer, which provides an integer $k$; for $a = 5$, $k = 2$.
The second line expands $(2k + 1)^2 - 1 = 4k^2 + 4k$, and $4 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 24$ agrees for $a = 5$.
The claim "$k(k + 1)$ is even" has no reason in the text, so we supply one: of two consecutive integers, one is even.
Then $k(k + 1) = 2m$ and $a^2 - 1 = 8m$, with $m = 3$ for $a = 5$.
Looking back, the hypothesis is needed, since $a = 2$ gives $a^2 - 1 = 3$.

Watch out: the line "$k(k + 1)$ is even" is a real step of the proof, and a reader who skips it has not checked the proof.

### L3 human

Research in mathematics education separates local and holistic understanding of a proof.
Local understanding concerns single lines: the meaning of the terms, the logical status of each statement, and the justification of each claim.
Holistic understanding concerns the whole: the main idea, the division into lemmas, the transfer of the method, and its illustration by examples.
The assessment model of Mejía-Ramos and coauthors uses this division to build tests of comprehension, and [[teaching reasoning and proof|VHGA6J4NZK]] surveys the research.

Self-explanation is a general method for learning from worked examples in many sciences, and the work of Hodds, Alcock and Inglis applies it to proofs.
A proof assistant gives the extreme form of local reading, a check of each line against the rules, but not holistic understanding, as [[proof assistants and their design|YM6PRDE88V]] discusses.
With a verdict instead of understanding as the aim, the same steps become [[checking a proof|4M98ZZNK0E]].
