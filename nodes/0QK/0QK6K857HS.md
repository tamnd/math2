## Summary

A bilinear form on a finite-dimensional space is fixed by its values on pairs of basis vectors, and the matrix of the form is the square table of those values.
With the matrix, every value of the form becomes a row times a matrix times a column.
Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$ with a [[basis|0KCQ9Y3QKA]] $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$, and let $B$ be a [[bilinear form|0SQZMXB6GP]] on $V$.
The *matrix of $B$* in this basis is the $n \times n$ matrix $A$ with entries
$$A_{ij} = B(e_i, e_j).$$
If $x$ and $y$ are the [[coordinate vectors|04CYQHN6MC]] of $u$ and $v$, written as columns, then
$$B(u, v) = x^{\mathsf{T}} A y.$$
The [[formal definition|0P24YW565F]] states the same.

## Motivation

A linear map is fixed by its values on a basis, and its matrix stores these values.
A bilinear form has two slots, so it is fixed by its values on pairs of basis vectors.
There are $n^2$ such pairs, and a square table is the natural place to store $n^2$ numbers.
With this table, the value $B(u, v)$ becomes the product $x^{\mathsf{T}} A y$, which a student or a computer can evaluate.

The matrix also turns properties of the form into properties of a matrix.
Symmetry of the form becomes symmetry of $A$, nondegeneracy becomes invertibility, and the rank of the form is the rank of $A$.
The price is that the matrix depends on the basis, and the next pages, on the [[change of basis rule|0SPYAGE7T0]] and on [[congruent matrices|WJWNYSQKK8]], say how.

## Notation

Books write $[B]_{\mathcal{E}}$, $[B]$, $M_B$ or simply $A$, where $\mathcal{E} = (e_1, \dots, e_n)$ is the basis.
When $B$ is an [[inner product|0NB0AWGJBW]], the matrix is the [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]] of the basis, and many books use the name Gram matrix for the matrix of any bilinear form.
Watch the index order: the row index belongs to the first slot, and the column index belongs to the second slot.
The matrix of a form and the [[matrix of a linear map|0YQSGNYE3K]] are both square tables, but they are different objects and they change in different ways under a change of basis.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take $B(x, y) = 2 x_1 y_1 - x_1 y_2 + 4 x_2 y_1$ on $\mathbb{Q}^2$ with the standard basis.
Each entry $A_{ij} = B(e_i, e_j)$ is the coefficient of $x_i y_j$:
$$B(e_1, e_1) = 2, \quad B(e_1, e_2) = -1, \quad B(e_2, e_1) = 4, \quad B(e_2, e_2) = 0.$$
So
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 4 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
For $x = (1, 1)$ and $y = (1, 2)$ we get $Ay = (2 - 2, 4 + 0) = (0, 4)$, and $x^{\mathsf{T}} A y = 0 + 4 = 4$.
Check: the formula gives $2 - 2 + 4 = 4$.

### Why the formula holds

Write $u = \sum_i x_i e_i$ and $v = \sum_j y_j e_j$.
Linearity in the first slot and then in the second slot expands the value into $n^2$ terms:
$$B(u, v) = \sum_{i} x_i B(e_i, v) = \sum_{i} \sum_{j} x_i B(e_i, e_j) y_j = \sum_{i, j} x_i A_{ij} y_j.$$
The last sum is the product $x^{\mathsf{T}} A y$.
Conversely, every $n \times n$ matrix is the matrix of exactly one form, namely $(u, v) \mapsto x^{\mathsf{T}} A y$.
So forms on $V$ and $n \times n$ matrices correspond one to one, by the [[correspondence theorem|ZVMJAF7D53]].

### More examples

The [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]] on $F^n$ has the identity matrix in the standard basis, because $e_i \cdot e_j$ is $1$ for $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise.
The determinant form $x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1$ on $F^2$ has $B(e_1, e_2) = 1$ and $B(e_2, e_1) = -1$, so its matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
The [[form $x_1 y_1 + 2 x_1 y_2 + 3 x_2 y_2$ on $\mathbb{Q}^2$|N9CJZW5596]] has the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, which is not symmetric.
The [[trace form on $M_2(F)$|CWKB533GHM]] has a $4 \times 4$ permutation matrix with determinant $-1$ in the basis of matrix units.

### What it is not

The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not the matrix of the dot product on $\mathbb{R}^2$ in any basis.
In a basis with the columns of $P$, the dot product has matrix $P^{\mathsf{T}} P$, whose determinant is $(\det P)^2 > 0$, but this matrix has determinant $1 - 4 = -3$.
A map on $V \times V$ that is not bilinear, such as the [[map $x_1 y_1 + x_2$|5JPJW2GJR6]], has no matrix of this kind: its values on basis pairs do not determine it, since the term $x_2$ is invisible when $x = e_1$.

### What the matrix tells you

The form is [[symmetric|0AXZNE5XNH]] exactly when $A$ is a [[symmetric matrix|0VR671PHE5]], because $B(e_i, e_j) = B(e_j, e_i)$ for all $i, j$ is the condition $A_{ij} = A_{ji}$, and bilinearity extends it from basis vectors to all vectors.
The form is [[nondegenerate|01NHFZF2SK]] exactly when $A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]], by the [[criteria for nondegeneracy|3ND2FNJ38Y]].
The map $u \mapsto B(u, \cdot)$ into the dual space has matrix $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ in the bases $(e_i)$ and $(e_i^*)$, and the map $v \mapsto B(\cdot, v)$ has matrix $A$.
In a new basis whose vectors are the columns of an invertible matrix $P$, the matrix becomes $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$, by the [[change of basis rule|0SPYAGE7T0]].

### Watch out

The matrix of a form does not change to $P^{-1} A P$ in a new basis.
For the dot product on $\mathbb{R}^2$ and the new basis $(2, 0), (0, 1)$, take $P = \operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$:
$$P^{-1} I P = I, \qquad P^{\mathsf{T}} I P = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The second answer is right, since $(2, 0) \cdot (2, 0) = 4$.
A second trap is the index order: if you put the first slot in the column index, you get $A^{\mathsf{T}}$, and the formula becomes $B(u, v) = y^{\mathsf{T}} A x$.

### Try it

First, find the matrix of $B(x, y) = x_1 y_2 - 3 x_2 y_2$ on $F^2$.
The values are $B(e_1, e_1) = 0$, $B(e_1, e_2) = 1$, $B(e_2, e_1) = 0$ and $B(e_2, e_2) = -3$, so the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$.

Second, write out the form on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
It is $x_1 y_1 + 3 x_1 y_2 + 3 x_2 y_1$, a symmetric form.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A bilinear form on the plane is fixed by four numbers.
They are its values on the pairs $(e_1, e_1)$, $(e_1, e_2)$, $(e_2, e_1)$ and $(e_2, e_2)$ of basis vectors.
We write them in a square of two rows and two columns.
For the dot product the square has $1, 0$ on top and $0, 1$ below.
From the square you can compute the form on any pair of vectors, with no other information.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: find all vectors $u$ with $B(u, v) = 0$ for every $v$, for the form with matrix
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$$
on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
The value $x^{\mathsf{T}} A y$ is $0$ for every $y$ exactly when the row $x^{\mathsf{T}} A$ is zero, that is, when $A^{\mathsf{T}} x = 0$.
Here $A^{\mathsf{T}} = A$, and $A x = 0$ means $x_1 + 2 x_2 = 0$, so the answer is the line spanned by $(-2, 1)$.
Check: $(-2, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} A = (-2 + 2, -4 + 4) = (0, 0)$.
The question about all $v$ became one linear system, and this is the main use of the matrix: rank, kernel and determinant answer questions about the form.
Watch out: for a form that is not symmetric you must solve $A^{\mathsf{T}} x = 0$, not $A x = 0$.

### L3 human

The matrix of a form is the coordinate form of the isomorphisms $\operatorname{Bil}(V) \cong V^* \otimes V^* \cong \operatorname{Hom}(V, V^*)$, in the basis $e_i^* \otimes e_j^*$.
A linear map $V \to V$ obeys the law $P^{-1} A P$, but a map $V \to V^*$ obeys the law $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$, because the dual basis changes by the inverse transpose.
So the matrices of forms and of operators carry two different actions of $GL_n(F)$, and their invariants differ: the determinant of a form is defined only up to nonzero squares.
For a [[sesquilinear form|054YEQ0Y8M]] the same idea gives the law $P^{\mathsf{T}} A \overline{P}$, and for an inner product the matrix is the Gram matrix of the sub-area on [[inner product spaces|1D4JTZ9VS3]].
