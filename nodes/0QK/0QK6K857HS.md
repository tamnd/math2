## Summary

Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$ with a [[basis|0KCQ9Y3QKA]] $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$, and let $B$ be a [[bilinear form|0SQZMXB6GP]] on $V$.
The matrix of $B$ in this basis is the $n \times n$ matrix $A$ with the entries $A_{ij} = B(e_i, e_j)$.
If $x$ and $y$ are the [[coordinate vectors|04CYQHN6MC]] of $u$ and $v$, then $B(u, v) = x^T A y$.
The [[formal definition|0P24YW565F]] states the same.
The matrix matters because it turns every question about a form on a finite-dimensional space into a question about a square matrix.

## Motivation

A linear map is fixed by its values on a basis, and its matrix stores these values.
A bilinear form has two slots, so it is fixed by its values on pairs of basis vectors.
There are $n^2$ such pairs, and a square array is the natural way to store $n^2$ numbers.
With this array, the value $B(u, v)$ becomes the product $x^T A y$, which a computer or a student can evaluate.

## Notation

- Common symbols are $[B]_{\mathcal{E}}$, $[B]$, $M_B$ and $A$.
- When $B$ is an inner product, the matrix of $B$ in a basis is the [[Gram matrix|00SPJSDT3Z]] of the basis. Many books use the name Gram matrix for the matrix of any bilinear form.
- The matrix of a form and the [[matrix of a linear map|0YQSGNYE3K]] are different objects. They change in different ways when the basis changes.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The [[dot product|0S52TNNTH9]] on $F^n$ has the identity matrix in the standard basis, because $e_i \cdot e_j$ is $1$ for $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise.
- The [[trace form on $M_2(F)$|CWKB533GHM]] has a $4 \times 4$ matrix with determinant $-1$ in the basis of matrix units.
- The determinant form $x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1$ on $F^2$ has the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, because $B(e_1, e_2) = 1$ and $B(e_2, e_1) = -1$.
- The [[form $x_1 y_1 + 2x_1 y_2 + 3x_2 y_2$ on $\Q^2$|N9CJZW5596]] has the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, which is not symmetric.
- The form $x_1 y_1 - x_2 y_2$ on $\R^2$ has the matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$.

### Non-examples

- The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not the matrix of the dot product on $\R^2$ in any basis. Each such matrix is $P^T P$ with determinant $(\det P)^2 > 0$, but this matrix has determinant $-3$.
- A map on $V \times V$ that is not bilinear, such as the [[map $x_1 y_1 + x_2$|5JPJW2GJR6]], has no matrix of this kind. Its values on basis pairs do not determine it.

### Basic properties

- [[Bilinear forms correspond to matrices and to linear maps into the dual|ZVMJAF7D53]]: the map $B \mapsto A$ is a linear bijection onto $M_n(F)$, and $B$ also corresponds to the linear map $V \to V^*$, $u \mapsto B(u, \cdot)$.
- The matrix of $u \mapsto B(u, \cdot)$ in the bases $(e_i)$ and $(e_i^*)$ is $A^T$, and the matrix of $v \mapsto B(\cdot, v)$ is $A$.
- $B$ is [[symmetric|0AXZNE5XNH]] exactly when $A$ is a [[symmetric matrix|0VR671PHE5]].
- $B$ is [[nondegenerate|01NHFZF2SK]] exactly when $A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]].
- In a new basis the matrix becomes $P^T A P$, by the [[change of basis rule|0SPYAGE7T0]].

### Common errors

- The rule $A_{ij} = B(e_i, e_j)$ puts the first slot in the row index. If we swap the slots, we get $A^T$, and the formula becomes $B(u, v) = y^T A x$.
- The matrix of a form does not change to $P^{-1} A P$ in a new basis. It changes to $P^T A P$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A bilinear form on the plane is fixed by four numbers.
These are its values on the pairs $(e_1, e_1)$, $(e_1, e_2)$, $(e_2, e_1)$ and $(e_2, e_2)$ of basis vectors.
We write them in a square of two rows and two columns.
For the dot product the square is $1, 0$ on top and $0, 1$ below.
From the square we can compute the form on any pair of vectors.

### L2 human

We compute the matrix of $B(x, y) = 2x_1 y_1 - x_1 y_2 + 4x_2 y_1$ on $\Q^2$.

1. $B(e_1, e_1) = 2$, from the term $2x_1 y_1$.
2. $B(e_1, e_2) = -1$, from the term $-x_1 y_2$.
3. $B(e_2, e_1) = 4$, from the term $4x_2 y_1$.
4. $B(e_2, e_2) = 0$, because no term contains $x_2 y_2$.
5. So $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 4 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and the coefficient of $x_i y_j$ is the entry $A_{ij}$.
6. A check: for $x = (1, 1)$ and $y = (1, 2)$, $Ay = (0, 4)$ and $x^T A y = 4$. The formula gives $2 - 2 + 4 = 4$.

The typical use is a test: the rank, the determinant and the symmetry of $A$ tell us the properties of $B$.
A trap: the coefficient of $x_1 y_2$ goes to place $(1, 2)$, not $(2, 1)$.

### L3 human

The matrix of a form is the coordinate form of the isomorphism $\operatorname{Bil}(V) \cong V^* \otimes V^* \cong \Hom(V, V^*)$.
A linear map $V \to V$ has the matrix law $P^{-1} A P$, but a map $V \to V^*$ has the law $P^T A P$, because the dual basis changes with the inverse transpose.
So the matrices of forms and of operators live in different representations of $GL_n(F)$.
For a [[sesquilinear form|054YEQ0Y8M]] the same idea gives the law $P^T A \overline{P}$, and for an inner product the matrix is the Gram matrix of [[sub-area 14|1D4JTZ9VS3]].
