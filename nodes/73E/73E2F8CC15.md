## Proof

We show that the determinant is a homomorphism onto $\{1, -1\}$ with kernel $SO(n)$, and then we solve the conditions for $SO(2)$ directly.

**Part 1.**

1. For $Q \in O(n)$ we have $\det Q \in \{1, -1\}$, by [[the determinant theorem for orthogonal matrices|QDZJMFATRW]], so $\det$ is a map from $O(n)$ to $\{1, -1\}$.
2. For $P, Q \in O(n)$ we have $\det(PQ) = \det P \det Q$, by [[multiplicativity of the determinant|04CPJ6AF00]], so the map is a group homomorphism.
3. The matrix $D = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, \dots, 1)$ has the orthonormal columns $-e_1, e_2, \dots, e_n$, so $D \in O(n)$, and $\det D = -1$.
4. Also $\det I_n = 1$, so the map takes both values and is surjective.
5. Its [[kernel|0Z424HK8CQ]] is $\{ Q \in O(n) : \det Q = 1 \}$, which is $SO(n)$ by definition.

**Part 2.**

6. The kernel of a homomorphism is a normal subgroup, so $SO(n)$ is normal in $O(n)$.
7. By the [[first isomorphism theorem|0CXXJNG5B6]] and step 4, $O(n)/SO(n) \cong \{1, -1\}$, a group of order $2$, so $SO(n)$ has index $2$.
8. Let $Q \in O(n)$ with $\det Q = -1$. Then $\det(DQ) = (-1)(-1) = 1$, so $DQ \in SO(n)$.
9. Since $D^2 = I_n$, we get $Q = D(DQ) \in D \cdot SO(n)$.
10. Conversely, for $P \in SO(n)$ step 2 gives $\det(DP) = (-1) \cdot 1 = -1$, so $D \cdot SO(n)$ is exactly the set of elements with determinant $-1$.

**Part 3.**

11. Let $z = a + ic$ with $a^2 + c^2 = 1$. The columns $(a, c)$ and $(-c, a)$ of $\phi(z)$ have length $1$, and their dot product is $-ac + ca = 0$, so $\phi(z)$ is orthogonal by [[the equivalent conditions|F7M8ZXB4P7]].
12. Its determinant is $a \cdot a - (-c) \cdot c = a^2 + c^2 = 1$, so $\phi(z) \in SO(2)$.
13. Let $w = b + id$ be a second point of the circle. Then $zw = (ab - cd) + i(ad + bc)$.
14. Multiplying the matrices gives
$$\phi(z)\phi(w) = \begin{pmatrix} a & -c \\ c & a \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} b & -d \\ d & b \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ab - cd & -(ad + bc) \\ ad + bc & ab - cd \end{pmatrix} = \phi(zw),$$
so $\phi$ is a homomorphism.
15. If $\phi(z) = I_2$, then $a = 1$ and $c = 0$, so $z = 1$, and $\phi$ is injective.
16. For surjectivity, let $Q = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in SO(2)$. Its first column $(a, c)$ has length $1$.
17. The second column $(b, d)$ is a unit vector orthogonal to $(a, c)$. The vectors orthogonal to $(a, c)$ are the multiples of $(-c, a)$, and the unit ones are $(-c, a)$ and $(c, -a)$.
18. If $(b, d) = (c, -a)$, then $\det Q = -a^2 - c^2 = -1$, which contradicts $Q \in SO(2)$. So $(b, d) = (-c, a)$.
19. So $Q = \phi(a + ic)$ with $\lvert a + ic \rvert = 1$, and $\phi$ is an isomorphism onto $SO(2)$.
20. Every $z$ in the circle group is $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i \sin\theta$ for some $\theta \in \R$, so $SO(2) = \{ R(\theta) : \theta \in \R \}$. $\blacksquare$

## Idea

The determinant sends $O(n)$ onto a group with two elements, and the rotations are what it sends to $1$, so they are a normal subgroup of index $2$.
In the plane, the first column of a rotation is a point of the unit circle.
That column fixes the second column up to a sign, and the condition $\det = 1$ fixes the sign.
So a rotation of the plane is the same thing as one point of the unit circle.

## Variants

One can skip the first isomorphism theorem and count cosets by hand: steps 8 to 10 show directly that the cosets are $SO(n)$ and $D \cdot SO(n)$.
Part 3 also follows from [[the classification of O(2)|C97Z06HHB2]], which lists every element of $O(2)$ at once.
A third route uses complex numbers from the start: the map $z \mapsto \alpha z$ with $\lvert \alpha \rvert = 1$ is a real linear map of $\C = \R^2$ that keeps lengths, and its matrix in the basis $1, i$ is $\phi(\alpha)$.
