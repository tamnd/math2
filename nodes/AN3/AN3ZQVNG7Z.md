## Statement

Let $L$ and $M$ be the luminosity and the mass of a main-sequence star, and $L_\odot$ and $M_\odot$ those of the Sun.
Then approximately $L / L_\odot \approx (M / M_\odot)^a$, where the exponent $a$ depends on the range of masses.
For stars of roughly solar mass, $a$ is between $3$ and $4$, and $a = 3.5$ is the value usually quoted.

## Remarks

The relation is empirical, and it is explained by the equations of [[stellar structure|SBKFDNEMFF]] through scaling arguments.
For a star of radius $R$ in [[hydrostatic equilibrium|4FC5XQA2RK]] made of ideal gas, the central temperature scales like $T \propto M/R$.
If energy is carried by radiation through gas of constant opacity, the luminosity scales like $L \propto R^4 T^4 / (\kappa M) \propto M^3$, independent of $R$.
Varying opacity and the change from radiative to convective transport bend the power law, which is why the exponent changes along the main sequence.
Example: a star of $10$ solar masses with $a = 3.5$ has luminosity about $10^{3.5} \approx 3000$ solar luminosities.
The relation does not hold for giants or white dwarfs, whose luminosity is not fixed by their mass alone.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Heavier stars are much brighter than lighter ones, far more than in proportion to their mass.
A star twice as heavy as the Sun is roughly ten times as bright.
So heavy stars burn their fuel much faster and live much shorter lives.

### L2 human

Since the fuel of a star is proportional to $M$ and the rate of burning is $L$, the main-sequence lifetime scales like $M / L \propto M^{1 - a}$, about $M^{-2.5}$.
The relation is a direct consequence of the [[Vogt–Russell theorem|RED4EF0DRT]]: on the main sequence, mass is the only free parameter.
It is used to estimate the masses of single stars from their luminosity, after calibration with binary stars, whose masses can be measured directly.
