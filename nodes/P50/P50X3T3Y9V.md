## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and let $B$ be the matrix that we get by squaring each entry:
$$B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 4 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
We show that $B$ is not $q(A)$ for any polynomial $q$, so it is not a [[polynomial in A|0E3MV6E6MG]].

1. Every [[value q(A)|0BNEXSKNRV]] is a combination of the powers $I, A, A^2, \dots$, and each power commutes with $A$.
   So $q(A)A = Aq(A)$ for every polynomial $q$.
2. Compute $AB$, row of $A$ times column of $B$:
$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 4 & 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 \\ 2 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 4 & 2 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
3. Compute $BA$ in the same way:
$$BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 2 & 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 \\ 4 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 2 & 4 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}.$$
4. So $AB \ne BA$, and by step 1, $B$ is not $q(A)$ for any polynomial $q$.
5. For comparison, the true square is the matrix product
$$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = 2I,$$
   which differs from $B$.

## Remarks

The entrywise product of two matrices of the same size is the Hadamard product.
It is a useful operation in statistics and in matrix analysis, but it does not follow the rule $q(A)v = q(\lambda)v$ for eigenvectors.
Here $A$ has the eigenvector $v = (1, \sqrt{2})^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $\sqrt{2}$, since $Av = (\sqrt{2}, 2)^{\mathsf{T}} = \sqrt{2}\, v$, and $A^2 v = 2v$ as the rule predicts.
But $Bv = (\sqrt{2}, 4)^{\mathsf{T}}$, which is not a multiple of $v$, because the ratio of the entries would have to be $\sqrt{2}$ in the first place and $4/\sqrt{2} = 2\sqrt{2}$ in the second.

The failure depends on the entries.
For a diagonal matrix, squaring each entry gives the same matrix as $A^2$, so the two operations agree there.
For $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the entrywise square is the matrix itself, which is $q(A)$ for $q(x) = x$, while $A^2 = I$; so the entrywise square can be a polynomial in $A$ without being $A^2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The square of a matrix means the matrix times itself, with rows times columns.
Squaring each entry is a different operation and gives a different matrix.
Here the entrywise square does not even commute with $A$, and every polynomial in $A$ does.

### L2 human

The lesson is that $A^2$ in $p(A)$ is always a matrix product, and the test in step 1 is a quick way to rule out a candidate: a matrix that does not commute with $A$ is not a polynomial in $A$.
Watch out: the converse of the test is false in general, since the identity matrix of size $2$ commutes with every matrix, and only multiples of $I$ are polynomials in $I$.
