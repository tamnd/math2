## Summary

A topological insulator is a material that is insulating in its interior but conducts along its boundary.
The reason is that its occupied electron bands carry a nontrivial topological invariant.
In the narrow sense it is a time-reversal-invariant insulator of spin-$1/2$ electrons with a nontrivial $\Z_2$ invariant.
This $\Z_2$ invariant was introduced by Charles Kane and Eugene Mele in 2005.
The invariant cannot change without closing the bulk energy gap or breaking the symmetry, and it forces gapless states on the surface.
The integer quantum Hall states, with their [[TKNN invariant|CX8GDHENTT]], are the earlier example without time-reversal symmetry.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An ordinary insulator does not conduct because all its electron bands are either full or empty.
A topological insulator is an insulator of this kind inside, but its full bands are twisted in a way that cannot be undone smoothly.
Where the material meets the vacuum, which is untwisted, the twist must unwind, and that forces conducting states to appear at the surface.
These surface states are robust: impurities that respect time reversal cannot remove them.
Mercury telluride quantum wells and bismuth selenide crystals are standard examples.
