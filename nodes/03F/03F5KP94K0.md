## Definition

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be an [[endomorphism|0PTF1PTJK0]].
Choose a basis $B$ of $V$.
The *determinant* of $T$ is
$$\det T = \det [T]_B,$$
the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]] of the [[matrix of $T$|0YYMY95FWS]] in the basis $B$, with $B$ used for both the domain and the codomain.
For $V = 0$, the convention is $\det T = 1$.

## Notation

- $\det T$ or $\det(T)$ is the determinant of $T$.
- $[T]_B$ is the $n \times n$ matrix whose column $j$ holds the coordinates of $T(b_j)$ in $B = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$.

## Remarks

The definition does not depend on the choice of $B$.
Another basis gives a [[similar|0E89HQATM8]] matrix $P^{-1} [T]_B P$, and the [[invariance statement|0KMN32XGPF]] shows that its determinant is the same.
The convention for $V = 0$ agrees with the value $1$ for the empty matrix.
Over a commutative ring, the same definition applies to an endomorphism of a free module of finite rank.

## Explanations

### L1 human

We define the determinant of a linear map with its matrix in some basis.
Another basis changes the matrix, but not the determinant.
So the determinant belongs to the map itself, like the trace.

### L2 human

Let $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ and $T(x, y) = (x + y, 2y)$.

1. In the standard basis, $[T] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\det T = 2$.
2. In the basis $(1, 0), (1, 1)$: $T(1, 0) = (1, 0)$ and $T(1, 1) = (2, 2) = 2 \cdot (1, 1)$.
3. So the matrix in this basis is $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$, again with determinant $2$.

The typical use is to compute the determinant in the basis where the matrix is simplest.
A trap: a basis for the domain and a different basis for the codomain give a matrix whose determinant is not $\det T$.

### L3 human

A definition without bases uses the top exterior power: $\Lambda^n T$ acts on the line $\Lambda^n V$ as multiplication by $\det T$.
In Mathlib, `LinearMap.det` is defined for every module over a commutative ring, with the value $1$ when no finite basis exists.
