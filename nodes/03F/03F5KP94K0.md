## Definition

The determinant of a linear map from a finite-dimensional space to itself is the determinant of its matrix in any one basis.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be an [[endomorphism|0PTF1PTJK0]].
Choose a basis $B$ of $V$.
The *determinant* of $T$ is
$$\det T = \det [T]_B,$$
the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]] of the [[matrix of $T$|0YYMY95FWS]] in the basis $B$, with $B$ used for both the domain and the codomain.
For $V = 0$ we set $\det T = 1$.

## Notation

We write $\det T$ or $\det(T)$.
For $B = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$, the matrix $[T]_B$ is the $n \times n$ matrix whose column $j$ holds the coordinates of $T(b_j)$ in $B$.

## Remarks

Each condition in the definition is there for a reason.
The map must be an endomorphism, so that its matrices are square and have a determinant at all.
The space must be finite-dimensional, so that the matrix is finite.
The one basis $B$ must serve both sides, because a second basis on the codomain would multiply the determinant by the determinant of a change of basis matrix.

The definition asks for a choice of $B$, so it is only meaningful because the choice does not matter.
Another basis gives a [[similar|0E89HQATM8]] matrix $P^{-1} [T]_B P$, and the [[invariance theorem|0KMN32XGPF]] shows that its determinant is the same.
The convention for $V = 0$ agrees with the value $1$ of the determinant of the empty matrix.
Over a commutative ring the same definition applies to an endomorphism of a free module of finite rank.

For a small example, take $T(x, y) = (2x, 3y)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
Its matrix in the standard basis is $\operatorname{diag}(2, 3)$, so $\det T = 6$.
A case that fails a condition is $T(x, y) = (x, y, 0)$ from $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\mathbb{R}^3$: it is not an endomorphism, so [[it has no determinant|WYHE1FWPHN]].
The [[concept page|0X0HQYSZ0K]] collects the rules and the uses of the determinant of a map.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To find the determinant of a linear map, we write its matrix in some basis and take the determinant of that matrix.
Another basis changes the matrix but not the determinant.
So the determinant belongs to the map itself, in the same way as the trace does.

### L2 human

Let $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ and $T(x, y) = (x + y, 2y)$.
In the standard basis the matrix and its determinant are
$$[T] = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det T = 1 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 0 = 2.$$
In the basis $(1, 0), (1, 1)$ we find $T(1, 0) = (1, 0)$ and $T(1, 1) = (2, 2) = 2 \cdot (1, 1)$.
So the matrix in this basis is $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$, again with determinant $2$.
The typical use is to compute the determinant in the basis where the matrix is simplest.
Watch out: a basis for the domain and a different basis for the codomain give a matrix whose determinant is in general not $\det T$.

### L3 human

A definition without bases uses the top [[exterior power|0MDMZ965VE]]: $\Lambda^n T$ acts on the line $\Lambda^n V$ as multiplication by $\det T$.
In Mathlib, `LinearMap.det` is defined for every module over a commutative ring, and it takes the value $1$ when no finite basis exists.
