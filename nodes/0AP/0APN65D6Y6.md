## Statement

When we add a subspace $U$ to a subspace $W$ and then divide by $W$, what is left is exactly the part of $U$ that is new beyond $U \cap W$.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $U$ and $W$ be subspaces of $V$.
Then the map
$$U / (U \cap W) \to (U + W) / W, \qquad u + (U \cap W) \mapsto u + W,$$
is well defined, and it is a linear [[isomorphism|0XSJ25Z9M4]].
Here $U + W$ is the [[sum of the subspaces|0RMKABS2S5]], $U \cap W$ is their [[intersection|0V33SPH7RR]], and both sides are [[quotient spaces|0SVR29SV1V]].

## Remarks

### A first case

In $\R^3$, let $U$ be the $xy$-plane and let $W$ be the line $\R(0, 1, 1)$.
A vector $t(0, 1, 1)$ lies in $U$ only when its last entry $t$ is $0$, so $U \cap W = \{0\}$ and $U / (U \cap W)$ is just $U$.
The sum $U + W$ has dimension $2 + 1 = 3$, so it is all of $\R^3$.
The theorem says that each coset of $W$ in $\R^3$ contains exactly one vector of the $xy$-plane.
We can see this directly, since we can subtract $c(0, 1, 1)$ from $(a, b, c)$:
$$(a, b, c) + W = (a, \, b - c, \, 0) + W.$$
Two vectors $(a, b, 0)$ and $(a', b', 0)$ of $U$ give the same coset only when their difference is some $t(0, 1, 1)$, which forces $t = 0$.
The example of [[two coordinate planes|W98F8VPP81]] works out a case where $U \cap W$ is a line.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The two subspaces must lie in one space $V$, so that $U + W$ and $U \cap W$ make sense.

The target must be $(U + W) / W$, not $V / W$.
The map $u + (U \cap W) \mapsto u + W$ into $V / W$ is still injective, but it is onto only when $U + W = V$.
For the $x$-axis $U$ and the $y$-axis $W$ in $\R^3$, the coset $(0, 0, 1) + W$ is not of the form $u + W$ with $u \in U$, since $(0, 0, 1) - (a, 0, 0)$ never lies on the $y$-axis.

The roles of $U$ and $W$ look different in the formula, but nothing in the hypotheses tells them apart.
Exchanging them gives the companion isomorphism $W / (U \cap W) \cong (U + W) / U$.

### Grassmann's formula again

Let $U$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional.
The [[dimension of a quotient space|0SXDQ6TN00]] turns the two sides of the theorem into differences of dimensions:
$$\dim U - \dim (U \cap W) = \dim (U + W) - \dim W.$$
Rearranged, this is [[Grassmann's formula|06HTJNCMPA]],
$$\dim (U + W) + \dim (U \cap W) = \dim U + \dim W,$$
and the [[proof by the second isomorphism theorem|MKF2J8YS8H]] gives the details.
For the two coordinate planes of $\R^3$, the four numbers are $3 + 1 = 2 + 2$.

### What it gives you

If $U \cap W = \{0\}$, the sum is direct and the theorem reads $(U \oplus W) / W \cong U$, as in the first case.
If $U + W = V$, it reads $V / W \cong U / (U \cap W)$, so a quotient of $V$ can be computed inside the smaller space $U$.
Together with the [[correspondence theorem|GCHY414Y11]], the theorem matches the subspaces between $U \cap W$ and $U$ with the subspaces between $W$ and $U + W$, through $X \mapsto X + W$ and back through $Y \mapsto Y \cap U$.
This matching is the modular law, the defining property of a [[modular lattice|QX7WZ92QAK]].

### Is there a converse?

The isomorphism is given by an explicit formula, so there is no converse in the usual sense.
What holds is a uniqueness statement.
A linear map $U / (U \cap W) \to (U + W) / W$ that sends the coset of each $u$ to $u + W$ is the map of the theorem, because the cosets $u + (U \cap W)$ are all the elements of the source.

### Watch out

A common slip is to write $(U + W) / W \cong U$ without checking that $U \cap W = \{0\}$.
Take $U = W = L$, a line in $\R^2$.
Then $(L + L) / L = L / L$ is the zero space, while $U = L$ is a line.
The correct statement divides $U$ by $U \cap W = L$, and indeed $L / L = \{0\}$.

## History

Emmy Noether stated the isomorphism theorems for modules in general form in a paper of 1927, and the vector space case is a special case.
The theorem is also called the diamond isomorphism theorem, after the shape of the diagram formed by $U \cap W$, $U$, $W$ and $U + W$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of $W$ as the directions you already have, and of $U$ as a new set of directions on offer.
Some of the directions in $U$ are already in $W$, namely those in the overlap $U \cap W$.
Adding $U$ to $W$ gains you exactly the other ones.
For the $xy$-plane and the $yz$-plane in space, the overlap is the $y$-axis, and the only new direction is the $x$-direction.
The theorem says that "what $U$ adds to $W$" and "what $U$ has outside the overlap" are the same space.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use, in $\R^4$.
Let $U$ be the set of $x$ with $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4 = 0$, and let $W$ be the set of $x$ with $x_1 = x_2$.
Each is the kernel of one nonzero linear form, so each has dimension $3$.

The map $\ell(x) = x_1 - x_2$ is linear from $\R^4$ onto $\R$, and its kernel is $W$.
So $\R^4 / W \cong \R$ by the [[first isomorphism theorem|0MTBGVZ4XJ]].
Now restrict $\ell$ to $U$.
Its kernel is $U \cap W$, and it is still onto $\R$, since $(1, 0, -1, 0)$ lies in $U$ and
$$\ell(1, 0, -1, 0) = 1 - 0 = 1.$$
So $U / (U \cap W) \cong \R$ as well, and $\dim (U \cap W) = 3 - 1 = 2$.

Grassmann's formula then gives $\dim (U + W) = 3 + 3 - 2 = 4$, so $U + W = \R^4$ and both sides of the theorem are copies of $\R$.
The lesson is that one linear form that kills $W$ measures both quotients at once.

### L3 human

The proof applies the first isomorphism theorem to the map $U \to (U + W) / W$, $u \mapsto u + W$, which is onto and has kernel $U \cap W$.
The same theorem holds for groups, with a normal subgroup in the role of $W$, for modules over any ring, and in every [[abelian category|Y9CZ3MTNF1]].
For groups and modules it is a step in the usual proofs of the Zassenhaus lemma and of the [[Jordan-Hölder theorem|0E088ZP533]].
In the lattice of subspaces it is the reason why that lattice is modular, even though it is not distributive once the dimension is at least $2$.
