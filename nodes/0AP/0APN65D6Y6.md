## Statement

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $U$ and $W$ be subspaces of $V$.
Then the map
$$U / (U \cap W) \to (U + W) / W, \qquad u + (U \cap W) \mapsto u + W,$$
is well defined, and it is a linear [[isomorphism|0XSJ25Z9M4]].
Here $U + W$ is the [[sum of the subspaces|0RMKABS2S5]], $U \cap W$ is their [[intersection|0V33SPH7RR]], and both sides are [[quotient spaces|0SVR29SV1V]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $U$ and $W$ must be subspaces of one space $V$, so that $U + W$ and $U \cap W$ are defined.
- The target must be $(U + W)/W$, not $V / W$. The map $U / (U \cap W) \to V / W$ is still injective, but it is not surjective unless $U + W = V$. For the $x$-axis $U$ and the $y$-axis $W$ in $\R^3$, the coset $(0, 0, 1) + W$ is not of the form $u + W$ with $u \in U$.
- The roles of $U$ and $W$ are not symmetric in the formula, but the theorem also holds with $U$ and $W$ exchanged, which gives $W / (U \cap W) \cong (U + W) / U$.

### Consequences

- For finite-dimensional $U$ and $W$, with the [[dimension of a quotient|0SXDQ6TN00]], it gives [[Grassmann's formula|06HTJNCMPA]] $\dim(U + W) + \dim(U \cap W) = \dim U + \dim W$, by [[a second proof|~db3e67d3]].
- $U + W$ is a direct sum exactly when $U \cap W = \{0\}$, and then $(U \oplus W)/W \cong U$.
- If $V = U + W$, then $V / W \cong U / (U \cap W)$.
- In the lattice of subspaces, the theorem says that the intervals $[U \cap W, U]$ and $[W, U + W]$ are isomorphic. This is the reason why the lattice is [[modular|QX7WZ92QAK]].

### Converse

The isomorphism is given by an explicit map, so there is no converse in the usual sense.
Any map $U / (U \cap W) \to (U + W)/W$ that commutes with the quotient maps from $U$ equals this one.

## History

The second isomorphism theorem for groups is due in substance to Hölder in 1889.
Noether gave the general form for modules in 1927, together with the [[first|0MTBGVZ4XJ]] and [[third|07RB3EMBWW]] theorems.
The theorem is sometimes called the diamond isomorphism theorem, after the shape of the diagram of $U \cap W$, $U$, $W$ and $U + W$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Adding $U$ to $W$ adds the same new directions as $U$ has beyond its common part with $W$.
The theorem makes this precise with quotient spaces.
It is the linear algebra form of a general algebraic theorem.

### L2 human

Let $U$ be the $xy$-plane and $W$ the $yz$-plane in $\R^3$, as in [[the example of two planes|~1d56dc39]].

1. $U \cap W$ is the $y$-axis, and $U + W = \R^3$.
2. $U / (U \cap W)$ is the set of lines $\{(a, t, 0) : t \in \R\}$ in $U$, labelled by $a$.
3. $(U + W)/W = \R^3 / W$ is the set of planes $x = a$, labelled by $a$.
4. The map sends the line with label $a$ to the plane with label $a$.

The trap is to think that the theorem needs $U \cap W = \{0\}$: it holds for all pairs of subspaces.

### L3 human

The proof applies the [[first isomorphism theorem|0MTBGVZ4XJ]] to the map $U \to (U + W)/W$, $u \mapsto u + W$, which is surjective with kernel $U \cap W$.
The theorem holds for modules over any ring and in any abelian category, where it is a statement about pullbacks and pushouts of subobjects.
It is the key step of the Schreier refinement theorem and the Jordan–Hölder theorem for modules.
