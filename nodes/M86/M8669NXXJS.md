## Summary

Fresnel diffraction, or near-field diffraction, is the diffraction of a wave observed at a moderate distance from an aperture.
At such distances the curvature of the wavefronts still matters.
It is computed with the Fresnel integral, a paraxial approximation of the [[Kirchhoff|W178NQAYS9]] and [[Rayleigh–Sommerfeld|3B5D567BEK]] formulas in which the phase is expanded to second order.
It applies when the [[Fresnel number|AK3TF7B3ZW]] is of order $1$ or larger, and it reduces to [[Fraunhofer diffraction|D7YGBADRV9]] when the Fresnel number is small.
Famous Fresnel patterns are the fringes at a straight edge, described with the [[Cornu spiral|CE8MVD4BF1]], and the [[Arago spot|C2E99WKJ4J]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Shine a laser through a small hole and put a screen not too far behind it.
The bright patch on the screen has roughly the shape of the hole, but its edges carry bright and dark rings.
Moving the screen changes the pattern, and the centre can even turn dark.
These near-field patterns are Fresnel diffraction.
