## Example

A sensor reads $2$ at time $1$, $3$ at time $2$ and $6$ at time $3$, and we want one smooth formula that agrees with all three readings.
The simplest candidate is a quadratic $p(t) = a + b t + c t^2$.
Each reading gives one condition $p(t_i) = y_i$, and each condition is linear in the unknown coefficients $a, b, c$.
So finding the curve is solving a linear system, and we do it in two ways: by the Vandermonde system and by the Lagrange basis.

**The Vandermonde system.**

1. The three readings give
$$a + b + c = 2, \qquad a + 2b + 4c = 3, \qquad a + 3b + 9c = 6.$$
2. The coefficient matrix is the [[Vandermonde matrix|5T4DY28JSK]]
$$V = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 & 9 \end{pmatrix}.$$
3. The [[Vandermonde determinant|0P84B554AS]] gives $\det V = (2 - 1)(3 - 1)(3 - 2) = 2$.
   This is not $0$, so $V$ is invertible by the [[invertible matrix theorem|ACDFDABVQK]], and the system has exactly one solution.
4. Subtract the first equation from the second and from the third.
   This gives $b + 3c = 1$ and $2b + 8c = 4$.
5. Subtract twice the first of these from the second.
   This gives $2c = 2$, so $c = 1$, then $b = 1 - 3 = -2$, and $a = 2 - b - c = 3$.
6. So $p(t) = t^2 - 2t + 3$.
   Check: $p(1) = 1 - 2 + 3 = 2$, $p(2) = 4 - 4 + 3 = 3$ and $p(3) = 9 - 6 + 3 = 6$.

**The Lagrange basis.**

1. For the nodes $1, 2, 3$ the three Lagrange polynomials are
$$L_1(t) = \frac{(t - 2)(t - 3)}{2}, \qquad L_2(t) = -(t - 1)(t - 3), \qquad L_3(t) = \frac{(t - 1)(t - 2)}{2}.$$
2. Each $L_j$ has the value $1$ at its own node $t_j$ and the value $0$ at the other two nodes.
   For example, $L_2(2) = -(1)(-1) = 1$, and $L_2(1) = L_2(3) = 0$.
3. So the three evaluations $f \mapsto f(1)$, $f \mapsto f(2)$, $f \mapsto f(3)$ form the [[dual basis|0ZD2CF6ZS0]] of $(L_1, L_2, L_3)$.
   This is the statement that [[evaluation at nodes is the dual basis of the Lagrange basis|J8RDPQXN1Z]].
4. The dual basis reads off the coordinates of a polynomial, so the coordinates of $p$ are its values:
$$p = p(1) L_1 + p(2) L_2 + p(3) L_3 = 2 L_1 + 3 L_2 + 6 L_3.$$
5. Expand each term: $2 L_1 = t^2 - 5t + 6$, $3 L_2 = -3t^2 + 12 t - 9$ and $6 L_3 = 3t^2 - 9t + 6$.
   The sum is $t^2 - 2t + 3$, the same polynomial as before.

## Remarks

### Two bases, one polynomial

The two methods describe the same polynomial in two bases of the space $P_2$ of polynomials of degree at most $2$.
In the monomial basis $(1, t, t^2)$ the coordinates of $p$ are $(a, b, c)$, and we must solve $V x = y$ to find them.
In the Lagrange basis the coordinates of $p$ are the data $y = (2, 3, 6)$ themselves, and nothing is left to solve.
So $V$ converts monomial coordinates into Lagrange coordinates, and $V^{-1}$ converts back.
In particular, column $j$ of $V^{-1}$ holds the monomial coefficients of $L_j$.
For example, column $1$ of $V^{-1}$ is $(3, -\tfrac52, \tfrac12)$, and indeed $L_1(t) = 3 - \tfrac52 t + \tfrac12 t^2$.

### The general rule

For $n + 1$ distinct nodes $t_0, \dots, t_n$ and any values $y_0, \dots, y_n$, exactly one polynomial of degree at most $n$ has $p(t_i) = y_i$ for all $i$.
The reason is the Vandermonde determinant
$$\det V = \prod_{i < j} (t_j - t_i),$$
which is not $0$ when the nodes are distinct.
For two nodes this is the familiar fact that one line passes through two points with different $t$ values.

### What changes when the data change

If two nodes are equal, then two rows of $V$ are equal and $\det V = 0$.
A solution then needs the two readings at that node to agree, and even then the polynomial is not unique.
For example, the nodes $1, 1, 2$ with values $2, 2, 3$ allow $p(t) = 1 + t$ and also $p(t) = 1 + t + (t - 1)(t - 2)$.

If you have more readings than coefficients, the system $V x = y$ usually has no solution at all.
Then you fit a polynomial by [[least squares|C170QFW8QB]] instead.

### Watch out

A high degree is not always a better fit.
For many equally spaced nodes, the interpolating polynomial agrees with the data at the nodes but can swing far away from it between them.
On a computer, a large Vandermonde system is also badly conditioned, so rounding errors grow, and the Lagrange or Newton form is the better tool.
The error bounds and the choice of nodes belong to numerical analysis.

### Try it

First, find the line through $(0, 1)$ and $(2, 5)$ by the Vandermonde system.
The equations are $a = 1$ and $a + 2b = 5$, so $b = 2$ and $p(t) = 1 + 2t$.

Second, write the quadratic through $(1, 0)$, $(2, 1)$ and $(3, 0)$ in the Lagrange basis above.
It is $0 \cdot L_1 + 1 \cdot L_2 + 0 \cdot L_3 = -(t - 1)(t - 3)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Through two points there is exactly one straight line.
Through three points with different $t$ values there is exactly one parabola, or a line if the three points happen to lie on one.
To find it, write one equation for each point and solve for the three unknown coefficients.
The three points $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$ and $(3, 6)$ give the parabola $t^2 - 2t + 3$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: a table gives $\sin$ at $0$, $\tfrac{\pi}{6}$ and $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ as $0$, $\tfrac12$ and $1$, and you want a quadratic that agrees with it.
With $s = t / (\pi / 6)$ the nodes become $0, 1, 3$, and $p(s) = a + b s + c s^2$ must satisfy $a = 0$, $b + c = \tfrac12$ and $3b + 9c = 1$.
Then $3b + 9c = 1$ and $3b + 3c = \tfrac32$ give $6c = -\tfrac12$, so $c = -\tfrac{1}{12}$ and $b = \tfrac{7}{12}$.
Check at $s = 3$: $\tfrac{21}{12} - \tfrac{9}{12} = 1$.
At $s = 2$, which is $t = \tfrac{\pi}{3}$, the quadratic gives $\tfrac{14}{12} - \tfrac{4}{12} = \tfrac56 \approx 0.833$, while $\sin \tfrac{\pi}{3} \approx 0.866$.
So three values already give a usable formula between the nodes, and the linear algebra guarantees that it is the only quadratic through them.

### L3 human

The map $P_n \to F^{n+1}$, $p \mapsto (p(t_0), \dots, p(t_n))$, is linear, and its matrix in the monomial basis is $V$.
Interpolation says that this map is an isomorphism when the nodes are distinct.
Its kernel is zero because a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $n$ has at most $n$ roots, and then rank-nullity gives surjectivity.
This proof needs no determinant, and it works over every field.
The Lagrange basis is the basis of $P_n$ that this isomorphism sends to the standard basis of $F^{n+1}$.
The Chinese remainder theorem for polynomials generalizes the statement, with pairwise coprime polynomials in place of the nodes $t - t_i$.
