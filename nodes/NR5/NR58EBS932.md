## Example

We find the polynomial $p(t) = a + b t + c t^2$ of degree at most $2$ through the three points $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$ and $(3, 6)$.
We do it in two ways: by a linear system for the coefficients, and by the Lagrange basis.

**The Vandermonde system.**

1. The condition $p(t_i) = y_i$ is linear in the unknown coefficients $a, b, c$.
2. For the three points, this gives
$$a + b + c = 2, \qquad a + 2b + 4c = 3, \qquad a + 3b + 9c = 6.$$
3. The coefficient matrix is the [[Vandermonde matrix|5T4DY28JSK]]
$$V = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 & 9 \end{pmatrix}.$$
4. By the [[Vandermonde determinant|0P84B554AS]], $\det V = (2 - 1)(3 - 1)(3 - 2) = 2$.
5. The determinant is not $0$, so $V$ is invertible, by the [[invertible matrix theorem|ACDFDABVQK]]. So the system has exactly one solution.
6. Subtract the first equation from the second and from the third: $b + 3c = 1$ and $2b + 8c = 4$.
7. Subtract twice the first of these from the second: $2c = 2$, so $c = 1$.
8. Then $b = 1 - 3 = -2$, and $a = 2 - b - c = 2 + 2 - 1 = 3$.
9. So $p(t) = t^2 - 2t + 3$.
10. Check: $p(1) = 1 - 2 + 3 = 2$, $p(2) = 4 - 4 + 3 = 3$ and $p(3) = 9 - 6 + 3 = 6$.

**The Lagrange basis.**

1. The three Lagrange polynomials for the nodes $1, 2, 3$ are
$$L_1(t) = \frac{(t - 2)(t - 3)}{2}, \qquad L_2(t) = -(t - 1)(t - 3), \qquad L_3(t) = \frac{(t - 1)(t - 2)}{2}.$$
2. Each $L_j$ has the value $1$ at the node $t_j$ and the value $0$ at the other two nodes. For example, $L_2(2) = -(1)(-1) = 1$, and $L_2(1) = L_2(3) = 0$.
3. So the three evaluations $f \mapsto f(1)$, $f \mapsto f(2)$, $f \mapsto f(3)$ form the [[dual basis|0ZD2CF6ZS0]] of $(L_1, L_2, L_3)$. This is the statement of [[evaluation at nodes is the dual basis of the Lagrange basis|J8RDPQXN1Z]].
4. The coordinates of a polynomial in a basis are the values of the dual basis on it. So $p = p(1) L_1 + p(2) L_2 + p(3) L_3 = 2 L_1 + 3 L_2 + 6 L_3$.
5. Expand: $2 L_1 = t^2 - 5t + 6$, $3 L_2 = -3t^2 + 12 t - 9$ and $6 L_3 = 3t^2 - 9t + 6$.
6. The sum is $t^2 - 2t + 3$, the same polynomial as in the first method.

## Remarks

The two methods solve the same problem in two bases of the space $P_2$ of polynomials of degree at most $2$.
In the monomial basis $(1, t, t^2)$, the coordinates of $p$ solve the system $V x = y$.
In the Lagrange basis, the coordinates of $p$ are the data $y$ itself, and no system is left to solve.
So $V$ is the change-of-coordinates matrix from the Lagrange basis to the monomial basis, read backwards: the column $j$ of $V^{-1}$ holds the coefficients of $L_j$.
For example, the column $1$ of $V^{-1}$ is $(3, -5/2, 1/2)$, and $L_1(t) = 3 - \tfrac{5}{2} t + \tfrac{1}{2} t^2$.

The general rule is the same.
For $n + 1$ distinct nodes $t_0, \dots, t_n$ and any values $y_0, \dots, y_n$, there is exactly one polynomial of degree at most $n$ with $p(t_i) = y_i$.
The reason is that the Vandermonde determinant $\prod_{i < j} (t_j - t_i)$ is not $0$ when the nodes are distinct.

If two nodes are equal, two rows of $V$ are equal and $\det V = 0$.
Then the data must agree at the equal nodes, and even then the polynomial is not unique.

With more points than coefficients, the system $V x = y$ usually has no solution.
Then we fit a polynomial by [[least squares|C170QFW8QB]] instead.
The numerical side of interpolation, such as the error bound and the choice of nodes, belongs to numerical analysis.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Through two points there is exactly one line.
Through three points with different $t$ values there is exactly one parabola, or a line if the points happen to lie on one.
To find it, we write one equation for each point and solve for the three coefficients.
The three points $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$, $(3, 6)$ give the parabola $t^2 - 2t + 3$.

### L2 human

To interpolate $n + 1$ points by a polynomial of degree at most $n$, do these steps.

1. Make the Vandermonde matrix, with row $i$ equal to $(1, t_i, t_i^2, \dots, t_i^n)$.
2. Solve $V x = y$ for the coefficients.
3. Or write $p = \sum_j y_j L_j$ with the Lagrange polynomials, and expand if you need the coefficients.

The typical use is a small table of values, where we want a formula that agrees with the table.
The trap is to use a high degree for many equally spaced points.
The polynomial agrees with the data at the nodes, but it can oscillate strongly between them.
A second trap is a large Vandermonde system on a computer: its matrix is badly conditioned, and the Lagrange or Newton form is better.

### L3 human

The map $P_n \to F^{n+1}$, $p \mapsto (p(t_0), \dots, p(t_n))$, is linear, and its matrix in the monomial basis is $V$.
Interpolation says that this map is an isomorphism when the nodes are distinct.
Its kernel is zero because a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $n$ has at most $n$ roots, and then rank-nullity gives surjectivity.
This proof does not use the determinant, and it works over every field.
The Lagrange basis is the basis of $P_n$ that this isomorphism sends to the standard basis of $F^{n+1}$.
The Chinese remainder theorem for polynomials generalizes the statement: it replaces the nodes by pairwise coprime polynomials.
