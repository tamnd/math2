## Definition

Dodgson condensation is the sequence of ever smaller matrices that we get from a square matrix by repeatedly taking connected $2 \times 2$ minors and dividing by the interior entries of the matrix two steps back.
Let $F$ be a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]], let $n \ge 2$, and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$.
Let $A^{(-1)}$ be the $(n+1) \times (n+1)$ matrix with every entry equal to $1$, and let $A^{(0)} = A$.
For $k = 1, \dots, n - 1$, let $A^{(k)}$ be the $(n-k) \times (n-k)$ matrix with entries
$$a^{(k)}_{ij} = \frac{a^{(k-1)}_{ij}\, a^{(k-1)}_{i+1,j+1} - a^{(k-1)}_{i,j+1}\, a^{(k-1)}_{i+1,j}}{a^{(k-2)}_{i+1,j+1}}, \qquad 1 \le i, j \le n - k.$$
The recursion is defined when every divisor $a^{(k-2)}_{i+1,j+1}$ that occurs is nonzero.
The *Dodgson condensation* of $A$ is the sequence $A^{(0)}, A^{(1)}, \dots, A^{(n-1)}$, and its result is the single entry of $A^{(n-1)}$.

## Remarks

### Reading the recursion

The numerator is the $2 \times 2$ determinant of the block of four neighboring entries of $A^{(k-1)}$ with top left corner $(i, j)$.
The divisor sits in the middle of the matching $3 \times 3$ block of $A^{(k-2)}$, so the divisors of step $k$ are the interior entries of $A^{(k-2)}$, the entries outside its first and last rows and columns.
The matrix $A^{(-1)}$ of ones is only a device that makes the first step fit the same formula.
For $k = 1$ every divisor is $1$, so $A^{(1)}$ is the matrix of connected $2 \times 2$ [[minors|0BT9BYWVRY]] of $A$.

### A small case

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$, the first step gives
$$A^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 & -3 \\ -3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The only divisor in the second step is the interior entry $a_{22} = 5$ of $A$:
$$A^{(2)} = \frac{(-3)(2) - (-3)(-3)}{5} = \frac{-15}{5} = -3.$$
Expansion along the first row also gives $\det A = 2 + 4 - 9 = -3$.

### Why the result is the determinant

When the recursion is defined, $a^{(k)}_{ij}$ is the connected minor of $A$ of order $k + 1$ with top left corner at $(i, j)$.
The proof is an induction on $k$ that uses the [[Desnanot-Jacobi identity|KWW7519E58]] for the connected block of order $k + 1$.
For $k = n - 1$ the only connected block is $A$ itself, so the single entry of $A^{(n-1)}$ is the [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] of $A$.

### The condition on the divisors

The field and the nonzero divisors are there so that each division has exactly one answer.
If a divisor is $0$, the numerator is $0$ too, because it equals the divisor times a minor, and the recursion meets $0/0$.
The matrix with rows $(1, 2, 3)$, $(4, 0, 5)$ and $(6, 7, 8)$ fails in this way, and the [[non-example|AD6GEXKCB1]] repairs it by a cyclic shift of the rows.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the same recursion works inside $\mathbb{Q}$, and each quotient is an integer because it is a minor of $A$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The method shrinks a matrix by one row and one column at each step.
It uses only $2 \times 2$ determinants of neighbors and a division by a number from two steps back.
When one number is left, that number is the determinant.
It stops only if it must divide by zero.

### L2 human

A $4 \times 4$ case is the [[worked example|FZJMAYK2DN]].
Its first step needs no divisions, its second step divides by the four interior entries $2, 1, 1, 2$ of the matrix, and its last step divides by the central entry $3$ of the first condensed matrix.
The result is $21$, and every division is exact.
Watch out: step $k$ divides by entries of $A^{(k-2)}$, not of $A^{(k-1)}$.
