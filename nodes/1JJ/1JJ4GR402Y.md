## Example

Conditional excluded middle is the schema
$$(\varphi > \psi) \vee (\varphi > \neg\psi).$$
We show that it is valid in Stalnaker's semantics and invalid in Lewis's sphere semantics of [[conditional logic|ZGYG7WN7EC]].

A Stalnaker model has worlds $W$, an absurd world $\lambda$ where every formula is true, and a selection function $f$.
For each $\varphi$ and $w \in W$, $f(\varphi, w)$ is a $\varphi$-world, or $\lambda$ when there is no $\varphi$-world; and $f(\varphi, w) = w$ when $w$ is a $\varphi$-world.

1. Fix $w$ and put $z = f(\varphi, w)$.
2. If $z = \lambda$, then $\psi$ is true at $z$, so $\varphi > \psi$ is true at $w$.
3. If $z \neq \lambda$, then $z$ is one world, and $\psi$ or $\neg\psi$ is true at $z$.
4. In the first case $\varphi > \psi$ holds at $w$, and in the second case $\varphi > \neg\psi$ holds at $w$.
5. So the schema is true at every world of every Stalnaker model.

Now take the sphere model with worlds $w, u, v$, spheres $\$_w = \set{\set{w}, \set{w, u, v}}$, $p$ true at $u$ and $v$ only, and $q$ true at $u$ only.

6. The smallest sphere with a $p$-world is $\set{w, u, v}$.
7. At $v$, $p$ is true and $q$ is false, so $p > q$ is false at $w$.
8. At $u$, $p$ is true and $\neg q$ is false, so $p > \neg q$ is false at $w$.
9. So $(p > q) \vee (p > \neg q)$ is false at $w$, and the schema is not valid in $\mathsf{VC}$.

## Remarks

The difference is a tie: in the sphere model, $u$ and $v$ are equally close $p$-worlds, and they disagree on $q$.
A Stalnaker selection function must break every tie, so it chooses one closest world.
If we split the outer sphere into $\set{w, u} \subset \set{w, u, v}$, the tie is gone, $p > q$ becomes true, and the schema holds at $w$.
Stalnaker's logic $\mathsf{C2}$ is $\mathsf{VC}$ plus conditional excluded middle.
The schema makes the "might" conditional collapse: $\neg(\varphi > \neg\psi)$ then gives $\varphi > \psi$, so "might" and "would" coincide when the antecedent is possible.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Ask: "If this coin had been tossed, would it have landed heads?"
Stalnaker says that there is one nearest situation where it is tossed, so the answer is yes or no.
Lewis says that there can be several equally near situations, some with heads and some with tails.
Then neither "it would have landed heads" nor "it would have landed tails" is true.
The schema above says that one of the two is always true, so Lewis rejects it.

### L2 human

1. In Stalnaker's semantics, compute $f(p, w)$; it is one world.
2. Read off $q$ at that world; it decides between $p > q$ and $p > \neg q$.
3. In Lewis's semantics, compute the first sphere that meets $p$; it can contain $p$-worlds with $q$ and without $q$.

The typical use is to decide between the two semantics for a given application, for example the analysis of chance or of questions under a counterfactual.
A trap: the schema is not the law of excluded middle $\varphi > (\psi \vee \neg\psi)$, which holds in both semantics.
