## Summary

The open-closed argument spreads a property over a connected space: it holds somewhere, it spreads to nearby points, and it cannot stop at a boundary, so it holds everywhere.

Here is the pattern on a real goal: a [[continuous|0X3WGX95FE]] function $f \colon [0, 1] \to \mathbb{Z}$ is constant.
Let $S = \{ x \in [0, 1] : f(x) = f(0) \}$, which contains $0$.
Continuity with $\varepsilon = \tfrac12$ gives, around each $x$, an interval on which $\lvert f(y) - f(x) \rvert < \tfrac12$, so $f(y) = f(x)$, since both are integers.
So each point of $S$ has a neighborhood inside $S$, and each point outside $S$ has a neighborhood outside $S$: $S$ is open and closed in $[0, 1]$.
Since $[0, 1]$ is connected and $S$ is not empty, $S = [0, 1]$.

In general, to show that every point of a [[connected space|0V032VWTHD]] $X$ has a property $P$, let $S$ be the set of points with $P$ and prove three facts: $S$ is non-empty, [[open|02BH3MCBQB]] and [[closed|0ZCK8DQYN1]].
A [[clopen set|DQ4E55T3BW]] of a connected space is $\varnothing$ or $X$, so $S = X$.

## Motivation

Many theorems say that a local property spreads over a whole space.
A function with zero derivative near each point should be constant.
Two solutions of a differential equation that agree at one time should agree at all times.
A holomorphic function that vanishes on a small disc should vanish on its whole domain.

In each case the property is easy to move a little way, but a proof must reach every point.
On the natural numbers, [[mathematical induction|0ASJVEFA9J]] does this with a base case and a step.
A continuum has no "next point", so the open-closed argument replaces the step with two local facts.
Openness moves the property from a point to all points near it.
Closedness lets it survive limits, so the spreading cannot stop at a boundary.
A global claim becomes three local ones, which are usually much easier.

## Notation

A set that is both open and closed is *clopen*.
Applied to a parameter $t \in [0, 1]$ for a family of problems, the argument is the *continuity method*, and $S$ is the set of parameters for which the problem is solvable.
Applied to a time interval in evolution equations, it is often called a *continuity argument*, and it frames [[bootstrapping|YKA0ADKYFR]].
In a [[metric space|0VKD1TP7F3]], "closed" is the same as "closed under limits of sequences", and many books use the second form.

## Remarks

### Why connectedness and a non-empty set are needed

The argument is valid exactly on connected spaces.
If $X = U \cup V$ with $U$ and $V$ disjoint, open and non-empty, then the property "$x \in U$" passes all three tests and fails on $V$.
[[A locally constant function on a disconnected set|87JHX7WQMR]] shows this on $(0, 1) \cup (2, 3)$.

All three tests are needed.
The empty set is open and closed, so without a starting point the argument proves nothing.
The set $(0, 1)$ is open and non-empty in $\mathbb{R}$, so without closedness the property can stop at a boundary.
For the [[smooth non-analytic function|09RTYYHXKA]] $f(x) = e^{-1/x}$ for $x > 0$ and $f(x) = 0$ for $x \le 0$, the set where all derivatives vanish is $(-\infty, 0]$, closed and non-empty but not open, and $f$ is not zero.

### How to check open and closed

"Open" means that each point of $S$ has a neighborhood inside $S$.
"Closed" means that each point outside $S$ has a neighborhood outside $S$, and many proofs use this form, as in the opening goal.
In a metric space, closedness also follows if the limit of every convergent sequence in $S$ lies in $S$.
Only closedness relative to $X$ is needed: $(0, 1]$ is closed in $X = (0, 2)$ but not in $\mathbb{R}$.

On an interval $[a, b]$ the argument is often written with a supremum.
Let $c = \sup \{ t : P \text{ holds on } [a, t] \}$, show that $P$ holds at $c$ (closedness), and show that $c < b$ is impossible (openness).
The [[intermediate value theorem|00KAFBR32E]] rests on the same fact: if a continuous $f$ on $[a, b]$ missed $y$, then $\{ f < y \}$ and $\{ f > y \}$ would split $[a, b]$ into two disjoint non-empty open sets.

### Where the pattern appears

[[A locally constant function on a connected space is constant|4NTM65AY4F]] is the cleanest case.
[[A connected topological group is generated by each neighborhood of the identity|3YY3JSV3E8]], since the subgroup generated is open, closed and non-empty.
In the [[identity theorem|0YBVKMYX0W]], the [[proof by connectedness|RZ4JTAZ1HM]] uses the set where all derivatives of $f - g$ vanish, closed by continuity and open by the power series.
[[Uniqueness for Lipschitz vector fields|025PKW8AMR]] uses the set of times where two solutions agree.
[[Open connected subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ are path-connected|0MXN7AFBPE]] by the [[proof by polygonal paths|187JR9B7HW]].

### Watch out

The most common slip proves "open and non-empty" and forgets "closed".
A second slip uses a space that is not connected, such as $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$, the domain of $1/x$.
A third proves $S$ closed in $\mathbb{R}^n$ when only closed in $X$ is needed, which is often harder or false.
Openness needs no uniform size of neighborhoods, since connectedness does the global work.

### History

The argument uses nothing beyond the definition of a connected space, and the identity theorem of complex analysis is a classical instance.
In geometric analysis, Shing-Tung Yau proved the [[Calabi conjecture|WJKA5YBTJ7]] by the continuity method, with openness from an implicit function theorem and closedness from a priori estimates.

### Try it

Let $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous with $f(x)^2 = 1$ for every $x$ and $f(0) = 1$.
Show that $f = 1$.
$S = \{ f = 1 \} = \{ f > 0 \}$ is open, $S = \{ f \ge 0 \}$ is closed, and $0 \in S$.

Why does the argument fail for $f(x) = x / \lvert x \rvert$ on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$?
The set $\{ f = 1 \} = (0, \infty)$ is open, closed and non-empty there, but the space is not connected.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Imagine a rumor that spreads in a town where every street connects to the others.
Somebody knows the rumor at the start.
Each person who knows it tells all the neighbors, so it spreads to everyone nearby.
A person whose neighbors all know it hears it too, so it cannot stop at an edge.
These three facts mean that everybody in the town learns the rumor.
If the town were two islands with no bridge, the rumor could stay on one island, which is why the space must be connected.

### L2 human

We prove that a differentiable $f \colon U \to \mathbb{R}$ with $\nabla f = 0$ on a connected open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ is constant.

1. Fix $p \in U$ and let $S = \{ x \in U : f(x) = f(p) \}$, which contains $p$.
2. For $x \in U$ take an open disc $D \subseteq U$ around $x$.
   For $y \in D$, $g(t) = f(x + t(y - x))$ has $g'(t) = \nabla f \cdot (y - x) = 0$, so $f(y) = f(x)$.
3. If $x \in S$, step 2 puts all of $D$ in $S$, so $S$ is open.
4. If $x \notin S$, step 2 keeps all of $D$ outside $S$, so $S$ is closed in $U$.
5. Since $U$ is connected, $S = U$.

The pattern fits uniqueness and rigidity theorems: a quantity that is locally fixed and cannot jump is globally fixed.
Watch out: the disc must lie inside $U$, because the segment from $x$ to $y$ has to stay where $\nabla f = 0$.

### L3 human

Connectedness says that the only clopen subsets of $X$ are $\varnothing$ and $X$, and the open-closed argument uses this fact directly.
In sheaf language, a locally constant sheaf of sets on a connected space has the same stalk everywhere, for the same reason.
The continuity method applies the argument to the parameter space $[0, 1]$ of equations $F_t(u) = 0$: openness comes from the [[implicit function theorem|0BXBADR94C]] in a Banach space, and closedness from [[a priori estimates|7CT1JTB4JJ]] and compactness.
On a time interval it becomes the [[bootstrapping|YKA0ADKYFR]] argument for long-time existence.
For discrete parameters the analogue is [[mathematical induction|0ASJVEFA9J]], and the open-closed argument is the form of induction suited to a continuum.
