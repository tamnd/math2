## Summary

The open-closed argument proves that every point of a [[connected space|0V032VWTHD]] $X$ has a property $P$.
Let $S$ be the set of points of $X$ that have $P$.
We prove three facts: $S$ is non-empty, $S$ is [[open|02BH3MCBQB]], and $S$ is [[closed|0ZCK8DQYN1]].
A set with the last two properties is a [[clopen set|DQ4E55T3BW]], and the only clopen sets of a connected space are $\varnothing$ and $X$.
Since $S \neq \varnothing$, we conclude $S = X$.
The argument matters because it turns a global claim into three local claims: a starting point, a step to nearby points, and a passage to the limit.

## Motivation

Many theorems say that a local property spreads over a whole space.
A function with derivative zero near each point should be constant.
Two solutions of a differential equation that agree at one time should agree at all times.
A holomorphic function that vanishes on a small disc should vanish on its whole domain.
In each case the property is easy to move a little way, but a proof must reach every point.
On the natural numbers, [[mathematical induction|0ASJVEFA9J]] does this job with a base case and a step.
On a connected space there is no "next point", and the open-closed argument replaces the step.
Openness says that the property moves from a point to all points near it.
Closedness says that the property survives a limit, so the spreading cannot stop at a boundary.

## Notation

1. A set that is both open and closed is called *clopen*.
2. In analysis, the argument applied to a parameter $t \in [0, 1]$ is called the *continuity method* or the *method of continuity*. The set is the set of parameters $t$ for which a problem is solvable.
3. In the theory of evolution equations, the argument applied to a time interval is often called a *continuity argument*, and it is the frame of [[bootstrapping|YKA0ADKYFR]].
4. Some books replace "closed" by "closed under limits of sequences". In a [[metric space|0VKD1TP7F3]] the two conditions are the same.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[A locally constant function on a connected space is constant|4NTM65AY4F]]: the set where $f$ equals $f(x_0)$ is non-empty, open and closed.
2. [[A connected topological group is generated by each neighbourhood of the identity|3YY3JSV3E8]]: the subgroup that the neighbourhood generates is open, closed and non-empty.
3. The [[identity theorem|0YBVKMYX0W]] for holomorphic functions: in the [[proof by connectedness|RZ4JTAZ1HM]], the set of points where all derivatives of $f - g$ vanish is closed by continuity and open by the power series expansion.
4. [[Uniqueness of solutions for Lipschitz vector fields|025PKW8AMR]]: on an interval $I$, the set of times where two solutions agree is closed because the solutions are continuous, and open because of local uniqueness.
5. [[Open connected subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ are path-connected|0MXN7AFBPE]]: the set of points that a polygonal path joins to a fixed point is open and closed, as in the [[proof by polygonal paths|187JR9B7HW]].

### Non-examples

1. [[A locally constant function on a disconnected set need not be constant|87JHX7WQMR]]: on $(0, 1) \cup (2, 3)$ the set $\{f = f(x_0)\}$ is open, closed and non-empty, but the space is not connected, so the argument does not apply.
2. For the [[smooth non-analytic function|09RTYYHXKA]] $f(x) = e^{-1/x}$ for $x > 0$ and $f(x) = 0$ for $x \le 0$, the set of points where all derivatives vanish is $(-\infty, 0]$. It is closed and non-empty, but it is not open, and indeed $f$ is not identically zero.
3. A proof that shows only "$S$ is open and non-empty" proves nothing about $X$. The set $(0, 1)$ is open and non-empty in $\mathbb{R}$, but it is not $\mathbb{R}$.

### Basic properties

1. The argument is valid exactly on connected spaces. If $X = U \cup V$ with $U$ and $V$ disjoint, open and non-empty, then the property "$x \in U$" passes all three tests, and it fails at the points of $V$.
2. The test "open" is equivalent to: each point of $S$ has a neighbourhood inside $S$. The test "closed" is equivalent to: each point outside $S$ has a neighbourhood outside $S$. Many proofs of closedness use this second form.
3. In a metric space, $S$ is closed when the limit of each convergent sequence of points of $S$ lies in $S$. This is the usual test in analysis.
4. On an interval $[a, b]$ the argument is often written with a supremum: let $c = \sup \{ t : P \text{ holds on } [a, t] \}$, show that $P$ holds at $c$ (closedness) and that $c < b$ is impossible (openness).
5. The [[intermediate value theorem|00KAFBR32E]] is a consequence of the same fact on connected sets: if a continuous $f$ on $[a, b]$ missed a value $y$, then $\{ f < y \}$ and $\{ f > y \}$ would split $[a, b]$ into two disjoint non-empty open sets.

### History

The argument uses only the definition of a connected space, and it is as old as the modern definition of connectedness.
The identity theorem and the uniqueness of analytic continuation are classical instances from complex analysis.
In geometric analysis, Shing-Tung Yau proved the [[Calabi conjecture|WJKA5YBTJ7]] by the continuity method, with openness from an implicit function theorem and closedness from a priori estimates.

### Common errors

1. A student proves that $S$ is open and non-empty and forgets closedness. The spreading can then stop at a boundary point, as in non-example 3.
2. A student applies the argument on a space that is not connected, for example on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ or on the domain of $1/x$.
3. A student proves that $S$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}^n$ instead of closed in $X$. Only closedness relative to $X$ is needed, and it is often easier: $(0, 1]$ is closed in $X = (0, 2)$ but not in $\mathbb{R}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Imagine a rumour that spreads in a town where every street connects to the others.
Somebody knows the rumour at the start.
Each person who knows it tells all the neighbours, so it spreads to everyone nearby.
And a person whose neighbours all know it in the end hears it too, so it cannot stop at an edge.
These three facts together mean that everybody in the town learns the rumour.
If the town were two islands with no bridge, the rumour could stay on one island, and this is why the space must be connected.

### L2 human

We prove that a continuous function $f \colon [0, 1] \to \mathbb{Z}$ is constant.

1. Let $S = \{ x \in [0, 1] : f(x) = f(0) \}$. Then $0 \in S$, so $S$ is non-empty.
2. Let $x \in [0, 1]$. By [[continuity|0X3WGX95FE]] with $\varepsilon = \tfrac12$, there is $\delta > 0$ with $\lvert f(y) - f(x) \rvert < \tfrac12$ whenever $\lvert y - x \rvert < \delta$.
3. Two integers that differ by less than $\tfrac12$ are equal. So $f(y) = f(x)$ for all $y \in [0, 1]$ with $\lvert y - x \rvert < \delta$.
4. If $x \in S$, step 3 gives $f(y) = f(0)$ near $x$. So $S$ is open in $[0, 1]$.
5. If $x \notin S$, step 3 gives $f(y) = f(x) \neq f(0)$ near $x$. So the complement of $S$ is open, and $S$ is closed.
6. The interval $[0, 1]$ is connected. So $S = [0, 1]$, and $f$ is constant.

The typical use is a uniqueness or rigidity theorem: a quantity that cannot jump and that is locally fixed is globally fixed.
A trap is to prove openness with a neighbourhood that depends on the point in an uncontrolled way and then to claim that one step covers the whole interval.
The argument needs no uniform size of neighbourhoods, because connectedness does the global work.

### L3 human

Connectedness says that the lattice of clopen subsets of $X$ is $\{ \varnothing, X \}$, and the open-closed argument is the direct use of this fact.
In sheaf language, a locally constant sheaf of sets on a connected space has the same stalk everywhere, and the argument is the reason.
The continuity method in nonlinear analysis applies the argument to the parameter space $[0, 1]$ of a family of equations $F_t(u) = 0$: openness comes from the [[implicit function theorem|0BXBADR94C]] in a Banach space, and closedness from [[a priori estimates|7CT1JTB4JJ]] and compactness.
The same frame governs long-time existence for evolution equations, where it becomes the [[bootstrapping|YKA0ADKYFR]] argument on a time interval.
For discrete parameters the analogue is [[mathematical induction|0ASJVEFA9J]], and for well-founded sets it is well-founded induction; the open-closed argument is the form of induction adapted to a continuum.
