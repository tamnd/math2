## Proof

We compute the entries of $Q^* Q$ and $Q Q^*$ as inner products, and we use that a one-sided inverse of a square matrix is a two-sided inverse.
Write $Q = (q_{lm})$, so that the column $q_j$ has the entries $q_{1j}, \dots, q_{nj}$.

*Part 1.*

1. By the definition of the [[conjugate transpose|0DXKE10PHF]], $(Q^*)_{il} = \overline{q_{li}}$.
2. By the rule for matrix products,
$$(Q^* Q)_{ij} = \sum_{l=1}^{n} \overline{q_{li}}\, q_{lj}.$$
3. By the definition of the [[standard inner product|TFZ8DYVJ1J]], this sum is $\ip{q_j}{q_i}$, since the conjugate falls on the entries of the second vector $q_i$.

*Part 2.*

4. By part 1, $Q^* Q = I_k$ means $\ip{q_j}{q_i} = \delta_{ij}$ for all $i, j$.
5. Since $\delta_{ij} = \delta_{ji}$, this is the condition $\ip{q_i}{q_j} = \delta_{ij}$ that defines an [[orthonormal family|09VYHEF2VT]].

*Part 3.* Let $k = n$.

6. If the columns form an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]], they form an orthonormal family, so $Q^* Q = I_n$ by part 2.
7. Conversely, let $Q^* Q = I_n$.
   By part 2 the $n$ columns are orthonormal, so they are [[linearly independent|0JWM6GJNQW]].
8. By the [[right-size criterion|096V7H13H9]], $n$ independent vectors in $\mathbb{K}^n$ form a basis, so the columns form an orthonormal basis.
9. Let $Q^* Q = I_n$ again.
   Then $Q^*$ is a left inverse of the square matrix $Q$.
10. By the [[invertible matrix theorem|ACDFDABVQK]], a square matrix with a left inverse is invertible, and the left inverse is the inverse.
    So $Q^{-1} = Q^*$ and $Q Q^* = I_n$.
11. In the same way, $Q Q^* = I_n$ makes $Q^*$ a right inverse of $Q$, and the same theorem gives $Q^* Q = I_n$.
12. Let $r_i = (q_{i1}, \dots, q_{in})$ be the $i$-th row of $Q$, read as a vector of $\mathbb{K}^n$.
    By the rule for matrix products,
$$(Q Q^*)_{ij} = \sum_{l=1}^{n} q_{il}\, \overline{q_{jl}} = \ip{r_i}{r_j}.$$
13. So $Q Q^* = I_n$ holds exactly when $\ip{r_i}{r_j} = \delta_{ij}$ for all $i, j$, that is, when the rows form an orthonormal family.
    With steps 6 to 11, this proves that the four conditions of part 3 are equivalent.

*Part 4.*

14. Steps 9 and 10 show that $Q$ is invertible with $Q^{-1} = Q^*$.

## Idea

The entry $(i, j)$ of $Q^* Q$ is the inner product of column $j$ with column $i$.
So the single matrix equation $Q^* Q = I$ packs all the conditions $\ip{q_j}{q_i} = \delta_{ij}$ into one line.
For a square matrix, a one-sided inverse is automatically a two-sided inverse.
That moves the condition from $Q^* Q$ to $Q Q^*$, and the entries of $Q Q^*$ are the inner products of the rows.

## Variants

Over $\mathbb{R}$ every conjugate does nothing, and the same proof shows that $Q^{\mathsf{T}} Q = I$ exactly when the columns are orthonormal for the dot product.
In place of the invertible matrix theorem, step 10 can use rank-nullity.
If $Q^* Q = I_n$ and $Q x = 0$, then $x = Q^* Q x = 0$, so $Q$ is injective, and an injective linear map $\mathbb{K}^n \to \mathbb{K}^n$ is bijective by the rank-nullity theorem.
Then multiplying $Q^* Q = I_n$ on the right by $Q^{-1}$ gives $Q^* = Q^{-1}$.
