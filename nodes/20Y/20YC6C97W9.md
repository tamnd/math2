## Example

Let $P_3$ be the real [[polynomials|0Q9EZS90GY]] of degree at most $3$, and $P_2$ those of degree at most $2$.
Let $D \colon P_3 \to P_2$ be differentiation, $D(p) = p'$, and let $W = \R$ be the constant polynomials.
We check the [[universal property|0ZERJX6PH6]] for $D$ and $W$.

1. $D$ is linear, since $(p + q)' = p' + q'$ and $(ap)' = a p'$.
2. $D(c) = 0$ for every constant $c$, so $W \subseteq \ker D$. In fact $\ker D = W$.
3. The property gives $\bar D \colon P_3 / W \to P_2$ with $\bar D(p + W) = p'$ and $\bar D \circ \pi = D$, where $\pi$ is the [[quotient map|0J0SK5FF4M]].
4. Well defined: $(x^3 + 2x + 5) + W = (x^3 + 2x - 1) + W$, and both representatives have derivative $3x^2 + 2$.
5. $\bar D$ is injective: $\bar D(p + W) = 0$ means $p' = 0$, so $p$ is constant and $p + W = 0 + W$.
6. $\bar D$ is surjective: $a + bx + cx^2 = D\big(ax + \tfrac{b}{2} x^2 + \tfrac{c}{3} x^3\big)$.
7. So $\bar D$ is an [[isomorphism|0XSJ25Z9M4]] $P_3 / \R \cong P_2$, and $\dim(P_3 / \R) = 4 - 1 = 3 = \dim P_2$.

## Remarks

The inverse of $\bar D$ sends $q$ to the coset of any antiderivative of $q$.
This is the algebraic meaning of the constant of integration: an antiderivative is defined only up to a constant, that is, as an element of $P_3 / \R$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two polynomials that differ by a constant have the same derivative.
So differentiation does not need to know the constant, and it is a map on polynomials modulo constants.

### L2 human

The step that needs care is step 4: a formula on cosets must give the same value for every representative.
Here every representative is $p + c$, and $(p + c)' = p'$, so the check is immediate.
