## Example

On the [[polynomial ring|0SDC934R52]] $\R[x]$, let $D(p) = p'$ and $J(p)(x) = \int_0^x p(t)\, dt$.
Both maps are linear, as checked in the [[examples of linear maps|0SS770NS8B]].
So $D \circ J$ and $J \circ D$ are linear maps $\R[x] \to \R[x]$.

1. On a monomial, $J(x^k) = x^{k+1}/(k+1)$ and $D\left(x^{k+1}/(k+1)\right) = x^k$. So $(D \circ J)(x^k) = x^k$ for each $k \ge 0$.
2. Both sides of step 1 are linear in $p$, and the monomials span $\R[x]$. So $D \circ J = \id$.
3. On a monomial with $k \ge 1$, $D(x^k) = k x^{k-1}$ and $J(k x^{k-1}) = x^k$. So $(J \circ D)(x^k) = x^k$.
4. For $k = 0$, $D(1) = 0$ and $J(0) = 0$. So $(J \circ D)(1) = 0 \ne 1$.
5. Steps 3 and 4 give $(J \circ D)(p) = p - p(0)$ for each $p$.
6. Numerical check with $p = x^2 + 3$: $J(p) = x^3/3 + 3x$ and $D(J(p)) = x^2 + 3 = p$.
7. Also $D(p) = 2x$ and $J(2x) = x^2$. So $(J \circ D)(p) = x^2 = p - 3$, and $p(0) = 3$.

So $D \circ J = \id$, but $J \circ D \ne \id$.

## Remarks

- $D$ has a right inverse $J$ but no left inverse, since $D$ is not injective: $D(1) = D(0)$.
- This cannot happen for a map $V \to V$ with $V$ finite-dimensional, by the [[theorem on injective and surjective endomorphisms|0912EEEVCT]].
- Step 5 is the fundamental theorem of calculus for polynomials: $\int_0^x p'(t)\, dt = p(x) - p(0)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Integrate and then differentiate: the polynomial comes back.
Differentiate and then integrate: the constant term is lost.
So the two orders of composition give different maps.

### L2 human

The example shows a one-sided inverse in infinite dimension.
The map $J \circ D$ is the projection that removes the constant term, and its kernel is the space of constants.
The trap is to conclude from $D \circ J = \id$ that $D$ and $J$ are inverse to each other.
