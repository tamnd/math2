## Example

Integrating and then differentiating gives back every polynomial, but differentiating and then integrating loses the constant term.
On the [[polynomial ring|0SDC934R52]] $\R[x]$, let $D(p) = p'$ and $J(p)(x) = \int_0^x p(t)\, dt$.
Both maps are linear, as the [[examples of linear maps|0SS770NS8B]] check, so $D \circ J$ and $J \circ D$ are linear maps $\R[x] \to \R[x]$.

1. On a monomial, $J(x^k) = x^{k+1}/(k + 1)$ and $D\bigl(x^{k+1}/(k + 1)\bigr) = x^k$, so $(D \circ J)(x^k) = x^k$ for each $k \ge 0$.
2. Both $D \circ J$ and $\id$ are linear, and they agree on the monomials, which span $\R[x]$, so $D \circ J = \id$.
3. On a monomial with $k \ge 1$, $D(x^k) = k x^{k-1}$ and $J(k x^{k-1}) = x^k$, so $(J \circ D)(x^k) = x^k$.
4. For $k = 0$, $D(1) = 0$ and $J(0) = 0$, so $(J \circ D)(1) = 0 \ne 1$.
5. Writing $p = p(0) + (p - p(0))$, where $p - p(0)$ has no constant term, steps 3 and 4 give $(J \circ D)(p) = p - p(0)$ for each $p$.

As a check, take $p = x^2 + 3$.
Then $J(p) = x^3/3 + 3x$ and $D(J(p)) = x^2 + 3 = p$, as step 2 says.
In the other order, $D(p) = 2x$ and $J(2x) = x^2 = p - 3$, and indeed $p(0) = 3$.
So $D \circ J = \id$, but $J \circ D \ne \id$.

## Remarks

The example shows a one-sided inverse.
The map $D$ has $J$ as a right inverse, but $D$ has no left inverse, because it is not injective: $D(1) = D(0) = 0$.
In finite dimension this cannot happen for a map from a space to itself, by the [[theorem on injective and surjective endomorphisms|0912EEEVCT]], so the infinite dimension of $\R[x]$ is essential here.

Step 5 is the fundamental theorem of calculus for polynomials, $\int_0^x p'(t)\, dt = p(x) - p(0)$.
If we change the lower limit of the integral from $0$ to $a$, then $J \circ D$ becomes $p \mapsto p - p(a)$, and $D \circ J$ is still the identity.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Integrate and then differentiate: the polynomial comes back.
Differentiate and then integrate: the constant term is lost.
For example, $x^2 + 3$ goes to $2x$ and then to $x^2$, so the $3$ is gone.
So the two orders of composition give different maps.

### L2 human

The map $J \circ D$ removes the constant term, so it is a projection: applying it twice is the same as applying it once.
Its kernel is the space of constants, and its image is the space of polynomials with $p(0) = 0$.
This one-sided behavior is typical of infinite-dimensional spaces, and the [[shift maps on sequences|N1G1WQW59J]] show it again.
Watch out: from $D \circ J = \id$ alone you cannot conclude that $D$ and $J$ are inverse to each other.
