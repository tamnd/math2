## Definition

Let $(M, g)$ be a time-oriented spacetime that is [[asymptotically flat|3HTF65XMF7]] at null infinity, with future null infinity $\mathcal{I}^+$.
Let $J^-(\mathcal{I}^+)$ be the [[causal past|KZWG5F0TSB]] of $\mathcal{I}^+$, and $\mathcal{B} = M \setminus J^-(\mathcal{I}^+)$ the [[black hole|YHJ0PDNY8T]] region.
The *event horizon* of $(M, g)$ is the boundary of $J^-(\mathcal{I}^+)$ in $M$,
$$\mathcal{H} = \partial J^-(\mathcal{I}^+) \cap M,$$
which is also the boundary of $\mathcal{B}$ in $M$.

## Remarks

Example: for the Schwarzschild spacetime of mass $m$ in ingoing [[Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates|SG9GAYQT4R]], $J^-(\mathcal{I}^+) \cap M = \set{r > 2m}$, so $\mathcal{H}$ is the null hypersurface $r = 2m$.
Check that it is null: there $g_{vv} = -(1 - 2m/r) = 0$, so the tangent vector $\partial_v$ of the horizon generators is null, and the induced metric on $r = 2m$ is degenerate.

Non-example: the photon sphere $r = 3m$ of the same spacetime is not an event horizon.
Points on it can send outward light rays to $\mathcal{I}^+$, so it lies inside $J^-(\mathcal{I}^+)$, not on its boundary.

Basic properties: $\mathcal{H}$ is an achronal, closed, Lipschitz hypersurface.
It is generated by null geodesics that have no future endpoint in $M$, although they may enter it from the past.
For stationary black holes in many settings, $\mathcal{H}$ is a [[Killing horizon|9ZXYW3NX4A]].
Replacing $\mathcal{I}^+$ by the world line of one observer gives the event horizon of that observer.
An example is the Rindler horizon of a uniformly accelerated observer in Minkowski space.
