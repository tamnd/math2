## Example

In the *ultimatum game* a proposer and a responder divide $10$ coins.
This is an [[extensive-form game|4Y8AF9H9HA]] of perfect information.
The proposer offers $x \in \set{0, 1, \dots, 10}$ coins to the responder.
The responder sees $x$ and accepts, giving payoffs $(10 - x, x)$, or rejects, giving $(0, 0)$.

In a [[subgame perfect equilibrium|HE8K9M4EXN]] the responder accepts every $x \ge 1$, since $x > 0$.
At $x = 0$ she is indifferent, so there are two subgame perfect equilibria.
In one she accepts $0$ and the proposer offers $0$.
In the other she rejects only $0$ and the proposer offers $1$.

There are many more [[Nash equilibria|745WYP9YJ0]].
For each $k$, "offer $k$" together with "accept if and only if $x \ge k$" is one.
For $k \ge 2$ the responder's threat to reject the offers $1, \dots, k - 1$ is not credible, because carrying it out costs her coins.

## Explanations

### L1 human

One person proposes how to split a sum and the other can only accept or reject it.
Theory with purely selfish players says the proposer should offer almost nothing and the responder should accept.
In laboratory experiments, low offers are often rejected, which suggests that people care about fairness.
