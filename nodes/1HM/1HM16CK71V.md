## Example

Two points fix a line, and once the line is written with a parameter, testing a point means finding one value of $t$ that fits every coordinate.
Let $A = (1, 2, 3)$ and $B = (4, 0, 5)$.
We write the [[line|0399QFDFS8]] through $A$ and $B$ in three forms and then test two points.

1. The direction vector is the step from $A$ to $B$:
   $$d = B - A = (3, -2, 2).$$
2. The vector form is $r = (1, 2, 3) + t(3, -2, 2)$.
3. The parametric form is $x = 1 + 3t$, $y = 2 - 2t$, $z = 3 + 2t$.
4. All components of $d$ are nonzero, so the symmetric form exists:
   $$\frac{x - 1}{3} = \frac{y - 2}{-2} = \frac{z - 3}{2}.$$
5. Test $P = (7, -2, 7)$. The $x$ equation $1 + 3t = 7$ gives $t = 2$. Then $y = 2 - 4 = -2$ and $z = 3 + 4 = 7$, both correct, so $P$ is on the line.
6. Test $Q = (2, 1, 4)$. The $x$ equation gives $t = \tfrac{1}{3}$. Then $y = 2 - \tfrac{2}{3} = \tfrac{4}{3}$, which is not $1$, so $Q$ is not on the line.
7. The midpoint of $AB$ sits halfway between $t = 0$ and $t = 1$, at $t = \tfrac{1}{2}$, which gives $(\tfrac{5}{2}, 1, 4)$.

## Remarks

The parameter also measures position along the line.
The point $P$ is at $t = 2$ and $B$ is at $t = 1$, so $B$ is exactly halfway from $A$ to $P$.

The same line has other equations.
The direction $2d = (6, -4, 4)$ or $-d = (-3, 2, -2)$ gives the same set of points, traced at another speed or backward.
With base point $B$ the vector form is $(4, 0, 5) + s(3, -2, 2)$, and the point at $s$ is the point at $t = s + 1$ in the first equation.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To go from $A$ to $B$ we move by the arrow $(3, -2, 2)$.
Every point of the line is $A$ plus some multiple of this arrow.
To test a point, find the multiple from one coordinate, and then check that the other coordinates agree.

### L2 human

The test in steps 5 and 6 uses one coordinate to find $t$ and the other two to check it.
A point is on the line only if one value of $t$ fits all three coordinates.
With the symmetric form the same test reads: the three fractions must be equal.
For $Q$ they are $\tfrac{1}{3}$, $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}$.
Watch out: two fractions that agree are not enough, and here the first one breaks the tie.
