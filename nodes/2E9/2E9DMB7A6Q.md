## Summary

Quantum logic is the study of the propositions about a quantum system and of the logical operations on them.
In the approach of Birkhoff and von Neumann, a proposition is a closed subspace of the [[Hilbert space|HM2HHE3J51]] of the system.
These propositions form an [[orthomodular lattice|6FPKEFX0X4]].
When the Hilbert space has dimension at least $2$, this lattice is not [[distributive|0T36P6TXQ6]], so the distributive law of classical logic fails.
Abstract quantum logics are studied as orthomodular lattices or orthomodular partially ordered sets.

## Motivation

In quantum mechanics, a yes-no observable is a projection, and projections correspond to closed subspaces.
A state, given by a unit vector $u$, answers "yes" with certainty exactly when $u$ lies in the subspace.
Von Neumann noted that these projections allow a kind of logical calculus.
Birkhoff and von Neumann took meet and join of subspaces as conjunction and disjunction, also for projections that do not commute.
The failure of distributivity then becomes a fact about the logic of physical propositions.

## Notation

For a Hilbert space $H$, $\mathcal{L}(H)$ is the set of closed subspaces, ordered by inclusion.
We write $M \wedge N = M \cap N$, $M \vee N$ for the closure of $M + N$, and $M^\perp$ for the [[orthogonal complement|0XJXTBCB2T]].
The bottom is $0 = \set{0}$ and the top is $1 = H$.
The precise definition is in [[quantum logic (definition)|TWEYQ0XAY4]].

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[Distributivity fails in the subspace lattice of the plane|RRWDAKDMJQ]]: with the $x$-axis $a$, the $y$-axis $b$ and the diagonal $c$, $a \wedge (b \vee c) = a$ but $(a \wedge b) \vee (a \wedge c) = 0$.
- [[The subspace lattice of the plane has no truth valuation|QY4Z31VFNM]]: no lattice homomorphism from $\mathcal{L}(\R^2)$ onto $\set{0, 1}$ exists, so the connectives are not truth-functional.
- Commuting projections. If $PQ = QP$, the sublattice generated by $P$, $Q$, $P^\perp$ and $Q^\perp$ is Boolean. So classical logic holds for each family of commuting propositions.

### Non-examples

- [[The hexagon ortholattice is not orthomodular|9Y3BG1Y7QA]]: it has an orthocomplement, but it fails the orthomodular law, so it is not a quantum logic in the abstract sense.
- The power set of the state space of a classical system is a Boolean algebra; its logic is classical propositional logic.

### Basic properties

- $\mathcal{L}(H)$ is a complete orthomodular lattice, for every Hilbert space $H$.
- Distributivity fails as soon as $\dim H \geq 2$, because a closed subspace has many complements.
- Gleason's theorem: if $3 \leq \dim H$ and $H$ is separable, every probability measure on $\mathcal{L}(H)$ comes from a density operator, see [[Gleason's theorem|CEEZG4XCKC]].
- [[The Kochen-Specker theorem|X2RF2WKWV9]]: for $3 \leq \dim H < \infty$, there is no assignment of $0$ or $1$ to the rays of a suitable finite set such that each orthonormal basis in the set gets exactly one $1$. So no hidden-variable valuation respects all measurement contexts.
- Piron's theorem: a complete, atomic, irreducible orthomodular lattice with the covering law and at least four orthogonal atoms is the lattice of a generalized Hilbert space over an involutive division ring.

### History

Von Neumann described the logical calculus of projections in his 1932 book.
Birkhoff and von Neumann proposed in 1936 that the distributive law is "the weakest link in the algebra of logic".
Mackey (1957, 1963) gave axioms that lead to orthomodular partially ordered sets, and Piron proved his representation theorem in 1964.
Gleason proved his theorem in 1957, and Kochen and Specker published their theorem in 1967.
In the 1960s, Finkelstein and Putnam argued that quantum mechanics shows that logic is empirical; Putnam's paper "Is Logic Empirical?" appeared in 1968.

### Common errors

- To think that quantum logic is a many-valued logic with a truth table. Its connectives are not truth-functional.
- To think that orthomodular means modular. The orthomodular law is weaker, and it holds for every Hilbert space, see [[orthomodular lattice|6FPKEFX0X4]].
- To take Putnam's dissolution of the measurement problem as accepted. The SEP entry reports that it is widely regarded as mistaken.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In classical physics, a statement such as "the position is in this interval" is true or false of each state.
Such statements combine like subsets, with "and", "or" and "not".
In quantum mechanics, a yes-no question corresponds to a closed subspace of a Hilbert space, the states for which the answer is surely yes.
"And" is intersection, "not" is the orthogonal complement, and "or" is the smallest closed subspace containing both.
With these operations "$p$ and ($q$ or $r$)" can differ from "($p$ and $q$) or ($p$ and $r$)".
This reflects the fact that some quantum questions cannot be answered at the same time.

### L2 human

The join of two lines in the plane is the whole plane, even though a vector on a third line lies on neither of them.
This is the source of the failure of distributivity.
Commuting propositions still form Boolean algebras, so quantum logic is a family of overlapping classical logics.
A trap is to think that a state makes each proposition true or false; the Kochen-Specker theorem shows that no such assignment respects all contexts.

### L3 human

The abstract theory replaces $\mathcal{L}(H)$ by orthomodular lattices, orthomodular posets or orthoalgebras.
Gleason's theorem identifies the states on $\mathcal{L}(H)$ with density operators, and Piron's theorem brings an abstract lattice close to a Hilbert space.
Kochen and Specker worked with partial Boolean algebras, the commuting parts of $\mathcal{L}(H)$.
They gave a finite set of projections in dimension $3$ that admits no assignment of $0$ and $1$ that is additive on each compatible family.
