## Definition

A predicate is an expression with free variables that becomes a proposition for every choice of values of its variables.

Let $D$ be a [[set|0BSJ7RQ9BW]], called the domain of discourse, and let $n \ge 1$ be an integer.
An $n$-ary predicate on $D$ is an expression $P(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ with the following two properties.

1. The free variables of $P$ are exactly $x_1, \dots, x_n$, and each of them ranges over $D$.
2. For each [[tuple|9HFER6N708]] $(a_1, \dots, a_n) \in D^n$, the expression $P(a_1, \dots, a_n)$, obtained by replacing each free occurrence of $x_i$ by $a_i$, is a [[proposition|0ZGC7ZESYN]].

The number $n$ is the arity of $P$, and a predicate of arity $1$, $2$ or $3$ is unary, binary or ternary.
The truth set of $P$ is
$$T_P = \{(a_1, \dots, a_n) \in D^n \mid P(a_1, \dots, a_n) \text{ is true}\}.$$
Two $n$-ary predicates $P$ and $Q$ on $D$ are equivalent on $D$ when $T_P = T_Q$.

## Notation

Predicates are written $P(x)$ and $Q(x, y)$, or $Px$ and $Qxy$ in many logic books, and binary predicates are often written between the variables, as in $x < y$.
The truth set is also written $\{x \in D : P(x)\}$ and called the extension or the solution set of $P$.
The domain $D$ is also called the universe of discourse.

## Remarks

The first condition fixes which variables are free.
A variable is free when no quantifier binds it, so in $\exists y \in \mathbb{Z} \; (x = 2y)$ only $x$ is free, and the expression is a unary predicate in $x$.
The condition also names the domain: a predicate is always a predicate on some set $D$, and the same expression on another set is another predicate.

The second condition is what separates a predicate from a term.
The predicate "$x > 3$" on $\mathbb{Z}$ gives the true proposition "$5 > 3$" and the false proposition "$1 > 3$", and its truth set is $\{4, 5, 6, \dots\}$.
The expression $x^2 + 1$ fails the condition, because $x = 2$ gives the number $5$, which is not a proposition.

The truth set $T_P$ is a subset of $D^n$, that is, an $n$-ary relation on $D$, and for $n = 2$ it is a [[binary relation|08EJC6Q25H]].
For a unary predicate it is the set $\{x \in D \mid P(x)\}$ of [[set-builder notation|0TMZ89Q5KA]].
The concept page [[predicate|0QHEWQD30B]] gives worked examples, non-examples and the first properties.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A predicate is a sentence with one or more blanks, such as "___ is greater than $3$".
Each blank can be filled with an element of a fixed set, the domain.
After all blanks are filled, the sentence is true or false.
The truth set collects the fillings that make it true: for the whole numbers, "___ is greater than $3$" has the truth set $\{4, 5, 6, \dots\}$.

### L2 human

Check that "$x + y$ is even" is a binary predicate on $D = \{0, 1\}$, and find its truth set.
The free variables are $x$ and $y$, and both range over $D$.
Each of the $4$ pairs gives a proposition:
$$0 + 0 = 0, \quad 0 + 1 = 1, \quad 1 + 0 = 1, \quad 1 + 1 = 2.$$
The sums $0$ and $2$ are even and the sums $1$ are odd, so the truth set is $\{(0, 0), (1, 1)\}$, the equality relation on $D$.
So on $D = \{0, 1\}$ the predicates "$x + y$ is even" and "$x = y$" are equivalent.
Watch out: on $\{0, 1, 2\}$ the pairs $(0, 2)$ and $(2, 0)$ also make "$x + y$ is even" true, and the two predicates are no longer equivalent.
