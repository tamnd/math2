## Summary

The material implication of two [[propositions|0ZGC7ZESYN]] $p$ and $q$ is the proposition $p \to q$.
It is false when $p$ is true and $q$ is false, and true in the three other cases.
The proposition $p$ is the antecedent, and the proposition $q$ is the consequent.
The [[definition|0ZNX6DQ3EJ]] gives its truth table in the standard row order.
The formula $p \to q$ is read "if $p$, then $q$", "$p$ only if $q$", "$p$ implies $q$" and "$p$ is sufficient for $q$".
It has the same truth value as $\neg p \lor q$ under every assignment of truth values.
Most theorems of mathematics have the form of an implication, so this connective fixes what a theorem claims and what its proof must show.

## Motivation

A theorem such as "for every integer $n$, if $4$ divides $n$, then $n$ is even" must be true for each integer.
For $n = 6$ and $n = 7$ the antecedent is false, and the theorem must not fail at these numbers.
So an implication with a false antecedent must count as true.
An implication with a true antecedent and a true consequent, as for $n = 8$, is also true.
The only case that refutes the theorem is an integer with the antecedent true and the consequent false.
Material implication keeps exactly this one case as false.

The word "material" says that only the truth values of $p$ and $q$ count.
No link of meaning, cause or time between $p$ and $q$ is required.
This makes the connective truth-functional, so a [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]] can compute it.
The English side of the conditional, with its many phrasings and its hidden quantifiers, is on the page [[conditional statements|FA6YZGR2SS]].

## Notation

This page writes $p \to q$.
Older books and many philosophy books write $p \supset q$, the horseshoe of Principia Mathematica.
Many proofs write $p \Rightarrow q$.
In Polish notation the implication is $Cpq$.
In Lean, the implication of two propositions `P` and `Q` is the arrow `P → Q`, the same arrow as the type of functions.

A warning on the double arrow: many authors use $\Rightarrow$ and $\Leftrightarrow$ in the metalanguage, between statements about formulas.
For example, "$\models \varphi$ and $\models \varphi \to \psi$ $\Rightarrow$ $\models \psi$" is a claim about the formulas $\varphi$ and $\psi$.
The single arrow $\to$ is a symbol inside a formula, and $p \to q$ is one formula.
The pages of this topic keep the two levels apart: $\to$ inside formulas, and words or $\Rightarrow$ between statements.

The words have fixed roles.
"Antecedent" (also hypothesis or premise) is the part before the arrow, and "consequent" (also conclusion) is the part after it.
The phrase "only if" comes before the consequent, and the word "if" alone comes before the antecedent: "$q$ if $p$" is $p \to q$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The implication "if $n$ is prime, then $n$ is odd" for $n = 7, 2, 9, 4$|4D6PNR2FP6]]: the four values of $n$ give the four rows of the truth table, and only $n = 2$ makes the implication false.
2. [[Implications between unrelated statements|6RWNS70KF3]]: "if $2 + 2 = 4$, then $7$ is prime" is true, although its parts have no link of meaning.
3. "If $1 = 2$, then $5$ is even" is true, because its antecedent is false. This is [[vacuous truth|0V4QXPGJQK]].
4. "If $3 > 1$, then $3 > 2$" is true, because both parts are true. Its [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] "if $3 > 2$, then $3 > 1$" is also true for this number, but the converse of a true implication can be false in general.
5. The formula $p \to p$ is true for both values of $p$, so it is a [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]].

### Non-examples

1. [[A counterfactual conditional is not a material implication|YPJF856FAN]]: two counterfactuals with false parts get different verdicts.
So their truth is not a function of the truth values of the parts.
2. "Because $9$ is odd, $9^2$ is odd" is not an implication. The word "because" claims that $9$ is odd, while $p \to q$ makes no claim about $p$.
3. The metalanguage claim "$p \land q \models p$" is not the formula $(p \land q) \to p$. It is a statement about formulas: every assignment that makes $p \land q$ true makes $p$ true.

### Basic properties

1. $p \to q$ is false exactly when $p$ is true and $q$ is false.
2. $p \to q$ has the same truth value as $\neg p \lor q$. Check the four rows: both are false only in the row $p = \mathsf{T}$, $q = \mathsf{F}$.
3. The negation $\neg(p \to q)$ has the same truth value as $p \land \neg q$. This is the connective [[material nonimplication|4EZWY60NYT]].
4. $p \to q$ has the same truth value as its [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] $\neg q \to \neg p$.
5. $p \to q$ need not have the same truth value as the converse $q \to p$ or the [[inverse|VB7B8766D2]] $\neg p \to \neg q$. At $p = \mathsf{F}$, $q = \mathsf{T}$, the formula $p \to q$ is true and $q \to p$ is false.
6. The implication is not commutative, and it is not associative. At $p = \mathsf{F}$, $q = \mathsf{T}$, $r = \mathsf{F}$, the formula $(p \to q) \to r$ is false and $p \to (q \to r)$ is true.
7. From $p$ and $p \to q$ we may infer $q$. This is [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]], and it is the elimination rule of the implication.
8. The formulas $q \to (p \to q)$, $\neg p \to (p \to q)$ and $(p \to q) \lor (q \to p)$ are tautologies. They are the [[paradoxes of material implication|3T7ZJFC9QF]].

### History

Philo of Megara, a Greek logician of about 300 BC, held that a conditional is false only when its antecedent is true and its consequent is false.
This is the [[Philonian conditional|V4HMDD1R0D]], and it is the material implication of modern logic.
Gottlob Frege took this conditional as a basic connective of his Begriffsschrift of 1879.
Whitehead and Russell wrote it with the horseshoe $\supset$ in Principia Mathematica.

### Common errors

1. A student reads "$p$ only if $q$" as $q \to p$. The phrase "only if" introduces the consequent, so "$p$ only if $q$" is $p \to q$.
2. A student thinks that $p \to q$ asserts $p$. It asserts only that the case "$p$ true, $q$ false" does not occur.
3. A student negates $p \to q$ as $p \to \neg q$. The correct negation is $p \land \neg q$. At $p = \mathsf{F}$ both $p \to q$ and $p \to \neg q$ are true, so $p \to \neg q$ is not the negation.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An implication "if $p$, then $q$" is a promise.
The promise "if a number ends in $0$, then it is even" is broken only by a number that ends in $0$ and is odd.
The number $20$ keeps the promise, because it ends in $0$ and is even.
The number $13$ does not test the promise, because it does not end in $0$.
A promise that is not tested is not broken, so the implication counts as true for $13$.
No number breaks this promise, so the statement is true.

### L2 human

We compute the truth value of $(p \to q) \to (\neg q \to \neg p)$ for $p = \mathsf{T}$ and $q = \mathsf{F}$.

1. $p \to q$ has antecedent $\mathsf{T}$ and consequent $\mathsf{F}$, so its value is $\mathsf{F}$.
2. $\neg q$ is $\mathsf{T}$, and $\neg p$ is $\mathsf{F}$.
3. $\neg q \to \neg p$ has antecedent $\mathsf{T}$ and consequent $\mathsf{F}$, so its value is $\mathsf{F}$.
4. The whole formula has antecedent $\mathsf{F}$ (step 1), so its value is $\mathsf{T}$.
5. The other three rows give $\mathsf{T}$ as well, so the formula is a tautology.

The typical use of the identity $p \to q \equiv \neg p \lor q$ is to remove arrows before other laws are applied.
For example, $\neg(p \to q) \equiv \neg(\neg p \lor q) \equiv p \land \neg q$ by [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]] and the [[double negation law|0NFAGWH84K]].

A trap: the true formula $q \to (p \to q)$ seems to say that a true statement is implied by every statement.
That is what it says for material implication, and it is harmless in mathematics.
It does not say that $p$ explains $q$ or is relevant to $q$.

### L3 human

Material implication is the residual of conjunction in the [[two-element Boolean algebra|0WG43AC0EV]]: $r \land p \le q$ holds exactly when $r \le (p \to q)$.
In a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]] the implication is $\neg p \lor q$, and it equals the top element exactly when $p \le q$.
In a [[Heyting algebra|NG943XNQWH]] the implication is defined by the same residual property, and $\neg p$ is $p \to \bot$.
Lean follows this view: `Not` is defined as `a → False`.

In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], $\neg p \lor q$ implies $p \to q$, but the converse direction is not valid.
Mathlib proves the equivalence of $p \to q$ and $\neg p \lor q$ as `imp_iff_not_or`, with classical logic.
The [[deduction theorem|9X08XK5RV2]] links the connective to proofs: $\psi$ follows from the hypothesis $\varphi$ exactly when $\varphi \to \psi$ is provable.
The paradoxes of material implication led to the [[strict conditional|5JW54NP52K]] of [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]] and to relevance logics, which ask for a link of content between antecedent and consequent.
