## Summary

An implication "if $p$, then $q$" is a promise, and it is false only when the promise is broken: $p$ true and $q$ false.
A parent says "if it is sunny on Saturday, we go to the beach", and only a sunny Saturday at home breaks that promise.
For [[propositions|0ZGC7ZESYN]] $p$ and $q$, the material implication $p \to q$ is false when $p$ is true and $q$ is false, and true in the three other cases.
The proposition $p$ is the antecedent and $q$ is the consequent.
The implication has the same truth value as $\neg p \lor q$ in every row, and the [[formal definition|0ZNX6DQ3EJ]] gives its table.
Most theorems have the form of an implication, so this connective fixes what a theorem claims and what its proof must show.

## Motivation

Consider the theorem "for every integer $n$, if $4$ divides $n$, then $n$ is even".
It must be true at each integer.
At $n = 6$ and $n = 7$ the antecedent is false, and the theorem must not fail there.
So an implication with a false antecedent counts as true.
At $n = 8$ both parts are true, and the implication is true.
The only integer that could refute the theorem has the antecedent true and the consequent false, and no such integer exists.
Material implication keeps exactly this one case as false.

The word "material" says that only the truth values of $p$ and $q$ count.
No link of meaning, cause or time between $p$ and $q$ is required.
This makes the connective truth-functional, so a [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]] can compute it.
The English side of "if", with its many phrasings and its hidden quantifiers, is treated in [[conditional statements|FA6YZGR2SS]].

## Notation

We write $p \to q$.
Older books and many philosophy books write $p \supset q$, the horseshoe of Principia Mathematica, and many proofs write $p \Rightarrow q$.
In Polish notation the implication is $Cpq$.
In Lean, the implication of two propositions `P` and `Q` is the arrow `P → Q`, the same arrow as the type of functions.

Many authors keep $\Rightarrow$ for the metalanguage, between statements about formulas, and $\to$ for the symbol inside a formula.
For example, "$\models \varphi$ and $\models \varphi \to \psi$ $\Rightarrow$ $\models \psi$" is a claim about the formulas $\varphi$ and $\psi$, while $p \to q$ is one formula.
"Antecedent" is also called hypothesis or premise, and "consequent" is also called conclusion.

## Remarks

### The truth table

Take $p$ = "it is sunny on Saturday" and $q$ = "we go to the beach", and look at four Saturdays.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \to q$ | the Saturday |
|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | sunny, and we go: promise kept |
| T | F | F | sunny, and we stay home: promise broken |
| F | T | T | rainy, and we go anyway: not broken |
| F | F | T | rainy, and we stay home: not broken |

On a rainy Saturday the promise is not tested, so it is not broken, and the implication counts as true.
The column agrees with $\neg p \lor q$ in every row, since both are F only in the row TF.
The negation is $\neg(p \to q) \equiv p \land \neg q$, the connective [[material nonimplication|4EZWY60NYT]].
Two shortcuts follow from the table: a false antecedent makes $p \to q$ true, which is [[vacuous truth|0V4QXPGJQK]], and a true consequent makes it true.

### Ways to say it

All of these sentences mean $p \to q$: "if $p$, then $q$", "$p$ implies $q$", "$q$ if $p$", "$p$ only if $q$", "$p$ is sufficient for $q$" and "$q$ is necessary for $p$".
The word "if" alone comes before the antecedent.
The phrase "only if" comes before the consequent.
So "$n$ is prime only if $n$ is odd" is "if $n$ is prime, then $n$ is odd", which is false at $n = 2$, as [[the four rows for $n = 7, 2, 9, 4$|4D6PNR2FP6]] show.

### Converse, inverse and contrapositive

From $p \to q$ we can form three related implications.
The [[contrapositive|04T1CT7212]] $\neg q \to \neg p$ has the same table as $p \to q$.
The [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]] $q \to p$ and the [[inverse|VB7B8766D2]] $\neg p \to \neg q$ agree with each other but not with $p \to q$.
In the row $p$ F, $q$ T, the implication $p \to q$ is T and the converse is F.
Implication is neither commutative nor associative.
At $p$ F, $q$ T, $r$ F, the formula $(p \to q) \to r$ is F, while $p \to (q \to r)$ is T.

### Proving and using an implication

To prove $p \to q$, assume $p$ and derive $q$.
The [[deduction theorem|9X08XK5RV2]] justifies this: $q$ follows from the hypothesis $p$ exactly when $p \to q$ is provable.
To use $p \to q$, combine it with $p$ to get $q$, which is [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]].
In Lean a proof of `P → Q` is a function that turns each proof of `P` into a proof of `Q`, and modus ponens is function application.

### Strange true implications

"If $2 + 2 = 4$, then $7$ is prime" is true, although its parts have no link of meaning, as [[implications between unrelated statements|6RWNS70KF3]] works out.
The formulas $q \to (p \to q)$, $\neg p \to (p \to q)$ and $(p \to q) \lor (q \to p)$ are [[tautologies|05006AJ7DZ]].
They are the [[paradoxes of material implication|3T7ZJFC9QF]].
They are harmless in mathematics, where a theorem has a hidden "for every", and the implication must hold at every value for a reason.

### What is not an implication

A [[counterfactual|YPJF856FAN]] "if $9$ were even, it would be divisible by $2$" is not a material implication, because its truth does not depend only on the truth values of its parts.
"Because $9$ is odd, $9^2$ is odd" is not an implication either, since "because" asserts that $9$ is odd, while $p \to q$ asserts nothing about $p$.
And the metalanguage claim "$p \land q \models p$" is a statement about formulas, not the formula $(p \land q) \to p$.

### Watch out

The negation of $p \to q$ is $p \land \neg q$, not $p \to \neg q$.
At $p$ F both $p \to q$ and $p \to \neg q$ are T, so $p \to \neg q$ cannot be the negation.
For example, the negation of "if $n$ is prime, then $n$ is odd" is "$n$ is prime and $n$ is even", which holds at $n = 2$.

### History

Philo of Megara, a Greek logician of about 300 BC, held that a conditional is false only when its antecedent is true and its consequent is false.
This is the [[Philonian conditional|V4HMDD1R0D]], and it is the material implication of modern logic.
Gottlob Frege took this conditional as a basic connective of his Begriffsschrift of 1879, and Whitehead and Russell wrote it with the horseshoe $\supset$ in Principia Mathematica.

### Try it

Is "if $1 = 2$, then $5$ is even" true?
The antecedent is F, so the implication is T.

Write the negation of "if $x > 2$, then $x^2 > 4$" without the word "if", and decide whether it can hold for a real $x$.
The negation is "$x > 2$ and $x^2 \le 4$".
It never holds, since $x > 2$ gives $x^2 > 4$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An implication "if $p$, then $q$" is a promise.
The promise "if a number ends in $0$, then it is even" is broken only by a number that ends in $0$ and is odd.
The number $20$ keeps the promise, because it ends in $0$ and is even.
The number $13$ does not test the promise, because it does not end in $0$.
A promise that is not tested is not broken, so the implication counts as true for $13$.
No number breaks this promise, so the statement is true.

### L2 human

Show that $(p \to q) \to (\neg q \to \neg p)$ is a tautology.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \to q$ | $\neg q$ | $\neg p$ | $\neg q \to \neg p$ | whole formula |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | F | F | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | F | F | T |
| F | T | T | F | T | T | T |
| F | F | T | T | T | T | T |

The columns of $p \to q$ and $\neg q \to \neg p$ agree in every row, so the whole formula is T in every row.
The identity $p \to q \equiv \neg p \lor q$ removes arrows before other laws apply.
For example, $\neg(p \to q) \equiv \neg(\neg p \lor q) \equiv p \land \neg q$ by [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]] and the [[double negation law|0NFAGWH84K]].
Watch out: the tautology $q \to (p \to q)$ does not say that $p$ explains $q$ or is relevant to $q$.

### L3 human

Material implication is the residual of conjunction in the [[two-element Boolean algebra|0WG43AC0EV]]: $r \land p \le q$ holds exactly when $r \le (p \to q)$.
In a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]] the implication is $\neg p \lor q$, and it equals the top element exactly when $p \le q$.
In a [[Heyting algebra|NG943XNQWH]] the implication is defined by the same residual property, and $\neg p$ is $p \to \bot$.
Lean follows this view: `Not a` is defined as `a → False`.
In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], $\neg p \lor q$ implies $p \to q$, but the converse direction is not valid, since it would give $p \lor \neg p$ from $p \to p$.
The paradoxes of material implication led to the [[strict conditional|5JW54NP52K]] of [[modal logic|C3RZY20ETK]] and to relevance logics, which ask for a link of content between antecedent and consequent.
