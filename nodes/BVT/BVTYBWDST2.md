## Proof

Let $(\Z, +)$ be the [[additive group of integers|0BSCJA5G53]].
The operation is $+$, the identity is $0$, and the inverse of $a$ is $-a$.

Part 1, the classification.

1. In additive notation, the $k$-th power of $a \in \Z$ is $ka$. So the [[cyclic subgroup|08ABT85339]] generated by $a$ is $\langle a \rangle = a\Z$.
2. In particular $\Z = \langle 1 \rangle$, so $\Z$ is cyclic, with generator $1$.
3. Let $H$ be a [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] of $\Z$. If $H = \set{0}$, then $H = 0\Z$.
4. Suppose $H \neq \set{0}$. Since [[subgroups of cyclic groups are cyclic|0KB1ND7XDS]], there is a least positive integer $n$ with $n \cdot 1 = n \in H$, and $H = \langle n \rangle$.
5. By step 1, $\langle n \rangle = n\Z$. So $H = n\Z$, and $n$ is the least positive element of $H$. This proves existence.
6. Uniqueness follows from part 2, which we prove next. If $n\Z = n'\Z$ with $n, n' \geq 0$, then $n \mid n'$ and $n' \mid n$.
7. If $n = 0$, then $n'$ is a multiple of $0$, so $n' = 0$. If $n > 0$, then $n' \neq 0$, because $n' \mid n$ and $0$ divides only $0$. A positive divisor of a positive integer is at most that integer. So $n \leq n'$ and $n' \leq n$, and $n = n'$.

Part 2, containment.

8. Suppose $m\Z \subseteq n\Z$. Then $m = m \cdot 1 \in n\Z$, so $m = nc$ for some $c \in \Z$. So $n$ [[divides|0PG2H7WKGG]] $m$.
9. Conversely, suppose $m = nc$ with $c \in \Z$. Each element of $m\Z$ is $mk = n(ck)$, which lies in $n\Z$. So $m\Z \subseteq n\Z$.

Part 3, sums.

10. Let $S = a\Z + b\Z$. Then $0 = a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 0 \in S$, so $S \neq \emptyset$.
11. For $s = ax + by$ and $s' = ax' + by'$ in $S$, we have $s - s' = a(x - x') + b(y - y') \in S$. By the [[subgroup criterion|05N4TQKPH0]], $S$ is a subgroup of $\Z$.
12. The set $S$ contains $a = a \cdot 1 + b \cdot 0$ and $b = a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 1$. One of them is not $0$, so $S \neq \set{0}$.
13. By part 1, $S = d\Z$, where $d$ is the least positive element of $S$.
14. Since $a \in S = d\Z$, $d$ divides $a$. In the same way, $d$ divides $b$. So $d$ is a positive common divisor of $a$ and $b$.
15. Since $d \in S$, there are $x, y \in \Z$ with $d = ax + by$. Let $c$ be a common divisor of $a$ and $b$. Then $c$ divides $ax + by = d$.
16. So $\abs{c} \leq d$, because $d > 0$. Thus $d$ is the greatest of the common divisors, and $d = \gcd(a, b)$ by the definition of the [[greatest common divisor|0C8WYW4KYQ]].
17. So $a\Z + b\Z = \gcd(a, b)\Z$.

Part 4, intersections.

18. Let $m, n \geq 1$. The set $m\Z \cap n\Z$ is a subgroup of $\Z$, because the [[intersection of subgroups|0ZTGMDNVTB]] is a subgroup.
19. It contains $mn$, and $mn > 0$. So by part 1, $m\Z \cap n\Z = L\Z$, where $L$ is its least positive element.
20. The positive elements of $m\Z \cap n\Z$ are the positive common multiples of $m$ and $n$. So $L$ is the least positive common multiple.
21. By the definition of the [[least common multiple|007NPQX34K]], $L = \operatorname{lcm}(m, n)$. So $m\Z \cap n\Z = \operatorname{lcm}(m, n)\Z$. $\blacksquare$

## Idea

The integers form a cyclic group generated by $1$.
So each subgroup is generated by its least positive element $n$, and it is the set $n\Z$ of multiples of $n$.
The sum $a\Z + b\Z$ and the intersection $m\Z \cap n\Z$ are subgroups too, so each is the set of multiples of its least positive element.
For the sum, that element divides $a$ and $b$ and is a combination $ax + by$, so it is the $\gcd$.
For the intersection, that element is the least positive common multiple.

## Variants

Part 1 has a direct proof that does not use cyclic groups.
Let $n$ be the least positive element of $H$, and divide an element $h \in H$ by $n$: $h = qn + r$ with $0 \leq r < n$.
Then $r = h - qn \in H$, so $r = 0$ by the choice of $n$, and $h \in n\Z$.

Part 3 can also be proved with the Euclidean algorithm.
The step from $(a, b)$ to $(b, a - qb)$ does not change the subgroup $a\Z + b\Z$.
For $a, b \geq 0$, the algorithm ends at the pair $(\gcd(a, b), 0)$.
