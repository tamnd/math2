## Example

Let $p$ be an integer, and compare two definitions of a [[prime number|01826KMG2H]].
Definition D1 says that $p > 1$ and the only positive divisors of $p$ are $1$ and $p$.
Definition D2 says that $p > 1$ and, for all integers $a$ and $b$, if $p \mid ab$, then $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$.
We prove that D1 and D2 are [[equivalent definitions|NZ42PS2P1A]], one direction at a time.

First we show that D1 implies D2.

1. Assume D1, and let $p \mid ab$.
   If $p \mid a$ we are done, so assume $p \nmid a$.
2. The [[greatest common divisor|0C8WYW4KYQ]] $\gcd(p, a)$ is a positive [[divisor|0PG2H7WKGG]] of $p$, so by D1 it is $1$ or $p$.
3. It is not $p$, because $p \nmid a$, so $\gcd(p, a) = 1$.
4. By [[Bézout's identity|0DQVHMYAGA]] there are integers $x$ and $y$ with $px + ay = 1$.
5. Multiply by $b$:
   $$pbx + aby = b.$$
6. The number $p$ divides $pbx$, and it divides $aby$ because $p \mid ab$, so $p$ divides the sum $b$.

Next we show that D2 implies D1.

1. Assume D2, and let $d$ be a positive divisor of $p$.
   Write $p = de$, where $e$ is a positive integer because $p$ and $d$ are positive.
2. Then $p \mid de$, so D2 gives $p \mid d$ or $p \mid e$.
3. A positive divisor of $p$ is at most $p$, so if $p \mid d$, then $d = p$.
4. If $p \mid e$, then $e = p$ in the same way, and $p = dp$ gives $d = 1$.
5. So every positive divisor of $p$ is $1$ or $p$, and D1 holds.

Check with $p = 7$, $a = 3$ and $b = 14$: we have $\gcd(7, 3) = 1$ with $7 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot (-2) = 1$, and multiplying by $14$ gives $98 - 84 = 14$.

## Remarks

The condition $p > 1$ is needed in both definitions.
Without it, D1 would admit $1$, whose only positive divisor is $1 = p$.
The product part of D2 would admit $1$ and $-1$, which divide every integer, and $0$, since $0 \mid ab$ means $ab = 0$, which forces $a = 0$ or $b = 0$.

The direction D1 implies D2 is [[Euclid's lemma|051VYKA1EX]].
It is the hard direction, because it needs Bézout's identity or the division algorithm, while the other direction is a few lines.

In a general integral domain, D1 becomes the definition of an irreducible element and D2 the definition of a prime element.
They agree in every principal ideal domain, but [[not in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$|5BM35ES8WK]].
School books use D1, because it is easy to test on small numbers, and ring theory uses D2, because it keeps the useful property in more rings.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A prime number is a whole number bigger than $1$ that only $1$ and itself divide, like $2$, $3$, $5$ and $7$.
There is a second way to say what a prime does.
If a prime divides a product of two numbers, it divides at least one of the two numbers.
For example, $3$ divides $4 \cdot 6 = 24$, and $3$ divides $6$.
The number $4$ does not behave like this: $4$ divides $2 \cdot 6 = 12$, but $4$ divides neither $2$ nor $6$.
Mathematicians proved that the two descriptions pick out exactly the same numbers.

### L2 human

We use D2 to show that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.

1. Assume that $\sqrt{2} = a/b$ with positive integers $a$ and $b$ and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.
2. Then $a^2 = 2b^2$, so $2 \mid a \cdot a$.
3. The number $2$ is prime by D1, so it satisfies D2 by the theorem, and $2 \mid a$.
4. Write $a = 2c$.
   Then $4c^2 = 2b^2$, so $b^2 = 2c^2$ and $2 \mid b \cdot b$.
5. By D2 again $2 \mid b$.
   So $2$ divides both $a$ and $b$, which contradicts $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.

The usual pattern is to check primality with D1 and then use D2 in proofs.
Watch out: D2 is false for numbers that are not prime, as the example with $4$ in L1 shows, so step 3 needs the primality of $2$.
