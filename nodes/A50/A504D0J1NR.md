## Example

Take the [[real line|08J2M1JYAG]] $\R$ with its usual topology, and read $\square$ as [[interior|0RZH6W98HK]], as in [[topological semantics|HWJXMBNHQ0]].
We check that $\R$ validates the axioms of $\mathsf{S4}$, and that the formula $\square(p \vee q) \to \square p \vee \square q$ fails at $0$.

1. Axiom $\square p \to p$: for every $A \subseteq \R$, $\mathrm{Int}\, A \subseteq A$. So $V(\square p) \subseteq V(p)$ for every valuation.
2. Axiom $\square p \to \square \square p$: $\mathrm{Int}\, A$ is open, so $\mathrm{Int}\, \mathrm{Int}\, A = \mathrm{Int}\, A$.
3. Axiom $\square(p \to q) \to (\square p \to \square q)$: if $x$ has open neighbourhoods $U \subseteq (\R \setminus A) \cup B$ and $U' \subseteq A$, then $U \cap U'$ is an open neighbourhood of $x$ inside $B$.
4. Necessitation: if $V(\varphi) = \R$ for every valuation, then $V(\square \varphi) = \mathrm{Int}\, \R = \R$.
5. Now let $V(p) = (-\infty, 0]$ and $V(q) = (0, \infty)$. Then $V(p \vee q) = \R$, and $V(\square(p \vee q)) = \R$ contains $0$.
6. $V(\square p) = \mathrm{Int}(-\infty, 0] = (-\infty, 0)$, which does not contain $0$.
7. $V(\square q) = (0, \infty)$, which does not contain $0$. So $\square(p \vee q) \to \square p \vee \square q$ fails at $0$.

## Remarks

The example shows the typical behaviour of a boundary point: $0$ lies in the interior of $p \vee q$ but in the interior of neither part.
The formula $\square(p \vee q) \to \square p \vee \square q$ is not a theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$, so the failure is expected; on $\R$ it fails at every point $x$, under the valuation that splits $\R$ at $x$.
Changing the valuation to $V(p) = \mathbb{Q}$ refutes the McKinsey formula $\square \lozenge p \to \lozenge \square p$ everywhere on $\R$.
These failures are instances of the [[McKinsey-Tarski theorem|2VF0546VXK]]: the logic of $\R$ is exactly $\mathsf{S4}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Split the number line at $0$: "left" means at most $0$ and "right" means more than $0$.
Every point is either left or right, and this stays true near every point.
But near $0$ there are points of both kinds, so near $0$ it is neither "always left" nor "always right".

### L2 human

1. Take a point $x = -1$. Its neighbourhood $(-2, 0)$ lies inside $V(p)$, so $\square p$ holds at $-1$.
2. Take $x = 1$. Its neighbourhood $(0, 2)$ lies inside $V(q)$, so $\square q$ holds at $1$.
3. Take $x = 0$. Every interval $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$ contains negative and positive numbers, so neither $\square p$ nor $\square q$ holds at $0$.

The typical use of such valuations is to refute formulas on $\R$ by putting a boundary at one point.
A trap: the axiom $\square p \to \square \square p$ holds because interiors are open; for an arbitrary operator that only shrinks sets, it can fail.
