## Example

Take the [[real line|08J2M1JYAG]] $\mathbb{R}$ with its usual topology, and read $\square$ as [[interior|0RZH6W98HK]], as in [[topological semantics|HWJXMBNHQ0]].
We check that $\mathbb{R}$ validates the axioms and rules of $\mathsf{S4}$, and that the formula $\square(p \vee q) \to \square p \vee \square q$ fails at $0$.

1. For every $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ we have $\mathrm{Int}\, A \subseteq A$, so $V(\square p) \subseteq V(p)$, and $\square p \to p$ is valid.
2. The set $\mathrm{Int}\, A$ is open, so $\mathrm{Int}\, \mathrm{Int}\, A = \mathrm{Int}\, A$, and $\square p \to \square \square p$ is valid.
3. For the axiom $\square(p \to q) \to (\square p \to \square q)$, let $V(p) = A$ and $V(q) = B$.
   If $x$ has open neighborhoods $U \subseteq (\mathbb{R} \setminus A) \cup B$ and $U' \subseteq A$, then $U \cap U'$ is an open neighborhood of $x$ inside $B$.
4. If $V(\varphi) = \mathbb{R}$ for every valuation, then $V(\square \varphi) = \mathrm{Int}\, \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}$, so necessitation preserves validity.
5. Now let $V(p) = (-\infty, 0]$ and $V(q) = (0, \infty)$.
   Then $V(p \vee q) = \mathbb{R}$, so $\square (p \vee q)$ holds at $0$.
6. Compute the two boxes:
$$V(\square p) = \mathrm{Int}\,(-\infty, 0] = (-\infty, 0), \qquad V(\square q) = \mathrm{Int}\,(0, \infty) = (0, \infty).$$
7. Neither set contains $0$, so $\square p \vee \square q$ fails at $0$, and so does the formula.

## Remarks

The point $0$ is a boundary point: it lies in the interior of $p \vee q$ but in the interior of neither part.
The formula $\square(p \vee q) \to \square p \vee \square q$ is not a theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$, so its failure is expected.
On $\mathbb{R}$ it fails at every point $x$, under the valuation that splits the line at $x$ in the same way.

Other valuations refute other non-theorems.
With $V(p) = \mathbb{Q}$, every interval meets both $\mathbb{Q}$ and its complement, so $\lozenge p$ is true everywhere and $\square p$ nowhere.
Then the McKinsey formula $\square \lozenge p \to \lozenge \square p$ fails at every point.
These failures are instances of the [[McKinsey-Tarski theorem|2VF0546VXK]]: the logic of $\mathbb{R}$ is exactly $\mathsf{S4}$, so every non-theorem fails under some valuation.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Split the number line at $0$: "left" means at most $0$ and "right" means more than $0$.
Every point is left or right, and this stays true near every point.
But near $0$ there are points of both kinds.
So near $0$ it is neither "always left" nor "always right".

### L2 human

We check the truth of $\square p$ and $\square q$ at three points.
At $x = -1$, the neighborhood $(-2, 0)$ lies inside $V(p)$, so $\square p$ holds.
At $x = 1$, the neighborhood $(0, 2)$ lies inside $V(q)$, so $\square q$ holds.
At $x = 0$, every interval $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$ contains negative and positive numbers, so neither $\square p$ nor $\square q$ holds.
The typical use of such valuations is to refute a formula on $\mathbb{R}$ by putting a boundary at one point.
Watch out: the axiom $\square p \to \square \square p$ holds because interiors are open, and an operator that only shrinks sets need not satisfy it.
