## Definition

Consider a fault that separates two crustal blocks whose far-field parts move relative to each other at a steady velocity $v$.
*Elastic-rebound theory* is the model of earthquakes with the following three assumptions.

1. Between earthquakes the fault is locked and the rock around it deforms [[elastically|6W00ZECYT0]]. The shear [[stress|HXGCGHS070]] on the fault grows at a rate proportional to $v$.
2. An earthquake starts when the shear stress on the fault reaches the frictional strength of the fault.
3. During the earthquake the fault slips suddenly, the slip relieves the accumulated strain, and the rock near the fault returns towards its unstrained shape.

## Remarks

Example: the spring-slider model.
A block rests on a surface and is pulled by a spring of stiffness $k$ whose far end moves at speed $v$.
By [[Hooke's law|V56XAESDNE]] the spring force is $k(vt - x)$, where $x$ is the position of the block.
The block stays at rest until the force reaches the static friction $F_s$, and then slides until the force drops to the dynamic friction $F_d < F_s$.
So each slip has size $\Delta x = (F_s - F_d)/k$, and the slips recur with period $T = \Delta x / v = (F_s - F_d)/(kv)$.
The three clauses hold: the locked phase is clause 1, the threshold is clause 2, and the drop of the force is clause 3.
Non-example: steady aseismic creep, where the fault slides continuously at rate $v$, fails clause 1, since no strain accumulates.
Real faults are only roughly periodic, because strength, loading and interactions with other faults vary.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The theory says that a fault is stuck while stress builds up, and slips all at once when the stress becomes too large.
The slip undoes the bending of the rock that the slow motion of the plates caused.
