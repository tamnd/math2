## Statement

Let $G$ be a finite [[group|G5S1M9]], and let $H$ be a [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] of $G$.
Then the [[order|0B85S2V2DR]] $|H|$ of $H$ divides the order $|G|$ of $G$.
More precisely, $|G| = [G : H] \cdot |H|$, where $[G : H]$ is the number of [[left cosets|03DEH43F4H]] of $H$ in $G$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The group $G$ must be finite. The order of an infinite group is not a natural number, so "divides" has no meaning. The [[counting form|04DC0SRN2K]] $|G| = [G : H] \cdot |H|$ still holds as an equation of cardinal numbers.
- The set $H$ must be a subgroup. In $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$, the subset $\{0, 1\}$ has $2$ elements, and $2$ does not divide $3$. This subset is not closed under addition, because $1 + 1 = 2$.
- The structure $G$ must be a group, not only a monoid. The set $\{0, 1, 2\}$ with the operation $\max$ is a monoid with identity $0$. Its submonoid $\{0, 1\}$ has $2$ elements, and $2$ does not divide $3$.

### Consequences

- [[The order of $G$ is the index times the order of $H$|04DC0SRN2K]]: $|G| = [G : H] \cdot |H|$, so $[G : H] = |G| / |H|$.
- [[The order of each element divides the order of the group|08V7JHS3QZ]], because the element generates a cyclic subgroup of that order.
- [[Each element $g$ satisfies $g^{|G|} = e$|0ZEPNS9BXK]].
- [[A group of prime order is cyclic|05MM4EK4SA]].
- [[Fermat's little theorem|0M25PRN3WQ]] and [[Euler's theorem|0VC4DVE7DQ]] follow from the case of the [[group of units of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$|0F8X8ZRQ53]].
- A subgroup of a finite group $G$ with $|H| > |G|/2$ is equal to $G$, because $|H|$ divides $|G|$.

### Converse

The converse is false.
A divisor $d$ of $|G|$ need not be the order of a subgroup of $G$.
The [[alternating group|0Z5905Q73D]] $A_4$ has order $12$, but [[it has no subgroup of order $6$|0PDBVPP233]].
Some partial converses are true.
[[Cauchy's theorem|0JM7PA288B]] gives a subgroup of order $p$ for each prime $p$ that divides $|G|$.
The [[Sylow theorems|FESTJ0K9TX]] give a subgroup of order $p^k$ for each prime power $p^k$ that divides $|G|$.
A finite [[cyclic group|0KVGH0J5B3]] has exactly one subgroup of each order $d$ that divides $|G|$.
The groups for which the full converse holds are the [[CLT groups|~d0e7afc6]].

### Generalizations

- The [[tower law|0ZQJ7KEFQ5]] $[G : K] = [G : H] \cdot [H : K]$ holds for subgroups $K \le H \le G$ of any group.
- The [[product formula|08PY4GNAPK]] $|HK| = |H| \, |K| / |H \cap K|$ counts a product of two subgroups.
- The [[orbit-stabiliser theorem|0E2HSCKZ6C]] says that the size of an orbit is the index of a stabiliser. Lagrange's theorem is the case of the action of $G$ on $G/H$.
- The analogue for [[double cosets|~d8f44326]] fails: the size of a double coset need not divide $|G|$.

## History

Joseph-Louis Lagrange did not prove the theorem in this form.
In his memoir on the algebraic solution of equations of 1770 to 1771, he studied a polynomial in $n$ variables.
He showed that the number of different polynomials obtained by all $n!$ permutations of the variables divides $n!$.
In modern terms, this is the theorem for subgroups of the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_n$.
Carl Friedrich Gauss proved the case of the nonzero residues modulo a prime in 1801.
Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the case of the symmetric groups in 1844.
Camille Jordan proved the theorem for all permutation groups in 1861.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A group with $12$ elements can have a subgroup with $3$ elements or with $4$ elements.
It cannot have a subgroup with $5$ elements, because $5$ does not divide $12$.
The reason is that the copies of a subgroup cut the group into pieces of the same size.
If the subgroup has $3$ elements, then the pieces have $3$ elements each.
Thus $12$ is a whole number of pieces of size $3$, here $4$ pieces.
The theorem says that this is true for each finite group and each subgroup.

### L2 human

We check the theorem for $G = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ and $H = \{0, 4, 8\}$.

1. The set $H$ is a subgroup of order $3$, because it is the set of multiples of $4$ modulo $12$.
2. The left cosets are $H$, $1 + H = \{1, 5, 9\}$, $2 + H = \{2, 6, 10\}$ and $3 + H = \{3, 7, 11\}$.
3. These four cosets are disjoint, and their union is $G$.
4. Each coset has $3$ elements.
5. Thus $|G| = 4 \cdot 3 = 12$, and $|H| = 3$ divides $12$.

The typical use of the theorem is to exclude.
A group of order $35$ has subgroups of orders $1$, $5$, $7$ or $35$ only.
Thus each element other than $e$ has order $5$, $7$ or $35$.
A group of order $p$ prime has no subgroups other than $\{e\}$ and $G$.

A trap is to use the converse.
The theorem does not give a subgroup of each order that divides $|G|$.
The group $A_4$ of order $12$ has no subgroup of order $6$.

### L3 human

The theorem is a statement about the partition of $G$ into the orbits of $H$, acting by right multiplication.
All orbits are in bijection with $H$, so $|G|$ is a multiple of $|H|$.
The same argument gives the [[orbit-stabiliser theorem|0E2HSCKZ6C]] and, with the [[tower law|0ZQJ7KEFQ5]], the multiplicativity of the index.
In linear algebra, the analogue is $\dim V = \dim W + \dim (V/W)$, and for finite fields it gives that $|W|$ divides $|V|$.
In field theory, the analogue is the tower law for the degrees of field extensions.

The converse fails, and the study of its partial forms is a large part of finite group theory.
[[Cauchy's theorem|0JM7PA288B]], the [[Sylow theorems|FESTJ0K9TX]] and [[Hall's theorem on solvable groups|8Z8PE69SJS]] give subgroups of some orders.
The [[CLT groups|~d0e7afc6]] are the groups with subgroups of all possible orders.
Each CLT group is [[solvable|0FXJ7YFMMX]], and each [[supersolvable group|AWWM9RWETX]] is CLT.
