## Example

Let $L$ be the set of formulas of [[propositional logic|09K9MWD2QQ]] built from the variables $p, q, r, \dots$ with $\neg, \wedge, \vee, \to$.
A valuation is a map $v$ from the variables to $\{ 0, 1 \}$, extended to all formulas by the truth tables.
For $\Gamma \subseteq L$ and $\varphi \in L$, put $\Gamma \models \varphi$ when every valuation that gives $1$ to all formulas of $\Gamma$ gives $1$ to $\varphi$.
This is [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]] for propositional logic.

Two instances first, by truth tables over $p$ and $q$.
We have $p, p \to q \models q$: the only row with $p = 1$ and $p \to q = 1$ is $p = q = 1$, and there $q = 1$.
We have $p \to q, q \not\models p$: the row $p = 0$, $q = 1$ makes both premises $1$ and the conclusion $0$.

Now we check the three conditions of a [[consequence relation|J78Y1M4GND]], and then two extra properties.

1. Reflexivity.
   If $\varphi \in \Gamma$, a valuation that gives $1$ to all of $\Gamma$ gives $1$ to $\varphi$.
2. Monotonicity.
   Let $\Gamma \subseteq \Delta$ and $\Gamma \models \varphi$.
   A valuation that gives $1$ to all of $\Delta$ gives $1$ to all of $\Gamma$, and so to $\varphi$.
3. Cut.
   Let $\Gamma \models \psi$ for all $\psi \in \Delta$, and let $\Gamma \cup \Delta \models \varphi$.
   A valuation that gives $1$ to all of $\Gamma$ gives $1$ to all of $\Delta$, hence to all of $\Gamma \cup \Delta$, hence to $\varphi$.
4. Structurality.
   Let $\sigma$ be a substitution and $v$ a valuation, and define $v'(p) = v(\sigma(p))$.
   By induction on formulas, $v'(\alpha) = v(\sigma(\alpha))$ for every $\alpha$, because $\sigma$ commutes with the connectives.
   If $v$ gives $1$ to all of $\sigma[\Gamma]$, then $v'$ gives $1$ to all of $\Gamma$, so $v'(\varphi) = 1$, that is, $v(\sigma(\varphi)) = 1$.
5. Finitarity.
   Suppose $\Gamma_0 \not\models \varphi$ for every finite $\Gamma_0 \subseteq \Gamma$.
   Then each finite subset of $\Gamma \cup \{ \neg \varphi \}$ has a valuation that gives $1$ to all its members.
   By the [[compactness theorem|1NR3HAWG9D]], one valuation gives $1$ to all of $\Gamma \cup \{ \neg \varphi \}$, so $\Gamma \not\models \varphi$.

So $\models$ is a finitary structural consequence relation.

## Remarks

Steps 1 to 3 use nothing about truth tables, so they hold for every semantics given by a class of valuations.
Step 5 is the only step that needs a real theorem, because for an infinite $\Gamma$ there is no finite truth table to check.

Change the truth tables and the relation changes, but steps 1 to 4 survive.
In the strong Kleene logic with values $0, \tfrac12, 1$ and designated value $1$, the relation is again a structural consequence relation.
There $\emptyset \models p \vee \neg p$ fails, because the valuation $v(p) = \tfrac12$ gives $p \vee \neg p$ the value $\tfrac12$.
The second instance above, $p \to q, q \not\models p$, is the fallacy of affirming the consequent, seen as a failure of consequence.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An argument is valid when the conclusion is true in every row of the truth table where all premises are true.
"$p$" and "if $p$ then $q$" give "$q$", because the only row where both premises are true has $q$ true.
"If $p$ then $q$" and "$q$" do not give "$p$": the row with $p$ false and $q$ true is a counterexample.
The three rules of a consequence relation hold for this notion of validity, because they only talk about the rows where the premises are true.

### L2 human

We follow one case of cut with real formulas.
Let $\Gamma = \{ p, p \to q \}$ and $\Delta = \{ q \}$, so $\Gamma \models q$ by the first instance.
Let $\varphi = q \vee r$.
Every valuation with $q = 1$ gives $q \vee r = 1$, so $\Gamma \cup \Delta \models q \vee r$.
Cut now gives $\Gamma \models q \vee r$.
Check: a valuation with $p = 1$ and $p \to q = 1$ has $q = 1$, so $q \vee r = 1$.

The typical use of cut is to chain lemmas: once $\Gamma \models q$ is known, $q$ may serve as an extra premise.
Watch out: validity is a claim about every row in which the premises are true, so one good row proves nothing.
