## Example

Let $L$ be the set of formulas of [[propositional logic|09K9MWD2QQ]] built from the variables $p, q, r, \dots$ with $\neg, \wedge, \vee, \to$.
A valuation is a map $v$ from the variables to $\set{0, 1}$, extended to all formulas by the truth tables.
For $\Gamma \subseteq L$ and $\varphi \in L$, put $\Gamma \models \varphi$ when every valuation that gives $1$ to all formulas of $\Gamma$ gives $1$ to $\varphi$.
This is [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]] for propositional logic.
We check the conditions of a [[consequence relation|J78Y1M4GND]], and then two extra properties.

1. Reflexivity. If $\varphi \in \Gamma$, a valuation that gives $1$ to all of $\Gamma$ gives $1$ to $\varphi$.
2. Monotonicity. If $\Gamma \subseteq \Delta$ and $v$ gives $1$ to all of $\Delta$, then $v$ gives $1$ to all of $\Gamma$, so $\Gamma \models \varphi$ gives $v(\varphi) = 1$.
3. Cut. Let $\Gamma \models \psi$ for all $\psi \in \Delta$ and $\Gamma \cup \Delta \models \varphi$. A valuation $v$ that gives $1$ to all of $\Gamma$ gives $1$ to all of $\Delta$, hence to all of $\Gamma \cup \Delta$, hence to $\varphi$.
4. Structurality. Let $\sigma$ be a substitution and $v$ a valuation, and define $v'(p) = v(\sigma(p))$. By induction on formulas, $v'(\alpha) = v(\sigma(\alpha))$ for every $\alpha$, since $\sigma$ commutes with the connectives. If $v$ gives $1$ to all of $\sigma[\Gamma]$, then $v'$ gives $1$ to all of $\Gamma$, so $v'(\varphi) = 1$ and $v(\sigma(\varphi)) = 1$.
5. Finitarity. Suppose $\Gamma_0 \not\models \varphi$ for every finite $\Gamma_0 \subseteq \Gamma$. Then each finite subset of $\Gamma \cup \set{\neg \varphi}$ has a valuation that gives $1$ to all its members. By the [[compactness theorem|1NR3HAWG9D]], one valuation gives $1$ to all of $\Gamma \cup \set{\neg \varphi}$, so $\Gamma \not\models \varphi$.

So $\models$ is a finitary structural consequence relation.

Two instances, by truth tables over $p, q$.

6. $\set{p, p \to q} \models q$. The only row with $p = 1$ and $(p \to q) = 1$ is $p = 1, q = 1$, and there $q = 1$.
7. $\set{p \to q, q} \not\models p$. The row $p = 0, q = 1$ gives $(p \to q) = 1$ and $q = 1$, but $p = 0$.

## Remarks

Steps 1 to 3 use nothing about truth tables, so they hold for any semantics given by a class of valuations.
Step 5 is the only step that needs a real theorem; for an infinite $\Gamma$ there is no finite truth table to check.
Changing the truth tables changes the relation but keeps steps 1 to 4.
In the strong Kleene logic with three values $0, \tfrac12, 1$ and designated value $1$, the relation is again a structural consequence relation, but $\emptyset \models p \vee \neg p$ fails, as the valuation $v(p) = \tfrac12$ shows.
Instance 7 is the fallacy of affirming the consequent, seen as a failure of consequence.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An argument is valid when the conclusion is true in every row of the truth table where all premises are true.
"$p$" and "if $p$ then $q$" give "$q$", because the only row where both premises are true has $q$ true.
"If $p$ then $q$" and "$q$" do not give "$p$": the row with $p$ false and $q$ true is a counterexample.
The three rules of a consequence relation hold for this notion of validity, because they only talk about rows where premises are true.

### L2 human

We check one case of cut with concrete formulas.

1. Let $\Gamma = \set{p, p \to q}$ and $\Delta = \set{q}$; then $\Gamma \models q$ by instance 6.
2. Let $\varphi = q \vee r$. Then $\Gamma \cup \Delta \models q \vee r$, since every valuation with $q = 1$ gives $q \vee r = 1$.
3. Cut gives $\Gamma \models q \vee r$.
4. Direct check: a valuation with $p = 1$ and $(p \to q) = 1$ has $q = 1$, so $q \vee r = 1$.

The typical use of cut is to chain lemmas: once $\Gamma \models q$ is known, $q$ may be used as an extra premise.
A trap is to check validity on one row only; validity is a statement about every row in which the premises are true.
