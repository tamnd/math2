## Summary

The Kelvin–Helmholtz instability is the instability of a shear layer, where two layers of fluid slide past each other.
Small waves on the interface grow and roll up into a row of vortices.
The simplest model has two uniform streams separated by a sharp interface.
In it every wavelength is unstable unless gravity or surface tension holds the long or short waves back.
It is a basic mechanism of [[hydrodynamic stability|H028A17Z55]] and of mixing in the atmosphere, the ocean and astrophysical flows.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Wind blowing over water raises waves; this is the same effect in its simplest form.
Where the two layers meet, a small bump makes the faster fluid speed up over its crest, which lowers the pressure there and pulls the bump further out.
The bump grows, tips over and curls into a vortex.
Rows of such curls are sometimes visible in clouds, where they look like breaking ocean waves in the sky.
