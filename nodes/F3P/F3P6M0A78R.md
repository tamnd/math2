## Example

Let $U = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
We check that $U$ is [[unitary|0D6RMJ10M9]] with determinant $1$, and we diagonalize it with a unitary matrix.

1. Transpose and conjugate to get the [[conjugate transpose|0EX045E2BP]] $U^* = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Multiply:
$$U^*U = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 + (-i) \cdot i & i + (-i) \\ -i + i & (-i) \cdot i + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2.$$
So $U$ is unitary.
3. The determinant is $\det U = \frac{1}{2}(1 \cdot 1 - i \cdot i) = \frac{1}{2}(1 + 1) = 1$. So $U$ lies in the [[special unitary group|ZRYYPHWESP]] $SU(2)$.
4. The trace is $\tr U = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}$, so the characteristic polynomial is $t^2 - \sqrt{2}\, t + 1$.
5. The quadratic formula gives the [[eigenvalues|0ZND7NQ970]]
$$\lambda_\pm = \frac{\sqrt{2} \pm \sqrt{2 - 4}}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{2} \pm i\sqrt{2}}{2} = \frac{1 \pm i}{\sqrt{2}} = e^{\pm i\pi/4}.$$
Both have absolute value $1$.
6. For eigenvectors, $U(1, 1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1 + i, 1 + i) = \lambda_+ (1, 1)$, and $U(1, -1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1 - i, -1 + i) = \lambda_- (1, -1)$.
7. The two eigenvectors are orthogonal, since $\ip{(1, 1)}{(1, -1)} = 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (-1) = 0$.
8. Normalize them and put them as columns of $V = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$. Then $V$ is unitary and $U = V \operatorname{diag}(e^{i\pi/4}, e^{-i\pi/4}) V^*$.

Check: the product of the eigenvalues is $e^{i\pi/4} e^{-i\pi/4} = 1 = \det U$, and their sum is $\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2} = \tr U$.

## Remarks

The example shows every conclusion of [[the properties of unitary matrices|GZ7NG7J3J8]] at once.
The determinant and both eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, the eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and a unitary change of basis makes $U$ diagonal.

In the quaternion picture, $U$ has the shape $\begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ -\overline{\beta} & \overline{\alpha} \end{pmatrix}$ with $\alpha = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $\beta = \frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}$.
By [[the quaternion theorem|A763JSEVW8]], it is the image of the unit quaternion $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1 + k)$.

Powers are easy in the diagonal form.
Squaring gives
$$U^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 + i^2 & i + i \\ i + i & i^2 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
and $U^8 = I_2$, because $\lambda_\pm^8 = e^{\pm 2\pi i} = 1$.

If the entry $i$ were replaced by $1$, the matrix $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ would have determinant $0$ and would not be unitary.
Also note that $U$ is symmetric, $U^{\mathsf{T}} = U$, but not Hermitian, since $U^* \ne U$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

This matrix keeps all lengths in the complex space of two coordinates.
It has two special directions, $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$.
Along each of them it only multiplies by a complex number of size $1$.
These two numbers are an eighth of a turn forward and an eighth of a turn back on the unit circle.

### L2 human

Here is the typical use: the same steps diagonalize any $2 \times 2$ unitary matrix.
Find the eigenvalues from the trace and the determinant, find one eigenvector for each, normalize, and use them as the columns of $V$.
For a unitary matrix the eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal, so no Gram-Schmidt step is needed when the eigenvalues differ.
Watch out: the trace $\sqrt{2}$ and the determinant $1$ are real, but the eigenvalues are not, so a real trace and a real determinant do not give real eigenvalues.
