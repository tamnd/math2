## Example

Let $Q$ be the property of a world in a Kripke model with one relation: "the world has at least two distinct successors".
We show that no formula $\varphi$ of the [[modal mu-calculus|MZ0M2KR9S0]] defines $Q$.
That is, no $\varphi$ satisfies $\mathfrak{M}, w \models \varphi$ exactly when $w$ has property $Q$, for every model $\mathfrak{M}$ and world $w$.

Take two small models in which every propositional variable is false everywhere.
The model $\mathfrak{M}_1$ has worlds $w, u_1, u_2$ and the relation $R_1 = \{(w, u_1), (w, u_2)\}$.
The model $\mathfrak{M}_2$ has worlds $w', u'$ and the relation $R_2 = \{(w', u')\}$.
Let $Z = \{(w, w'), (u_1, u'), (u_2, u')\}$.

1. Atoms: every variable is false at every world of both models, so linked worlds agree on all variables.
2. Forth at $(w, w')$: the successors of $w$ are $u_1$ and $u_2$, and both are linked to $u'$, with $w' R_2 u'$.
3. Back at $(w, w')$: the only successor of $w'$ is $u'$, and $w R_1 u_1$ with $u_1 Z u'$.
4. Forth and back at $(u_1, u')$ and $(u_2, u')$: none of these worlds has a successor, so there is nothing to check.
5. By steps 1 to 4, $Z$ is a [[bisimulation|X27BR4YP2Y]] that links $w$ and $w'$.
6. By [[bisimulation invariance|TKFFTAX30E]], extended to fixed points, $w$ and $w'$ satisfy the same formulas of $L_\mu$.
7. World $w$ has the two successors $u_1 \neq u_2$, so it has property $Q$.
   World $w'$ has one successor, so it does not.
8. A formula that defined $Q$ would be true at $w$ and false at $w'$, against step 6.
   So no formula defines $Q$.

## Remarks

Counting successors is outside the $\mu$-calculus, although first-order logic says it at once with $\exists y \exists z (x R y \wedge x R z \wedge y \neq z)$.
On the other side, $L_\mu$ expresses reachability, which first-order logic cannot.
So the two logics are incomparable in expressive power.
Graded modal logic adds diamonds $\lozenge_{\geq k}$ that count, and its fixed-point extension is the graded $\mu$-calculus.

The argument depends on the two successors of $w$ looking alike.
If we make $p$ true at $u_1$ only, then $Z$ fails the atom clause at $(u_1, u')$, and indeed $\lozenge p \wedge \lozenge \neg p$ is true at $w$ and false at $w'$.
That formula forces two distinct successors, but it does not define $Q$, because a world with two successors that agree on $p$ falsifies it.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A modal formula only looks at what the next worlds are like, not at how many copies of them there are.
In the first model the world $w$ has two identical next worlds, and in the second model $w'$ has one.
Seen from inside the logic, the two pictures look the same.
So the logic cannot say "there are two next worlds".

### L2 human

We test one formula on both models to see the invariance at work.
Take $\varphi = \lozenge \square \bot$, "some successor has no successor".
In $\mathfrak{M}_1$, $w R_1 u_1$ and $u_1$ has no successor, so $u_1 \models \square \bot$ and $w \models \varphi$.
In $\mathfrak{M}_2$, $w' R_2 u'$ and $u'$ has no successor, so $w' \models \varphi$ too.
The two worlds agree on $\varphi$, as the invariance predicts.
This is the typical way to show that a property is not definable: find two bisimilar worlds that differ on it.
Watch out: the atom clause must hold for every variable, so you cannot ignore variables that the property does not mention.
