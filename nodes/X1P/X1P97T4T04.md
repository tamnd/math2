## Example

The claim is that for all integers $a$, $b$ and $c$, if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$.
The claim uses one defined term, "divides", three times.
By the method of [[unpacking definitions|41JZ39GE3F]], we replace each use by its definition, and then the proof is a calculation.

By the definition of [[divisibility|0PG2H7WKGG]], for integers $x$ and $y$, $x \mid y$ means that there is an integer $q$ with $y = xq$.
We unpack each part of the claim.

1. The given $a \mid b$ becomes: there is an integer $s$ with $b = as$.
2. The given $b \mid c$ becomes: there is an integer $u$ with $c = bu$.
   The letter is new, because the two quotients can differ.
3. The goal $a \mid c$ becomes: there is an integer $t$ with $c = at$, so the task is to find $t$.
4. We do not unpack "integer", "product" or "equals", because the rules of algebra for [[integers|0C4JS9RF5M]] are known results.

Now the proof is a substitution.

> Let $a$, $b$ and $c$ be integers with $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$.
> By the definition of divisibility, there are integers $s$ and $u$ with $b = as$ and $c = bu$.
> Substituting the first equation into the second gives $c = (as)u = a(su)$.
> The number $t = su$ is an integer, as a product of integers.
> So $c = at$ with an integer $t$, and hence $a \mid c$.
> $\square$

Check: $a = 2$, $b = 6$ and $c = 18$ give $s = 3$, $u = 3$ and $t = 9$, and indeed $18 = 2 \cdot 9$.

## Remarks

Before unpacking, the claim looks like a fact about a relation.
After unpacking, it is a fact about two equations, and the proof is one line of algebra.
The goal unpacks to an existential statement, so the proof must name the witness, here $t = su$, as the [[moves for each logical form|872K5XB580]] require.

Unpacking also shows when a similar claim fails.
Say that $a$ properly divides $b$ when $a \mid b$ and $a \ne b$.
Then $2$ properly divides $-2$, and $-2$ properly divides $2$, but $2$ does not properly divide $2$, because the second condition $2 \ne 2$ fails.
So proper division is not transitive, and the unpacked goal shows exactly which part breaks.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"$3$ divides $12$" means that $12$ is $3$ times a whole number, namely $4$.
To show that "$a$ divides $b$" and "$b$ divides $c$" give "$a$ divides $c$", we write out what each phrase means.
For example, $2$ divides $6$ because $6 = 2 \times 3$, and $6$ divides $18$ because $18 = 6 \times 3$.
Putting the two together, $18 = 2 \times 3 \times 3 = 2 \times 9$.
So $2$ divides $18$, and the same steps work for any numbers.

### L2 human

We unpack and prove: if $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$, then $a \mid bx + cy$ for all integers $x$ and $y$.
The givens unpack to $b = as$ and $c = at$ for integers $s$ and $t$, and the goal to "$bx + cy = aq$ for some integer $q$".
Substitution gives
$$bx + cy = asx + aty = a(sx + ty),$$
and $q = sx + ty$ is an integer, so $a \mid bx + cy$.

Check: $a = 3$, $b = 6$, $c = 9$, $x = 2$ and $y = -1$ give $bx + cy = 3$ and $q = 2 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot (-1) = 1$.
This pattern solves most exercises on divisibility and congruences.
Watch out: "$b = as$ and $c = as$" reuses a letter, and each existential statement needs its own witness.
