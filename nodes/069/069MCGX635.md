## Summary

The row space of a matrix collects every equation that follows from its rows by adding and scaling.
It is the space that row reduction works in, so row reduction never changes it, and the reduced form shows a basis of it.
For an $m \times n$ [[matrix|0VD9ZSWWJG]] $A$ over a field $F$ with rows $r_1, \dots, r_m$, the *row space* is the [[span|0DTY8B9HMS]] of the rows, a [[subspace|00YC1D652K]] of $F^n$:
$$\operatorname{Row}(A) = \operatorname{span}(r_1, \dots, r_m) \subseteq F^n.$$
The [[formal definition|0EP73HK4C4]] states it.
Equivalently, $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ is the column space of the [[transpose|09DXWYRD43]] $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.

## Motivation

Each row of $A$ holds the coefficients of one equation of the system $Ax = b$.
Adding two equations, or multiplying one by a number, gives a new equation that every solution still satisfies.
So the row space is the set of all linear consequences of the left sides of the equations.
Row reduction replaces the given rows by simpler rows with the same span, and the zero rows that appear show which equations were redundant.

The row space also measures the rank from the side of the equations.
Its dimension is the number of independent equations, and the theorem that [[row rank equals column rank|0TZAG60ZYT]] says that this equals the dimension of the column space.
The row space is one of the [[four fundamental subspaces|73DVECPAT6]], and over $\mathbb{R}$ it is the orthogonal complement of the null space.

## Notation

Books write $\operatorname{Row}(A)$, $\operatorname{Row} A$ or $C(A^{\mathsf{T}})$.
Strang writes $C(A^{\mathsf{T}})$, which treats the rows of $A$ as the columns of $A^{\mathsf{T}}$.
Some books keep the rows as row vectors in $F^{1 \times n}$, while others stand them up as vectors of $F^n$, and the two views give the same subspace up to this identification.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take the [[3 by 4 matrix with rows (1, 2, 0, 1), (2, 4, 1, 4) and (3, 6, 1, 5)|8ZAX51AECV]].
Row reduction gives the nonzero rows $u_1 = (1, 2, 0, 1)$ and $u_2 = (0, 0, 1, 2)$, and they span the same space as the three original rows.
Check this on the original rows:
$$2u_1 + u_2 = (2, 4, 1, 4), \qquad 3u_1 + u_2 = (3, 6, 1, 5).$$
So the row space is a plane in $\mathbb{R}^4$ with basis $u_1, u_2$.
To test whether $(1, 2, 1, 3)$ lies in it, read the coefficients at the pivot columns 1 and 3, which gives $1$ and $1$, and check that $u_1 + u_2 = (1, 2, 1, 3)$.

### Why row operations keep it

Each elementary row operation replaces the rows by combinations of the old rows, so the new row space lies inside the old one.
Each operation can also be undone by another row operation, so the old row space lies inside the new one.
So [[row equivalent|006BHFWMAP]] matrices have the same row space, by the [[theorem on row equivalence|E4GKK727ZM]].
The nonzero rows of any echelon form are then a basis, by the [[echelon basis theorem|1JGMW9DM4Z]], and the number of pivots is $\dim \operatorname{Row}(A)$.

The echelon form need not be reduced.
For the [[matrix with rows (0, 2, 4), (1, 1, 1) and (1, 3, 5)|NZ8PF59MTJ]], an echelon form has the rows $(1, 1, 1)$ and $(0, 2, 4)$, and these already form a basis of the plane $x - 2y + z = 0$.

### What it is not

The row space is not the column space, even for a square matrix.
For $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the [[column space is the line through (1, 0)|BGWTDZRS5T]], while the row space is the line through $(1, 1)$.
Column operations do not keep the row space either: subtracting column 1 from column 2 in that matrix gives $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, whose row space is the line through $(1, 0)$.

### The rules you can use

A product never has a larger row space than its right factor: $\operatorname{Row}(BA) \subseteq \operatorname{Row}(A)$ for every $k \times m$ matrix $B$, because each row of $BA$ is a combination of the rows of $A$ with coefficients from a row of $B$.
When $B$ is invertible the two row spaces are equal, which is the statement about row operations in matrix form.
The dimensions agree, $\dim \operatorname{Row}(A) = \dim \operatorname{Col}(A) = \operatorname{rank} A$.
An invertible $n \times n$ matrix has row space $F^n$, and the row space of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ is the line in $F^3$ through $(1, 2, 3)$.

### Watch out

Do not take the first $r$ rows of $A$ as a basis, where $r$ is the rank.
For the rows $(1, 1)$, $(2, 2)$ and $(0, 1)$, the rank is $2$, but the first two rows are dependent, since $(2, 2) = 2(1, 1)$.
The correct move is to row reduce and take the nonzero rows of the echelon form, here $(1, 1)$ and $(0, 1)$.
Also keep the spaces apart: the row space lies in $F^n$ and the column space in $F^m$.

### History

Elimination on the rows of a table of coefficients already appears in the Chinese text *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*.

### Try it

First, find a basis of the row space of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$.
Subtracting $3$ times row 1 from row 2 gives a zero row, so $(1, 2)$ is a basis.

Second, is $(1, 0, 1)$ in the row space of the matrix with rows $(0, 2, 4)$, $(1, 1, 1)$ and $(1, 3, 5)$?
The row space is the plane $x - 2y + z = 0$, and $1 - 0 + 1 = 2 \ne 0$, so no.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of each row as one equation.
You may add equations and multiply them by numbers, and every solution still satisfies the result.
The row space is the collection of everything you can make this way.
Row reduction only mixes the rows, so it keeps the row space.
For the rows $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 6)$, the second row is three times the first, so the row space is just the line through $(1, 2)$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical problem: describe the row space of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ by an equation.
Swap rows 1 and 2, subtract the new row 1 from row 3, and subtract row 2 from row 3, which gives the echelon form
$$U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
So $(1, 1, 1)$ and $(0, 2, 4)$ are a basis, and the row space is a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
A normal vector $n$ must satisfy $n_1 + n_2 + n_3 = 0$ and $2n_2 + 4n_3 = 0$, and $n = (1, -2, 1)$ works.
So the row space is the plane $x - 2y + z = 0$.
The problem shows the typical use: the row space and the null space determine each other, since $n$ also solves $An = 0$.

### L3 human

The row space of $A$ is the image of the transpose map $F^m \to F^n$, $y \mapsto A^{\mathsf{T}} y$.
In the language of duality, it is the image of the [[dual map|0AZ5ARP287]] of $x \mapsto Ax$, identified with a subspace of $F^n$ through the standard basis.
Its annihilator is the null space of $A$, and this gives a proof by [[duality|0ZPB3180AY]] that row rank equals column rank.
Two matrices of the same size are row equivalent exactly when they have the same row space, so the row space is the complete invariant of row reduction.
