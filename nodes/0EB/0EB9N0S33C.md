## Summary

Let $F$ be a field and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ [[square matrix|0JXZKX526R]] over $F$.
The matrix $A$ is invertible when there is an $n \times n$ matrix $B$ over $F$ with $AB = BA = I_n$.
Here $I_n$ is the [[identity matrix|06DW9BAWB1]] and the products are [[matrix products|032Q9FB8MT]].
Such a matrix $B$ is unique, by the [[uniqueness of the inverse|~139b320d]], and we call it the inverse $A^{-1}$ of $A$.
A square matrix that is not invertible is singular.
The [[formal definition|0TWTS88TWN]] states this over each commutative ring.
Invertible matrices matter because they are the matrices whose linear maps can be undone: the system $Ax = b$ then has exactly one solution for each $b$.

## Motivation

A single equation $ax = b$ with $a \ne 0$ has the solution $x = a^{-1} b$, because the number $a^{-1}$ undoes the multiplication by $a$.
A system of $n$ linear equations in $n$ unknowns has the form $Ax = b$ with an $n \times n$ matrix $A$.
We ask for a matrix that undoes the multiplication by $A$ in the same way.
If $BA = I_n$, then $Ax = b$ gives $x = I_n x = BAx = Bb$.
If $AB = I_n$, then $x = Bb$ is a solution, since $A(Bb) = I_n b = b$.
An inverse matrix does both jobs at once.
Matrix multiplication is not commutative, so we must ask for both products $AB$ and $BA$ in the definition.

## Notation

- The inverse is written $A^{-1}$. Its powers are written $A^{-k} = (A^{-1})^k$ for $k \ge 1$.
- Some books say nonsingular or regular for invertible, and degenerate for singular.
- The set of invertible $n \times n$ matrices over $F$ is the [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$.
- We never write $\frac{B}{A}$ for a matrix $A$, because $BA^{-1}$ and $A^{-1}B$ are different in general.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The identity matrix $I_n$ is invertible, and $I_n^{-1} = I_n$, because $I_n I_n = I_n$.
- The [[quarter turn|~7341928a]] $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is invertible, and its inverse is the quarter turn in the other direction.
- A [[diagonal matrix with nonzero diagonal entries|~b527539e]] is invertible, and its inverse is diagonal with the reciprocal entries.
- The $2 \times 2$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ with $ad - bc \ne 0$ is invertible, by the [[formula for the inverse of a 2 × 2 matrix|8XWC7RKNN1]].
- Each [[elementary matrix|01REXW3WWE]] is invertible, and its inverse undoes its row operation.
- The [[worked inversion by row reduction|~7e5fa2f4]] finds the inverse of a $3 \times 3$ matrix with integer entries.

### Non-examples

- The zero matrix of size $n \ge 1$ is singular: $0 \cdot B = 0 \ne I_n$ for each $B$.
- The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ is [[singular|~bea105a4]]: it sends the nonzero vector $(2, -1)$ to $0$.
- A non-square matrix is never invertible, because the definition needs $AB$ and $BA$ to be identity matrices of the same size. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has a [[right inverse but no left inverse|~710ae60f]].
- A matrix with a zero row is singular: the same row of $AB$ is zero for each $B$, so $AB \ne I_n$.

### Basic properties

- The inverse is unique, by [[uniqueness of the inverse|~139b320d]].
- If $A$ and $B$ are invertible, then $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}$, by the [[inverse of a product|0S4WTRE9GG]].
- $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$, $(A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1} = (A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $(cA)^{-1} = c^{-1} A^{-1}$ for $c \ne 0$, by the [[rules for inverses|~b26cc133]].
- For square matrices, $AB = I_n$ already gives $BA = I_n$, by [[one-sided inverses of square matrices|0753Y2X5K6]].
- $A$ is invertible if and only if its reduced row echelon form is $I_n$, by [[invertibility and row reduction|1ZK55FS2BF]]. The [[invertible matrix theorem|~9b681771]] lists many more equivalent conditions.
- [[Row reduction of the block matrix $[A \mid I]$|0Z83DHFMYM]] computes $A^{-1}$ or shows that $A$ is singular.
- Over a field, $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det A \ne 0$, by [[the determinant criterion|FBSAPTP1PR]] of the sub-area on determinants.

### History

Cayley introduced the inverse of a square matrix in his memoir on the theory of matrices of 1858.
He gave the inverse by the adjugate matrix divided by the determinant.
Elimination for linear systems is much older than matrices: the Chinese text The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art already uses it.

### Common errors

- Students write $(AB)^{-1} = A^{-1} B^{-1}$. The order must be reversed: $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}$.
- Students write $(A + B)^{-1} = A^{-1} + B^{-1}$. This is false: for $A = B = I_1 = (1)$ the left side is $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and the right side is $2$.
- Students conclude from $AB = I$ that $BA = I$ for non-square matrices. This fails for the $2 \times 3$ example of the non-examples.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An invertible matrix is a matrix that has an "undo" matrix.
If the matrix turns an input into an output, the undo matrix turns the output back into the input.
For example, the matrix that doubles both coordinates of a point is undone by the matrix that halves both coordinates.
The matrix that squashes every point of the plane onto a line has no undo matrix.
Two different points can land on the same point of the line, so we cannot tell which one we started from.

### L2 human

Worked example: we test whether $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is the inverse of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 & -2 + 2 \\ 1 - 1 & -1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$.
2. $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 & 1 - 1 \\ -2 + 2 & -1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2$.
3. Both products are $I_2$, so $A$ is invertible and $A^{-1} = B$.
4. We use it to solve $2x + y = 3$, $x + y = 2$: the solution is $B(3, 2)^{\mathsf{T}} = (3 - 2, -3 + 4)^{\mathsf{T}} = (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$.

The typical use is to solve $Ax = b$ for many right-hand sides $b$, or to undo a change of coordinates.
For square matrices step 2 follows from step 1, by the theorem on one-sided inverses.
A trap: this shortcut needs a square matrix, and it fails for a $2 \times 3$ matrix with a right inverse.

### L3 human

An invertible matrix is a unit of the [[matrix ring|0FHVPRAE60]] $M_n(F)$, and the units of a ring form a group, here $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$.
In the language of linear maps, $A$ is invertible exactly when $x \mapsto Ax$ is a [[linear isomorphism|0B8T10CE7N]] of $F^n$.
The sub-area on [[linear maps|9080WQTZ3M]] develops this view.
Over a commutative ring $R$, the matrix $A$ is invertible exactly when $\det A$ is a unit of $R$, by [[the determinant criterion over rings|03Q7RE2HXR]].
So $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is invertible over $\mathbb{Q}$ but not over $\mathbb{Z}$.
For non-square or singular matrices, the [[Moore-Penrose inverse|0YY8VX28JR]] and the other [[generalized inverses|FPHNJ5AAYF]] replace the inverse; they belong to matrix theory.
In numerical work one rarely forms $A^{-1}$, and the [[condition number|AB3S6MHJ8X]] of numerical analysis measures how close $A$ is to a singular matrix.
