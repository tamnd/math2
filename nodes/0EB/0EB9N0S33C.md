## Summary

An invertible matrix is a square matrix whose action another matrix can undo.
It plays the part for matrices that a nonzero number plays for numbers: you can divide by it, and the system $Ax = b$ then has exactly one solution for each $b$.
Let $F$ be a field and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ [[square matrix|0JXZKX526R]] over $F$.
The matrix $A$ is invertible when there is an $n \times n$ matrix $B$ over $F$ with
$$AB = BA = I_n,$$
where $I_n$ is the [[identity matrix|06DW9BAWB1]] and the products are [[matrix products|032Q9FB8MT]].
Such a matrix $B$ is unique, by the [[uniqueness of the inverse|FMCX4D7RD4]], and we call it the inverse $A^{-1}$ of $A$.
A square matrix that is not invertible is singular.
The [[formal definition|0TWTS88TWN]] states the same condition over any commutative ring.

## Motivation

One equation $ax = b$ with $a \ne 0$ has the solution $x = a^{-1} b$, because multiplication by $a^{-1}$ undoes multiplication by $a$.
A system of $n$ linear equations in $n$ unknowns is one matrix equation $Ax = b$, so we look for a matrix that undoes $A$ in the same way.
If $BA = I_n$, then every solution satisfies $x = I_n x = B(Ax) = Bb$, so there is at most one solution.
If $AB = I_n$, then $x = Bb$ is a solution, since $A(Bb) = I_n b = b$.
An inverse does both jobs at once, and the definition asks for both products because matrix multiplication does not commute.

Inverses appear wherever a process must run backward.
A change of coordinates is given by a matrix, and its inverse changes the coordinates back.
In computer graphics, the matrix that places a camera in a scene is inverted to express every point of the scene in the coordinates of the camera.
Later in the sub-area, the invertible matrices form a group, the general linear group, and elimination writes each of them as a product of elementary matrices.

## Notation

We write $A^{-1}$ for the inverse and $A^{-k} = (A^{-1})^k$ for its powers, with $k \ge 1$.
Some books say nonsingular or regular for invertible, and degenerate for singular.
The set of invertible $n \times n$ matrices over $F$ is the [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$.
Do not write a fraction $\frac{B}{A}$ with a matrix $A$ below the line, because $BA^{-1}$ and $A^{-1}B$ are different in general.

## Remarks

### A first inverse

Take the matrix $A$ and the candidate $B$ below:
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Multiply in both orders, row by column:
$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 & -2 + 2 \\ 1 - 1 & -1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2, \qquad BA = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 & 1 - 1 \\ -2 + 2 & -1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = I_2.$$
So $A$ is invertible and $A^{-1} = B$.
The candidate came from the [[formula for the inverse of a 2 × 2 matrix|8XWC7RKNN1]], since here $ad - bc = 2 - 1 = 1$.

Now use the inverse to solve the system $2x + y = 3$, $x + y = 2$.
Multiply the right side by $A^{-1}$:
$$\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 - 2 \\ -3 + 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $2 \cdot 1 + 1 = 3$ and $1 + 1 = 2$.

### Matrices you can invert at sight

The identity is its own inverse, because $I_n I_n = I_n$.
A [[diagonal matrix with nonzero diagonal entries|GG5VAMGJ2B]] is undone by the diagonal matrix of the reciprocals, for example $\operatorname{diag}(2, 3)^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3})$.
The [[quarter turn|787TZBHS1T]] $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is undone by the quarter turn the other way, $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Each [[elementary matrix|01REXW3WWE]] is undone by the row operation that reverses its own.
Most invertible matrices are not of these simple kinds: [[the matrix with rows (0, 2) and (1, 0)|YYBT7MBTY6]] is invertible but not elementary, and a general $3 \times 3$ matrix needs [[row reduction|743HGXHH7Q]].

### What singular looks like

A matrix is singular as soon as it sends some nonzero vector to $0$.
Suppose $Ax = 0$ with $x \ne 0$, and suppose $BA = I_n$.
Then $x = (BA)x = B(Ax) = B0 = 0$, which is false.
For example, [[the matrix with rows (1, 2) and (2, 4)|YSF9M0E66C]] sends $(2, -1)$ to $(2 - 2, 4 - 4) = (0, 0)$, so it has no inverse.

The zero matrix is singular for the same reason, and so is every matrix with a zero row, since that row of $AB$ is zero for every $B$.
A non-square matrix is never called invertible, because $AB$ and $BA$ are identity matrices of different sizes.
Such a matrix can still have a one-sided inverse: the $2 \times 3$ matrix in [[the example of a right inverse|540E86V3N9]] has a right inverse and no left inverse.

### The rules you can use

Most rules below name a candidate inverse, and a product confirms it.
The inverse is unique: if $CA = I_n$ and $AB = I_n$, then $C = C(AB) = (CA)B = B$.
Undoing twice gives back the start, $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$, because the equations $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I_n$ read the same with the roles of $A$ and $A^{-1}$ exchanged.
The inverse of a product reverses the order, by the [[inverse of a product|0S4WTRE9GG]]:
$$(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I_n.$$
The transpose of $AA^{-1} = I_n$ is $(A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}} = I_n$, so $(A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1} = (A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}}$.
For a scalar $c \ne 0$, $(cA)^{-1} = c^{-1} A^{-1}$.
The [[rules for inverses|2ZH4X4Q99D]] prove these last facts in full.

### How to decide invertibility

For square matrices one product is enough: $AB = I_n$ already gives $BA = I_n$, by [[one-sided inverses of square matrices|0753Y2X5K6]].
The working test is elimination.
A matrix is invertible exactly when its reduced row echelon form is $I_n$, by [[invertibility and row reduction|1ZK55FS2BF]], and [[row reduction of the block matrix $[A \mid I]$|0Z83DHFMYM]] then produces $A^{-1}$ as a by-product.
Over a field, $A$ is invertible exactly when $\det A \ne 0$, by [[the determinant criterion|FBSAPTP1PR]].
The [[invertible matrix theorem|ACDFDABVQK]] collects these tests and many more into one list of equivalent conditions.

### Watch out

The inverse of a product is not the product of the inverses in the same order.
Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so that $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the matrix of the first computation.
Then
$$A^{-1}B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
which is not $(AB)^{-1}$, while $B^{-1}A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ is.

The inverse of a sum is not the sum of the inverses either.
For the $1 \times 1$ matrices $A = B = (1)$, the left side $(A + B)^{-1}$ is $(\tfrac{1}{2})$ and the right side $A^{-1} + B^{-1}$ is $(2)$.
A sum of invertible matrices can even be singular, as $I_n + (-I_n) = 0$ shows.

### History

Cayley introduced the inverse of a square matrix in his memoir on the theory of matrices of 1858, and he built it from the adjugate matrix and the determinant.
Elimination for linear systems is far older than matrices: the Chinese text The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art already uses it.

### Try it

First, is $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ invertible?
No: it has a zero row, and it sends $(0, 1)$ to $(0, 0)$.

Second, suppose $A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and find $(A^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}$.
It is $(A^{-1})^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

Third, if $A^3 = I_n$, what is $A^{-1}$?
It is $A^2$, because $A \cdot A^2 = A^2 \cdot A = A^3 = I_n$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An invertible matrix is a matrix that has an "undo" matrix.
If the matrix turns an input into an output, the undo matrix turns the output back into the input.
For example, the matrix that doubles both coordinates of a point is undone by the matrix that halves both coordinates, so $(3, 5)$ goes to $(6, 10)$ and back to $(3, 5)$.
The matrix that squashes every point of the plane onto a line has no undo matrix.
Two different points can land on the same point of the line, so we cannot tell which one we started from.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: solve two systems that share the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, with right sides $b_1 = (4, 7)$ and $b_2 = (1, 1)$.
Since $ad - bc = 6 - 5 = 1$, the 2 × 2 formula gives
$$A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Each solution is now one matrix-vector product:
$$A^{-1} b_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 8 - 7 \\ -20 + 21 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^{-1} b_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 \\ -5 + 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check the second one: $3 - 2 = 1$ and $5 - 4 = 1$.
The problem shows when the inverse pays off: it costs some work once, and then every new right side is cheap.
For a single system, elimination is cheaper than forming $A^{-1}$, as [[solving a system with the inverse|3RQX3T70NX]] explains.

### L3 human

An invertible matrix is a unit of the [[matrix ring|0FHVPRAE60]] $M_n(F)$, and the units of any ring form a group, here $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$.
In the language of linear maps, $A$ is invertible exactly when $x \mapsto Ax$ is a [[linear isomorphism|0B8T10CE7N]] of $F^n$, and the sub-area on [[linear maps|9080WQTZ3M]] develops that view.
Over a commutative ring $R$, the matrix $A$ is invertible exactly when $\det A$ is a unit of $R$, by [[the determinant criterion over rings|03Q7RE2HXR]].
So $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is invertible over $\mathbb{Q}$ but not over $\mathbb{Z}$.

For non-square or singular matrices, the [[Moore-Penrose inverse|0YY8VX28JR]] and the other [[generalized inverses|FPHNJ5AAYF]] take the place of the inverse.
In numerical work one rarely forms $A^{-1}$, and the [[condition number|AB3S6MHJ8X]] measures how close $A$ is to a singular matrix.
