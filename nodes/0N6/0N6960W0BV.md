## Summary

An eigenvector of a linear map is a nonzero vector that the map does not turn: it only stretches it, shrinks it or flips it.
So it marks a line through the origin that the map sends into itself, and on that line the map is multiplication by one number.
Let $T \colon V \to V$ be an [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]] of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$.
An *eigenvector* of $T$ is a vector $v \in V$ with
$$v \ne 0 \quad \text{and} \quad T(v) = \lambda v \quad \text{for some } \lambda \in F.$$
The scalar $\lambda$ is then an [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $T$, and it is determined by $v$.
The eigenvectors for $\lambda$ are the nonzero vectors of the [[eigenspace|0G0RFPG8HR]] $\ker(T - \lambda \id_V)$, and the [[formal definition|0B5H4208Z9]] states all this for matrices too.

## Motivation

A linear map of the plane can shear, turn and stretch at the same time, and its matrix hides what it does.
Eigenvectors find the directions in which the map is simple.
If $V$ has a basis of eigenvectors, then the matrix of $T$ in that basis is diagonal, and $T$ splits into independent one-dimensional pieces.

This idea runs through applied mathematics.
A system of linear differential equations decouples along eigenvectors, the principal axes of a rotating body are eigenvectors of its inertia matrix, and the steady state of a Markov chain is an eigenvector for the eigenvalue $1$.
The sub-area on [[diagonalization|Z6S6PWMJPH]] asks when a basis of eigenvectors exists, and the [[Jordan form|GEBYZKYV3D]] describes what happens when it does not.

## Notation

There is no special symbol for an eigenvector; books write $v$ or $x$ and say "for the eigenvalue $\lambda$".
A pair $(\lambda, v)$ with $v \ne 0$ and $T(v) = \lambda v$ is called an *eigenpair*.
For a matrix $A$, an eigenvector is a column with $Av = \lambda v$, sometimes called a right eigenvector.
A row $y$ with $yA = \lambda y$ is a [[left eigenvector|32M8VY9A1M]], and it is a different object.
In Mathlib the statement is `Module.End.HasEigenvector T λ v`.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, which has the eigenvalue $3$.
To find the eigenvectors for $3$, solve $(A - 3I)v = 0$:
$$\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Both equations say $v_1 = v_2$, so the eigenvectors are $c(1, 1)$ with $c \ne 0$.
Check with $c = 1$: $A(1, 1) = (3, 3) = 3(1, 1)$.
In the same way the eigenvectors for the eigenvalue $1$ are $c(1, -1)$, as [[the symmetric matrix with rows (2, 1) and (1, 2)|Z6K3QPP7DY]] shows.

### Invariant lines

An eigenvector $v$ spans the line $\{cv : c \in F\}$, and $T(cv) = c\lambda v$ lies on the same line.
So the line is an [[invariant subspace|0ACX5QT8TE]] of dimension $1$, and $T$ acts on it as multiplication by $\lambda$.
The converse holds too.
If a line spanned by $v \ne 0$ is invariant, then $T(v)$ lies on it, so $T(v) = \lambda v$ for some $\lambda$, and $v$ is an eigenvector.
So "eigenvector" and "invariant line" are two names for one idea, the first algebraic and the second geometric.

### Four maps of the plane

The geometry often shows the eigenvectors before any computation, as [[projections, reflections and shears of the plane|HJ117SV1AN]] work out in detail.
The reflection in the line $y = x$, with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, fixes $(1, 1)$ and sends $(1, -1)$ to $(-1, 1)$, so these are eigenvectors for $1$ and $-1$.
The projection $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ fixes $(1, 0)$ and kills $(1, -1)$, so these are eigenvectors for $1$ and $0$.
The shear $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ fixes the horizontal axis and moves every other line, so its only eigenvectors are the multiples of $(1, 0)$.
A [[rotation by an angle that is not a multiple of pi|5AE98D7959]] moves every line, so it has no real eigenvector at all.

### The rules you can use

Each nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is an eigenvector for the same eigenvalue, because $T(cv) = c\lambda v = \lambda(cv)$.
The eigenvalue of an eigenvector is unique: if $\lambda v = \mu v$ with $v \ne 0$, then $(\lambda - \mu)v = 0$ forces $\lambda = \mu$.
[[Eigenvectors with distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent|0HNMFG8F8K]], so a map on an $n$-dimensional space has at most $n$ distinct eigenvalues.
If $T(v) = \lambda v$, then $v$ is also an eigenvector of $p(T)$ for $p(\lambda)$, for every polynomial $p$, by the theorem on [[eigenvalues of a polynomial in an endomorphism|F2S8WGVARA]].

### What it is not

The zero vector is never an eigenvector, because $T(0) = \lambda \cdot 0$ holds for every $\lambda$ and would give no information.
The sum of two eigenvectors for different eigenvalues is not an eigenvector.
For $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$, the vectors $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ are eigenvectors, but their sum $(1, 1)$ goes to $(1, 2)$, which is not a multiple of $(1, 1)$.
A right eigenvector of a matrix need not be a left eigenvector, as [[a right eigenvector that is not a left eigenvector|RQ2FE7RVVZ]] shows.

### Watch out

Similar matrices have the same eigenvalues but not the same eigenvectors.
If $Av = \lambda v$ and $B = S^{-1} A S$, then $B(S^{-1} v) = S^{-1} A v = \lambda S^{-1} v$, so the eigenvector of $B$ is $S^{-1} v$.
For example, let $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ and $S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so that $B = S^{-1} A S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
The vector $(0, 1)$ is an eigenvector of $A$ for $2$, but $B(0, 1) = (-1, 2)$ is not a multiple of $(0, 1)$.
The correct vector is $S^{-1}(0, 1) = (-1, 1)$, and indeed $B(-1, 1) = (-2, 2) = 2(-1, 1)$.

### History

The prefix "eigen" is the German word for "own" or "proper", so an eigenvector is a vector that is proper to the map.
Hilbert used "eigen" in this sense in 1904, in his work on integral operators.
English books also use the older term "characteristic vector".

### Try it

First, which of $(1, -1)$ and $(1, -2)$ is an eigenvector of $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$?
The first goes to $(2, 0)$, which is not a multiple of $(1, -1)$, and the second goes to $(3 - 2, 2 - 4) = (1, -2)$, so $(1, -2)$ is an eigenvector for $1$.

Second, find all eigenvectors of the shear $H = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Its only eigenvalue is $1$, and $(H - I)(x, y) = (2y, 0)$ is zero exactly when $y = 0$, so the eigenvectors are $(x, 0)$ with $x \ne 0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear map can turn, stretch and flip the arrows of the plane.
An eigenvector is an arrow that the map does not turn off its own line.
The map may stretch it, shrink it or flip it, and the factor is the eigenvalue.
For a mirror in a line, the arrows along the mirror stay as they are, so they are eigenvectors with factor $1$.
The arrows at a right angle to the mirror get flipped, so they are eigenvectors with factor $-1$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: compute $A^k (1, 0)$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
Write the vector in terms of the eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ for $3$ and $(1, -1)$ for $1$:
$$(1, 0) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1, 1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(1, -1).$$
Each power of $A$ multiplies the first part by $3$ and the second part by $1$, so
$$A^k (1, 0) = \tfrac{3^k}{2}(1, 1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(1, -1) = \left( \tfrac{3^k + 1}{2}, \tfrac{3^k - 1}{2} \right).$$
Check with $k = 2$: $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, whose first column is $(5, 4) = (\tfrac{10}{2}, \tfrac{8}{2})$.
In eigenvector coordinates, powers become powers of numbers, and this is why a basis of eigenvectors is so useful.
Watch out: an eigenvector is only fixed up to a nonzero factor, so two correct answers such as $(1, 1)$ and $(-2, -2)$ can look different.

### L3 human

An eigenvector generates a one-dimensional invariant subspace.
In the language of modules, $V$ is a module over $F[x]$ with $x$ acting as $T$, and an eigenvector for $\lambda$ generates a submodule isomorphic to $F[x]/(x - \lambda)$.
A [[generalized eigenvector|01H6QKWGDH]] is a vector killed by some power of $T - \lambda \id_V$, and the eigenvectors are the case of the first power; these vectors fill the gap when a matrix has too few eigenvectors.
For differential operators the eigenvectors are called eigenfunctions, and in quantum mechanics the states with a definite energy are the eigenvectors of the energy operator.
