## Summary

Let $T \colon V \to V$ be an [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]] of a vector space $V$ over a field $F$.
An eigenvector of $T$ is a vector $v \ne 0$ with $T(v) = \lambda v$ for some scalar $\lambda \in F$.
The scalar $\lambda$ is then an [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $T$, and it is unique for $v$.
The eigenvectors for $\lambda$ are the nonzero vectors of the [[eigenspace|0G0RFPG8HR]] $\ker(T - \lambda \id_V)$.

## Motivation

An eigenvector spans a line that the map sends into itself.
On that line, the map is the multiplication by a scalar.
A basis of eigenvectors makes the matrix of $T$ diagonal, so that $T$ splits into independent one-dimensional pieces.
This is the idea behind the solution of linear systems of differential equations and of linear recurrences.

## Notation

The formal definition is the [[definition of an eigenvector|0B5H4208Z9]].
A pair $(\lambda, v)$ with $v \ne 0$ and $T(v) = \lambda v$ is an eigenpair.
For matrices, eigenvectors are columns with $Av = \lambda v$, also called right eigenvectors.
Rows $y$ with $yA = \lambda y$ are [[left eigenvectors|~d0c39701]].

## Remarks

### Examples

- [[The symmetric matrix with rows (2, 1) and (1, 2)|~300f406e]] has the eigenvectors $(1, -1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $1$ and $(1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ for $3$.
- [[Projections, reflections and shears of the plane|~ceb3d262]] have eigenvectors that can be found from the geometry.
- [[The left shift on sequences|~56ecd655]] has the geometric sequences as eigenvectors.
- [[The derivative on smooth functions|~358c3d83]] has the exponentials $e^{\lambda x}$ as eigenvectors.
- [[A real matrix with complex eigenvalues|~8162274d]] has eigenvectors in $\C^2$ but none in $\R^2$.

### Non-examples

- [[A rotation of the plane by an angle that is not a multiple of pi|~805e3c17]] has no eigenvector in $\R^2$.
- The zero vector is never an eigenvector, by convention, because $T(0) = \lambda \cdot 0$ for every $\lambda$.
- [[A right eigenvector need not be a left eigenvector|~1a0caa3d]].

### Basic properties

- Each nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is an eigenvector for the same eigenvalue.
- The eigenvalue of an eigenvector $v$ is unique: if $\lambda v = \mu v$ and $v \ne 0$, then $\lambda = \mu$.
- [[Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent|0HNMFG8F8K]].
- If $v$ is an eigenvector of $T$ for $\lambda$, then $v$ is an eigenvector of $p(T)$ for $p(\lambda)$, for each polynomial $p$, by the theorem on [[eigenvalues of a polynomial in an endomorphism|~ceb4bf0e]].
- If $S$ is invertible and $Av = \lambda v$, then $S^{-1}AS$ has the eigenvector $S^{-1}v$ for $\lambda$.
- An eigenvector spans a one-dimensional [[invariant subspace|0ACX5QT8TE]], and each one-dimensional invariant subspace is spanned by an eigenvector.

### History

The prefix "eigen" comes from the German word for "own" or "proper".
Hilbert used "eigen" in this sense in his work on integral operators, in 1904.
Another English term is "characteristic vector".

### Common errors

- Calling the zero vector an eigenvector.
- Taking an eigenvector of $A$ to be an eigenvector of each matrix similar to $A$. The vector changes to $S^{-1}v$.
- Thinking that the sum of two eigenvectors is an eigenvector. This holds only when they have the same eigenvalue and the sum is nonzero.
- Normalising an eigenvector and then thinking it is the only one. Each nonzero multiple is also an eigenvector.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear map can turn, stretch and flip vectors.
An eigenvector is a nonzero vector that the map does not turn off its own line.
The map can only stretch it, shrink it or flip it.
For a mirror in a line, the vectors on the mirror line stay fixed.
The vectors at a right angle to the mirror get flipped.
Both kinds are eigenvectors.

### L2 human

We find all eigenvectors of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ for the eigenvalue $3$.

1. Solve $(A - 3I)v = 0$. The matrix $A - 3I$ has the rows $(-1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$.
2. Both equations say $v_1 = v_2$.
3. So the eigenvectors are $c (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$ with $c \ne 0$.
4. Check with $c = 1$: $A (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} = (3, 3)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $3 (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} = (3, 3)^{\mathsf{T}}$.

The typical use is a change of basis to eigenvectors, which makes the matrix diagonal.
A trap: an eigenvector is a direction, not one vector, so two correct answers can differ by a scalar factor.

### L3 human

An eigenvector is a generator of a one-dimensional [[invariant subspace|0ACX5QT8TE]].
In the language of modules, $V$ is a module over $F[x]$ with $x$ acting as $T$, and an eigenvector generates a submodule isomorphic to $F[x]/(x - \lambda)$.
A [[generalised eigenvector|01H6QKWGDH]] is killed by a power of $T - \lambda$, and the eigenvectors are the case of the first power.
In functional analysis, eigenvectors of differential operators are called eigenfunctions.
