## Statement

A linear map of a finite-dimensional space is a root of its own characteristic polynomial, with no basis in sight.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be a linear map with [[characteristic polynomial|0J5EMJRRFM]] $p_T \in F[x]$.
Then $p_T(T) = 0$ in $\operatorname{End}(V)$, where $p_T(T)$ is the [[value of the polynomial at T|0E3MV6E6MG]].

## Remarks

### How it follows from the matrix form

Choose a basis $B$ of $V$ and let $M = [T]_B$.
Passing from a map to its matrix keeps sums, scalar multiples and composition, so it sends $p(T)$ to $p(M)$ for every polynomial $p$.
The characteristic polynomial of $T$ is by definition $p_M$, and the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem for matrices|0F765KFC2J]] gives $p_M(M) = 0$.
A map whose matrix is zero is the zero map, so $p_T(T) = 0$.
The [[proof for linear maps|RSNG3V23MA]] writes out each step.

### A map without a matrix in view

Let $V$ be the space of real polynomials of degree at most $2$ and let $D$ be differentiation.
In the basis $1, x, x^2$ the matrix of $D$ is strictly upper triangular, so $p_D(x) = x^3$.
The theorem says $D^3 = 0$, and indeed three derivatives kill every polynomial of degree at most $2$: for $f = a + bx + cx^2$ we get $f' = b + 2cx$, $f'' = 2c$ and $f''' = 0$.
Here the statement about the map is easier to see than the statement about its matrix.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The space must be finite-dimensional, since otherwise $T$ has no characteristic polynomial.
For example, differentiation $D$ on all real polynomials is killed by no nonzero polynomial at all.
If $p$ has degree $d$ and leading coefficient $c_d \ne 0$, then $p(D) x^d$ has constant term $c_d \, d! \ne 0$, so $p(D) \ne 0$.
The field can be replaced by a commutative ring when $V$ is free of finite rank, with the same proof.

### Generalizations

For a finitely generated module over a commutative ring that is not free, there is no characteristic polynomial in the usual sense.
Still, the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem for modules|SH3R5VTMZH]] gives a monic polynomial that kills each endomorphism.

## History

The statement for linear maps is the basis-free form of the matrix theorem stated by Cayley in 1858 and proved in general by Frobenius in 1878; see the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem|0F765KFC2J]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

A linear map can be described by many matrices, one for each choice of axes.
All of these matrices have the same characteristic polynomial, and each of them is a root of it.
So the map itself is a root, whichever axes you choose.
For differentiation on polynomials of degree at most $2$, the polynomial is $x^3$, and three derivatives send every such polynomial to $0$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: express $T^3$ through lower powers for the map $T(a, b) = (a + b, 2b)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
In the standard basis the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, so $p_T(x) = (x - 1)(x - 2) = x^2 - 3x + 2$.
The theorem gives $T^2 = 3T - 2\,\mathrm{id}$, and then
$$T^3 = 3T^2 - 2T = 3(3T - 2\,\mathrm{id}) - 2T = 7T - 6\,\mathrm{id}.$$
Check on $(0, 1)$: $T(0, 1) = (1, 2)$, $T^2(0, 1) = (3, 4)$ and $T^3(0, 1) = (7, 8)$, while $7(1, 2) - 6(0, 1) = (7, 8)$.
Watch out: the identity in $\operatorname{End}(V)$ is the map $\mathrm{id}$, so the constant term $2$ of $p_T$ stands for $2\,\mathrm{id}$, not for the number $2$.

### L3 human

Viewing $V$ as a module over $F[x]$, with $x$ acting as $T$, the theorem says that $p_T$ lies in the annihilator of the module.
Since $F[x]$ is a principal ideal domain, that annihilator is generated by the [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]], and the structure theorem identifies $p_T$ as the product of all invariant factors.
The sub-area on canonical forms develops this view.
