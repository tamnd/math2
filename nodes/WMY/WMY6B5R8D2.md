## Definition

The *cosmic distance ladder* is the ordered sequence of methods of measuring astronomical distances.
Each method, or rung, applies over a range of distances.
Each rung is calibrated by distances obtained from rungs of smaller range.
The main rungs, from near to far, are:

1. radar ranging and celestial mechanics, which give the astronomical unit;
2. trigonometric [[stellar parallax|93CFD71XZ5]], with the astronomical unit as base;
3. standard candles, objects of known absolute magnitude, which give a [[distance modulus|E8W7YP4BMD]];
4. the Hubble law, which relates the redshift of distant galaxies to their distance.

## Remarks

Standard candles include Cepheid variable stars, whose period determines their luminosity, and type Ia supernovae.
Example: the period–luminosity relation of Cepheids is calibrated with Cepheids of known parallax in the Milky Way.
Then Cepheids in a nearby galaxy give its distance, which calibrates the type Ia supernovae seen in that galaxy.
Non-example: a single method used alone, such as radar ranging to Venus, is one rung, not the ladder.
Errors in a low rung propagate to all higher rungs, so the calibration of the lowest rungs matters most.
