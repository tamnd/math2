## Example

The infinite cyclic group is a [[cyclic group|0KVGH0J5B3]] with infinitely many elements.
Its standard model is the [[additive group of integers|0BSCJA5G53]] $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, with generator $1$.
We also write it $C_\infty$, or $\langle t \rangle = \{t^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ in multiplicative notation.
It has the following three properties.

1. Each infinite cyclic group is [[isomorphic|08MG7H8JW5]] to $\mathbb{Z}$.
2. It has exactly two generators.
3. Each nontrivial subgroup is again infinite cyclic, and has finite [[index|0DTXBQPA2S]].

Proof of property 1.

1. Let $G = \langle g \rangle$ be infinite. Then $g$ has infinite order: if $g$ had finite order $n$, then $|G| = n$ by [[the order of $\langle g \rangle$|031M8HX7DA]].
2. Define $\psi \colon \mathbb{Z} \to G$ by $\psi(k) = g^k$. It is a homomorphism, because $g^{j+k} = g^j g^k$ by the [[laws of exponents|00DRK90Z6K]].
3. It is surjective, because $G = \{g^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$.
4. It is injective, because $g$ has infinite order, so $g^j \neq g^k$ for $j \neq k$, by the same theorem.
5. So $\psi$ is an isomorphism, and $G \cong \mathbb{Z}$.

Proof of property 2.

6. By property 1, it is enough to find the generators of $\mathbb{Z}$, and then $g^k$ generates $G$ exactly when $k$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$.
7. If $k$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$, then $1 \in \langle k \rangle = k\mathbb{Z}$. So $1 = km$ for an integer $m$, and $k = \pm 1$.
8. Conversely, $\langle 1 \rangle = \langle -1 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}$. So the generators of $\mathbb{Z}$ are $1$ and $-1$, and those of $G$ are $g$ and $g^{-1}$.
9. These two generators are distinct: $g = g^{-1}$ would give $g^2 = e$, but $g$ has infinite order.

Proof of property 3.

10. By property 1, it is enough to treat $\mathbb{Z}$. By [[the classification of subgroups of the integers|0SRATCFF1W]], a nontrivial subgroup is $m\mathbb{Z}$ for an integer $m \geq 1$.
11. The map $k \mapsto mk$ is an isomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to m\mathbb{Z}$: it is a homomorphism, it is surjective by the definition of $m\mathbb{Z}$, and $mk = 0$ only for $k = 0$. So $m\mathbb{Z}$ is infinite cyclic, with generator $m$.
12. By the [[division algorithm|0CHQKZFK5S]], each integer is $qm + r$ with $0 \leq r \leq m - 1$, so it lies in one of the cosets $r + m\mathbb{Z}$.
13. These $m$ cosets are distinct. Indeed, $r + m\mathbb{Z} = r' + m\mathbb{Z}$ only if $m$ divides $r - r'$, and for two different numbers $r, r'$ in $0, \ldots, m-1$ we have $0 < |r - r'| < m$. So the index of $m\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ is $m$, which is finite. $\blacksquare$

## Remarks

1. Other models: the even integers [[$2\mathbb{Z}$|8WJPSS7TE9]] under addition, the [[powers of $2$ in $\mathbb{Q}^\times$|~5a3994de]], and the translations of the plane by the integer multiples of a fixed nonzero vector.
2. Each element other than the identity has infinite order. So the group has no nontrivial finite subgroups.
3. By property 3, the infinite cyclic group is isomorphic to each of its nontrivial subgroups, and these subgroups are proper when $m \geq 2$. So $\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to a proper subgroup of itself. A finite group never is, because a proper subgroup of a finite group has fewer elements.
4. Each cyclic group is a quotient of $\mathbb{Z}$: if $G = \langle g \rangle$, then $k \mapsto g^k$ is a surjective homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$ onto $G$.
5. A group can contain an element of infinite order without being cyclic. The group [[$\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$|~fac25186]] is an example.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The infinite cyclic group is the integers under addition.
Start at $0$ and step by $1$ forward or backward.
You reach every integer, and you never come back to a number you visited.
Every group that one element produces, without repeats, looks exactly like this.

### L2 human

We find the generators and the index of a subgroup in a concrete case.

1. In $\mathbb{Z}$, the element $3$ is not a generator: $\langle 3 \rangle = 3\mathbb{Z}$ does not contain $1$.
2. The subgroup $3\mathbb{Z}$ has the $3$ cosets $3\mathbb{Z}$, $1 + 3\mathbb{Z}$ and $2 + 3\mathbb{Z}$. So its index is $3$.
3. The map $k \mapsto 3k$ is an isomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to 3\mathbb{Z}$. So $3\mathbb{Z}$ is again infinite cyclic, with the generators $3$ and $-3$.

The typical use: to prove a fact about all infinite cyclic groups, prove it for $\mathbb{Z}$ and move it through the isomorphism of property 1.
A trap: "infinite cyclic" does not mean "infinite and abelian".
The group $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$ is infinite and abelian, but it is [[not cyclic|~b8d64d52]].

### L3 human

The infinite cyclic group is the [[free group|0KTW26C80J]] on one generator, and also the [[free abelian group|0WJX8MGG37]] of rank $1$.
So for each group $H$ and each $h \in H$ there is exactly one homomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to H$ with $1 \mapsto h$.
In topology it is the [[fundamental group|S1XK3HXHFX]] of the circle, where the generator is the loop that goes once around.
Its automorphism group is $\{\pm 1\}$, of order $2$, since an automorphism must send the generator $1$ to a generator.
