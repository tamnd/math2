## Summary

To negate a mathematical statement is to write a statement that is true exactly when the original is false.
The bare form "it is not the case that ..." is correct but hard to use, so in practice we push the word "not" inward until it stands next to the simplest parts.
Five rules do this.
The negation of "$P$ and $Q$" is "not $P$ or not $Q$", and the negation of "$P$ or $Q$" is "not $P$ and not $Q$".
The negation of "if $P$, then $Q$" is "$P$ and not $Q$".
The negation of "for every $x$, $P(x)$" is "there is $x$ such that not $P(x)$", and the negation of "there is $x$ such that $P(x)$" is "for every $x$, not $P(x)$".
A bounded quantifier keeps its domain: the negation of "for every $\varepsilon > 0$, $P(\varepsilon)$" is "there is $\varepsilon > 0$ such that not $P(\varepsilon)$".
Negation is the main tool for disproving a claim and for setting up [[proof by contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]] and [[proof by contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]].

## Motivation

A student who wants to show that a function is not continuous, or that a sequence does not converge, must know what "not continuous" or "not convergent" means.
The definitions of these words state the positive property only.
A definition such as "for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that for every $x$, ..." has three quantifiers and a conditional.
Its negation is hard to read off, and a wrong negation leads to a wrong proof.
The five rules turn the negation into a statement with the same shape, which says what a counterexample must provide.
The formal laws behind the rules are in logic, on the pages on [[negation|029JBBF693]] and [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]].

## Notation

The negation of $P$ is written $\neg P$, and older books write $\sim P$ or $\overline{P}$.
In words, it is "not $P$" or "it is not the case that $P$".
For atomic statements, mathematics uses negated symbols: $a \neq b$ for $\neg(a = b)$, $x \notin A$ for $\neg(x \in A)$ and $a \nmid b$ for $\neg(a \mid b)$.
The negation of $a < b$ is $a \geq b$ for real numbers, and the negation of $a \leq b$ is $a > b$.
In symbols, the five rules read:
$$\neg(P \land Q) \equiv \neg P \lor \neg Q, \qquad \neg(P \lor Q) \equiv \neg P \land \neg Q, \qquad \neg(P \Rightarrow Q) \equiv P \land \neg Q,$$
$$\neg \forall x \, P(x) \equiv \exists x \, \neg P(x), \qquad \neg \exists x \, P(x) \equiv \forall x \, \neg P(x).$$

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[The negation of continuity at a point, for the Heaviside step function|Y7428Y6TSB]]: there is $\varepsilon > 0$ such that every $\delta > 0$ fails.
2. [[The negation of "every prime is odd"|1FP20JXFMV]]: there is a prime that is not odd, and $2$ is such a prime.
3. The negation of "$0 < x < 1$", that is, of "$0 < x$ and $x < 1$", is "$x \leq 0$ or $x \geq 1$".
4. The negation of "$n$ is even or $n$ is a multiple of $3$" is "$n$ is odd and $n$ is not a multiple of $3$". For $n = 5$, the negation is true, so the original is false.
5. A sequence $(a_n)$ converges to $L$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $N$ such that $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$ for every $n \geq N$. It does not converge to $L$ if there is $\varepsilon > 0$ such that for every $N$ there is $n \geq N$ with $|a_n - L| \geq \varepsilon$.

### Non-examples

1. [[The statement "if $P$, then not $Q$" is not the negation of "if $P$, then $Q$"|H71VVPDD9K]]: both can be true at once, when $P$ is false.
2. "Every prime is even" is not the negation of "every prime is odd". Both statements are false: $3$ is a prime that is not even, and $2$ is a prime that is not odd.
3. "$x < 0$" is not the negation of "$x > 0$". For $x = 0$, both are false, and the correct negation is "$x \leq 0$".

### Basic properties

1. A statement and its negation have opposite truth values. So exactly one of them is true, as the [[law of excluded middle|0RYYK8PG4S]] says.
2. The negation of the negation of $P$ is [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]] to $P$.
3. Negation swaps "and" with "or", and "for every" with "there is". It keeps the order of the quantifiers and the domains of bounded quantifiers.
4. Negation does not swap the hypothesis and the conclusion of a conditional. The negation of a conditional is a conjunction, not a conditional.
5. To negate a statement with several parts, we apply the rules from the outside in. Each step removes one "not" from the front of a part and moves it one level down.

### History

Aristotle's square of opposition pairs "every $A$ is $B$" with "some $A$ is not $B$" as contradictories, that is, as negations of each other.
The laws that swap "and" and "or" are named after Augustus De Morgan, a British logician of the 19th century.
The symbol $\neg$ is used in most modern texts, and older texts use the tilde $\sim$.

### Common errors

1. A student negates "for every $x$, $P(x)$" as "for every $x$, not $P(x)$". The correct negation needs one $x$ only: "there is $x$ such that not $P(x)$".
2. A student negates a conditional as a conditional, for example as "if $P$, then not $Q$" or as "if not $P$, then not $Q$". The correct negation is "$P$ and not $Q$".
3. A student negates the domain of a bounded quantifier. The negation of "for every $\varepsilon > 0$, $P(\varepsilon)$" is not "there is $\varepsilon \leq 0$ with not $P(\varepsilon)$".
4. A student negates "$a < b$" as "$a > b$". The case $a = b$ is lost, and the correct negation is "$a \geq b$".
5. A student keeps "and" in the negation of "$P$ and $Q$". The negation of "$x > 0$ and $y > 0$" is "$x \leq 0$ or $y \leq 0$".

## Explanations

### L1 human

The statement "all the doors are locked" is false when at least one door is open.
So its negation is "some door is not locked".
It is not "all the doors are unlocked", which says much more.
In numbers: "every number in the list $2, 4, 7$ is even" is false.
The negation "some number in the list is not even" is true, and the number $7$ shows it.

### L2 human

We prove that the sequence $a_n = (-1)^n$ does not converge to $L = 1$.

1. Write the definition: $a_n \to 1$ means $\forall \varepsilon > 0 \; \exists N \; \forall n \geq N \; |a_n - 1| < \varepsilon$.
2. Negate the first quantifier: $\exists \varepsilon > 0 \; \neg(\exists N \; \forall n \geq N \; |a_n - 1| < \varepsilon)$.
3. Negate the second: $\exists \varepsilon > 0 \; \forall N \; \neg(\forall n \geq N \; |a_n - 1| < \varepsilon)$.
4. Negate the third and the inequality: $\exists \varepsilon > 0 \; \forall N \; \exists n \geq N \; |a_n - 1| \geq \varepsilon$.
5. Take $\varepsilon = 1$. Let $N$ be arbitrary, and let $n$ be an odd number with $n \geq N$.
6. Then $a_n = -1$, and $|a_n - 1| = 2 \geq 1$. So the negation holds, and $(-1)^n$ does not converge to $1$.

The typical use of the negation is to see what a disproof must provide: here, one $\varepsilon$, and an $n$ for each $N$.

A trap: in step 5, $\varepsilon$ is chosen first and must not depend on $N$.
The negation has "there is $\varepsilon$" before "for every $N$".

### L3 human

The five rules hold in classical logic, and they put every formula into negation normal form, where "not" stands only before atomic formulas.
Mathematicians name many negated properties with positive words, such as "unbounded", "divergent" and "discontinuous", and they work with the negation normal form of the definition.
In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], the rule $\neg \forall x \, P(x) \Rightarrow \exists x \, \neg P(x)$ fails, and so does $\neg(P \land Q) \Rightarrow \neg P \lor \neg Q$.
A constructive proof that a function is not continuous may therefore not give the $\varepsilon$ that the classical negation asks for.
The rule for the conditional also depends on classical logic, since $\neg(P \Rightarrow Q) \Rightarrow P$ is not intuitionistically valid.

The proof methods that rest on negation, proof by contradiction and proof by contrapositive, are in the topic [[proof methods|G9JFJGY4TQ]].
The proofs of the quantifier rules are on the pages [[proof of the negation of a universal statement|DTM9GQV88K]] and [[proof of the negation of an existential statement|GANHAKFNTA]].
