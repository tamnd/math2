## Summary

To show that something fails, you must know exactly what failing means, and that is what negation gives you.
Take "$f$ is not injective".
A function $f$ is injective when, for all $x$ and $y$, $f(x) = f(y)$ implies $x = y$.
So "$f$ is not injective" means that there are $x$ and $y$ with $f(x) = f(y)$ and $x \ne y$, and for $f(x) = x^2$ the pair $1$ and $-1$ shows it.

To *negate* a mathematical statement is to write a statement that is true exactly when the original is false.
The bare form "it is not the case that ..." is correct but hard to use, so in practice we push the word "not" inward until it stands next to the simplest parts.
Five rules do this:

1. "not ($P$ and $Q$)" is "not $P$ or not $Q$";
2. "not ($P$ or $Q$)" is "not $P$ and not $Q$";
3. "not (if $P$, then $Q$)" is "$P$ and not $Q$";
4. "not (for every $x$, $P(x)$)" is "there is $x$ such that not $P(x)$";
5. "not (there is $x$ such that $P(x)$)" is "for every $x$, not $P(x)$".

A bounded quantifier keeps its domain: the negation of "for every $\varepsilon > 0$, $P(\varepsilon)$" is "there is $\varepsilon > 0$ such that not $P(\varepsilon)$".

## Motivation

The definitions of mathematics state the positive property only.
A book defines "continuous", "convergent" and "injective", and leaves "discontinuous", "divergent" and "not injective" to the reader.
A definition such as "for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such that for every $x$, ..." has three quantifiers and a conditional, and its negation is hard to read off by instinct.
A wrong negation leads to a wrong proof, so we use the five rules, which turn the negation into a statement with the same shape.
That statement tells you what a counterexample must provide.

Negation also sets up [[proof by contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]] and [[proof by contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]], which both start from a negated statement.
The formal laws behind the rules are in logic, under [[negation|029JBBF693]] and [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]].

## Notation

The negation of $P$ is written $\neg P$, and older books write $\sim P$ or $\overline{P}$.
In words it is "not $P$" or "it is not the case that $P$".
For simple statements, mathematics uses crossed symbols: $a \ne b$ for $\neg(a = b)$, $x \notin A$ for $\neg(x \in A)$, and $a \nmid b$ for $\neg(a \mid b)$.
For real numbers the negation of $a < b$ is $a \ge b$, and the negation of $a \le b$ is $a > b$.
In symbols the five rules read:
$$\neg(P \land Q) \equiv \neg P \lor \neg Q, \qquad \neg(P \lor Q) \equiv \neg P \land \neg Q, \qquad \neg(P \Rightarrow Q) \equiv P \land \neg Q,$$
$$\neg \forall x \, P(x) \equiv \exists x \, \neg P(x), \qquad \neg \exists x \, P(x) \equiv \forall x \, \neg P(x).$$

## Remarks

### A first negation, step by step

Negate "$f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is injective" one rule at a time.
The definition in symbols is
$$\forall x \;\; \forall y \;\; \big( f(x) = f(y) \Rightarrow x = y \big).$$
Rule 4 turns the first "for all" into "there is", and then the second:
$$\exists x \;\; \exists y \;\; \neg \big( f(x) = f(y) \Rightarrow x = y \big).$$
Rule 3 turns the negated conditional into a conjunction:
$$\exists x \;\; \exists y \;\; \big( f(x) = f(y) \text{ and } x \ne y \big).$$
For $f(x) = x^2$, take $x = 1$ and $y = -1$.
Check: $f(1) = 1 = f(-1)$ and $1 \ne -1$, so $x^2$ is not injective.

### Pushing not inward

Each rule can be tested with numbers.
The negation of "$0 < x < 1$", that is, of "$0 < x$ and $x < 1$", is "$x \le 0$ or $x \ge 1$".
The negation of "$n$ is even or $n$ is a multiple of $3$" is "$n$ is odd and $n$ is not a multiple of $3$"; for $n = 5$ the negation is true, so the original is false.
[[The negation of "every prime is odd"|1FP20JXFMV]] is "there is a prime that is not odd", and $2$ is such a prime.
For a statement with several parts, apply the rules from the outside in.
Each step moves one "not" one level down, until every "not" sits next to a simple statement.

### Negating a definition

The rules show their value on the definitions of analysis.
A sequence $(a_n)$ converges to $L$ when
$$\forall \varepsilon > 0 \;\; \exists N \;\; \forall n \ge N \;\; |a_n - L| < \varepsilon.$$
Three applications of the quantifier rules and one of $\neg(a < b) \equiv a \ge b$ give the negation:
$$\exists \varepsilon > 0 \;\; \forall N \;\; \exists n \ge N \;\; |a_n - L| \ge \varepsilon.$$
In words: some fixed tolerance $\varepsilon$ is broken again and again, however far along the sequence you go.
[[The negation of continuity for the Heaviside step function|Y7428Y6TSB]] does the same for continuity at a point, and it finds the $\varepsilon$ for a jump.

Negation keeps the order of the quantifiers and swaps each one.
The $\varepsilon$ still comes first, so in a proof of the negation you choose $\varepsilon$ before you see $N$.

### What a negation is not

[["If $P$, then not $Q$" is not the negation of "if $P$, then $Q$"|H71VVPDD9K]]: when $P$ is false, both are true.
"Every prime is even" is not the negation of "every prime is odd", because both are false: $3$ is a prime that is not even, and $2$ is a prime that is not odd.
"$x < 0$" is not the negation of "$x > 0$": for $x = 0$ both are false, and the correct negation is "$x \le 0$".
In each case the test is the same: a statement and its negation must have opposite truth values in every case.

### Watch out

The first error is to negate "for every $x$, $P(x)$" as "for every $x$, not $P(x)$".
The correct negation needs only one bad $x$: "there is $x$ such that not $P(x)$".
The second error is to negate a conditional as a conditional, either as "if $P$, then not $Q$" or as "if not $P$, then not $Q$".
The negation of a conditional is the conjunction "$P$ and not $Q$".
The third error is to negate the domain of a bounded quantifier.
The negation of "for every $\varepsilon > 0$, $P(\varepsilon)$" is not "there is $\varepsilon \le 0$ with not $P(\varepsilon)$".
The fourth error is to keep "and" in the negation of "and": the negation of "$x > 0$ and $y > 0$" is "$x \le 0$ or $y \le 0$".

### History

Aristotle, in On Interpretation, called "every $A$ is $B$" and "some $A$ is not $B$" contradictory: in every case exactly one of the two is true.
Later commentators drew these relations in a diagram, the square of opposition.
The laws that swap "and" and "or" under negation are named after Augustus De Morgan, a British logician of the 19th century.

### Try it

First, negate "every student in the class passed the test or was absent".
The negation is "some student in the class did not pass the test and was not absent".

Second, negate "$f$ is bounded", that is, "there is $M$ such that $|f(x)| \le M$ for every $x$".
The negation is "for every $M$ there is $x$ with $|f(x)| > M$".

## Explanations

### L1 human

The statement "all the doors are locked" is false when at least one door is open.
So its negation is "some door is not locked".
It is not "all the doors are unlocked", which says much more.
With numbers: "every number in the list $2, 4, 7$ is even" is false.
Its negation, "some number in the list is not even", is true, and the number $7$ shows it.

### L2 human

Prove that the sequence $a_n = (-1)^n$ does not converge to $L = 1$.
By the negation above, we need one $\varepsilon > 0$ such that for every $N$ some $n \ge N$ has $|a_n - 1| \ge \varepsilon$.
Take $\varepsilon = 1$.
Let $N$ be arbitrary, and let $n$ be an odd number with $n \ge N$, for example $n = 2N + 1$.
Then $a_n = -1$, and
$$|a_n - 1| = |-1 - 1| = 2 \ge 1.$$
So the negation holds, and $(-1)^n$ does not converge to $1$.

The negated definition told us what to produce: one $\varepsilon$, and an $n$ for each $N$.
Watch out: $\varepsilon$ is chosen first and must not depend on $N$, because "there is $\varepsilon$" comes before "for every $N$".

### L3 human

The five rules hold in classical logic, and they bring every formula to negation normal form, where "not" stands only before simple formulas.
Mathematicians name many negated properties with positive words, such as "unbounded", "divergent" and "discontinuous", and they work with the negation normal form of the definition.
In [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]] the rule $\neg \forall x \, P(x) \Rightarrow \exists x \, \neg P(x)$ fails, and so does $\neg(P \land Q) \Rightarrow \neg P \lor \neg Q$.
So a constructive proof that a function is not continuous may not give the $\varepsilon$ that the classical negation asks for.
The rule for the conditional also depends on classical logic, since $\neg(P \Rightarrow Q) \Rightarrow P$ is not intuitionistically valid.

The proof methods that rest on negation are in the topic [[proof methods|G9JFJGY4TQ]].
The proofs of the quantifier rules are [[the proof of the negation of a universal statement|DTM9GQV88K]] and [[the proof of the negation of an existential statement|GANHAKFNTA]].
