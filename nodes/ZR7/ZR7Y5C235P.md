## Statement

Let $L$ be a first-order signature, and let $T$ be a satisfiable $L$-theory.
Let $\kappa$ be an infinite [[cardinal|0EBACQFAZW]] with $\kappa \ge \abs{L}$, where $\abs{L}$ is the number of symbols of $L$.
Suppose that two hypotheses hold.

1. $T$ has no finite models.
2. $T$ is [[$\kappa$-categorical|1JJCGNX240]]: any two models of $T$ of cardinality $\kappa$ are isomorphic.

Then $T$ is [[complete|655K8Y7VS8]]: for every $L$-sentence $\varphi$, either $T \models \varphi$ or $T \models \neg\varphi$.
The proof is [[here|K7Z88ZYSZ7]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- No finite models. The empty theory in the empty signature has the nonempty sets as models, and it is $\kappa$-categorical for every infinite $\kappa$. It is not complete: $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x = y)$ holds in a model of size $1$ and fails in a model of size $2$.
- Categoricity. The theory of dense linear orders, with endpoints allowed, has no finite models in our convention of at least two elements. It is not $\aleph_0$-categorical and not complete, since "there is a least element" holds in $[0, 1] \cap \Q$ and fails in $\Q$.
- The bound $\kappa \ge \abs{L}$. Let $L$ have constants $c_\alpha$ for $\alpha < \omega_1$. Let $T$ say that there are infinitely many elements, that $c_0 = c_1$ implies $c_\alpha = c_\beta$ for all $\alpha, \beta$, and that $c_0 \ne c_1$ implies $c_\alpha \ne c_\beta$ for all $\alpha \ne \beta$.
- In that $T$, a countable model cannot have $\aleph_1$ distinct constants, so all constants name one element. Any two such countable models are isomorphic, so $T$ is $\aleph_0$-categorical with no finite models. But $T$ does not decide $c_0 = c_1$, since an uncountable model can make all constants distinct.

### Consequences

- The theory of [[dense linear orders without endpoints|2W4S2H4VYA]] is complete, since it is $\aleph_0$-categorical.
- The theory of algebraically closed fields of a fixed characteristic is complete, since it is $\aleph_1$-categorical.
- The theory of infinite sets and the theory of nonzero [[vector spaces over the rationals|97YPZ0YHEB]] are complete.
- The theory of the Rado graph, axiomatized by the extension axioms, is complete, since it is $\aleph_0$-categorical.
- A complete theory with a computably enumerable set of axioms is decidable. So each theory above is decidable.

### Converse

The converse is false.
The theory of $(\Z, <)$ is complete, has no finite models, and is [[categorical in no infinite cardinal|91WRBX69MB]].
The theory of algebraically closed fields of characteristic $0$ is complete and is not $\aleph_0$-categorical.

### Generalizations

The same proof shows: if every two models of $T$ of size $\kappa$ are elementarily equivalent, and $T$ has no finite models, then $T$ is complete.

## History

Jerzy Łoś and Robert Vaught found the test independently, and both published it in 1954.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A theory is complete when it answers every yes-or-no question that its language can ask.
The test gives a short way to check this.
Suppose that all infinite worlds of the theory of one size look the same, and that there are no finite worlds.
Then every question gets the same answer in all worlds, so the theory answers it.
For example, every countable dense order without endpoints looks like the fractions, so the rules of such orders answer every question about order.

### L2 human

We apply the test to the theory $T$ of infinite sets in the empty signature.

1. $T$ has the axioms "there are at least $n$ elements" for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, so $T$ has no finite models.
2. The signature is empty, so $\abs{L} = 0 \le \aleph_0$.
3. Two countable sets are in bijection, and a bijection is an isomorphism in the empty signature. So $T$ is $\aleph_0$-categorical.
4. The test gives that $T$ is complete. For example, $T$ decides $\exists x \, \exists y \, (x \ne y)$, which holds in every infinite set.

The typical use is a two-step proof of completeness: first a back-and-forth or dimension argument for categoricity, then the test.
A trap is to apply the test with $\kappa = \aleph_0$ when the signature is uncountable; the example for the bound $\kappa \ge \abs{L}$ above shows that this can fail.

### L3 human

The test reduces a syntactic question, completeness, to a question about isomorphism of models.
The [[Ryll-Nardzewski theorem|1513J40TKA]] and [[Morley's categoricity theorem|V7N84P60SJ]] say when its main hypothesis holds.
Many complete theories are not categorical in any cardinal, such as real closed fields; for them one uses quantifier elimination or model completeness instead.
The same argument works in any logic with a downward and upward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem for the theory at hand.
