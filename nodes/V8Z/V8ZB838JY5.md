## Summary

The forward-backward method is a way to find a proof by working from both ends at once: forward from what you know, and backward from what you want, until the two chains meet.
It does not change what a proof is, but it tells you where the first line of a proof comes from.

Here is a small search.
We want to prove that if $n$ is an odd integer, then $8 \mid n^2 - 1$.
Backward: the goal asks for $n^2 - 1 = 8m$, and $n^2 - 1 = (n - 1)(n + 1)$, so it suffices to show that $(n - 1)(n + 1)$ is $8$ times an integer.
Forward: $n$ odd gives $n = 2k + 1$, so $n - 1 = 2k$ and $n + 1 = 2(k + 1)$.
The chains meet:
$$(n - 1)(n + 1) = 4k(k + 1),$$
and one of $k$ and $k + 1$ is even, so $k(k + 1) = 2j$ and $n^2 - 1 = 8j$.

In general, to prove "if $A$, then $B$", the *backward process* replaces the goal $B$ by a statement $B_1$ with $B_1 \Rightarrow B$, and repeats.
The *forward process* derives consequences $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ of $A$.
The search ends when some $A_k$ equals some $B_j$, and the proof is written forward:
$$A \Rightarrow A_1 \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow A_k = B_j \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow B_1 \Rightarrow B.$$
The result is a [[direct proof|0NTF6DMVAP]].

## Motivation

A finished proof runs from the hypothesis to the conclusion, but its author seldom found it in that order.
From the hypothesis alone you can derive many consequences, and most lead nowhere.
The conclusion tells you which consequences are useful: to prove that a triangle is isosceles, you look for two equal sides, so you turn the hypothesis into an equation between sides.
Working from both ends narrows the search.
It also explains why printed proofs often start with a step that seems to come from nowhere, such as "let $\delta = \varepsilon / 2$": the author found that step by working backward and then hid the search.

## Notation

Daniel Solow calls the question of the backward process the *key question*: "How can I show that $B$ is true?"
Velleman organizes the same search as a list of givens and a goal, where each step either adds a given or replaces the goal, according to the logical form of the goal.
Scratch work often uses two columns, with the forward statements on the left and the backward statements on the right.
In Lean, the goal view shows the givens above the goal; the tactic `apply` works backward by replacing the goal, and `have` works forward by adding a given.

## Remarks

### The rule for a backward step

A backward step must replace the goal $B$ by a *sufficient* condition, a statement $B'$ with $B' \Rightarrow B$.
A *necessary* condition, a statement $B'$ with only $B \Rightarrow B'$, cannot replace the goal.
[[Squaring the goal|RDNHWAVSAZ]] shows the failure: replacing the goal $x + 1 \ge 0$ by $(x + 1)^2 \ge 0$ makes the new goal true for every $x$, including $x = -2$, where the old goal is false.
Forward steps have the mirror rule: each new statement $A'$ must follow from what you already have, $A \Rightarrow A'$.

### The form of the goal suggests the step

The logical form of the goal usually tells you the next backward step.
For "for all $x$", let $x$ be arbitrary, as in [[proving universal statements|CX38X989TB]].
For "if $P$, then $Q$", assume $P$ and make $Q$ the goal.
For "there is an $x$", look for a witness; for "$P$ and $Q$", prove both; for "$P$ if and only if $Q$", prove two directions.
For "not $P$", assume $P$ and look for a contradiction.
Unfolding a defined word in the goal is also a backward step, and it is often the most useful one.

### A search, step by step

In [[the composition of injective functions|DYMYNTBWEE]], the goal "$g \circ f$ is injective" unfolds to: for all $x, y$, if $g(f(x)) = g(f(y))$, then $x = y$.
So we let $x$ and $y$ be arbitrary and assume $g(f(x)) = g(f(y))$.
Backward: to get $x = y$ from the injectivity of $f$, it suffices to show $f(x) = f(y)$.
Forward: the injectivity of $g$, applied to $g(f(x)) = g(f(y))$, gives exactly $f(x) = f(y)$.
The chains meet, and the proof is done.

For the [[right triangle with area c^2/4|7X20PX8YB3]], the backward chain goes from "isosceles" to $a = b$ to $(a - b)^2 = 0$, and the forward chain goes from the area $\frac{ab}{2} = \frac{c^2}{4}$ and the Pythagorean theorem to $2ab = a^2 + b^2$.
These are the same equation.

### Finding a witness backward

The method is the usual way to find the object in an existence proof.
To prove that $f(x) = 3x + 2$ maps $\mathbb{R}$ onto $\mathbb{R}$, you need, for each $y$, an $x$ with $3x + 2 = y$.
Solve backward to get $x = \frac{y - 2}{3}$, then check it forward:
$$f\left(\frac{y - 2}{3}\right) = (y - 2) + 2 = y.$$
That check is a [[constructive proof|01XM7JEKHY]].
In an $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ proof that $\lim_{x \to 1} 2x = 2$, the backward step "$|2x - 2| < \varepsilon$ follows from $|x - 1| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$" finds $\delta = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}$.

### Watch out

The scratch work is not the proof.
A text that starts "$a^2 + b^2 \ge 2ab$" and ends "$(a - b)^2 \ge 0$, which is true" proves nothing until each step is reversed, as [[an argument that starts from the conclusion|2VA8Q8BG5Y]] explains.
So write the final proof in the forward order: the hypothesis, the forward steps, then the backward steps in reverse.
A second trap is a forward step that quietly uses the conclusion, which makes the argument circular.
A list of consequences of $A$ with no eye on $B$ is not wrong, but it is a search without direction.

### History

Pappus of Alexandria, in Book 7 of his Collection, described analysis, which assumes what is sought and works back to something known, and synthesis, which reverses the analysis to give the proof.
George Pólya's How to Solve It (1945) treats working backwards and the method of Pappus as heuristics for solving problems.
Daniel Solow's book How to Read and Do Proofs (1982) teaches the method under the name "forward-backward method", together with the key question.

### Try it

First, plan a proof that if $x > 3$, then $x^2 - 2x > 3$.
Backward, the goal is $x^2 - 2x - 3 = (x - 3)(x + 1) > 0$; forward, $x > 3$ gives $x - 3 > 0$ and $x + 1 > 4 > 0$, and the product of two positive numbers is positive.

Second, which is a valid backward step for the goal $x \ge 0$: "it suffices that $x^2 \ge 0$" or "it suffices that $x \ge 1$"?
The second, because $x \ge 1$ implies $x \ge 0$; the first fails at $x = -1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To plan a trip to a new place, it helps to look at the map from both ends.
From home, you know a few streets that you can reach.
From the new place, you see which streets lead to it.
When the two sets of streets meet, you have a route.
A proof can be planned in the same way: from what you know, and back from what you want, until the two meet in the middle.

### L2 human

Find a proof that for real $a, b > 0$, $\frac{a + b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab}$.
Backward: both sides are positive, so it suffices to show the squared form $\frac{(a + b)^2}{4} \ge ab$, that is, $(a + b)^2 \ge 4ab$.
Expand and move $4ab$ to the left:
$$(a + b)^2 - 4ab = a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = (a - b)^2.$$
Forward: a square is never negative, so $(a - b)^2 \ge 0$.
The chains meet, and the written proof runs forward: $(a - b)^2 \ge 0$, so $(a + b)^2 \ge 4ab$, and taking positive square roots gives $\frac{a + b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab}$.
Check: $a = 1$ and $b = 4$ give $2.5 \ge 2$.
The backward step chose the form of the goal, and the forward step supplied the one fact needed.
Watch out: squaring is a valid backward step here only because both sides are positive.

### L3 human

In automated theorem proving, forward chaining derives consequences of the facts and backward chaining reduces goals to subgoals, and logic programming in Prolog works backward from the query.
In interactive proof assistants, a proof state is a list of hypotheses and goals, most tactics are backward steps, and the proof term that results reads forward.
Pólya's heuristics and other strategies for finding proofs are treated in the topic [[heuristics of discovery|X1NSTVGDNQ]], and the layout of the final write-up in the topic [[writing and reading proofs|STSM6PR7TX]].
