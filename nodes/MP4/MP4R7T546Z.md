## Statement

The law of noncontradiction says that a statement and its negation are never both true.
"The number $7$ is even and the number $7$ is not even" is false, and it stays false whatever number replaces $7$.

Let $p$ be a propositional variable.
The formula
$$\neg(p \land \neg p)$$
is a [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]] of classical propositional logic, and it is also provable in [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]].
Here $\neg$ is [[negation|029JBBF693]] and $\land$ is [[conjunction|025YZN3VJW]].
Equivalently, $p \land \neg p$ is false under every valuation, so $p \land \neg p \equiv \bot$.
By substitution, $\neg(\varphi \land \neg\varphi)$ is a tautology for every formula $\varphi$.

There are two proofs: the [[proof by a truth table|82TG5066FF]], and the [[proof by natural deduction|RN3Y6804V7]], which uses only intuitionistic rules.
In Lean, where `Not p` is defined as `p → False`, a proof of `¬(p ∧ ¬p)` is the function `fun h => (And.right h) (And.left h)`.

## Remarks

### The two rows

Let $p$ be "$7$ is even".
Then $p$ is F and $\neg p$ is T, so the conjunction $p \land \neg p$ is $\mathrm{F} \land \mathrm{T}$, which is F, and its negation is T.
If $p$ is a true statement, the roles swap: $\neg p$ is F and the conjunction is F again.

| $p$ | $\neg p$ | $p \land \neg p$ | $\neg(p \land \neg p)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| T | F | F | T |
| F | T | F | T |

In each row one of the two parts is false, so the conjunction is never true.

### Why the exact form matters

The law has no hypotheses on $p$, so the useful question is which close variants fail.
The same variable must occur twice: $\neg(p \land q)$ is F in the row $p$ T, $q$ T.
The inner negation must be there: $\neg(p \land p)$ is equivalent to $\neg p$, which is F when $p$ is T.

The truth-table proof also uses two truth values.
In Kleene's three-valued logic, the third value U satisfies $\neg \mathrm{U} = \mathrm{U}$ and only T counts as true.
With $p = \mathrm{U}$, the formula $\neg(p \land \neg p)$ takes the value U, so the law is not a tautology of that logic.

### Valid in both logics

Unlike the [[law of excluded middle|0RYYK8PG4S]], the law of noncontradiction needs no classical principle.
To prove a negation $\neg\varphi$ intuitionistically, you assume $\varphi$ and derive a contradiction.
Here $\varphi$ is $p \land \neg p$, which hands you both $p$ and $\neg p$, and applying $\neg p$ to $p$ gives the contradiction at once.
The derivation uses no rule about the false statement either, so the law holds even in minimal logic.

Classically the two laws are equivalent.
By [[De Morgan's laws|09PKJKA6J0]] and the [[double negation law|0NFAGWH84K]],
$$\neg(p \land \neg p) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg\neg p \equiv \neg p \lor p.$$
The first step uses $\neg(a \land b) \to \neg a \lor \neg b$, the direction of De Morgan's law that fails intuitionistically.
So only the law of noncontradiction survives there.

### What the law gives you

All contradictions are equivalent to $p \land \neg p$, since all of them have a column with only F.
If an assumption $r$ leads to both $q$ and $\neg q$, then $r$ is false, because $q \land \neg q$ is never true; this is the last step of every [[proof by contradiction|0J0XBKG64S]].
The formula $(p \land \neg p) \to q$ is a tautology, since its hypothesis is false in every row; this is the principle of [[explosion|TTKZGJEEK7]].
For sets, $A \cap A^c = \varnothing$ for each subset $A$ of a universal set, where $A^c$ is the [[complement|0RKF1ZT5JJ]] of $A$.

### The converse and generalizations

The law is a single formula, so it has no converse in the strict sense.
Read as "$p \land \neg p$ is a contradiction", it has a true converse: every contradiction is equivalent to $p \land \neg p$.

In a [[Boolean algebra|0XADJKM699]] and in a [[Heyting algebra|NG943XNQWH]], each element satisfies $a \land \neg a = 0$.
In a Heyting algebra, $\neg a$ is defined as the largest element $b$ with $a \land b = 0$, so the law is built into the definition.
For a predicate $P(x)$ on a domain $D$, the law gives $\forall x \, \neg(P(x) \land \neg P(x))$.

### The dialetheist challenge

In the Logic of Paradox of Graham Priest, a [[paraconsistent logic|C7YWCR87QJ]], the third value B means "both true and false", and B counts as true.
With $p = \mathrm{B}$ we get $\neg p = \mathrm{B}$ and $p \land \neg p = \mathrm{B}$, so the formula $\neg(p \land \neg p)$ has the value B and still counts as true.
But $p \land \neg p$ counts as true as well.
So the formula survives while it no longer excludes a contradiction, and this is what dialetheists claim for sentences such as the [[liar sentence|KBYY281V8T]], "this sentence is false".

### Try it

Is $\neg(p \land \neg q)$ a tautology?
No: in the row $p$ T, $q$ F, the conjunction $p \land \neg q$ is T, so its negation is F.

Name the two statements whose conjunction gives the contradiction when you assume that $\sqrt{2} = a/b$ in lowest terms.
They are "$a$ and $b$ are both even", which the algebra proves, and its negation, which "lowest terms" says.

## History

Aristotle stated the law in Book IV (Gamma) of the Metaphysics.
His formulation says that one property cannot both belong and not belong to the same thing, at the same time and in the same respect.
He called it the most certain of all principles, and he held that it cannot be proved from anything more basic: it can only be defended against an opponent who says something.
In the twentieth century, paraconsistent logics gave semantics in which a contradiction does not imply every statement.
Dialetheism, defended above all by Graham Priest, goes further and holds that some contradictions are true.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The law says that a statement and its opposite cannot both be true.
"The door is open and the door is not open" cannot be true.
If a calculation shows that a number is even and also not even, then the calculation contains an error, or one of its assumptions is false.
Nearly every argument in mathematics leans on this rule.

### L2 human

The law does the last step in the proof that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
Assume that $\sqrt{2} = a/b$ with integers $a$ and $b \neq 0$, in lowest terms, and let $q$ be "$a$ and $b$ are both even".
Lowest terms means that $\neg q$ holds.
Squaring gives
$$a^2 = 2b^2,$$
so $a^2$ is even, hence $a$ is even, say $a = 2c$.
Then $4c^2 = 2b^2$, so $b^2 = 2c^2$ is even and $b$ is even, and $q$ holds.
Now $q \land \neg q$ holds, which the law forbids, so the assumption is false.
Watch out: two contrary statements are not yet a contradiction.
"$x > 0$" and "$x < 0$" cannot both be true, but both are false for $x = 0$, so neither is the negation of the other.

### L3 human

In the [[Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov interpretation|7B53ZGXXEQ]], a proof of $\neg(p \land \neg p)$ is a method that turns a pair, a proof $a$ of $p$ and a method $f$ from proofs of $p$ to absurdity, into an absurdity, and $f(a)$ does this.
Through the [[Curry-Howard correspondence|A77FER1Q5H]] this is the Lean term above: function application.
That is why the law holds in intuitionistic and minimal logic, while its classical twin, the law of excluded middle, does not.

The dialetheist does not reject the formula $\neg(p \land \neg p)$ in every logic.
The claim is rather that the formula fails to exclude the truth of $p \land \neg p$ for some sentences.
A dialetheist must use a paraconsistent logic, since in classical logic a single true contradiction would make every statement true by explosion.
