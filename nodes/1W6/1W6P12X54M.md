## Example

The statement is: for all integers $a$, $b$ and $c$, if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$.
Here $a \mid b$ means that $b = am$ for some integer $m$, by the definition of [[divisibility|0PG2H7WKGG]].
The proof in the text reads as follows.

> Suppose $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$.
> Then $b = am$ and $c = bn$.
> So $c = a(mn)$, and $a \mid c$.

We read it by the method of [[reading proofs|40GCSM2G56]].

1. The statement. The hypotheses are (H1) "$a \mid b$" and (H2) "$b \mid c$". The conclusion is "$a \mid c$". The integers $a$, $b$, $c$ are arbitrary.
2. A test case before the proof. Take $a = 3$, $b = 6$ and $c = 24$. Then $6 = 3 \cdot 2$ and $24 = 6 \cdot 4$, so both hypotheses hold, and $24 = 3 \cdot 8$, so the conclusion holds.
3. The structure. The proof is a [[direct proof|0NTF6DMVAP]]: it assumes both hypotheses and derives the conclusion.
4. Line "Then $b = am$". The reason is (H1) and the definition of divisibility. The proof leaves out that $m$ is an integer, and we add it.
5. Line "and $c = bn$". The reason is (H2) and the definition, with an integer $n$. The proof uses a new letter $n$, not $m$ again, and this is correct: the two multipliers can differ.
6. Line "So $c = a(mn)$". We substitute $b = am$ into $c = bn$ and get $c = (am)n$. The associative law of multiplication gives $(am)n = a(mn)$.
7. Line "and $a \mid c$". The number $mn$ is an integer, because a product of integers is an integer. So $c$ is $a$ times an integer, which is the definition of $a \mid c$.
8. The use of the hypotheses. (H1) is used in line 4, and (H2) in line 5. Both are needed for line 6.
9. The proof run on the test case. Line 4 gives $m = 2$, line 5 gives $n = 4$, and line 6 gives $24 = 3 \cdot (2 \cdot 4) = 3 \cdot 8$. Each line is true for these numbers.
10. The summary. Write each divisibility as a product, substitute one product into the other, and read off the multiplier $mn$.

## Remarks

1. The reading added two reasons that the text leaves out: that $m$ and $n$ are integers, and that $mn$ is an integer. Without the second one, the last line does not follow from the definition.
2. Looking back, we ask if (H1) is needed. With $a = 4$, $b = 6$ and $c = 18$, (H2) holds, (H1) fails, and $4 \nmid 18$. So (H1) cannot be dropped.
3. In the same way (H2) is needed. With $a = 3$, $b = 6$ and $c = 10$, (H1) holds, (H2) fails, and $3 \nmid 10$.
4. The proof uses no property of the integers other than the closure under multiplication and the associative law. So the same proof works for [[divisibility in a commutative ring|07RGNK57ED]], which is a generalization that the reading reveals.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The claim is: if $3$ goes into $6$ exactly, and $6$ goes into $24$ exactly, then $3$ goes into $24$ exactly.
The proof says: $6$ is $3$ times $2$, and $24$ is $6$ times $4$.
So $24$ is $3$ times $2$ times $4$, that is, $3$ times $8$.
A careful reader checks each of these sentences with the numbers.
The reader also notices that the proof works the same way for any three numbers of this kind.

### L2 human

The reading has three outputs, and each output is a short written list.

1. The reasons: line 4 uses (H1), and line 5 uses (H2). Line 6 uses substitution and the associative law. Line 7 uses the closure of the integers under multiplication.
2. The test: the numbers $3$, $6$, $24$ with $m = 2$ and $n = 4$.
3. The idea: the multipliers multiply, so the multiplier of $c$ over $a$ is $mn$.

The typical use of such a reading is to prepare a proof for an examination.
The student who has these three lists can rebuild the proof.
A trap is to write $b = am$ and $c = bm$ with the same letter $m$.
This silently assumes that the two multipliers are equal, and then the proof covers only special cases such as $3$, $6$, $12$.
