## Example

The statement is that for all integers $a$, $b$ and $c$, if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$.
Here $a \mid b$ means that $b = am$ for some integer $m$, by the definition of [[divisibility|0PG2H7WKGG]].
The proof in the text reads as follows.

> Suppose $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$.
> Then $b = am$ and $c = bn$.
> So $c = a(mn)$, and $a \mid c$.

We read it by the method of [[reading proofs|40GCSM2G56]], starting with the statement.
Its hypotheses are (H1) $a \mid b$ and (H2) $b \mid c$, its conclusion is $a \mid c$, and $a$, $b$ and $c$ are arbitrary integers.
Before the proof we test the statement on $a = 3$, $b = 6$ and $c = 24$: here $6 = 3 \cdot 2$ and $24 = 6 \cdot 4$, and indeed $24 = 3 \cdot 8$.
The proof is a [[direct proof|0NTF6DMVAP]], which assumes both hypotheses and derives the conclusion.

Now line by line.
"Then $b = am$" rests on (H1) and the definition, and the text leaves out that $m$ is an integer, so we add it.
"And $c = bn$" rests on (H2), with a new letter $n$, which is correct because the two multipliers can differ.
"So $c = a(mn)$" substitutes $b = am$ into $c = bn$ to get $(am)n$, and the associative law gives $a(mn)$.
"And $a \mid c$" needs the fact that $mn$ is an integer, as a product of integers, which the text does not say.

Run on the test case, the lines give $m = 2$, $n = 4$ and $24 = 3 \cdot (2 \cdot 4)$, so each line is true for these numbers.
In summary: write each divisibility as a product, substitute one into the other, and read off the multiplier $mn$.

## Remarks

The reading added two reasons that the text leaves out: that $m$ and $n$ are integers, and that $mn$ is an integer.
Without the second, the last line does not follow from the definition.

Looking back, both hypotheses are needed.
With $a = 4$, $b = 6$ and $c = 18$, (H2) holds, (H1) fails, and $4 \nmid 18$.
With $a = 3$, $b = 6$ and $c = 10$, (H1) holds, (H2) fails, and $3 \nmid 10$.

The proof uses no property of the integers except closure under multiplication and the associative law.
So the same proof works for [[divisibility in a commutative ring|07RGNK57ED]], a generalization that the reading itself reveals.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The claim is: if $3$ goes into $6$ exactly, and $6$ goes into $24$ exactly, then $3$ goes into $24$ exactly.
The proof says that $6$ is $3$ times $2$, and $24$ is $6$ times $4$.
So $24$ is $3$ times $2$ times $4$, that is, $3$ times $8$.
A careful reader checks each of these sentences with the numbers.
The reader also notices that the proof works the same way for any three numbers of this kind.

### L2 human

The reading leaves three short written results.
The first is the list of reasons: line two uses (H1) for $b = am$ and (H2) for $c = bn$, and line three uses substitution, the associative law and the closure of the integers under multiplication.
The second is the test case $3$, $6$ and $24$, with $m = 2$ and $n = 4$.
The third is the idea: the multipliers multiply, so the multiplier of $c$ over $a$ is $mn$.
A student who has these three results can rebuild the proof in an examination.

Watch out: writing $b = am$ and $c = bm$ with the same letter silently assumes that the two multipliers are equal.
The proof then covers only special cases such as $3$, $6$ and $12$.
