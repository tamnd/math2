## Example

Let $F$ be a field, let $V = M_n(F)$ with $n \ge 1$, and let $U = \{ X \in V : \operatorname{tr} X = 0 \}$ be the subspace of trace-zero matrices, where $\operatorname{tr}$ is the [[trace|07PTPSZPME]].
We show that the [[annihilator|0R6M158SQ9]] $U^0$ is the line $F \cdot \operatorname{tr}$ of multiples of the trace.

1. The trace vanishes on $U$ by the definition of $U$, so $\operatorname{tr} \in U^0$, and then $F \cdot \operatorname{tr} \subseteq U^0$ because $U^0$ is a subspace.
2. The trace is a nonzero form, since $\operatorname{tr} E_{11} = 1$ for the [[matrix unit|4H93XF4RVH]] $E_{11}$. So $U = \ker(\operatorname{tr})$ is a hyperplane, and $\dim U = n^2 - 1$, by the theorem on [[hyperplanes and linear forms|XR0EQN0H8T]].
3. By the theorem on the [[dimension of the annihilator|06Q1EFVH0Q]], $\dim U^0 = n^2 - (n^2 - 1) = 1$.
4. The line $F \cdot \operatorname{tr}$ lies in $U^0$ by step 1, and both have dimension $1$. So $U^0 = F \cdot \operatorname{tr}$.

For $n = 2$ we can also check this by hand with the [[trace pairing|4F7GWKTQ1H]].
Every linear form on $M_2(F)$ is $\tau_A(X) = \operatorname{tr}(AX)$ for exactly one matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{pmatrix}$.

5. The matrices $E_{12}$, $E_{21}$ and $E_{11} - E_{22}$ have trace $0$, so they lie in $U$.
6. The product $A E_{12}$ has $a_{11}, a_{21}$ in its second column and zeros elsewhere, so $\tau_A(E_{12}) = a_{21}$. In the same way $\tau_A(E_{21}) = a_{12}$. So $\tau_A \in U^0$ forces $a_{12} = a_{21} = 0$.
7. Next, $\tau_A(E_{11} - E_{22}) = a_{11} - a_{22}$. So $\tau_A \in U^0$ also forces $a_{11} = a_{22}$.
8. Hence $A = aI$ for some $a \in F$, and $\tau_A(X) = \operatorname{tr}(aX) = a \operatorname{tr} X$. So $U^0 = F \cdot \operatorname{tr}$, as in step 4.

## Remarks

In words, a linear form on $M_n(F)$ that gives $0$ on every trace-zero matrix is a multiple of the trace.
Under the trace pairing, the annihilator corresponds to the scalar matrices $F \cdot I$, and that is what steps 5 to 8 found.

The three matrices of step 5 are independent and lie in $U$, and $\dim U = 4 - 1 = 3$ for $n = 2$, so they are a basis of $U$.
That is why testing $\tau_A$ on these three matrices was enough.

The dimension argument of steps 1 to 4 works for every nonzero form $\varphi$ on a finite-dimensional space: the annihilator of $\ker \varphi$ is the line $F \varphi$.
The trace is just one case of this rule, and the hand computation for $n = 2$ confirms it.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The trace-zero matrices are the square matrices whose diagonal entries add up to $0$.
The only linear rules that give $0$ on all of them are the multiples of the trace itself.
The reason is that the trace-zero matrices are cut out by one equation, and a set cut out by one equation has that equation as its only rule, up to a factor.

### L2 human

The method here is a dimension count, and it saves solving a large linear system.
First find one nonzero form in the annihilator, here the trace.
Then compute the dimension of the annihilator, here $n^2 - (n^2 - 1) = 1$.
A nonzero form in a space of dimension $1$ spans it, so the trace spans the annihilator.
This is the typical way to show that a form is the unique form with a given kernel, up to a scalar.
Watch out: $U^0$ is a set of forms on matrices, not a set of matrices, and the scalar matrices appear only after the trace pairing identifies forms with matrices.
