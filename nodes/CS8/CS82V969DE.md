## Summary

A formula is in prenex normal form when all its quantifiers stand at the front, followed by a part without quantifiers.
In classical first-order logic every formula is equivalent to a formula in prenex normal form, by the [[prenex normal form theorem|~7674d52e]].
The string of quantifiers in front measures the logical complexity of the formula, as in the [[arithmetical hierarchy|7CVJCQKY41]].
Prenex form is also the first step towards [[Skolem normal form|GCPQGMJYEV]].

## Motivation

A [[formula|HYJ10AF6RT]] can hide its quantifiers deep inside connectives, as in $\neg \forall x \, P(x) \lor \exists y \, Q(y)$.
Such a formula is hard to compare with other formulas, and hard to classify.
In prenex form the quantifiers come first, so the pattern of $\forall$ and $\exists$ is visible at once.
Proofs about all formulas can then treat a quantifier prefix and a propositional matrix separately.
Skolemization, Herbrand's theorem and resolution all start from prenex formulas.

## Notation

1. A prenex formula is written $Q_1 x_1 \cdots Q_n x_n \, \psi$, with each $Q_i \in \set{\forall, \exists}$ and $\psi$ quantifier-free.
2. The string $Q_1 x_1 \cdots Q_n x_n$ is the prefix, and $\psi$ is the matrix.
3. The prefix type lists the quantifiers alone, such as $\forall \exists$. Blocks of equal quantifiers count once for $\Sigma_n$ and $\Pi_n$.
4. A formula is $\Sigma_n$ when its prefix has $n$ blocks and starts with $\exists$, and $\Pi_n$ when it starts with $\forall$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. $\forall x \, \exists y \, (x < y)$ is prenex, with prefix type $\forall \exists$.
2. Every quantifier-free formula is prenex, with an empty prefix.
3. [[An implication between quantified formulas|~6f51c260]]: $(\forall x \, P(x)) \to \exists y \, Q(y)$ has the prenex form $\exists x \, \exists y \, (P(x) \to Q(y))$.
4. [[The continuity of a function|~a2e7a985]] has the prenex form $\forall a \, \forall \varepsilon \, \exists \delta \, \forall x \, \mu$ with a quantifier-free $\mu$.

### Non-examples

1. $\neg \forall x \, P(x)$ is not prenex: a quantifier stands inside a negation. Its prenex form is $\exists x \, \neg P(x)$.
2. [[A formula that begins with a quantifier|~64ef149b]], such as $\forall x \, (P(x) \to \exists y \, R(x, y))$, need not be prenex.
3. $\forall x \, P(x) \land \forall y \, Q(y)$ is not prenex, although every quantifier in it is universal.

### Basic properties

1. Every formula is [[logically equivalent|0JMR2HM2N2]] to a prenex formula with the same [[free variables|~2fd0cd75]]. This uses classical logic and nonempty domains.
2. The prenex form is not unique. Different orders of moving quantifiers out can give different prefix types.
3. The negation of a $\Sigma_n$ prenex formula is equivalent to a $\Pi_n$ prenex formula: each quantifier changes to its dual, and the matrix is negated.
4. A prenex formula with a universal prefix is preserved in substructures. One with an existential prefix is preserved in extensions.

### History

Skolem used prenex forms in his work of 1920 and 1922 on the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem.
Hilbert and Ackermann's book of 1928 presented the conversion to prenex form for the predicate calculus.
Later, Kleene and Mostowski classified the arithmetical formulas by their prenex prefixes, which gave the arithmetical hierarchy.

### Common errors

1. A student moves a quantifier out of the left side of $\to$ without changing it. The correct rule is $(\forall x \, \varphi) \to \chi \equiv \exists x \, (\varphi \to \chi)$, for $x$ not free in $\chi$.
2. A student moves a quantifier past a formula in which its variable is free. In $P(x) \land \exists x \, Q(x)$, the bound $x$ must first be renamed.
3. A student merges two quantifiers, as $\forall x \, P(x) \lor \forall x \, Q(x)$ into $\forall x \, (P(x) \lor Q(x))$. The two are not equivalent.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The formula $\forall x \, \exists y \, (x < y \land y < x + 2)$ has all its quantifiers at the start, so it is in prenex form.
The formula $(\forall x \, P(x)) \to Q(c)$ is not, because the quantifier sits inside the implication.
It can be rewritten as $\exists x \, (P(x) \to Q(c))$, which means the same thing and is in prenex form.
Pulling quantifiers to the front like this is always possible in classical logic.

### L2 human

We put $\varphi = \forall x \, P(x) \to \forall x \, Q(x)$ in prenex form.

1. The two bound variables have the same name, so we rename the second one: $\varphi \equiv \forall x \, P(x) \to \forall y \, Q(y)$.
2. The quantifier $\forall x$ is on the left of $\to$, and $x$ is not free in $\forall y \, Q(y)$. So it moves out and becomes $\exists x$: $\exists x \, (P(x) \to \forall y \, Q(y))$.
3. The quantifier $\forall y$ is on the right of $\to$, and $y$ is not free in $P(x)$. So it moves out unchanged: $\exists x \, \forall y \, (P(x) \to Q(y))$.
4. Moving $\forall y$ first gives $\forall y \, \exists x \, (P(x) \to Q(y))$. This is another prenex form of $\varphi$.

The typical use is to read off the complexity: the first form is $\Sigma_2$ and the second is $\Pi_2$.
A trap: in step 2 the quantifier changes from $\forall$ to $\exists$, because $\alpha \to \beta$ means $\neg \alpha \lor \beta$.

### L3 human

The [[prenex normal form theorem|~7674d52e]] reduces each classical formula to a quantifier prefix over a propositional matrix.
The alternations of the prefix measure complexity: $\Sigma_n$ and $\Pi_n$ in arithmetic, and the levels of the polynomial hierarchy for quantified Boolean formulas.
Preservation theorems link prefix shape and semantics: the Łoś-Tarski theorem says that a theory preserved under substructures has a universal axiomatization.
In intuitionistic logic the prenex theorem fails, so constructive proof theory measures complexity by other means.
In [[Skolem normal form|GCPQGMJYEV]] the existential quantifiers of the prefix are replaced by function symbols.
