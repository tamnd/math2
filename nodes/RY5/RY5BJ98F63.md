## Example

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ over $\R$.

1. The leading entry of row 1 is at $(1, 2)$, and the leading entry of row 2 is at $(2, 1)$.
2. The second lies to the left of the first, so $A$ is not in [[row echelon form|0F27PBRWD7]].
3. So these leading entries are not pivots in the sense of the [[definition|0BCMGDHZGR]].
4. Swap $R_1$ and $R_2$: the result is $I_2$, which is in row echelon form.
5. So the pivot positions of $A$ are $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 2)$, and the entries of $A$ there are $0$.

## Remarks

The pivot positions of a matrix are defined through an echelon form, so they can sit at zero entries of the original matrix.
Both columns are pivot columns, so the rank is $2$ in either reading.

## Explanations

### L1 human

You must first bring the table into staircase shape; only then can you point at the pivots.
Here one swap is enough.

### L2 human

1. Reduce to echelon form.
2. Read the pivot positions there.
3. Transfer the positions, not the numbers, back to the original matrix.

A trap: the pivot columns of $A$ are the same here as the columns of $I_2$, but the pivot rows are not the rows where $A$ has its leading entries.
