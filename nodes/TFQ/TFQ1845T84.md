## Summary

An "if and only if" statement makes two promises, one in each direction, and a proof must keep both.
So you prove it as two separate implications, and each one may use its own method.

Here is a small one: an integer $n$ is even if and only if $n^2$ is even.
For the direction $(\Rightarrow)$, if $n = 2k$, then $n^2 = 2(2k^2)$ is even.
For the direction $(\Leftarrow)$, we prove the [[contrapositive|0BB5TPGQ1N]]: if $n = 2k + 1$ is odd, then
$$n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$$
is odd.
Both directions hold, so the equivalence holds.

In general, a [[biconditional|0S6GMNJ62Y]] "$P$ if and only if $Q$" is proved by proving "if $P$, then $Q$" and "if $Q$, then $P$"; this is the rule of [[biconditional introduction|R698F4HZ51]].
A list $P_1, \ldots, P_n$ of statements is shown to be equivalent, which books write "the following are equivalent" (TFAE), by a chain of implications that returns to its start, such as
$$P_1 \Rightarrow P_2 \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow P_n \Rightarrow P_1.$$

## Motivation

Many theorems say that two conditions pick out the same objects.
A square matrix is [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] if and only if its determinant is nonzero, and an integer greater than $1$ is prime if and only if it has the property of [[Euclid's lemma|051VYKA1EX]], as [[two definitions of a prime number|787PD6B171]] shows.
Such a theorem lets you replace one condition by the other in any later proof, so it is one of the most useful kinds of result.
[[Equivalent definitions|NZ42PS2P1A]] of one notion are justified in the same way.

The two directions usually need different arguments, and often one is much harder than the other.
So it is natural to split the proof into two labeled parts.
For a list of $n$ conditions, proving all $n(n - 1)$ implications one by one would be wasteful, and a cycle of $n$ implications is enough.

## Notation

The biconditional is written $P \iff Q$, "$P$ if and only if $Q$", or "$P$ iff $Q$"; [[conditional statements|FA6YZGR2SS]] explains the words "only if", "necessary" and "sufficient".
The two parts of the proof are labeled $(\Rightarrow)$ for "if $P$, then $Q$" and $(\Leftarrow)$ for "if $Q$, then $P$".
Some books write "necessity" and "sufficiency", but authors attach these words to the two directions in different ways, so the arrows are safer.
For a list, the parts are labeled $(1) \Rightarrow (2)$, $(2) \Rightarrow (3)$, and so on.
In Lean, the tactic `constructor` (or the term `Iff.intro`) splits a goal `P ↔ Q` into `P → Q` and `Q → P`.
Mathlib defines `List.TFAE` for a list of equivalent statements, with the tactics `tfae_have` and `tfae_finish`.

## Remarks

### Why two directions are enough

The biconditional $P \iff Q$ is true exactly when $P$ and $Q$ have the same truth value.
That is the same as saying that $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow P$ are both true: the first rules out "$P$ true, $Q$ false", and the second rules out "$Q$ true, $P$ false".
Since $Q \Rightarrow P$ is equivalent to its contrapositive $\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q$, you may also prove the second direction in the form "if not $P$, then not $Q$".
In the example of the Summary, the second direction was proved in exactly this way.

### Chains of equivalences

Sometimes each step of a computation can be reversed, and then a chain of "$\iff$" proves the whole equivalence at once.
For real $x$,
$$x^2 = 1 \iff (x - 1)(x + 1) = 0 \iff x - 1 = 0 \text{ or } x + 1 = 0 \iff x = 1 \text{ or } x = -1.$$
The first step is algebra that works both ways, and the second uses that a product of real numbers is $0$ exactly when a factor is $0$.
Such a chain is valid only if you have checked every step in both directions.
Squaring, multiplying by a number that may be $0$, and dividing by a variable often work in one direction only.

### Lists of equivalent statements

To prove that $P_1, P_2, P_3$ are equivalent, it is enough to prove $P_1 \Rightarrow P_2$, $P_2 \Rightarrow P_3$ and $P_3 \Rightarrow P_1$.
Then $P_2 \Rightarrow P_1$, for example, follows by chaining $P_2 \Rightarrow P_3 \Rightarrow P_1$, because implication is transitive.
[[Three equivalent conditions for a subset|1JKK56DKWW]] proves that $A \subseteq B$, $A \cap B = A$ and $A \cup B = B$ are equivalent by such a cycle.

A cycle is not the only shape that works.
Draw an arrow from $P_i$ to $P_j$ for each implication you prove.
The list is equivalent as soon as each statement can reach every other one along arrows, and a single cycle through all $n$ statements is the cheapest way to arrange that.
The order of the cycle is your choice, so put each arrow where its proof is short.

### What does not prove a biconditional

[[Two proofs of the same direction do not prove a biconditional|K08FN2E081]] shows a student who proves $P \Rightarrow Q$ and then its contrapositive $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$.
These are the same implication in two forms, so the direction $Q \Rightarrow P$ is never proved.
Proving $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow R$ does not show that $P$, $Q$ and $R$ are equivalent either, since the chain does not return to $P$.
And a chain in which one step holds only one way proves nothing in the other direction: from $x = 2$ we get $x^2 = 4$, but $x^2 = 4$ allows $x = -2$.

### Watch out

The most common error is to prove one direction and call the result an equivalence.
Label each direction, and check at the end that both labels appear.

A quieter error is to prove the same direction twice.
Write each part as "if $X$, then $Y$" and form its contrapositive.
If the two parts agree up to contraposition, only one direction is proved.

The third error is to write "$\iff$" between lines that are linked only by "$\Rightarrow$".
From $x = -3$ squaring gives $x^2 = 9$, but the converse step loses the sign, so the arrow must point one way.

### Writing it up

Announce the plan ("We prove the two directions separately.").
Start each part with its label and its assumption, for example "$(\Leftarrow)$ Assume that $n^2$ is even."
For a list, state the cycle before you start ("We prove $(1) \Rightarrow (2) \Rightarrow (3) \Rightarrow (1)$.").
End with a sentence that says the cycle is closed or that both directions are done.

### History

The abbreviation "iff" for "if and only if" is often credited to Paul Halmos, and it is now standard in mathematical writing.
The rule of biconditional introduction belongs to the natural deduction calculi that go back to Gerhard Gentzen's work of 1934.

### Try it

First, prove that for real $a, b \ge 0$, $a = b$ if and only if $a^2 = b^2$.
$(\Rightarrow)$ is squaring; for $(\Leftarrow)$, $0 = a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$, so $a = b$ or $a + b = 0$, and $a + b = 0$ with $a, b \ge 0$ forces $a = b = 0$.

Second, a student proves $(1) \Rightarrow (2)$, $(2) \Rightarrow (1)$ and $(2) \Rightarrow (3)$ for a list of three conditions.
Is the list proved equivalent?
No: no arrow leaves $(3)$, so nothing shows that $(3)$ implies the others.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"$P$ if and only if $Q$" makes two promises.
The first promise is that whenever $P$ is true, $Q$ is true.
The second promise is that whenever $Q$ is true, $P$ is true.
For example, "a whole number is even if and only if its last digit is $0$, $2$, $4$, $6$ or $8$".
A proof must check that every even number ends in such a digit, and also that every number that ends in such a digit is even.
Checking only one promise proves only half of the sentence.

### L2 human

Let us prove that for real $a \ge 0$ and $b \ge 0$, $a \le b$ if and only if $a^2 \le b^2$.
For $(\Rightarrow)$, assume $a \le b$, and multiply this inequality once by $a \ge 0$ and once by $b \ge 0$:
$$a^2 \le ab \quad \text{and} \quad ab \le b^2, \quad \text{so} \quad a^2 \le b^2.$$
For $(\Leftarrow)$ we prove the contrapositive: if $a > b$, then $a^2 > b^2$.
From $a > b \ge 0$ we get $a > 0$, and multiplying $a > b$ by $a > 0$ and by $b \ge 0$ gives
$$a^2 > ab \quad \text{and} \quad ab \ge b^2, \quad \text{so} \quad a^2 > b^2.$$
Both directions are proved, so the equivalence holds.
The problem shows the typical use: label each direction and choose a method for each one separately.
Watch out: the hypotheses $a, b \ge 0$ matter, since $a = -3$ and $b = 1$ give $a \le b$ but $a^2 = 9 > 1 = b^2$.

### L3 human

In logic, $P \iff Q$ is the conjunction of two implications, and the proof of a TFAE list is a proof that a directed graph of implications is strongly connected.
Mathlib packages this as `List.TFAE`, and `tfae_finish` closes the goal once the proved implications connect every pair.
Equivalence of statements is the propositional shadow of isomorphism: in homotopy type theory the univalence axiom identifies equivalent types, and for propositions it says that logically equivalent propositions are equal.
Characterization theorems, such as the many equivalent forms of compactness or of the [[axiom of choice|0HTGB13FE2]], are the main use of the method, and the topic [[definitions in mathematics|BP0WAF23HB]] explains why a field keeps several equivalent definitions of one notion.
The special cases of two inclusions between sets and two inequalities between numbers are in the topic [[proof patterns for common goals|MEKKHHQTKB]].
