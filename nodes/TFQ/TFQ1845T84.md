## Summary

A [[biconditional|0S6GMNJ62Y]] "$P$ if and only if $Q$" is proved by proving the two implications "if $P$, then $Q$" and "if $Q$, then $P$", each by any method.
This is the rule of [[biconditional introduction|R698F4HZ51]].
A list of statements $P_1, \ldots, P_n$ is shown to be equivalent, written "the following are equivalent" (TFAE), by proving a chain of implications that returns to its start, such as $P_1 \Rightarrow P_2 \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow P_n \Rightarrow P_1$.
Characterization theorems and [[equivalent definitions|NZ42PS2P1A]] have this form, so the method is used in every field of mathematics.

## Motivation

Many theorems say that two conditions single out the same objects: a matrix is invertible if and only if its determinant is nonzero, and an integer is even if and only if its square is even.
Such a theorem lets a reader replace one condition by the other in any later proof.
The two directions usually have different proofs, and often one of them is much harder than the other.
So it is natural to split the proof into two parts and to give each part a label.
For a list of $n$ conditions, proving all $n(n - 1)$ implications would be wasteful, and a cycle of $n$ implications is enough.

## Notation

The biconditional is written $P \iff Q$, "$P$ if and only if $Q$", or "$P$ iff $Q$"; see [[conditional statements|FA6YZGR2SS]] for the words "only if", "necessary" and "sufficient".
The two parts of the proof are labelled $(\Rightarrow)$ for "if $P$, then $Q$" and $(\Leftarrow)$ for "if $Q$, then $P$".
Some books write "necessity" and "sufficiency", but the two words are attached to the two directions in different ways by different authors, so the arrows are safer.
For a list, the parts are labelled $(1) \Rightarrow (2)$, $(2) \Rightarrow (3)$ and so on.
In Lean, the tactic `constructor` splits a goal `P ↔ Q` into `P → Q` and `Q → P`; Mathlib defines `List.TFAE` for lists of equivalent statements, with the tactics `tfae_have` and `tfae_finish`.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. [[An integer is even if and only if its square is even|T7C1TEZBRD]]: the direction $(\Rightarrow)$ is a direct proof, and $(\Leftarrow)$ is a proof by contrapositive.
2. [[Three equivalent conditions for a subset|1JKK56DKWW]]: $A \subseteq B$, $A \cap B = A$ and $A \cup B = B$ are proved equivalent by a cycle of three implications.
3. For real $x$, $x^2 = 1$ if and only if $x = 1$ or $x = -1$. Here a chain of equivalences works: $x^2 = 1 \iff (x - 1)(x + 1) = 0 \iff x - 1 = 0 \text{ or } x + 1 = 0$, and each step holds in both directions.
4. A square matrix $A$ is [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] if and only if $\det A \neq 0$. This is one entry of the long TFAE list of the invertible matrix theorem.
5. Two definitions of a prime number, by divisors and by [[Euclid's lemma|051VYKA1EX]], are equivalent for positive integers; see [[two definitions of a prime number|787PD6B171]].

### Non-examples

1. [[Two proofs of the same direction do not prove a biconditional|K08FN2E081]]: a direct proof of $P \Rightarrow Q$ and a proof of its contrapositive $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ prove the same implication twice.
2. Proving $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow R$ does not prove that $P$, $Q$ and $R$ are equivalent. The chain must return to $P$, for example by $R \Rightarrow P$.
3. A chain of steps "$\iff$" in which one step holds in one direction only. From $x = 2$ one gets $x^2 = 4$, but $x^2 = 4$ does not give $x = 2$, so writing $x = 2 \iff x^2 = 4$ is false.

### Basic properties

1. $P \iff Q$ is logically equivalent to $(P \Rightarrow Q) \land (Q \Rightarrow P)$, and also to $(P \Rightarrow Q) \land (\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q)$. So the second direction can be proved in the form "if not $P$, then not $Q$".
2. Logical equivalence is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. Transitivity is the reason why a cycle of implications proves all equivalences in a list.
3. For $n$ statements, a cycle needs $n$ implications. Any set of proved implications works if, in the directed graph whose arrows are the implications, each statement can reach each other statement.
4. A chain $P \iff P_1 \iff \cdots \iff Q$ of equivalences proves $P \iff Q$, but only if each step is checked in both directions.

### History

The abbreviation "iff" for "if and only if" is often credited to Paul Halmos, and it is now standard in mathematical writing.
The rule of biconditional introduction is part of the natural deduction calculi that go back to Gerhard Gentzen's work of 1934.

### Common errors

1. Proving one direction and calling the result an equivalence. Each direction must be labelled and proved.
2. Proving the same direction twice in two forms, as in non-example 1.
3. Using "$\iff$" between lines that are linked only by "$\Rightarrow$". A step such as squaring, multiplying by a number that may be $0$, or dividing by a variable often holds in one direction only.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"$P$ if and only if $Q$" makes two promises.
The first promise: whenever $P$ is true, $Q$ is true.
The second promise: whenever $Q$ is true, $P$ is true.
For example, "a whole number is even if and only if its last digit is $0$, $2$, $4$, $6$ or $8$".
A proof must check both promises: every even number ends in such a digit, and every number that ends in such a digit is even.
Checking only one promise proves only half of the sentence.

### L2 human

We prove: for real $a \geq 0$ and $b \geq 0$, $a \leq b$ if and only if $a^2 \leq b^2$.

1. $(\Rightarrow)$ Assume $a \leq b$. Multiply by $a \geq 0$: $a^2 \leq ab$. Multiply $a \leq b$ by $b \geq 0$: $ab \leq b^2$. So $a^2 \leq b^2$.
2. $(\Leftarrow)$ We prove the contrapositive: if $a > b$, then $a^2 > b^2$.
3. Assume $a > b$. Then $a > b \geq 0$, so $a > 0$.
4. Multiply $a > b$ by $a > 0$: $a^2 > ab$. Multiply $a > b$ by $b \geq 0$: $ab \geq b^2$. So $a^2 > b^2$.
5. Both directions are proved, so the equivalence holds.

The typical use: label each direction, and choose a separate method for each one.

A trap: the hypotheses $a \geq 0$ and $b \geq 0$ are needed in both directions.
For $a = -3$ and $b = 1$, we have $a \leq b$ but $a^2 = 9 > 1 = b^2$.

### L3 human

In logic, $P \iff Q$ is the conjunction of two implications, and a proof of a TFAE list is a proof that a directed graph of implications is strongly connected.
In Lean's Mathlib, `List.TFAE` packages this, and `tfae_finish` computes the strongly connected closure of the proved implications.
Equivalence of statements is the propositional shadow of isomorphism: in homotopy type theory, the univalence axiom identifies equivalent types, and for propositions it says that logically equivalent propositions are equal.
Characterization theorems, such as the many equivalent forms of compactness or of the [[axiom of choice|0HTGB13FE2]], are the main use of the method, and the topic [[definitions in mathematics|BP0WAF23HB]] explains why a field keeps several equivalent definitions of one notion.
The special case of two inclusions between sets, or two inequalities between numbers, is treated in the topic [[proof patterns for common goals|MEKKHHQTKB]].
