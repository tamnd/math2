## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $x_1, \dots, x_n \in K$.
Let $V$ be the $n \times n$ [[Vandermonde matrix|Q3JWBZ7EFY]] with entries $V_{ij} = x_i^{\,j-1}$.
Then
$$\det V = \prod_{1 \le i < j \le n} (x_j - x_i),$$
where $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The ring must be commutative, so that the determinant is defined.
The criterion "$\det V \ne 0$ if and only if the $x_i$ are distinct" needs a ring without zero divisors.
Over $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, the distinct points $0, 2, 3$ give $\det V = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 = 6 = 0$.

### Consequences

- Some books use the transpose, with $x_j^{\,i-1}$ in position $(i, j)$; the determinant is the same, by the [[transpose rule|0ZZQ2E1MTE]].
- Over a field, or over an integral domain, $\det V \ne 0$ if and only if the $x_i$ are pairwise distinct.
- [[Polynomial interpolation|EKDSDN89FR]]: if the $x_i$ are distinct points of a field, then for all $y_1, \dots, y_n$ there is exactly one polynomial $p$ of degree less than $n$ with $p(x_i) = y_i$.
- The reason: the coefficients $c$ of $p$ solve $Vc = y$, and $V$ is invertible.
- [[Lagrange interpolation|QGQTYT6YZR]] writes this polynomial down without solving the system.

### Converse

Over a field, the converse direction also holds: if $\det V \ne 0$, then the $x_i$ are distinct, because two equal points give two equal rows.
There are two proofs of the formula: [[by column operations|TJP9GSDM1S]] and [[by roots of polynomials|R653HXJ20N]].

### Generalizations

The square of $\det V$ is the discriminant of $\prod_i (t - x_i)$.
Dividing a determinant with rows $(x_i^{\lambda_1 + n - 1}, \dots, x_i^{\lambda_n})$ by $\det V$ gives a Schur polynomial; this belongs to combinatorics.

## History

The matrix is named after Alexandre-Théophile Vandermonde.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A Vandermonde matrix lists the powers $1, x, x^2, \dots$ of some numbers, one row for each number.
Its determinant is the product of all differences of the numbers.
So it is nonzero exactly when the numbers are distinct.

### L2 human

Take $x = (1, 2, 4)$, so $V = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 4 & 16 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. The formula gives $(2 - 1)(4 - 1)(4 - 2) = 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 6$.
2. Check by the [[rule of Sarrus|7H2P40X0MV]]: $32 + 4 + 4 - 2 - 16 - 16 = 6$.

For interpolation, the parabola through $(1, 1)$, $(2, 3)$ and $(4, 13)$ has coefficients $c$ with $Vc = (1, 3, 13)$.
The solution is $c = (1, -1, 1)$, so $p(t) = 1 - t + t^2$; check $p(2) = 3$ and $p(4) = 13$.
A trap: the order in each factor matters; $\prod_{i<j} (x_i - x_j)$ differs from the formula by the sign $(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}$.

### L3 human

$\det V$ is the basic alternating polynomial: every alternating polynomial in $x_1, \dots, x_n$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ is $\det V$ times a symmetric polynomial.
