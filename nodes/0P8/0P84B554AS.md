## Statement

The matrix of powers of $n$ numbers has a determinant equal to the product of all differences of the numbers, so it is nonzero exactly when the numbers are distinct.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $x_1, \dots, x_n \in K$.
Let $V$ be the $n \times n$ [[Vandermonde matrix|Q3JWBZ7EFY]] with entries $V_{ij} = x_i^{\,j-1}$, so that row $i$ is $(1, x_i, x_i^2, \dots, x_i^{n-1})$.
Then
$$\det V = \prod_{1 \le i < j \le n} (x_j - x_i),$$
where $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].
For $n = 1$ the product is empty, and both sides are $1$.

## Remarks

### Small cases

For $n = 2$ the formula is the [[determinant of a 2 × 2 matrix|0ZN2J42SK7]]:
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_1 \\ 1 & x_2 \end{pmatrix} = x_2 - x_1.$$
For $n = 3$ it has three factors, one for each pair of points.
Take $x = (1, 2, 4)$:
$$V = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 4 & 16 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det V = (2 - 1)(4 - 1)(4 - 2) = 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 6.$$
Check by the [[rule of Sarrus|7H2P40X0MV]], with the three products down to the right first:
$$\det V = 32 + 4 + 4 - 2 - 16 - 16 = 6.$$
For general $n$ there are $n(n-1)/2$ factors, one for each pair $i < j$.

### Two proofs

The [[proof by column operations|TJP9GSDM1S]] subtracts $x_1$ times each column from the next one.
This clears the first row except for its $1$, and it leaves a factor $x_i - x_1$ in each other row and a smaller Vandermonde matrix.
The [[proof by roots of polynomials|R653HXJ20N]] treats $\det V$ as a polynomial in $x_n$ of degree $n - 1$.
It vanishes at $x_n = x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}$, since then two rows are equal, and so it is a multiple of $\prod_{i<n} (x_n - x_i)$.

### When the determinant is zero

Over a field, or more generally over an integral domain, $\det V \ne 0$ if and only if the $x_i$ are pairwise distinct.
If $x_i = x_j$ for some $i \ne j$, then rows $i$ and $j$ are equal, and a factor of the product is $0$.
If the $x_i$ are distinct, every factor is nonzero, and a product of nonzero elements of an integral domain is nonzero.

Watch out: over a ring with zero divisors, distinct points can still give $\det V = 0$.
Over $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, the distinct points $0, 2, 3$ give
$$\det V = (2 - 0)(3 - 0)(3 - 2) = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 = 6 = 0.$$

### What it gives you: interpolation

Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ be distinct points of a field, and let $y_1, \dots, y_n$ be any values.
A polynomial $p(t) = c_0 + c_1 t + \dots + c_{n-1} t^{n-1}$ satisfies $p(x_i) = y_i$ for all $i$ exactly when $Vc = y$.
Since $\det V \ne 0$, this system has exactly one solution.
So there is exactly one polynomial of degree less than $n$ through the $n$ given points, which is the basic theorem of [[polynomial interpolation|EKDSDN89FR]].

For example, the parabola through $(1, 1)$, $(2, 3)$ and $(4, 13)$ has coefficients $c$ with $Vc = (1, 3, 13)$, where $V$ is the matrix above.
The solution is $c = (1, -1, 1)$, so $p(t) = 1 - t + t^2$.
Check: $p(1) = 1$, $p(2) = 1 - 2 + 4 = 3$ and $p(4) = 1 - 4 + 16 = 13$.
[[Lagrange interpolation|QGQTYT6YZR]] writes the same polynomial down without solving the system.

### Watch out for the order and the transpose

The order inside each factor decides the sign.
The product $\prod_{i<j} (x_i - x_j)$ differs from $\prod_{i<j} (x_j - x_i)$ by $(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}$, so for $n = 3$ it is $-6$ in the example above.
Some books also put the powers in the columns, with $x_j^{\,i-1}$ in position $(i, j)$.
That matrix is $V^{\mathsf{T}}$, and it has the same determinant by the [[transpose rule|0ZZQ2E1MTE]].
Books that write the powers in decreasing order, $x_i^{\,n-j}$, reverse the columns, and their determinant is $\prod_{i<j} (x_i - x_j)$.

### Try it

Compute the Vandermonde determinant of the points $0, 1, 2, 3$.
The six differences are $1, 2, 3$ (from $0$), $1, 2$ (from $1$) and $1$ (from $2$), so the determinant is $1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 12$.

For which $t$ is the Vandermonde matrix of $1, 2, t$ over $\mathbb{R}$ singular?
Its determinant is $(2 - 1)(t - 1)(t - 2)$, so it is singular exactly for $t = 1$ and $t = 2$.

## History

The matrix and its determinant are named after Alexandre-Théophile Vandermonde.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A Vandermonde matrix lists the powers $1, x, x^2, \dots$ of some numbers, one row for each number.
Its determinant is the product of all the differences between the numbers, each later number minus each earlier one.
For the numbers $1, 2, 4$ the differences are $1$, $3$ and $2$, and the determinant is $6$.
So the determinant is zero exactly when two of the numbers are the same.
This is why one curve of the right degree passes through any set of points with different first coordinates.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: are the vectors $(1, 1, 1, 1)$, $(1, 2, 4, 8)$, $(1, 3, 9, 27)$, $(1, -1, 1, -1)$ a basis of $\mathbb{R}^4$?
They are the rows of the Vandermonde matrix of the points $1, 2, 3, -1$.
The determinant is the product over the six pairs:
$$(2 - 1)(3 - 1)(-1 - 1)(3 - 2)(-1 - 2)(-1 - 3) = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot (-2) \cdot 1 \cdot (-3) \cdot (-4) = -48.$$
It is nonzero, so the four vectors form a basis.
In fact you only need that the four points are distinct; the value $-48$ is a bonus.
The same reasoning shows that the functions $1, t, t^2, \dots, t^{n-1}$ are linearly independent as functions on any set with at least $n$ points.

### L3 human

The Vandermonde determinant is the basic alternating polynomial.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$, every polynomial in $x_1, \dots, x_n$ that changes sign under each swap of two variables is $\det V$ times a [[symmetric polynomial|0VHSHB9B6J]].
Its square $\prod_{i<j} (x_j - x_i)^2$ is the [[discriminant|0VVK64Q4JK]] of the monic polynomial with roots $x_1, \dots, x_n$.
Quotients of alternants by the Vandermonde determinant in decreasing powers define the [[Schur polynomials|43VFMJCDYJ]] of combinatorics.
Vandermonde matrices also appear as the matrix of the [[discrete Fourier transform|TST0GH66A5]], with the $n$-th roots of unity as points, and in the proof that [[eigenvectors with distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent|0HNMFG8F8K]].
