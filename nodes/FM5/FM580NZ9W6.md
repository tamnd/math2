## Statement

The coincidence lemma says that a formula only looks at the values of its own free variables and at the meanings of its own symbols.

Let $L$ be a signature, let $\mathcal{M}$ be an $L$-[[structure|983GZBQYT4]] with domain $M$, and let $s_1, s_2 \colon \mathrm{Var} \to M$ be assignments.

1. Let $t$ be an $L$-[[term|6D3WA7N2W4]].
   If $s_1(x) = s_2(x)$ for every variable $x$ that occurs in $t$, then $t^{\mathcal{M}}[s_1] = t^{\mathcal{M}}[s_2]$.
2. Let $\varphi$ be an $L$-[[formula|HYJ10AF6RT]].
   If $s_1(x) = s_2(x)$ for every free variable $x$ of $\varphi$, then $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi[s_1]$ if and only if $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi[s_2]$.
3. Let $L \subseteq L'$, let $\mathcal{M}'$ be an $L'$-structure, and let $\mathcal{M}$ be its reduct to $L$, with the same domain and the interpretations of the symbols of $L$ only.
   Then for every $L$-formula $\varphi$ and every assignment $s$, we have $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi[s]$ if and only if $\mathcal{M}' \models \varphi[s]$.

Here $\models$ is [[satisfaction|1WTCZB4DVB]].

## Remarks

### A first check

Take the structure $(\{0, 1, 2\}, <)$ and the formula $\varphi = \exists y \, (x < y)$.
Its only free variable is $x$, because the $y$ is bound by the quantifier.
Let $s_1$ send $x$ to $1$ and $y$ to $0$, and let $s_2$ send $x$ to $1$ and $y$ to $2$.
The two assignments agree at $x$, so part 2 predicts the same answer for both.

Under $s_1$, the clause for $\exists y$ asks for some $b$ in the domain with $1 < b$, and $b = 2$ works.
Under $s_2$, the clause asks exactly the same question, because it throws away the old value of $y$ and tries every $b$.
So both assignments satisfy $\varphi$.
The old value of $y$ never entered the computation, and that is the whole content of the lemma.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Agreement on the free variables cannot be dropped.
In $(\mathbb{N}, <, 1)$, with a constant symbol for $1$, the formula $x < 1$ is satisfied when $x$ has the value $0$ and not when $x$ has the value $5$.

Agreement on the bound variables is not needed, and the first check shows why.
The sentence $\exists x \, (x < 1)$ is satisfied by both of the assignments above, although they differ at $x$.

In part 3, the two structures must give the same meaning to the symbols of $\varphi$.
Let $\mathcal{M}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ both have domain $\{0, 1\}$, with $P^{\mathcal{M}} = \{0\}$ and $P^{\mathcal{N}} = \{1\}$.
The assignment $x \mapsto 0$ satisfies $P(x)$ in $\mathcal{M}$ and not in $\mathcal{N}$, so a change in a used symbol changes the answer.

### What it gives you

For a [[sentence|DKFGQTRYZE]] $\sigma$ there are no free variables, so all assignments give the same answer.
This is why a sentence is simply [[true or false|K7XDVK5854]] in $\mathcal{M}$, with no assignment in sight.

If the free variables of $\varphi$ are among $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, then whether $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi[s]$ depends only on the values $a_i = s(x_i)$.
That justifies the short notation $\mathcal{M} \models \varphi[a_1, \ldots, a_n]$, and it lets us read $\varphi$ as a description of a set of $n$-tuples.
The page on [[definable relations|1DQ1M6ZWS4]] builds on this reading.

Part 3 says that adding new symbols to a language never changes the truth of old sentences.
So truth and [[validity|5SDS9T8E7J]] of a sentence do not depend on the larger signature in which we happen to read it.

### The converse

The converse of part 2 is false.
In $(\mathbb{Z}, <)$, the assignments $x \mapsto 0$ and $x \mapsto 1$ satisfy exactly the same formulas whose only free variable is $x$.
The reason is that the shift $n \mapsto n + 1$ is an automorphism, so [[isomorphism invariance of truth|NDTJ2E895C]] applies.
Yet the two assignments differ at the free variable $x$.

### Watch out

A variable can occur both free and bound in one formula.
In $P(x) \wedge \exists x \, Q(x)$, the first $x$ is free, so the value of $x$ matters for the formula as a whole.
The test is "is $x$ a free variable of $\varphi$", not "is there a quantifier on $x$ somewhere in $\varphi$".

A second slip is to apply part 2 to variables that merely occur in $\varphi$.
The lemma asks for less: agreement on the free variables is enough, even when the assignments differ on bound ones.

### Generalizations

The same lemma, with the same proof, holds for many-sorted logic, for second-order logic with assignments to the second-order variables, and for infinitary logics.

### Try it

In $(\mathbb{Z}, +, 0)$, does $\forall y \, (x + y = y)$ hold under an assignment with $x \mapsto 0$ and $y \mapsto 7$?
Yes: only $x$ is free, and $0 + b = b$ for every integer $b$, so the value $7$ of $y$ plays no role.

Which assignments must agree for $x < y \wedge \forall y \, (y < z)$ to get the same answer?
They must agree at $x$, $y$ and $z$, because the first $y$ is free even though the second one is bound.

## History

Tarski's 1933 definition of truth uses satisfaction by infinite sequences of objects rather than assignments.
He noted that satisfaction by a sequence depends only on the members of the sequence that belong to the free variables of the formula.
A sentence is then satisfied by every sequence or by none, and Tarski called a sentence true when every sequence satisfies it.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Whether a statement with blanks holds depends only on what you put in its blanks.
Take "$x$ is less than some number" about the numbers $0$, $1$ and $2$.
The letter $x$ is a blank, but the "some number" is not, because the statement itself searches for it.
So if you put $1$ in the blank, the answer is yes, whatever you were thinking for the other letter.
A complete sentence has no blanks, so it has one fixed answer.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: we show that "$x$ is even" in $(\mathbb{N}, +)$ gives a well-defined set.
Write $\varphi(x) = \exists y \, (y + y = x)$, whose only free variable is $x$.
Two assignments that both send $x$ to $6$ may disagree at $y$, say with $y \mapsto 0$ and $y \mapsto 9$.
By part 2 they give the same answer, and the clause for $\exists y$ finds the witness $3$:
$$3 + 3 = 6.$$
So the answer at $6$ is "yes", fixed by the value $6$ alone, and the formula defines a set of numbers.
Without the lemma we could not speak of "the set defined by $\varphi$" at all.
Watch out for formulas where $y$ is also free, such as $y + y = x \wedge \exists y \, (y = y)$; there the value of $y$ matters.

### L3 human

The lemma says that the satisfaction relation factors through the restriction of the assignment to the free variables.
So the meaning of $\varphi(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ in $\mathcal{M}$ is a subset of $M^n$, the set that $\varphi$ defines.
Part 3 says that this meaning is also stable under reducts and expansions, so the semantics is compositional in the signature.
In Mathlib, bound variables are de Bruijn indices, and the matching facts are lemmas about `BoundedFormula.Realize` under changes of the map that interprets the free variables.
The [[substitution lemma|A7S460KHBM]] and [[isomorphism invariance|NDTJ2E895C]] are proved with the same induction, and both use this lemma in their quantifier steps.
