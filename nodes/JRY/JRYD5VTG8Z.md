## Example

Let $F$ be a field and $A = \operatorname{diag}(0, 1)$.
The cyclic subspaces $F e_1$ and $F e_2$ give $F^2 = Z(e_1) \oplus Z(e_2)$ with annihilators $x$ and $x - 1$.
We show that $x, x - 1$ is not the list of [[invariant factors|F7GJPMVBXA]] of $A$.

1. Divisibility fails: $x - 1 = 1 \cdot x - 1$, so the remainder of $x - 1$ on division by $x$ is $-1 \ne 0$. So $x \nmid x - 1$, and also $x - 1 \nmid x$.
2. Let $v = e_1 + e_2 = (1, 1)$. Then $Av = (0, 1)$, and $v, Av$ is a basis since $\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1$.
3. So $F^2 = Z(v)$, with annihilator $x(x - 1) = x^2 - x$.
4. By the [[cyclic decomposition theorem|R3Z20J19BR]], the invariant factors of $A$ are the single polynomial $x^2 - x$.
5. The polynomials $x$ and $x - 1$ are the elementary divisors of $A$, not its invariant factors.

## Remarks

The example shows that a decomposition into cyclic subspaces gives the invariant factors only when the annihilators divide each other.
Coprime annihilators can always be merged, as in the [[lemma on a vector of maximal annihilator|QPE5Y86Y8V]].
For $\operatorname{diag}(0, 0)$ instead, the list $x, x$ is the list of invariant factors, since $x \mid x$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix keeps the first axis fixed at zero and the second axis fixed at one.
Cutting the plane into the two axes gives two pieces, but their polynomials, $x$ and $x$ minus one, do not divide each other.
The rule of invariant factors requires each polynomial to divide the next.
So we glue the two pieces into one, generated by the point one, one, with the polynomial $x$ times $x$ minus one.

### L2 human

1. List the elementary divisors: $x$ and $x - 1$.
2. Each prime appears once, so the last invariant factor takes both: $d_1 = x(x - 1)$.
3. No prime is left, so there is no other invariant factor.

The typical use is to convert elementary divisors into invariant factors.
The trap is to read the diagonal entries of a diagonal matrix as its invariant factors.
