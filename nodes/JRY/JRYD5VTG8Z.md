## Example

Let $F$ be a field and $A = \operatorname{diag}(0, 1)$.
The two axes are cyclic subspaces, and they give $F^2 = Z(e_1) \oplus Z(e_2)$ with the annihilators $x$ and $x - 1$.
We show that the list $x, x - 1$ is nevertheless not the list of [[invariant factors|F7GJPMVBXA]] of $A$, and we find the correct list.

1. The list fails the divisibility condition.
   Division with remainder gives $x - 1 = 1 \cdot x + (-1)$, and the remainder $-1$ is not zero, so $x \nmid x - 1$.
   Both polynomials have degree $1$ and they are different monic polynomials, so $x - 1 \nmid x$ either.
2. Let $v = e_1 + e_2 = (1, 1)$, so that $A v = (0, 1)$.
   The two vectors form a basis, since
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1 \ne 0.$$
3. So $F^2 = Z(v)$ is a single cyclic subspace.
   Its annihilator has degree $2$, and $A(A - I) = 0$ because $A$ is diagonal with entries $0$ and $1$, so the annihilator is $x(x - 1) = x^2 - x$.
4. By the [[cyclic decomposition theorem|R3Z20J19BR]], the list of invariant factors of $A$ is the single polynomial $x^2 - x$.
5. The polynomials $x$ and $x - 1$ are the elementary divisors of $A$, not its invariant factors.

## Remarks

The example shows that a decomposition into cyclic subspaces gives the invariant factors only when the annihilators divide each other.
Pieces with coprime annihilators can always be merged into one cyclic piece, as in the proof of the [[lemma on a vector of maximal annihilator|QPE5Y86Y8V]].
Here the merge turns the two axes into the single cyclic subspace generated by $(1, 1)$.

If one diagonal entry changes, the answer changes with it.
For $\operatorname{diag}(0, 0)$ the list $x, x$ is the list of invariant factors, since $x \mid x$ and the zero matrix has no cyclic vector.
For $\operatorname{diag}(0, 2)$ the same merge works, and the single invariant factor is $x(x - 2)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix squashes the first axis to zero and keeps the second axis fixed.
Cutting the plane into the two axes gives two pieces, but their polynomials, $x$ and "$x$ minus one", do not divide each other.
The rule for invariant factors asks each polynomial to divide the next.
So we glue the two pieces into one, generated by the point $(1, 1)$, and its polynomial is $x$ times "$x$ minus one".

### L2 human

The elementary divisors give the same answer by the sorting rule.
List them: $x$ and $x - 1$.
Each prime occurs once, so the last invariant factor takes both, $d_1 = x(x - 1)$.
No prime has a second power left, so there is no other invariant factor.
Check: the degree of $d_1$ is $2$, the size of the matrix.

The typical use is the conversion of elementary divisors into invariant factors.
The trap is to read the diagonal entries of a diagonal matrix as its invariant factors.
That works only for a scalar matrix $cI$, where all the entries agree.
