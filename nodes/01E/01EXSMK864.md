## Statement

Multiplying a whole $n \times n$ matrix by $c$ multiplies its determinant by $c^n$, not by $c$.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$, and let $c \in K$.
Then
$$\det(cA) = c^n \det A,$$
where $cA$ is the [[scalar multiple|09V6DDACXR]] and $\det$ is the [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]].

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, with $\det A = 4 - 6 = -2$, and $c = 3$:
$$\det(3A) = \det \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 9 & 12 \end{pmatrix} = 36 - 54 = -18 = 3^2 \cdot (-2).$$
Each of the two rows carries one factor $3$, so the determinant gets the factor $9$.

### Why it holds

The matrix $cA$ is $A$ with each of its $n$ rows multiplied by $c$.
By the rule for [[scaling a row|009CY8JXFX]], each row brings out one factor $c$, so $n$ rows bring out $c^n$.
The [[proof|NFCBC71T08]] does this by induction on the number of scaled rows.

### What it gives you

With $c = -1$ the rule gives $\det(-A) = (-1)^n \det A$.
So for odd $n$, the determinant changes sign when every entry changes sign.
This leads to the fact that a real [[skew-symmetric matrix|0PBY4E9GQF]] of odd size is singular: from $A^{\mathsf{T}} = -A$ we get $\det A = \det A^{\mathsf{T}} = \det(-A) = -\det A$, so $\det A = 0$.
The theorem on [[skew-symmetric matrices of odd size|55RX9C0ZTH]] treats this in full.
The rule also shows that $\det$ is not a linear map on matrices when $n \ge 2$, as the [[counterexample on sums|D5VBC7621G]] explains.

### Watch out

The wrong rule $\det(cA) = c \det A$ holds only for $n = 1$.
For $A = I_3$ and $c = 2$ it would give $2$, while $2 I_3$ is diagonal with determinant $8$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a matrix as a recipe that turns a cube into a slanted box, and of the determinant as the factor by which volume grows.
Scaling the matrix by $c$ stretches every edge of the box by $c$.
In the plane this multiplies area by $c^2$, and in space it multiplies volume by $c^3$.
So doubling a $3 \times 3$ matrix multiplies its determinant by $8$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: a $4 \times 4$ matrix $A$ has $\det A = 5$; find $\det(-2A)$ and $\det(\tfrac{1}{2} A)$.
The rule with $n = 4$ gives
$$\det(-2A) = (-2)^4 \cdot 5 = 80, \qquad \det\big(\tfrac{1}{2} A\big) = \big(\tfrac{1}{2}\big)^4 \cdot 5 = \tfrac{5}{16}.$$
Notice that the sign of $c$ does not matter when $n$ is even.
The rule is often used together with [[multiplicativity|04CPJ6AF00]], for example to see that $\det(c A^2) = c^n (\det A)^2$.
