## Definition

Let $\mathbb{K}$ be $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.
An *inner product space* over $\mathbb{K}$ is a [[vector space|05EGHEQZAW]] $V$ over $\mathbb{K}$ together with an [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot} \colon V \times V \to \mathbb{K}$.
For $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ it is a *real inner product space*, and for $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$ it is a *complex inner product space*.
A real inner product space of finite dimension is a *Euclidean space*, and a complex one of finite dimension is a *unitary space*.

## Notation

- We write $V$ alone when the inner product is fixed.
- When two inner products on the same space appear, we write $(V, \ip{\cdot}{\cdot}_1)$ and $(V, \ip{\cdot}{\cdot}_2)$.
- Another name is pre-Hilbert space.

## Remarks

- The Mathlib class `InnerProductSpace 𝕜 E` is a variant of this definition. It starts from a normed space `E` and asks for an inner product whose real part on the diagonal is $\norm{x}^2$.
- Mathlib's `inner` is conjugate-linear in the first argument and linear in the second, the opposite of the convention here.
- Each inner product space has the [[norm induced by its inner product|0MFJJV97RJ]], $\norm{x} = \sqrt{\ip{x}{x}}$.
- An inner product space that is complete for this norm is a [[Hilbert space|HM2HHE3J51]]. Every inner product space of finite dimension is complete.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An inner product space is a vector space where we can measure lengths and angles.
The space $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product is the main example.
In it, the vector $(1, 1)$ has length $\sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt{2}$.
Much of plane geometry, such as the theorem of Pythagoras, works in every inner product space.

### L2 human

Worked example: a subspace inherits the structure.

1. Let $V = \mathbb{R}^3$ with the dot product, and let $U = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0\}$.
2. For $x, y \in U$, the dot product $x \cdot y$ is defined, and it satisfies the three axioms, because they hold for all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
3. So $U$ is a real inner product space of dimension $2$.
4. The vectors $(1, -1, 0)$ and $(1, 1, -2)$ lie in $U$, and their dot product is $1 - 1 + 0 = 0$.

The typical use is to work in a subspace, for example a plane of solutions, with the geometry of the big space.
A trap: the pair $(V, \ip{\cdot}{\cdot})$ is the inner product space, not $V$ alone.
So a change of the inner product gives a different inner product space.
