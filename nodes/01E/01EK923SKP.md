## Definition

An inner product space is a vector space together with a chosen inner product.
Let $\mathbb{K}$ be $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.
An *inner product space* over $\mathbb{K}$ is a pair $(V, \ip{\cdot}{\cdot})$ of a [[vector space|05EGHEQZAW]] $V$ over $\mathbb{K}$ and an [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot} \colon V \times V \to \mathbb{K}$.
For $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ it is a *real inner product space*, and for $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$ it is a *complex inner product space*.
A real inner product space of finite dimension is a *Euclidean space*, and a complex one of finite dimension is a *unitary space*.

## Notation

We write $V$ alone when the inner product is fixed.
When two inner products on the same space appear, we write $(V, \ip{\cdot}{\cdot}_1)$ and $(V, \ip{\cdot}{\cdot}_2)$.
Another name is *pre-Hilbert space*.

## Remarks

The definition has two parts, and both matter.
The vector space gives sums and multiples, and the inner product gives lengths and angles, so a change of the inner product gives a different inner product space on the same set.
For example, $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product and $\mathbb{R}^2$ with $2x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$ are two inner product spaces.
A pair that fails the definition is $\mathbb{C}^2$ with $x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$, because that form gives $0$ on $(1, i)$ and so is not an inner product.

Each inner product space has the [[norm induced by its inner product|0MFJJV97RJ]], $\norm{x} = \sqrt{\ip{x}{x}}$.
A space that is complete for this norm is a [[Hilbert space|HM2HHE3J51]], and every inner product space of finite dimension is complete.
The Mathlib class `InnerProductSpace 𝕜 E` is a variant of the definition: it starts from a normed space `E` and asks for an inner product with $\operatorname{Re}\langle x, x \rangle = \norm{x}^2$.
Its `inner` is conjugate-linear in the first slot and linear in the second, the opposite of the convention of math2.
The concept page on [[inner product spaces|0QVNCJXRY3]] gives the main examples.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An inner product space is a vector space where we can measure lengths and angles.
The space $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product is the main example.
In it, the vector $(1, 1)$ has length $\sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt{2}$.
Much of plane geometry, such as the theorem of Pythagoras, works in every inner product space.

### L2 human

A subspace inherits the structure.
Let $V = \mathbb{R}^3$ with the dot product, and let $U = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0\}$.
For $x, y \in U$ the dot product $x \cdot y$ is defined, and the three axioms hold because they hold on all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
So $U$ is a real inner product space of dimension $2$.
The vectors $(1, -1, 0)$ and $(1, 1, -2)$ lie in $U$, and
$$(1, -1, 0) \cdot (1, 1, -2) = 1 - 1 + 0 = 0,$$
so they are perpendicular in $U$.
This is how we work in a plane of solutions with the geometry of the big space.
Watch out: the pair $(V, \ip{\cdot}{\cdot})$ is the inner product space, not $V$ alone.
