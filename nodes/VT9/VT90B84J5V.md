## Example

Let $V = \mathbb{C}$, a complex inner product space of dimension $1$, with $\ip{z}{w} = z\overline{w}$, the [[standard inner product|TFZ8DYVJ1J]] for $n = 1$.
Let $x = 1$ and $y = i$.

1. The squared norms are $\norm{x}^2 = 1 \cdot \overline{1} = 1$ and $\norm{y}^2 = i \cdot \overline{i} = i \cdot (-i) = 1$.
2. The sum is $x + y = 1 + i$, and
$$\norm{x + y}^2 = (1 + i)\overline{(1 + i)} = (1 + i)(1 - i) = 2.$$
3. So $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + \norm{y}^2$, and the Pythagorean identity holds.
4. The inner product is $\ip{x}{y} = 1 \cdot \overline{i} = -i \neq 0$, so $x$ and $y$ are not [[orthogonal|0TS7VZJBWD]].
5. Its real part is $\operatorname{Re}(-i) = 0$.
   This explains step 3, since $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} + \norm{y}^2$ for all $x$ and $y$.

So the converse of the [[Pythagorean theorem|0BVTDM872V]] fails over $\mathbb{C}$.

## Remarks

Over $\mathbb{C}$ the Pythagorean identity detects only $\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} = 0$.
The pair $1$ and $i$ is in fact as far from orthogonal as a pair can be.
Since $y = ix$, the two vectors are linearly dependent, and $\abs{\ip{x}{y}} = 1 = \norm{x}\,\norm{y}$ is the largest value that the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality allows.

Over $\mathbb{R}$ the converse holds, because there $\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} = \ip{x}{y}$.
Over $\mathbb{C}$ a second test repairs it: $x \perp y$ exactly when both $\norm{x + y}^2$ and $\norm{x + iy}^2$ equal $\norm{x}^2 + \norm{y}^2$.
For $x = 1$ and $y = i$ the second test fails, since
$$\norm{1 + i \cdot i}^2 = \abs{0}^2 = 0 \neq 2.$$
If you change $y$ to $y = i\lambda$ for any real $\lambda \neq 0$, the same thing happens: the first test passes and the inner product $-i\lambda$ is not $0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In the plane, a triangle with sides $a$, $b$ and $c$ with $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ has a right angle.
For complex vectors this test is not enough.
The numbers $1$ and $i$ have length $1$, and $1 + i$ has squared length $2 = 1 + 1$.
But $i$ is $1$ multiplied by the scalar $i$, so the two vectors lie on one complex line and do not make a right angle at all.

### L2 human

Here is the full test for orthogonality in $\mathbb{C}^2$ on the pair $x = (1, 0)$ and $y = (i, 0)$, where $\norm{x}^2 = \norm{y}^2 = 1$.
The first test passes:
$$\norm{x + y}^2 = \abs{1 + i}^2 = 2 = 1 + 1.$$
The second test fails:
$$\norm{x + iy}^2 = \abs{1 + i \cdot i}^2 = 0 \neq 2.$$
So $x$ and $y$ are not orthogonal, and indeed $\ip{x}{y} = 1 \cdot \overline{i} = -i$.
The example is a warning for any proof over $\mathbb{R}$ that you copy to $\mathbb{C}$.
The real part of the inner product controls the length of $x + y$, while the imaginary part controls the length of $x + iy$, and orthogonality needs both to vanish.
