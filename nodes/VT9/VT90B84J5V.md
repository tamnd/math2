## Example

Let $V = \mathbb{C}$, a complex inner product space of dimension $1$, with $\ip{z}{w} = z\overline{w}$, the [[standard inner product|TFZ8DYVJ1J]] for $n = 1$.
Let $x = 1$ and $y = i$.

1. The norms are $\norm{x}^2 = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$ and $\norm{y}^2 = i \cdot \overline{i} = i \cdot (-i) = 1$.
2. The sum is $x + y = 1 + i$, with $\norm{x + y}^2 = (1 + i)(1 - i) = 2$.
3. So $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + \norm{y}^2$: the Pythagorean identity holds.
4. The inner product is $\ip{x}{y} = 1 \cdot \overline{i} = -i \neq 0$. So $x$ and $y$ are not [[orthogonal|0TS7VZJBWD]].
5. Its real part is $\operatorname{Re}(-i) = 0$. This explains step 3, since $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} + \norm{y}^2$.

So the converse of the [[Pythagorean theorem|0BVTDM872V]] fails over $\mathbb{C}$.

## Remarks

The example shows that over $\mathbb{C}$ the Pythagorean identity detects only $\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} = 0$.
In fact $x$ and $y$ are linearly dependent, since $y = i x$, so they are as far from orthogonal as possible.
Over $\mathbb{R}$ the converse holds, because there $\operatorname{Re}\ip{x}{y} = \ip{x}{y}$.
Over $\mathbb{C}$, two vectors are orthogonal exactly when both $\norm{x + y}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + \norm{y}^2$ and $\norm{x + iy}^2 = \norm{x}^2 + \norm{y}^2$ hold.
For $x = 1$ and $y = i$, the second test fails: $\norm{1 + i \cdot i}^2 = 0 \neq 2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In the plane, a triangle with sides $a$, $b$ and $c$ and $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ has a right angle.
For complex vectors this test is not enough.
The numbers $1$ and $i$ have length $1$, and $1 + i$ has squared length $2 = 1 + 1$.
But $i$ is just $1$ multiplied by the number $i$, so the two vectors lie on one complex line.

### L2 human

Worked example: the full test for orthogonality in $\mathbb{C}^2$.

1. Let $x = (1, 0)$ and $y = (i, 0)$. Then $\norm{x}^2 = \norm{y}^2 = 1$.
2. First test: $\norm{x + y}^2 = |1 + i|^2 = 2 = 1 + 1$. It passes.
3. Second test: $\norm{x + iy}^2 = |1 + i \cdot i|^2 = 0 \neq 2$. It fails.
4. So $x$ and $y$ are not orthogonal, and indeed $\ip{x}{y} = 1 \cdot \overline{i} = -i$.

The typical use of this example is as a warning when a proof over $\mathbb{R}$ is copied to $\mathbb{C}$.
A trap: the real part of the inner product controls lengths of sums, but the imaginary part controls lengths of $x + iy$.
