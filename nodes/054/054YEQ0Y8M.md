## Summary

Let $V$ be a vector space over $\C$.
A sesquilinear form on $V$ is a map $h \colon V \times V \to \C$ that is linear in the first slot and conjugate-linear in the second slot: $h(au + u', v) = a\,h(u, v) + h(u', v)$ and $h(u, av + v') = \bar a\,h(u, v) + h(u, v')$.
In a basis $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ with the matrix $M_{ij} = h(e_i, e_j)$, the form is $h(x, y) = x^T M \bar y$, and a change of basis with the matrix $P$ replaces $M$ by $P^T M \bar P$.
The [[formal definition|0W44K2VNBD]] states the axioms.
Sesquilinear forms replace [[bilinear forms|0SQZMXB6GP]] over $\C$, because they allow positive values $h(v, v) > 0$, and so they give the complex [[inner products|0NB0AWGJBW]].

## Motivation

On $\C^n$ the bilinear form $\sum_i x_i y_i$ cannot measure length.
For $v = (1, i)$ it gives $1 + i^2 = 0$, and for $iv$ it gives $-1$ times the value at $v$.
The fix is to [[conjugate|0FTH2E9TAS]] one slot: $\sum_i x_i \bar y_i$ gives $\sum_i \abs{x_i}^2 \geq 0$ on the diagonal.
The price is that the form is no longer linear in the conjugated slot.
A sesquilinear form is a form with exactly this mixture: linear in one slot, conjugate-linear in the other.

## Notation

- We use the convention of most mathematics books: linear in the first slot, conjugate-linear in the second.
- Physics books, and Mathlib, use the other convention: conjugate-linear in the first slot, linear in the second. It comes from the bra-ket notation of Dirac, where $\langle x | y \rangle$ is conjugate-linear in the bra $\langle x |$.
- In the physics convention, the matrix formula is $h(x, y) = x^* A y$, with the [[conjugate transpose|0DXKE10PHF]] $x^* = \bar x^T$, and a change of basis replaces $A$ by $P^* A P$.
- The prefix "sesqui" means one and a half: the form is linear in one slot and "half" linear in the other.
- Over a field $F$ with an automorphism $\sigma$ of order $2$, a $\sigma$-sesquilinear form satisfies $h(u, av) = \sigma(a)\,h(u, v)$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The standard form $h(x, y) = \sum_i x_i \bar y_i$ on $\C^n$ is sesquilinear, with the identity matrix. It is the standard complex inner product.
- [[The form $x_1 \bar y_1 + 2 x_1 \bar y_2 + 3 x_2 \bar y_2$ on $\C^2$|HTRVMDMNH0]] is sesquilinear, with the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, but it is not Hermitian.
- [[The form on $\C^2$ with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ -i & 2 \end{pmatrix}$|8T68MSTVM7]] is a positive definite [[Hermitian form|48P4A1CX85]].
- For each $n \times n$ complex matrix $M$, $h(x, y) = x^T M \bar y$ is sesquilinear on $\C^n$.
- On the space of continuous functions $[0, 1] \to \C$, $h(f, g) = \int_0^1 f(t) \overline{g(t)}\,dt$ is sesquilinear.

### Non-examples

- [[The bilinear form $x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$ on $\C^2$|2A38WWPDES]] is not sesquilinear, because $B(x, iy) = i\,B(x, y)$ and not $-i\,B(x, y)$.
- The map $h(x, y) = \bar x_1 \bar y_1$ on $\C^2$ is not sesquilinear in our convention, because it is conjugate-linear in both slots.
- The map $h(x, y) = \abs{x_1 y_1}$ is not sesquilinear, because it is not additive: $h(e_1 - e_1, e_1) = 0$ but $h(e_1, e_1) + h(-e_1, e_1) = 2$.

### Basic properties

- $h(x, y) = \sum_{i, j} x_i M_{ij} \bar y_j = x^T M \bar y$, by linearity in $x$ and conjugate-linearity in $y$.
- If $P$ has the new basis vectors as its columns, then the new matrix is $P^T M \bar P$, since $h(b'_k, b'_l) = \sum_{i, j} P_{ik} M_{ij} \bar P_{jl}$.
- Two matrices $M, M'$ of the same form have the same rank, since $P$ and $\bar P$ are invertible. So the radical and nondegeneracy are defined as for [[bilinear forms|0QK6K857HS]].
- $\det M' = \det P \cdot \overline{\det P} \cdot \det M = \abs{\det P}^2 \det M$.
- The polarization identity recovers $h$ from its values $h(v, v)$: $4h(x, y) = \sum_{k = 0}^{3} i^k h(x + i^k y, x + i^k y)$.

### History

The name uses the Latin prefix "sesqui", one and a half.
Birkhoff and von Neumann showed in 1936 that the correlations of a Desarguesian projective geometry correspond to nondegenerate sesquilinear forms.
Reinhold Baer extended the notion to vector spaces over division rings.
The physics convention, with the first slot conjugate-linear, goes back to the bra-ket notation of Dirac.

### Common errors

- Mixing the two conventions. In our convention $h(x, y) = x^T M \bar y$ and $h(x, ay) = \bar a h(x, y)$. In the physics convention $h(x, y) = x^* A y$ and $h(ax, y) = \bar a h(x, y)$. Always check which slot a book conjugates.
- Using $P^T M P$ or $P^{-1} M P$ for a change of basis. The correct rule is $P^T M \bar P$ in our convention, and $P^* A P$ in the physics convention.
- Thinking that a sesquilinear form is bilinear over $\R$ and therefore "almost bilinear" over $\C$. It is bilinear over $\R$, but over $\C$ the conjugation changes all signs of $i$ in one slot.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Over the complex numbers, a plain bilinear form cannot give a length, because $i \cdot i = -1$.
A sesquilinear form conjugates the numbers in the second slot.
Then $h(v, v)$ can be a positive real number, as for the dot product $\sum_i x_i \bar y_i$.
Every complex inner product is a sesquilinear form.

### L2 human

We compute with $h(x, y) = x^T M \bar y$ on $\C^2$, $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. For $x = (1, 1)$ and $y = (i, 1)$: $\bar y = (-i, 1)$, $M \bar y = (-i + i, 2) = (0, 2)$, and $h(x, y) = 0 + 2 = 2$.
2. For $2i\,y = (-2, 2i)$: the conjugate is $(-2, -2i)$, so $M \overline{2i y} = (-2 + 2, -4i) = (0, -4i)$ and $h(x, 2iy) = -4i$.
3. This agrees with conjugate-linearity: $\overline{2i} \cdot h(x, y) = -2i \cdot 2 = -4i$.
4. In the basis $b'_1 = (1, 1)$, $b'_2 = (0, 1)$, with $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ real, the new matrix is $P^T M P = \begin{pmatrix} 3 + i & 2 + i \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
5. A check: $h(b'_1, b'_1) = 1 + i + 0 + 2 = 3 + i$.

The typical use is to compute inner products and Hermitian forms on $\C^n$ through their matrices.
A trap: with a real $P$, $\bar P = P$ and the rule looks like $P^T M P$; with a complex $P$ the conjugation matters.

### L3 human

A sesquilinear form on $V$ is the same as a linear map from $V$ to the space of conjugate-linear forms on $V$, or a bilinear form on $V \times \bar V$, where $\bar V$ is $V$ with the conjugated scalar multiplication.
Over a division ring with an anti-automorphism, sesquilinear forms give the classical groups: orthogonal, symplectic and unitary groups are the isometry groups of the reflexive forms.
