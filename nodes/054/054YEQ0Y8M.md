## Summary

A sesquilinear form is the complex version of a [[bilinear form|0SQZMXB6GP]]: it pairs two vectors, but it conjugates the scalars in one of the two slots.
That one conjugation lets the form give real, positive lengths on a complex space, so it is the shape of every complex [[inner product|0NB0AWGJBW]].
Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{C}$.
A *sesquilinear form* on $V$ is a map $h \colon V \times V \to \mathbb{C}$ that is linear in the first slot and conjugate-linear in the second slot:
$$h(au + u', v) = a\, h(u, v) + h(u', v), \qquad h(u, av + v') = \bar a\, h(u, v) + h(u, v'),$$
for all $u, u', v, v' \in V$ and $a \in \mathbb{C}$, where $\bar a$ is the [[complex conjugate|0FTH2E9TAS]] of $a$.
The [[formal definition|0W44K2VNBD]] states the same axioms.
In a basis $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$, the matrix $M$ with entries $M_{ij} = h(e_i, e_j)$ gives $h(x, y) = x^{\mathsf{T}} M \bar y$ for the coordinate columns $x$ and $y$.

## Motivation

Why not keep bilinear forms over $\mathbb{C}$?
The bilinear form $x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ gives $1 + i^2 = 0$ on the nonzero vector $v = (1, i)$, so it cannot measure length.
Worse, every bilinear form satisfies $B(iv, iv) = i^2 B(v, v) = -B(v, v)$, so no bilinear form on a complex space is positive on all nonzero vectors.
The fix is to conjugate one slot.
The form $x_1 \bar y_1 + x_2 \bar y_2$ gives $\lvert x_1 \rvert^2 + \lvert x_2 \rvert^2$ on the diagonal, and that is $2$ for $v = (1, i)$.
The price is that the form is no longer linear in the second slot, and a sesquilinear form is exactly a form with this mixture.

Sesquilinear forms are the setting of complex geometry and of quantum mechanics.
The state space of a quantum system is a complex inner product space, and transition amplitudes are values of a sesquilinear form.
The [[Hermitian forms|48P4A1CX85]] are the sesquilinear forms with $h(v, u) = \overline{h(u, v)}$, and they play the part that [[symmetric bilinear forms|0AXZNE5XNH]] play over $\mathbb{R}$.

## Notation

We write $h(x, y)$, or $\langle x, y \rangle$ for an inner product.
The prefix "sesqui" means one and a half: the form is linear in one slot and "half linear" in the other.
Two conventions are in use, and they differ in which slot is conjugated.
We follow most books of mathematics and the inner product pages: linear in the first slot, conjugate-linear in the second.
Physics books and Mathlib conjugate the first slot instead, and then $h(x, y) = x^* A y$, where $x^* = \bar x^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the [[conjugate transpose|0DXKE10PHF]].
In Dirac's bra-ket notation, $\langle x \mid y \rangle$ is conjugate-linear in the bra $\langle x \mid$, which is the physics convention.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take the standard form $h(x, y) = x_1 \bar y_1 + x_2 \bar y_2$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$, with $x = (1, i)$ and $y = (1 + i, 2)$.
Multiply each entry of $x$ by the conjugate of the matching entry of $y$, and add:
$$h(x, y) = 1 \cdot (1 - i) + i \cdot 2 = 1 + i.$$
In the other order the conjugates fall on the entries of $x$:
$$h(y, x) = (1 + i) \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot (-i) = 1 - i.$$
So the two orders give conjugate numbers, as they do for every Hermitian form.
Now scale each slot by $i$:
$$h(ix, y) = i(1 + i) = -1 + i, \qquad h(x, iy) = \bar i\,(1 + i) = -i(1 + i) = 1 - i.$$
Check: $iy = (-1 + i, 2i)$ has conjugate $(-1 - i, -2i)$, and $1 \cdot (-1 - i) + i \cdot (-2i) = -1 - i + 2 = 1 - i$.

### The matrix and the change of basis

Let $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ be a basis and put $M_{ij} = h(e_i, e_j)$.
Expand $x = \sum_i x_i e_i$ in the first slot and $y = \sum_j y_j e_j$ in the second; the scalars of $y$ come out conjugated:
$$h(x, y) = \sum_{i, j} x_i M_{ij} \bar y_j = x^{\mathsf{T}} M \bar y.$$
So on $\mathbb{C}^n$, each $n \times n$ complex matrix $M$ gives exactly one sesquilinear form, as for the [[matrix of a bilinear form|0QK6K857HS]].

Let the new basis vectors $b_1, \dots, b_n$ be the columns of an invertible matrix $P$.
The same expansion gives $h(b_k, b_l) = \sum_{i, j} P_{ik} M_{ij} \bar P_{jl}$, so the new matrix is
$$M' = P^{\mathsf{T}} M \bar P.$$
This is the sesquilinear form of the [[change of basis rule|0SPYAGE7T0]] $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$ for bilinear forms.
For example, take $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $b_1 = (1, i)$, $b_2 = (0, 1)$, so $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\bar P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Multiply out:
$$M \bar P = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & i \\ -2i & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad P^{\mathsf{T}} M \bar P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & i \\ -2i & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3i \\ -2i & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: $\bar b_1 = (1, -i)$, $M \bar b_1 = (1 + 1, -2i) = (2, -2i)$, and $h(b_1, b_1) = 1 \cdot 2 + i \cdot (-2i) = 4$.

### More examples

The [[form $x_1 \bar y_1 + 2 x_1 \bar y_2 + 3 x_2 \bar y_2$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$|HTRVMDMNH0]] has the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.
It is sesquilinear but not Hermitian, since $h(e_1, e_2) = 2$ while $h(e_2, e_1) = 0$.
The [[form with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ -i & 2 \end{pmatrix}$|8T68MSTVM7]] is Hermitian and positive definite, so it is a second inner product on $\mathbb{C}^2$.
On the space of continuous functions $[0, 1] \to \mathbb{C}$, the integral $h(f, g) = \int_0^1 f(t)\, \overline{g(t)}\, dt$ is sesquilinear, because the integral is linear and the conjugate of $c\,g$ is $\bar c\, \bar g$.

### What it is not

The [[bilinear form $x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$|2A38WWPDES]] is not sesquilinear, because it gives $B(x, iy) = i\,B(x, y)$ where a sesquilinear form gives $-i\,h(x, y)$.
The map $(x, y) \mapsto \bar x_1 \bar y_1$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ fails in the first slot: it is conjugate-linear in both slots.
The map $(x, y) \mapsto \lvert x_1 y_1 \rvert$ is not additive in the first slot.
With $x = e_1$ and $x' = -e_1$, the sum $x + x' = 0$ gives $0$, but the two values add to $1 + 1 = 2$.

### The rules you can use

A sesquilinear form is bilinear when $V$ is viewed as a real vector space, because a real $a$ has $\bar a = a$.
So $h(0, y) = h(x, 0) = 0$, and sums expand as usual, as long as complex scalars leave the second slot conjugated.
The rank of $M$ does not depend on the basis, since $P$ and $\bar P$ are invertible, so the radical and nondegeneracy are defined as for bilinear forms.
The determinant changes by a positive factor:
$$\det M' = \det P \cdot \det M \cdot \overline{\det P} = \lvert \det P \rvert^2 \det M.$$
In the example above, $\det P = 1$, and indeed $\det M' = 8 - (3i)(-2i) = 8 - 6 = 2 = \det M$.

Unlike a bilinear form, a sesquilinear form is fixed by its values on the diagonal.
The polarization identity says
$$4\, h(x, y) = \sum_{k = 0}^{3} i^k\, h(x + i^k y, x + i^k y),$$
and the [[characterization of Hermitian forms|AXH4S084MR]] proves it.
Each sesquilinear form is also $h_1 + i h_2$ for two Hermitian forms $h_1$ and $h_2$, as the matrix $M = \tfrac12 (M + M^*) + i \cdot \tfrac{1}{2i}(M - M^*)$ shows.

### Watch out

The two conventions give different formulas, so check which slot a book conjugates before you copy a formula.
In our convention $h(x, ay) = \bar a\, h(x, y)$ and $h(x, y) = x^{\mathsf{T}} M \bar y$; in the physics convention $h(ax, y) = \bar a\, h(x, y)$ and $h(x, y) = x^* A y$, with the change of basis $P^* A P$.

A second trap is to drop the bar in the change of basis.
In the example above the wrong rule gives
$$P^{\mathsf{T}} M P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ 2i & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 3i \\ 2i & 2 \end{pmatrix},$$
and its corner $-2$ is not $h(b_1, b_1) = 4$.
With a real $P$ the two rules agree, which is why the error can hide in examples with real numbers.

### Try it

First, find the matrix of $h(x, y) = 2 x_1 \bar y_2 + i x_2 \bar y_1$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$, and decide whether $h$ is Hermitian.
The matrix is $M = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and $M^* = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \ne M$, so $h$ is not Hermitian.

Second, for the standard form on $\mathbb{C}^2$, compute $h(v, v)$ for $v = (2, i)$.
The answer is $2 \cdot 2 + i \cdot \bar i = 4 + 1 = 5$, a positive real number.

## Explanations

### L1 human

On a complex space, a plain product of two vectors cannot give a length, because $i \cdot i = -1$.
For the vector $(1, i)$, the sum $1 \cdot 1 + i \cdot i$ is $0$, although the vector is not zero.
A sesquilinear form fixes this by taking the conjugate of the second vector first.
Then the vector $(1, i)$ gives $1 \cdot 1 + i \cdot (-i) = 2$, a positive number.
Every complex inner product works in this way.

### L2 human

Here is a typical problem: split a sesquilinear form into a "real part" and an "imaginary part" that are both Hermitian.
Take $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$, so $M^* = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -i & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
The two parts have the matrices
$$H_1 = \tfrac12 (M + M^*) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \tfrac{i}{2} \\ -\tfrac{i}{2} & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad H_2 = \tfrac{1}{2i} (M - M^*) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Both equal their conjugate transpose, and $H_1 + i H_2 = M$.
For $v = (1, 1)$ the form gives $h(v, v) = 1 + i + 0 + 2 = 3 + i$, which is not real.
The parts give $H_1(v, v) = 1 + \tfrac{i}{2} - \tfrac{i}{2} + 2 = 3$ and $H_2(v, v) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$.
Check: $3 + i \cdot 1 = 3 + i$.
So the real part of $h(v, v)$ comes from $H_1$ and the imaginary part from $H_2$, in the same way as $z = \operatorname{Re} z + i \operatorname{Im} z$ for a number.

### L3 human

A sesquilinear form on $V$ is the same as a linear map from $V$ to the space of [[conjugate-linear maps|BNPDDM95KF]] $V \to \mathbb{C}$.
It is also a bilinear form on $V \times \bar V$, where $\bar V$ is $V$ with the scalar multiplication $a \cdot v = \bar a v$.
Over any field $F$ with an automorphism $\sigma$ of order $2$, a $\sigma$-sesquilinear form replaces $\bar a$ by $\sigma(a)$, and finite fields $\mathbb{F}_{q^2}$ with $\sigma(a) = a^q$ give the finite unitary groups.
By the [[theorem on reflexive forms|M0G958M917]], a complex sesquilinear form for which orthogonality is symmetric is a scalar multiple of a Hermitian form.
The isometry groups of nondegenerate Hermitian forms are the [[unitary groups|VW5H9XFNJB]], next to the orthogonal and symplectic groups of symmetric and alternating forms.
