## Example

Let $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{R}$, the matrix that swaps the two coordinates.
Both diagonal entries are $0$, and $S$ is not triangular, since both entries off the diagonal are nonzero.
We show that $0$ is not an eigenvalue of $S$.

1. The characteristic polynomial is
$$p_S(t) = \det \begin{pmatrix} t & -1 \\ -1 & t \end{pmatrix} = t^2 - 1 = (t - 1)(t + 1).$$
   Check: $\tr S = 0$ and $\det S = -1$, so $t^2 - 0 \cdot t - 1$ agrees.
2. So the [[eigenvalues|0ZND7NQ970]] of $S$ are $1$ and $-1$, and the [[spectrum|04EHB7YDQS]] is $\{1, -1\}$.
3. Check with vectors: $S(1, 1) = (1, 1)$ and $S(1, -1) = (-1, 1) = -(1, -1)$.
4. The scalar $0$ is not an eigenvalue, because $\det S = -1 \ne 0$, so $S$ is invertible and $Sv = 0$ only for $v = 0$.
5. So the set $\{0\}$ of diagonal entries is not the spectrum of $S$.

## Remarks

The diagonal still carries some information.
The sum of the diagonal entries is the sum of the eigenvalues, $0 + 0 = 1 + (-1)$, because both sides equal the trace.
But the individual entries are not the eigenvalues.

The rule "the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries" holds for triangular matrices only, as in [[a triangular matrix|NHR1K0GPS1]].
Change $S$ to $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ by deleting the lower entry.
The new matrix is upper triangular, its characteristic polynomial is $t^2$, and now $0$ is its only eigenvalue, as the diagonal says.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix swaps the two coordinates, so it sends $(3, 5)$ to $(5, 3)$.
Its diagonal entries are zero, but it does not send any nonzero vector to zero.
The vectors on the line $y = x$ stay fixed, and the vectors on the line $y = -x$ get flipped.
So its eigenvalues are $1$ and $-1$, not $0$.

### L2 human

The lesson is that eigenvalues are the roots of $p_A$, not entries of $A$.
For a matrix that is not triangular, always compute $\det(tI - A)$.
Watch out: for $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ with $b \ne 0$ and $c \ne 0$, the diagonal entry $a$ is never an eigenvalue.
The reason is that $p(t) = (t - a)(t - d) - bc$, so $p(a) = -bc \ne 0$.
