## Example

Let $S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Its diagonal entries are $0$ and $0$, and $S$ is not triangular.

1. $p_S(t) = \det(tI - S) = t^2 - 1$. Second method: $\tr S = 0$ and $\det S = -1$.
2. So the [[eigenvalues|0ZND7NQ970]] of $S$ are $1$ and $-1$, and the [[spectrum|04EHB7YDQS]] is $\{1, -1\}$.
3. Check: $S(1, 1) = (1, 1)$ and $S(1, -1) = (-1, 1) = -(1, -1)$.
4. $0$ is not an eigenvalue, because $\det S = -1 \ne 0$, so $\ker S = 0$.
5. So the diagonal entries do not give the eigenvalues of $S$.

## Remarks

- The sum of the diagonal entries is still the sum of the eigenvalues: $0 + 0 = 1 + (-1)$.
- Reading eigenvalues from the diagonal is correct for triangular matrices only, as in [[a triangular matrix|NHR1K0GPS1]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix swaps the two coordinates.
Its diagonal entries are zero, but it does not kill any nonzero vector.
Its eigenvalues are $1$ and $-1$.

### L2 human

The typical lesson is that eigenvalues are roots of $p_A$, not entries of $A$.
A trap is to apply the triangular rule to a matrix with nonzero entries below the diagonal.
