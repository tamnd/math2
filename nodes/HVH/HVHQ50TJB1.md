## Statement

Determinants can measure the rank of any matrix, square or not: the rank is the size of the largest square block whose determinant is not zero.
Let $F$ be a [[field|0QA8CHH8MG]], and let $A$ be a nonzero $m \times n$ matrix over $F$.
Then the [[rank|0EWQEYRVA8]] of $A$ is the largest integer $r$ such that $A$ has a nonzero [[minor|0SD528P0EK]] of order $r$.
Equivalently, for each $r \ge 1$, $\operatorname{rank} A \ge r$ if and only if some $r \times r$ submatrix of $A$ has nonzero determinant.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The only minor of order $3$ is $\det A$, and expansion along row $3$ gives
$$\det A = 1 \cdot (2 \cdot 6 - 3 \cdot 4) - 0 + 1 \cdot (1 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 2) = 0 + 0 = 0.$$
So the rank is less than $3$.
The minor with rows $1, 3$ and columns $1, 2$ is
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = -2 \ne 0,$$
so the rank is at least $2$, and $\operatorname{rank} A = 2$.
Check by elimination: $R_2 - 2R_1 = 0$ and $R_3 - R_1 = (0, -2, -2)$, which leaves two pivots.

### Lower bounds are cheap, upper bounds are not

One nonzero minor of order $r$ proves $\operatorname{rank} A \ge r$.
To prove $\operatorname{rank} A < r$ we need every minor of order $r$ to vanish, and one zero minor says nothing.
For example, in $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ the minor on rows $1, 2$ is $0$, but the minor on rows $1, 3$ is $1$, so the rank is $2$.
For the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ all three minors of order $2$ vanish, $4 - 4 = 6 - 6 = 12 - 12 = 0$, and so its rank is $1$.
For large matrices, elimination is much faster than a search through minors, since an $m \times n$ matrix has $\binom{m}{r} \binom{n}{r}$ minors of order $r$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The zero matrix has rank $0$ and no nonzero minor at all, so the statement either excludes it or uses the convention that the empty minor of order $0$ equals $1$.
The field is needed in the step from a nonzero minor to independent columns.
Over the ring $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, the $1 \times 1$ matrix $(2)$ has the nonzero minor $2$, yet its column is not linearly independent, because $2 \cdot 2 = 0$.
Over an integral domain such as $\mathbb{Z}$ the statement still holds, with the rank taken over the field of fractions.

### What it gives you

For a square $n \times n$ matrix, $\operatorname{rank} A = n$ exactly when $\det A \ne 0$, which is the [[invertibility criterion|FBSAPTP1PR]] again.
The rank of $A$ equals the rank of $A^{\mathsf{T}}$, because the minors of $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ are the minors of $A$, by the [[determinant of the transpose|0ZZQ2E1MTE]].
The [[rank of the adjugate|9CJME5W96T]] follows from the criterion, since the entries of the adjugate are minors of order $n - 1$ up to sign.
Over $\mathbb{R}$, a nonzero minor stays nonzero when the entries move a little, so the rank cannot drop near a given matrix; in other words, rank can only go down under limits.

### Generalizations

The condition $\operatorname{rank} A < r$ says that a list of polynomials in the entries, the minors of order $r$, all vanish.
The ideal generated by these minors is a [[determinantal ideal|SXD7X0WQH1]], and its zero set, the matrices of rank less than $r$, is a [[determinantal variety|6WH08A4BTK]].
Over a commutative ring the ideals of minors replace the rank, and they lead to the Fitting ideals of a module in commutative algebra.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To find the rank of a table of numbers, look for the largest square piece inside it whose determinant is not zero.
The size of that piece is the rank.
For the table with rows $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 4)$, the whole table has determinant $4 - 4 = 0$, but the single entry $1$ is not zero, so the rank is $1$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: for which $t$ does $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & t & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & t \end{pmatrix}$ have rank $3$, rank $2$ or rank $1$?
Subtract row $1$ from rows $2$ and $3$, which does not change the determinant, and expand:
$$\det = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & t - 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & t - 1 \end{pmatrix} = (t - 1)^2.$$
So the rank is $3$ for $t \ne 1$.
For $t = 1$ every entry is $1$, so every minor of order $2$ is $1 - 1 = 0$, while the entries are nonzero, and the rank is $1$.
The rank is never $2$ for this family.
Watch out: the rank is at least $r$ as soon as one minor of order $r$ is nonzero, but it is less than $r$ only when all of them vanish.

### L3 human

The criterion says that the set of matrices of rank at most $r$ is cut out by polynomial equations, so it is a closed algebraic set, and rank is lower semicontinuous in the Zariski topology and in the usual topology over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$.
These determinantal varieties are a central example in algebraic geometry and commutative algebra.
The [[proof|DPK9X2057G]] uses only the invertibility criterion and the equality of row rank and column rank.
