## Example

Work in the language of pure equality, with no symbols except $=$.
Let $T$ be the theory with the single axiom
$$\forall x \, \forall y \, (x = y),$$
which says "there is at most one thing".
Since structures have non-empty domains, the models of $T$ are exactly the one-element sets.
We check that $T$ is [[negation complete|319P85G2C1]].

1. Any two models of $T$ are isomorphic.
   If $A = \{a\}$ and $B = \{b\}$, the map $a \mapsto b$ is a bijection, and it preserves equality because there is only one pair to check.
2. Isomorphic structures satisfy the same [[sentences|DKFGQTRYZE]].
   So for each sentence $\varphi$, either every model of $T$ satisfies $\varphi$, or every model satisfies $\neg \varphi$.
3. In the first case $T \models \varphi$, and the [[completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]] gives $T \vdash \varphi$.
   In the second case it gives $T \vdash \neg \varphi$ in the same way.
4. So $T$ proves $\varphi$ or $\neg \varphi$ for every sentence $\varphi$, and $T$ is negation complete.

Two sample sentences show the result.
The sentence $\exists x \, \forall y \, (x = y)$ holds in a one-element set, so $T$ proves it.
The sentence $\exists x \, \exists y \, \neg (x = y)$, "there are two different things", fails in a one-element set, so $T$ proves its negation.

## Remarks

The example shows the model route to negation completeness: if all models are isomorphic, the completeness theorem does the rest.
The same argument works for the theory "there are exactly $n$ elements", for each $n \ge 1$, since any two sets of size $n$ are in bijection.

Change the theory a little and the result fails.
The empty theory in the same language has models of every size, and it decides neither $\forall x \, \forall y \, (x = y)$ nor its negation.
Add a unary predicate $P$ to the language, and $T$ no longer decides $\exists x \, P(x)$, because the one point may or may not lie in $P$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

If the rules say "there is only one thing", every question about which things are equal has one answer.
Is there something equal to everything?
Yes, the one thing.
Are there two different things?
No.
So the rules settle every question that their language can ask.

### L2 human

The argument has two steps, and each uses a different tool.
First, all models are isomorphic, so they agree on every sentence.
Second, the completeness theorem turns "true in all models" into "provable".
This is the typical use of the example: it is the first case of a complete theory that is proved complete through its models and not by writing proofs.
Watch out: without the completeness theorem, step two fails, and "all models agree" gives only $T \models \varphi$, not $T \vdash \varphi$.
