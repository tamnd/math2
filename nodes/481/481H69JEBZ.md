## Definition

*General relativity* is the physical theory in which a model of the universe, or of a part of it, consists of the following data.

1. A [[spacetime|63ZKC7AFX2]] $(M, g)$, whose metric $g$ describes the gravitational field.
2. Matter fields on $M$ with a [[stress–energy tensor|REDZ92MKXX]] $T$.
3. The metric and the matter satisfy the [[Einstein field equations|3MCZ97SW6V]].
$$\mathrm{Ric}_g - \tfrac12 R_g \, g + \Lambda g = \frac{8 \pi G}{c^4} T.$$
Here $\mathrm{Ric}_g$ is the Ricci tensor, $R_g$ the scalar curvature, $\Lambda$ the [[cosmological constant|WVKKDY9V61]], $G$ Newton's gravitational constant and $c$ the speed of light.
4. Freely falling test particles move on timelike [[geodesics|RNRTS8WWNM]] of $g$, and light rays in vacuum on null geodesics.

## Remarks

Example: Minkowski space with $T = 0$ and $\Lambda = 0$ is a model.
Its curvature vanishes, so both sides of the field equations are $0$.
Its geodesics are straight lines, so it is special relativity without gravity.
The [[Schwarzschild metric|6Y50T68WKY]] is the model outside a static, spherically symmetric star or black hole, with $T = 0$ and $\Lambda = 0$.
Non-example: $\R^4$ with a Riemannian metric is not a model, since clause 1 needs a Lorentzian metric.
The field equations imply $\nabla^\mu T_{\mu\nu} = 0$ by the contracted Bianchi identity, so energy and momentum of matter are locally conserved.
Clause 4 for small bodies can be derived from the field equations under suitable assumptions, so it is not fully independent of clause 3.
