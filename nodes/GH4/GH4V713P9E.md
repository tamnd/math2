## Example

The Girard translation sends a formula of the implicational fragment of [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]] to a formula of [[linear logic|H408H4WHBA]] by $p^{\circ} = p$ for atoms and $(A \to B)^{\circ} = {!A^{\circ}} \multimap B^{\circ}$.
For $A = p \to (q \to p)$, the translation is $!p \multimap (!q \multimap p)$, and it is derivable.

1. The translation of $q \to p$ is $!q \multimap p$.
   So $A^{\circ} = {!p} \multimap ({!q} \multimap p)$.
2. In one-sided form, $!p \multimap C = ?p^{\perp} \mathbin{\wp} C$, so $A^{\circ} = ?p^{\perp} \mathbin{\wp} (?q^{\perp} \mathbin{\wp} p)$.
3. $\vdash p^{\perp}, p$ is an axiom.
4. Dereliction $?D$ gives $\vdash ?p^{\perp}, p$.
5. Weakening $?W$ gives $\vdash ?p^{\perp}, ?q^{\perp}, p$.
6. The rule $\mathbin{\wp}$ gives $\vdash ?p^{\perp}, ?q^{\perp} \mathbin{\wp} p$, and $\mathbin{\wp}$ again gives $\vdash ?p^{\perp} \mathbin{\wp} (?q^{\perp} \mathbin{\wp} p)$.

## Remarks

The intuitionistic formula $p \to (q \to p)$ uses weakening, since $q$ is not used.
In the translation, the unused hypothesis is $!q$, and the rule $?W$ discards it.
Dereliction in step 4 says that one use of $!p$ is one use of $p$.
Without the exponentials, $p \multimap (q \multimap p)$ is [[not derivable|V1ZRAX3GBM]].
The Wikipedia article on linear logic encodes intuitionistic implication as $!A \multimap B$, and it reports that a translation of this kind preserves and reflects provability.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Intuitionistic logic allows a hypothesis to be used many times or not at all.
Linear logic marks such hypotheses with "!".
The translation puts "!" on every hypothesis of an implication.
Then the linear proof may throw away "!$q$" and use "!$p$" once, as the intuitionistic proof does.

### L2 human

We translate the contraction axiom $(p \to (p \to q)) \to (p \to q)$ and see why it is still derivable.

1. The translation is $!(!p \multimap (!p \multimap q)) \multimap (!p \multimap q)$.
2. The hypothesis $!p$ of the conclusion is used twice in the intuitionistic proof.
3. In the linear proof, two copies of $?p^{\perp}$ arise from the two uses, and $?C$ merges them into one.
4. So the translation turns the hidden contraction of the intuitionistic proof into an explicit use of $?C$.

The typical use of the translation is to study intuitionistic proofs inside linear logic, where each copy and each deletion is visible.
A trap: the translation of the conclusion $B$ of $A \to B$ gets no "!", so $(A \to B)^{\circ}$ is not $!(A^{\circ} \multimap B^{\circ})$.
