## Summary

An [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]] $T \colon V \to V$ is [[nilpotent|0M8MHYERTY]] when $T^k = 0$ for some $k \ge 1$, and the least such $k$ is its index.
In dimension $n$, this is equivalent to $T^n = 0$, to a strictly upper triangular matrix in some basis, and to $p_T = t^n$.
Over an algebraically closed field it is also equivalent to having $0$ as the only eigenvalue.
Nilpotent maps are the second ingredient, after diagonalizable maps, of the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]].

## Motivation

On a [[generalized eigenspace|0AJ035ZTZE]] $V_\lambda$, the map $T - \lambda$ is nilpotent.
So, after the [[decomposition into generalized eigenspaces|0Z3GDY89T5]], every map over $\C$ is a sum of scalars and nilpotent maps on the pieces.
Understanding nilpotent maps is then the key to the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].

## Notation

For matrices, the notion is the [[nilpotent matrix|07BSPZPSMF]]: $A$ is nilpotent when its map $x \mapsto A x$ is nilpotent.
The index is also called the degree or the order of nilpotency.
In Mathlib, `IsNilpotent x` means that some power of $x$ is $0$, for elements of any ring, and in particular for endomorphisms.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The [[derivative on quadratic polynomials|29A5DCNMHJ]] has index $3$, since the third derivative of a quadratic is $0$.
- The [[derivative on cubic polynomials|~6463dbd0]] has index $4$.
- The [[matrix with no zero entries|YHCD6MTSV8]] $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ -1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$ has index $2$.
- The shift $e_1 \mapsto 0$, $e_2 \mapsto e_1$, $e_3 \mapsto e_2$ on $F^3$ has index $3$. It is $J - 2I$ for the [[Jordan block of size 3|~6530bcca]].
- Every strictly upper triangular matrix is nilpotent, by the [[triangular power argument|AW5RBMW7RK]].

### Non-examples

- The [[shear|~58e14c85]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is invertible, so none of its powers is $0$.
- The real matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has only the real eigenvalue $0$, but its square is $\operatorname{diag}(0, -1, -1)$, and it is not nilpotent.
- A nonzero projection $P$ satisfies $P^k = P \ne 0$ for all $k \ge 1$.

### Basic properties

- The [[characterizations of nilpotent maps|~662223db]] give $T^n = 0$, a strictly upper triangular form, and $p_T = t^n$.
- A nilpotent map is not injective when $V \ne 0$, and its trace and determinant are $0$.
- The only nilpotent [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] map is $0$.
- If $S$ and $T$ are nilpotent and commute, then $S + T$ and $S T$ are nilpotent. This uses the [[binomial theorem|DS9TS6AFMW]].
- If $N$ is nilpotent of index $k$, then $I - N$ is invertible with inverse $I + N + \cdots + N^{k-1}$.

### History

Peirce introduced the word "nilpotent" in his Linear Associative Algebra of 1870, for elements of an algebra with a vanishing power.
Nilpotent matrices appear in the Jordan form of Jordan's 1870 treatise, as the off-diagonal part of each block.

### Common errors

- Testing nilpotence by the entries. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ -1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$ has no zero entry, but its square is $0$.
- Concluding nilpotence from the eigenvalues over $\R$. The second non-example above has only the real eigenvalue $0$.
- Thinking that the sum of two nilpotent maps is nilpotent. For $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the sum squares to $I$. Commutation is needed.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A nilpotent map is a map that destroys everything after a few steps.
Each application pushes vectors further towards zero, until they vanish.
For example, differentiation of polynomials of degree at most $2$ gives $0$ after three steps.
In size $n$, a nilpotent matrix always vanishes after at most $n$ steps.

### L2 human

Let $T$ be the derivative on polynomials of degree at most $2$, with basis $1, x, x^2$.

1. $T(1) = 0$, $T(x) = 1$ and $T(x^2) = 2x$.
2. The matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is strictly upper triangular.
3. So $p_T = t^3$, and $T^3 = 0$.
4. $T^2(x^2) = 2 \ne 0$, so the index is exactly $3$.

The typical use is to recognize the nilpotent part $N$ of a map $T = D + N$, and to compute $e^{N}$ or $(I - N)^{-1}$ by a finite sum.
The trap is to expect a nilpotent matrix to look triangular in the standard basis; it is triangular only in a suitable basis.

### L3 human

In a ring, an element $x$ is nilpotent when $x^k = 0$ for some $k$.
The nilpotent elements of a commutative ring form its nilradical.
Up to similarity, the nilpotent $n \times n$ matrices over a field correspond to the partitions of $n$, through the sizes of their Jordan blocks.
In Lie theory, Engel's theorem says that a Lie algebra of nilpotent matrices is simultaneously strictly upper triangular.
