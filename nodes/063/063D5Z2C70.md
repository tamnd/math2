## Summary

A nilpotent map is a map that sends every vector to zero after a fixed number of steps.
Such maps are what is left of a linear map after we remove its eigenvalues, so they carry all the difficulty of the Jordan normal form.
An [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]] $T \colon V \to V$ is [[nilpotent|0M8MHYERTY]] when
$$T^k = 0 \quad \text{for some integer } k \ge 1,$$
and the least such $k$ is the *index* of $T$.
In dimension $n$ this is equivalent to $T^n = 0$, to a strictly upper triangular matrix in some basis, and to $p_T = t^n$.

## Motivation

On a [[generalized eigenspace|0AJ035ZTZE]] $V_\lambda$, the map $T - \lambda$ is nilpotent.
So, after the [[decomposition into generalized eigenspaces|0Z3GDY89T5]], every complex matrix is a scalar plus a nilpotent map on each piece.
To understand all matrices up to similarity, it is then enough to understand nilpotent ones, and the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]] does exactly that.

Nilpotent maps also make some infinite processes finite.
The exponential $e^{N} = I + N + \frac{1}{2} N^2 + \cdots$ stops after the term $N^{k-1}/(k-1)!$, and the same happens for the geometric series of $(I - N)^{-1}$.
That is why the nilpotent part of the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]] gives the polynomial factors $t^j$ in the solutions of linear differential equations.

## Notation

For matrices, the notion is the [[nilpotent matrix|07BSPZPSMF]]: $A$ is nilpotent when the map $x \mapsto A x$ is nilpotent.
The index is also called the degree or the order of nilpotency.
In Mathlib, `IsNilpotent x` means that some power of $x$ is $0$, for an element $x$ of any ring, and in particular for an endomorphism.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Let $T$ be the derivative on real polynomials of degree at most $2$, with the basis $1, x, x^2$.
The images are $T(1) = 0$, $T(x) = 1$ and $T(x^2) = 2x$, so the matrix is
$$[T] = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Apply $T$ again and again to $x^2$:
$$x^2 \mapsto 2x \mapsto 2 \mapsto 0.$$
So $T^3 = 0$ and $T^2 \ne 0$, and the index is $3$, as for the [[derivative on quadratic polynomials|29A5DCNMHJ]].
In the same way, the [[derivative on real cubic polynomials|7DGJWWZ8DW]] has index $4$.

### Nilpotent matrices that look harmless

A nilpotent matrix need not have any zero entry.
The [[matrix with no zero entries|YHCD6MTSV8]] below squares to zero:
$$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ -1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 - 4 & 8 - 8 \\ -2 + 2 & -4 + 4 \end{pmatrix} = 0.$$
The shift $e_1 \mapsto 0$, $e_2 \mapsto e_1$, $e_3 \mapsto e_2$ on $F^3$ has index $3$, and it is $J - 2I$ for the [[Jordan block of size 3|1YGY396SAS]].
Every strictly upper triangular matrix is nilpotent, by the [[triangular power argument|AW5RBMW7RK]].

### What it is not

The [[shear|SBDF8APDF7]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $1$, so it is invertible and none of its powers is $0$.
A nonzero projection $P$ has $P^k = P \ne 0$ for all $k \ge 1$.
The real matrix
$$R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$
has $0$ as its only real eigenvalue, but $R^2 = \operatorname{diag}(0, -1, -1)$, and $R$ is not nilpotent.
Its characteristic polynomial $t(t^2 + 1)$ does not split over $\R$, and over $\C$ it has the eigenvalues $\pm i$.

### The rules you can use

The [[characterizations of nilpotent maps|FSKECQK1GW]] give the equivalent forms $T^n = 0$, a strictly upper triangular matrix, and $p_T = t^n$.
From $p_T = t^n$, the trace and the determinant of a nilpotent map are $0$, so a nilpotent map on $V \ne 0$ is not injective.
The only nilpotent [[diagonalizable|07SQCNV233]] map is $0$, since a diagonal matrix with $D^k = 0$ has zero entries.

If $S$ and $T$ are nilpotent and commute, then $S + T$ and $S T$ are nilpotent, by the [[binomial theorem for commuting matrices|DS9TS6AFMW]].
If $N$ is nilpotent of index $k$, then $I - N$ is invertible with inverse $I + N + \cdots + N^{k-1}$.
Check: the product $(I - N)(I + N + \cdots + N^{k-1})$ telescopes to $I - N^k = I$.

### Watch out

The first trap is to judge nilpotence by the eigenvalues over $\R$.
The matrix $R$ above has only the real eigenvalue $0$ and is not nilpotent.
The correct test is $p_T = t^n$, or $0$ as the only eigenvalue over an algebraically closed field.

The second trap is to add nilpotent maps that do not commute.
For $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ the sum is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, whose square is $I$.

### History

Benjamin Peirce introduced the words "nilpotent" and "idempotent" in his *Linear Associative Algebra* of 1870, for elements of an algebra.

### Try it

First, find the index of $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Here $N^2$ has the single nonzero entry $1$ in the upper right corner, and $N^3 = 0$, so the index is $3$.

Second, compute $(I - N)^{-1}$ for $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Since $N^2 = 0$, the inverse is $I + N = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A nilpotent map destroys everything after a few steps.
Differentiation of polynomials of degree at most $2$ is an example.
Start with $x^2$: one derivative gives $2x$, the next gives $2$, and the third gives $0$.
Every such polynomial vanishes after three steps, and a nilpotent matrix of size $n$ always vanishes after at most $n$ steps.

### L2 human

Solve $x' = N x$ for $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ with $x(0) = (a, b)$.
The solution is $x(t) = e^{tN} x(0)$, and $N^2 = 0$ makes the exponential series stop after two terms:
$$e^{tN} = I + t N = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad x(t) = \begin{pmatrix} a + b t \\ b \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check: the second entry is constant, and the first has derivative $b$, which is the second entry, as $x' = N x$ requires.
This shows the typical use: a nilpotent part turns an infinite series into a polynomial, and the solution grows like a polynomial in $t$ instead of an exponential.
Watch out: a nilpotent matrix is triangular only in a suitable basis, so do not expect to see its zeros in the standard basis.

### L3 human

In a ring, an element $x$ is nilpotent when $x^k = 0$ for some $k$, and in a commutative ring the nilpotent elements form the nilradical.
Up to similarity, the nilpotent $n \times n$ matrices over a field correspond to the partitions of $n$, through the sizes of their Jordan blocks, which the sub-area on canonical forms proves.
In Lie theory, [[Engel's theorem|V0ZWMA3FTH]] says that a Lie algebra of nilpotent matrices is strictly upper triangular in one common basis.
