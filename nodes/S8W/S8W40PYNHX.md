## Summary

A model $M$ of a [[complete theory|655K8Y7VS8]] $T$ is *prime* when it has an [[elementary embedding|CGH8C8EA9N]] into every [[model|MVCH6N9SNW]] of $T$.
It is the smallest model of $T$ in a strong sense.
For a countable signature, the prime models are exactly the countable [[atomic models|~fbef995e]], in which every tuple satisfies an isolated [[type|9V71Q3SD1G]].
A prime model need not exist, and when it exists in a countable signature it is unique up to isomorphism.
The field of algebraic numbers is the prime model of the theory of algebraically closed fields of characteristic $0$.

## Motivation

Every model of a complete theory contains elements that the theory forces to exist, and may contain more.
A prime model contains only the forced elements, in the sense that it fits elementarily inside every model.
Prime models are the first step in describing the countable models of a theory, and the opposite end from saturated models, which realize as many types as possible.

## Notation

There is no special symbol.
Some books write $M_0$ for the prime model of $T$, and say that $M$ is prime over $A$ in the relative notion below.
A model $M$ is *prime over* a set $A \subseteq M$ when every [[partial elementary map|XTNYY0WRQ5]] from $A$ into a model $N$ of $T$ extends to an elementary embedding of $M$ into $N$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The algebraic numbers in $\mathrm{ACF}_0$; see [[this example|~5820c59d]].
- $(\Q, <)$ in the theory of dense linear orders without endpoints; see [[this example|~309e2d86]].
- $(\N, S)$ in the theory of the successor function on the natural numbers. A model is $\N$ plus disjoint copies of $\Z$, and $\N$ embeds elementarily as the part generated by $0$.
- In an $\aleph_0$-categorical theory, the countable model is prime.

### Non-examples

- The complex field is not prime for $\mathrm{ACF}_0$; see [[this non-example|~528f8a9a]].
- $(\R, <)$ is not prime for dense linear orders, though it is atomic.
- The theory of [[independent unary predicates|~a949e251]] has no prime model at all.

### Basic properties

- A prime model of a theory in a signature $L$ has size at most $\max(\abs{L}, \aleph_0)$, by the [[Löwenheim-Skolem theorem|484M0TZ1S7]].
- For a countable signature: prime if and only if countable and atomic, and prime models are unique up to isomorphism; see [[this theorem|~ddda0a28]].
- For a countable signature, a prime model exists if and only if isolated types are dense; this holds for small theories; see [[this theorem|~24829d6c]].
- In an $\omega$-stable theory, every set $A$ inside a model has a prime model over $A$, and it is unique up to isomorphism over $A$.
- In $\mathrm{ACF}_p$, the prime model over $A$ is the algebraic closure of the subfield generated by $A$.

### History

Prime models over sets became a main tool in the proof of [[Morley's categoricity theorem|V7N84P60SJ]] and in stability theory.

### Common errors

- Prime does not mean minimal. A model is minimal when it has no proper elementary substructure. $(\Q, <)$ is prime, but $\Q \cap (0, 1)$ is a proper elementary substructure of it.
- A prime model embeds elementarily into every model, but the embedding is not unique in general. In $\mathrm{ACF}_0$, the algebraic numbers embed into $\C$ in many ways, one for each automorphism of $\overline{\Q}$.
- Prime is defined relative to a complete theory. An incomplete theory can have models that are not elementarily equivalent, so no model can embed into all of them.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A prime model is a smallest world for a set of rules: a copy of it sits inside every world that obeys the rules.
For fields like the complex numbers, the smallest world is the algebraic numbers, the roots of polynomials with integer coefficients.
Every such field contains them, and nothing smaller has all the needed roots.

### L2 human

We check that $(\N, S)$ is the prime model of $T = \operatorname{Th}(\N, S)$, with $S$ the successor function.

1. Let $\theta_n(x)$ say that $x = S^n(y)$ for some $y$ that is not a successor. In every model of $T$, exactly one element satisfies $\theta_n$, because $T$ says that exactly one element is not a successor.
2. Since $T$ is complete, for each formula $\psi(x)$ it proves $\forall x \, (\theta_n \to \psi)$ or $\forall x \, (\theta_n \to \neg\psi)$. So $\theta_n$ isolates the type of $n$.
3. A tuple $(n_1, \dots, n_k)$ is isolated by $\theta_{n_1}(x_1) \wedge \dots \wedge \theta_{n_k}(x_k)$, in the same way.
4. So $(\N, S)$ is countable and atomic, and it is prime.

The typical use of a prime model is as the starting point for building other countable models.
A trap is to confuse the prime model with the countable model of least size; all countable models have the same size.

### L3 human

In an $\omega$-stable theory, a prime model over a set $A$ can be built by adding, one at a time, elements whose type over $A$ and the earlier elements is isolated.
Prime models over sets reduce questions about arbitrary models to questions about sets of parameters, which is how they enter the proof of Morley's theorem.
