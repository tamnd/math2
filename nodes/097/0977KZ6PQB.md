## Statement

Cramer's rule writes each unknown of a square linear system with nonzero determinant as a quotient of two determinants.
Let $F$ be a [[field|0QA8CHH8MG]], let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$ with $\det A \ne 0$, and let $b \in F^n$.
For $1 \le i \le n$, let $A_i$ be the matrix that we get from $A$ when we replace column $i$ by $b$.
Then the [[linear system|0JTRMBQ1SA]] $Ax = b$ has exactly one solution $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$, and
$$x_i = \frac{\det A_i}{\det A} \qquad (1 \le i \le n).$$

## Remarks

### A system of two equations

Solve $2x + y = 5$ and $x + 3y = 10$.
The matrix of the system and its determinant are
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det A = 2 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 1 = 5.$$
For $x$, put the right side $(5, 10)$ in column 1, and for $y$, put it in column 2:
$$\det A_1 = \det \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 10 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = 15 - 10 = 5, \qquad \det A_2 = \det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 10 \end{pmatrix} = 20 - 5 = 15.$$
So $x = 5/5 = 1$ and $y = 15/5 = 3$.
Check: $2 \cdot 1 + 3 = 5$ and $1 + 3 \cdot 3 = 10$.

The same steps on the general system $ax + by = e$, $cx + dy = f$ with $ad - bc \ne 0$ give the formulas that many students learn first:
$$x = \frac{ed - bf}{ad - bc}, \qquad y = \frac{af - ec}{ad - bc}.$$
They are the $2 \times 2$ case of the rule, and the [[determinant of a 2 × 2 matrix|0ZN2J42SK7]] supplies each numerator and the denominator.

### Why the determinant must be nonzero

When $\det A = 0$, the rule would divide by zero, and the system has no solution or more than one.
Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
For $b = (1, 2)$ the two equations say $x + y = 1$ and $x + y = 2$, so there is no solution.
For $b = (1, 1)$ every pair with $x + y = 1$ is a solution.

The hypothesis is also necessary for uniqueness.
If $Ax = b$ has exactly one solution for one $b$, then $Av = 0$ forces $v = 0$, since $x + v$ would be a second solution.
So $A$ is injective, hence invertible, and $\det A \ne 0$ by the [[invertibility criterion|FBSAPTP1PR]].

### Two proofs

The [[proof by the adjugate|4GY0RA4W28]] starts from the formula $A^{-1} = \operatorname{adj}(A) / \det A$ of the [[inverse via the adjugate|0ENVM493VK]].
Coordinate $i$ of $\operatorname{adj}(A)\, b$ is the [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]] of $\det A_i$ along its column $i$.
The [[proof by multilinearity|V0XZ0D7A18]] writes $A_i$ as the product of $A$ with a matrix whose determinant is $x_i$, and then uses [[multiplicativity|04CPJ6AF00]].

### What it costs

The rule needs $n + 1$ determinants of size $n$.
Each of them costs about $n^3/3$ multiplications by row reduction, so the rule costs about $n^4/3$.
One run of [[Gaussian elimination|0JVJ2WCXZH]] solves the system in about $n^3/3$.
If you compute the determinants by cofactor expansion instead, the cost grows like $n!$.
So the rule is a tool for proofs and for systems of size 2 or 3, not for large computations.

### Over a commutative ring

The [[proof by multilinearity|V0XZ0D7A18]] uses no division until its last line.
So over every commutative ring $K$, each solution of $Ax = b$ satisfies $x_i \det A = \det A_i$.
When $\det A$ is a unit of $K$, the rule holds in its usual form.
For example, an integer system with $\det A = \pm 1$ has an integer solution for every integer right side.
Mathlib states the rule in this division-free form: the vector of the $\det A_i$ equals $\operatorname{adj}(A)\, b$.

### Watch out

The right side goes into a column of $A$, not a row.
For a symmetric $A$, as in the first example, the two choices give the same numbers, and this hides the error.
Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $b = (3, 1)$, so the system is $x + y = 3$, $y = 1$, with solution $x = 2$, $y = 1$.
Putting $b$ in column 1 gives the right answer:
$$x = \det \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 3 - 1 = 2.$$
Putting $b$ in row 1 gives $\det \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 3$, which is wrong.

### Try it

Solve $x + 2y = 4$, $3x - y = 5$ by the rule.
Here $\det A = -1 - 6 = -7$, $\det A_1 = -4 - 10 = -14$ and $\det A_2 = 5 - 12 = -7$, so $x = 2$ and $y = 1$.

For which $t$ does the rule apply to $tx + y = 1$, $x + ty = 0$?
It applies when $\det A = t^2 - 1 \ne 0$, that is, for $t \ne \pm 1$, and then $x = t/(t^2 - 1)$.

## History

Gabriel Cramer published the rule for any number of unknowns in 1750.
Colin Maclaurin's Treatise of Algebra, published in 1748 after his death, already gave the rule for two and three unknowns.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Cramer's rule gives each unknown as one determinant divided by another.
The bottom number is always the determinant of the coefficients.
For the top number of the first unknown, copy the coefficients and put the right sides in the first column.
For $2x + y = 5$ and $x + 3y = 10$, the bottom number is $6 - 1 = 5$ and the top number for $x$ is $15 - 10 = 5$, so $x = 1$.

### L2 human

Here is a $3 \times 3$ case: solve $x + y + z = 6$, $y + z = 5$, $x + 2z = 7$.
The coefficient matrix and its determinant, by expansion along the first column, are
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det A = 1 \cdot (2 - 0) + 1 \cdot (1 - 1) = 2.$$
Put $b = (6, 5, 7)$ in column 1 and expand along the first row:
$$\det A_1 = 6(2 - 0) - 1(10 - 7) + 1(0 - 7) = 12 - 3 - 7 = 2.$$
Put $b$ in column 2 and expand along the first column:
$$\det A_2 = 1(10 - 7) + 1(6 - 5) = 4.$$
Put $b$ in column 3 and expand along the first column:
$$\det A_3 = 1(7 - 0) + 1(5 - 6) = 6.$$
So $x = 2/2 = 1$, $y = 4/2 = 2$ and $z = 6/2 = 3$.
Check: $1 + 2 + 3 = 6$, $2 + 3 = 5$ and $1 + 6 = 7$.
Even at size 3 the rule took four determinants, while elimination would have taken a few row operations.
Its strength is that it gives one unknown alone, as an explicit formula in the data.

### L3 human

The rule shows that the solution $A^{-1} b$ is a rational function of the entries of $A$ and $b$, with denominator $\det A$.
So the solution depends continuously, even smoothly, on the data wherever $\det A \ne 0$.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$ it says that $\det A$ times a rational solution is an integer vector, which bounds the denominators that can occur.
In the language of the [[adjugate matrix|07W9H8QF38]], the rule is the identity $\operatorname{adj}(A)\, A = \det A \cdot I$ applied to one vector.
Geometrically, $x_i$ is a ratio of two [[signed volumes|RC5BTSG8T2]]: when the column $a_i$ is replaced by $b$, the signed volume of the columns is multiplied by $x_i$.
