## Statement

Let $F$ be a [[field|0QA8CHH8MG]], let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$ with $\det A \ne 0$, and let $b \in F^n$.
For $1 \le i \le n$, let $A_i$ be the matrix that results when column $i$ of $A$ is replaced by $b$.
Then the [[linear system|0JTRMBQ1SA]] $Ax = b$ has exactly one solution $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$, and
$$x_i = \frac{\det A_i}{\det A} \qquad (1 \le i \le n).$$

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

If $\det A = 0$, then the system has no solution or more than one.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $b = (1, 2)$ there is no solution; for $b = (1, 1)$ there are infinitely many.
Over a commutative ring $K$, the rule holds when $\det A$ is a unit of $K$, with the same proofs.

### Consequences

- For $n = 2$, the system $ax + by = e$, $cx + dy = f$ has the solution $x = (ed - bf)/(ad - bc)$ and $y = (af - ec)/(ad - bc)$.
- The solution of an integer system with $\det A = \pm 1$ is an integer vector.
- The rule needs $n + 1$ determinants of size $n$, about $n^4/3$ operations by elimination, against about $n^3/3$ for one [[Gaussian elimination|0JVJ2WCXZH]].
- So the rule is a tool for theory and for small systems, not for large computations.

There are two proofs: [[by the adjugate|4GY0RA4W28]] and [[by multilinearity|~97eedb97]].

### Converse

If $Ax = b$ has exactly one solution for some $b$, then $\det A \ne 0$ over a field, since a nonzero kernel vector would give a second solution.
So the hypothesis $\det A \ne 0$ is also necessary for a unique solution.

### Generalizations

Over any commutative ring $K$, every solution of $Ax = b$ satisfies $x_i \det A = \det A_i$ for each $i$.
The [[second proof|~97eedb97]] shows this.
Mathlib states the rule in this form: the vector $(\det A_i)_i$ equals $\operatorname{adj}(A)\, b$.

## History

Cramer published the rule for an arbitrary number of unknowns in 1750.
Maclaurin had published special cases of the rule in 1748.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Cramer's rule gives each unknown as a quotient of two determinants.
The denominator is always $\det A$.
For the numerator of unknown $i$, put the right side $b$ in column $i$ of $A$.

### L2 human

Solve $2x + y = 5$ and $x + 3y = 10$.

1. $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\det A = 6 - 1 = 5$.
2. $A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 10 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\det A_1 = 15 - 10 = 5$ and $x = 5/5 = 1$.
3. $A_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\det A_2 = 20 - 5 = 15$ and $y = 15/5 = 3$.
4. Check by substitution: $2 \cdot 1 + 3 = 5$ and $1 + 3 \cdot 3 = 10$.
5. Check by elimination: subtract twice the second equation from the first to get $-5y = -15$, so $y = 3$ and $x = 10 - 9 = 1$.

A common error is to replace a row of $A$ by $b$, not a column.
Here this error gives the correct numbers only because $A$ is symmetric.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $b = (3, 1)$, the solution is $x = 2$, $y = 1$.
Replacing row 1 by $b$ gives $\det \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 3$, so the row version gives the wrong value $x = 3$.

### L3 human

The rule shows that the solution $A^{-1} b$ is a rational function of the entries of $A$ and $b$, with denominator $\det A$.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$, it bounds the denominators of a rational solution by $\det A$.
