## Statement

To check that a matrix is scalar, it is enough to test it against the matrices with a single entry $1$.
Exactly: let $R$ be a ring with identity, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A = (a_{kl}) \in M_n(R)$.
If $A E_{ij} = E_{ij} A$ for all [[matrix units|4H93XF4RVH]] $E_{ij}$ with $1 \le i, j \le n$, then $A = a_{11} I_n$, a [[scalar matrix|0FRM62SG7D]].
In particular, a matrix that commutes with every $n \times n$ matrix is scalar.

## Remarks

### What the test computes in size 2

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, the two products with $E_{12}$ are
$$E_{12} A = \begin{pmatrix} c & d \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A E_{12} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & a \\ 0 & c \end{pmatrix}.$$
They agree exactly when $c = 0$ and $d = a$.
The test with $E_{21}$ gives $b = 0$ and $d = a$ in the same way, so $A = aI$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

Commuting with the diagonal matrix units $E_{ii}$ is not enough, because every diagonal matrix commutes with them.
For example, $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ commutes with $E_{11}$ and $E_{22}$ but is not scalar.
Commuting with a single off-diagonal unit is not enough either: in $M_3(\mathbb{R})$ the matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 2)$ commutes with $E_{12}$, since both products equal $E_{12}$, but it is not scalar.
The [[proof|GZFKSNNPG5]] shows that the $n$ units $E_{11}, E_{12}, \dots, E_{1n}$ in the first row already suffice.

### The converse and the consequences

Every scalar matrix $cI$ commutes with every $E_{ij}$, since $(cI)E_{ij} = cE_{ij} = E_{ij}(cI)$.
Here $c$ need not be central in $R$, because the entries of $E_{ij}$ are $0$ and $1$, which commute with everything.
The lemma is the main step in the [[center theorem|DVZ1V4A884]], which identifies the center of a matrix ring.
Over a field it also says that a linear map of $F^n$ that commutes with every linear map is a multiple of the identity.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix units are the simplest matrices: a single $1$ and zeros elsewhere.
If a matrix commutes with all of them, then its entries off the diagonal must be $0$ and its diagonal entries must be equal.
So it is a number times the identity matrix.

### L2 human

Multiplying by $E_{ij}$ on the left copies row $j$ into row $i$, and multiplying on the right copies column $i$ into column $j$; everything else becomes $0$.
Equating the two results gives the equations $a_{jl} = 0$ for $l \ne j$ and $a_{ii} = a_{jj}$, and the [[proof|GZFKSNNPG5]] writes them out.
The typical use is to show that a matrix commuting with a large family of matrices is scalar.
Watch out: the lemma needs the off-diagonal units, since diagonal tests alone only show that $A$ is diagonal.
