## Statement

Let $R$ be a ring with identity, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A \in M_n(R)$.
If $A E_{ij} = E_{ij} A$ for all [[matrix units|4H93XF4RVH]] $E_{ij}$, then $A = cI_n$ with $c = a_{11}$, a [[scalar matrix|0FRM62SG7D]].
In particular, a matrix that commutes with every $n \times n$ matrix is scalar.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- It is not enough to commute with the diagonal matrix units $E_{ii}$: every diagonal matrix does that.
- Commuting with one matrix unit is far from enough. The matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 2)$ commutes with $E_{12}$ in $M_3(\mathbb{R})$, but it is not scalar.

### Consequences

- The [[centre of a matrix ring|DVZ1V4A884]] consists of the scalar matrices $cI$ with $c$ central in $R$.
- A linear operator on $F^n$ that commutes with every operator is a multiple of the identity.

### Converse

- Every scalar matrix $cI$ commutes with every $E_{ij}$, since $(cI)E_{ij} = cE_{ij} = E_{ij}(cI)$; here $c$ need not be central, because the entries of $E_{ij}$ are $0$ and $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix units are the simplest matrices, with a single $1$.
If a matrix commutes with all of them, its diagonal entries must be equal and its other entries must be zero.

### L2 human

Multiplying by $E_{ij}$ on the left copies row $j$ to row $i$, and on the right copies column $i$ to column $j$.
Equating the two results gives the equations $a_{jl} = 0$ for $l \neq j$ and $a_{ii} = a_{jj}$; the [[proof|GZFKSNNPG5]] writes them out.
