## Summary

Elementary row operations are the three moves of elimination, made precise.
We may swap two rows, multiply a row by a nonzero number, or add a multiple of one row to another row.
Each move can be undone by a move of the same kind, so on an [[augmented matrix|0T6MWGZSXA]] none of them gains or loses a solution, by the [[theorem on row operations|K41GT10YM6]].
They are the only steps of [[Gaussian elimination|0JVJ2WCXZH]], and every result about row reduction rests on them.
The [[formal definition|0DE5R5EB4D]] states them for a [[matrix|0VD9ZSWWJG]] over a [[field|04C5ZBG1WT]].

## Motivation

To solve $x + y = 3$, $x - y = 1$ by hand, we add the two equations and get $2x = 4$.
Then we divide by $2$ and get $x = 2$.
Sometimes we also write the equations in another order, to have a convenient equation on top.

These three moves suffice to solve every linear system.
Stating them exactly does two things.
It lets us prove that elimination gives the right answer, because we can check each kind of move once and for all.
And it lets a computer carry out elimination, since the moves are mechanical.

## Notation

For a matrix with rows $R_1, \dots, R_m$ we write the three types as follows.

1. Swap, or interchange: $R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$ with $i \ne j$.
2. Scale: $R_i \leftarrow cR_i$ with $c \ne 0$, also written $cR_i \to R_i$.
3. Replacement, or row addition: $R_i \leftarrow R_i + cR_j$ with $i \ne j$, also written $R_i + cR_j \to R_i$.

Lay's textbook calls the three types "interchange", "scaling" and "replacement".

## Remarks

### The three moves on one matrix

Start with $M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.
The replacement $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 3R_1$ gives $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$, with a zero below the first entry.
The scaling $R_2 \leftarrow -\tfrac12 R_2$ then gives the row $(0, 1)$, with a leading $1$.
A swap $R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2$ is useful when the top entry of a column is $0$ and a lower entry is not.
[[The example with inverses|2CETQX9HTP]] does each move and undoes it.

### Each move is a matrix product

Doing a row operation to the identity $I_m$ gives an [[elementary matrix|01REXW3WWE]] $E$.
Doing the same operation to any matrix $M$ with $m$ rows gives the product $EM$.
For the replacement above, $E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, and indeed $EM = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$.
So a whole sequence of row operations is left multiplication by one invertible matrix.

### What the moves keep

On an augmented matrix, each move keeps the solution set, by the [[theorem on row operations|K41GT10YM6]].
Row operations also keep the row space and the null space, by the theorem on [[row equivalence|E4GKK727ZM]].
They keep the linear relations among the columns: if $\sum_j x_j a_j = 0$ for the columns $a_j$ of $M$, then the same relation holds for the columns of $EM$.
This last fact is why the pivot columns of a reduced matrix point to the pivot columns of the original one.

### A swap from the other two moves

The swap is not strictly needed.
The four steps $R_i \leftarrow R_i + R_j$, $R_j \leftarrow R_j - R_i$, $R_i \leftarrow R_i + R_j$, $R_j \leftarrow -R_j$ turn the rows $(a, b)$ into $(a + b, b)$, then $(a + b, -a)$, then $(b, -a)$, and finally $(b, a)$.
We keep the swap anyway, because it is one step instead of four.

### What is not a row operation

Multiplying a row by $0$ is not allowed, because it throws an equation away and can add solutions.
Replacing $R_1$ by $R_1 + R_2$ and $R_2$ by $R_2 + R_1$ at the same time, both from the old rows, is not allowed either.
[[The non-examples|EBR5JMWFAE]] show how both mistakes enlarge the solution set of $x + y = 3$, $x - y = 1$.
Squaring a row entry by entry is not a row operation at all, because it is not linear.

### Watch out

Always say which row changes.
The note "$R_1 - 3R_2$" without an arrow does not say whether $R_1$ or $R_2$ is replaced.
And apply each move to the whole row of the augmented matrix, right side included.

### History

Elimination by these moves is the method of the "Fangcheng" chapter of the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*.
Liu Hui wrote his commentary on that book in the 3rd century.

### Try it

Apply $R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2$ and then $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$ to the rows $(0, 1, 4)$ and $(1, 3, 2)$.
The swap gives $(1, 3, 2)$ and $(0, 1, 4)$, and the replacement gives $(1, 3, 2)$ and $(-2, -5, 0)$.
Which single move undoes the replacement?
It is $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 + 2R_1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The three row operations are the moves we make when we solve equations by hand.
We can swap two equations, multiply an equation by a nonzero number, or add a multiple of one equation to another.
For example, from $x + y = 3$ and $x - y = 1$, adding the equations gives $2x = 4$.
None of these moves gains or loses a solution, and each move can be undone.

### L2 human

We check the four-step swap on $M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, so $R_1 = (1, 2)$ and $R_2 = (3, 4)$.

1. $R_1 \leftarrow R_1 + R_2$ gives the rows $(4, 6)$ and $(3, 4)$.
2. $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1$ gives the rows $(4, 6)$ and $(-1, -2)$.
3. $R_1 \leftarrow R_1 + R_2$ gives the rows $(3, 4)$ and $(-1, -2)$.
4. $R_2 \leftarrow -R_2$ gives the rows $(3, 4)$ and $(1, 2)$, which is the swap.

The typical use of row operations is elimination: use one row to create zeros in the other rows.

### L3 human

Over a field, the elementary matrices generate the general linear group $\mathrm{GL}_m(F)$.
So row equivalence classes are the orbits of $\mathrm{GL}_m(F)$ acting on matrices by left multiplication.
Each orbit contains exactly one matrix in reduced row echelon form, by the [[uniqueness theorem|BF9SMZMJZE]].
Column operations are right multiplications by elementary matrices, and using both kinds gives the normal form $\begin{bmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, where $r$ is the rank.
Over the integers, scaling is allowed only by $\pm 1$, and the same idea gives the Hermite and Smith normal forms.
