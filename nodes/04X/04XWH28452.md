## Summary

Let $F$ be a field and let $M$ be a matrix over $F$ with rows $R_1, \dots, R_m$.
The elementary row operations on $M$ are: swapping two rows $R_i$ and $R_j$ with $i \ne j$; replacing $R_i$ by $cR_i$ with $c \in F$, $c \ne 0$; and replacing $R_i$ by $R_i + cR_j$ with $i \ne j$ and $c \in F$.
The exact definition is in [[the definition node|0DE5R5EB4D]].
Each operation can be undone by an operation of the same type, and on an [[augmented matrix|0T6MWGZSXA]] none of them changes the solution set, see [[the theorem|~6410ee51]].
They matter because they are the only steps of [[Gaussian elimination|0JVJ2WCXZH]], and every result about row reduction rests on them.

## Motivation

When we solve two equations by hand, we add a multiple of one equation to the other to remove an unknown.
We sometimes divide an equation by a number, or write the equations in another order.
These three moves suffice to solve every linear system.
Making them precise lets us prove that elimination gives the right answer, and lets a computer do it.

## Notation

1. Swap: $R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$.
2. Scale: $R_i \leftarrow cR_i$, also written $cR_i \to R_i$.
3. Replacement or row addition: $R_i \leftarrow R_i + cR_j$, also written $R_i + cR_j \to R_i$.
4. Lay calls the three types "replacement", "interchange" and "scaling".

## Remarks

### Examples

1. On $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, the step $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 3R_1$ gives $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$, see [[the example with inverses|~e7ca3aaf]].
2. The swap $R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2$ brings a nonzero entry to the top of a column whose first entry is $0$.
3. Scaling $R_2$ by $-\tfrac12$ turns the row $(0, -2 \mid -9)$ into $(0, 1 \mid \tfrac92)$, with a leading $1$.
4. Each operation on $I_m$ gives an [[elementary matrix|01REXW3WWE]], and the operation on $M$ is the product $EM$.

### Non-examples

1. Multiplying a row by $0$ is not elementary, and it can add solutions, see [[the non-examples|~00b8c869]].
2. Replacing $R_1$ by $R_1 + R_2$ and $R_2$ by $R_2 + R_1$ at the same time is not elementary, see [[the non-examples|~00b8c869]].
3. Replacing $R_i$ by $R_i^2$, entry by entry, is not a row operation at all, because it is not linear.

### Basic properties

1. Each operation has an inverse of the same type, see [[the theorem|~6410ee51]].
2. Each operation is left multiplication by an invertible elementary matrix, see [[the theorem|~6410ee51]].
3. On an augmented matrix, each operation keeps the solution set, see [[the theorem|~6410ee51]].
4. Row operations keep the row space and the null space, see [[the theorem on row equivalence|~e3949f1c]].
5. Row operations keep the linear relations among the columns: if $\sum_j x_j a_j = 0$ for the columns $a_j$ of $M$, the same holds for the columns of $EM$.
6. A swap can be written as a sequence of the other two types: $R_i \leftarrow R_i + R_j$, $R_j \leftarrow R_j - R_i$, $R_i \leftarrow R_i + R_j$, $R_j \leftarrow -R_j$.

### History

Elimination with these steps is in the "Fangcheng" chapter of the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*, commented on by Liu Hui in the 3rd century.
The names and the link to elementary matrices are from the 20th century textbook tradition.

### Common errors

1. Multiplying a row by $0$, or by an expression in the unknowns.
2. Doing two additions at once with the old rows.
3. Applying the operation to the coefficient part but not to the right-hand side.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The three row operations are the moves we make when we solve equations by hand.
We can swap two equations, multiply an equation by a nonzero number, or add a multiple of one equation to another.
For example, from $x + y = 3$ and $x - y = 1$, adding the equations gives $2x = 4$.
None of these moves gains or loses a solution, and each move can be undone.

### L2 human

We check property 6 on $M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, so $R_1 = (1, 2)$ and $R_2 = (3, 4)$.

1. $R_1 \leftarrow R_1 + R_2$ gives rows $(4, 6)$ and $(3, 4)$.
2. $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1$ gives rows $(4, 6)$ and $(-1, -2)$.
3. $R_1 \leftarrow R_1 + R_2$ gives rows $(3, 4)$ and $(-1, -2)$.
4. $R_2 \leftarrow -R_2$ gives rows $(3, 4)$ and $(1, 2)$, which is the swap.

The typical use of row operations is elimination: use one row to create zeros in the other rows.
A trap: always write which row changes; "$R_1 - 3R_2$" without an arrow does not say whether $R_1$ or $R_2$ is replaced.

### L3 human

The elementary matrices generate the general linear group $\mathrm{GL}_m(F)$, so row equivalence classes are the orbits of $\mathrm{GL}_m(F)$ acting on matrices by left multiplication.
Each orbit contains exactly one matrix in reduced row echelon form, see [[the uniqueness theorem|BF9SMZMJZE]].
Column operations are right multiplications by elementary matrices; using both gives the normal form $\begin{bmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, where $r$ is the rank.
Over a principal ideal domain, scaling is restricted to units, and the same idea gives the Hermite and Smith normal forms.
