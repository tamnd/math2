## Summary

The determinant of $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the volume of the box that they span, with a sign that says how the vectors are turned.
So a determinant is a measurement: its size is a volume, and its sign is an [[orientation|0QJJAQ6ZYZ]].

Let $n \ge 1$ and let $v_1, \dots, v_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be the columns of a real $n \times n$ matrix $A$.
The *parallelotope* spanned by them is
$$P = \{ t_1 v_1 + \dots + t_n v_n : 0 \le t_i \le 1 \text{ for every } i \},$$
which is the image of the unit cube $[0, 1]^n$ under $x \mapsto Ax$.
The *signed volume* of the ordered family $(v_1, \dots, v_n)$ is the [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] $\det A$.
Its absolute value $\lvert \det A \rvert$ is the $n$-dimensional volume of $P$.
When $\det A \ne 0$, its sign is positive if $(v_1, \dots, v_n)$ has the orientation of the standard basis $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$, and negative if it has the opposite orientation.
The theorem on the [[determinant as a volume scaling factor|RC5BTSG8T2]] proves the statement about the volume and extends it to every region.

## Motivation

Coordinates give lengths through the Pythagorean theorem, and we want areas and volumes in the same direct way, without angles or heights.
The determinant does this: the area of a parallelogram is one difference of two products, and the volume of a box in space is one $3 \times 3$ determinant.

Why keep a sign at all, when a volume is never negative?
With the sign, the volume becomes linear in each edge, and so it obeys the same rules as the determinant.
The sign is also useful by itself.
In computational geometry, the sign of $\det(q - p, r - p)$ tells whether the path $p \to q \to r$ turns left or right, and this one test drives many algorithms for polygons.
In analysis, the same idea gives the [[Jacobian determinant|0ZCSMYHTBP]] in the [[change of variables formula|00N5FHP1CV]].

## Notation

Books write $\det A$, $\det(v_1, \dots, v_n)$ or $\det [v_1 \ \cdots \ v_n]$ for the signed volume, and $\operatorname{vol}(P)$ or $\operatorname{vol}_n(P)$ for the volume.
Some books write $\lvert A \rvert$ for the determinant itself.
In those books the bars do not mean an absolute value, so we write $\lvert \det A \rvert$ for the volume to keep the two meanings apart.

## Remarks

### A first area

Take $v_1 = (4, 1)$ and $v_2 = (2, 3)$ in the plane.
The signed area is
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = 4 \cdot 3 - 2 \cdot 1 = 10.$$
Check by cutting.
The parallelogram has the corners $0$, $(4, 1)$, $(6, 4)$ and $(2, 3)$, and it lies in the rectangle $[0, 6] \times [0, 4]$ of area $24$.
The part of the rectangle outside it consists of two triangles of area $2$, two triangles of area $3$ and two small rectangles of area $2$, in total $14$.
So the area is $24 - 14 = 10$, as the determinant says.

### What the sign means

Swap the two vectors and the box stays the same, but the sign changes:
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 2 \cdot 1 - 4 \cdot 3 = -10.$$
In the plane the sign is positive when the turn from $v_1$ to $v_2$, through the angle less than a half turn, is counterclockwise.
The vector $(4, 1)$ points below $(2, 3)$, so the turn from $(4, 1)$ to $(2, 3)$ is counterclockwise and the sign is $+$.
The reflection $\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ sends the unit square to a square of area $1$, and its determinant is $-1$.

The sign depends on a choice.
We call the standard basis positive, and every other basis gets its sign by comparison with it.
If we had chosen $(e_2, e_1)$ as the positive basis, every sign would flip, while every volume would stay the same.

### Boxes in space

In $\mathbb{R}^3$ take the columns $(2, 0, 0)$, $(1, 3, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 4)$.
The matrix is upper triangular, so by the rule for [[triangular matrices|0K1KKMFHYH]]
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 24,$$
and the [[parallelepiped|0SQV2VJTGY]] has volume $24$.
The sign is $+$, so the three edges form a right-handed system, like the thumb, the index finger and the middle finger of the right hand.
The family $(e_1, e_2, -e_3)$ has determinant $-1$, and it is left-handed.
In space the signed volume of $u, v, w$ is also the scalar triple product $u \cdot (v \times w)$ with the [[cross product|0N5N15HWZ7]].

### Why the determinant is the right formula

Signed volume has the three properties that define the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].
If you stretch one edge by a factor $c$, the volume is multiplied by $c$, and a negative $c$ also reverses the orientation.
If you add a multiple of one edge to another edge, the box is sheared: the base and the height stay the same, so the volume stays the same.
If two edges are equal, the box is flat and its volume is $0$, and the unit cube has volume $1$.
The determinant is the only function of $n$ vectors with these properties, so the signed volume and the determinant agree.

### What it is not

Dependent vectors do not span a box.
For $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 4)$ the parallelogram is a line segment, its area is $0$, and the determinant is $1 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 2 = 0$.
Fewer than $n$ vectors have no determinant at all.
The parallelogram in $\mathbb{R}^3$ on $u = (1, 0, 0)$ and $v = (0, 1, 1)$ has area $\sqrt{\det(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)}$, where $A$ has the columns $u$ and $v$:
$$A^{\mathsf{T}} A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sqrt{\det(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)} = \sqrt{2}.$$
Check: $u$ and $v$ are perpendicular, with lengths $1$ and $\sqrt{2}$, so the area is $\sqrt{2}$.
The [[Cauchy-Binet formula|08WE2W7DYS]] explains this rule.

### Watch out

A volume is $\lvert \det A \rvert$, never $\det A$ itself, so a determinant of $-10$ means an area of $10$.
The rows of $A$ span a different box from the columns, but with the same volume, because $\det A^{\mathsf{T}} = \det A$.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ the columns $(1, 0), (1, 1)$ and the rows $(1, 1), (0, 1)$ give two different parallelograms, each of area $1$.
A triangle on two edges has half the area of the parallelogram, and a tetrahedron on three edges has one sixth of the volume of the box.

### Try it

First, find the area of the triangle with corners $(0, 0)$, $(3, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$.
It is $\tfrac{1}{2} \lvert 3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 \rvert = \tfrac{5}{2}$.

Second, is the family $(1, 2, 0), (0, 1, 0), (0, 0, -1)$ right-handed?
The matrix with these columns is lower triangular with diagonal $1, 1, -1$, so its determinant is $-1$, and the family is left-handed, with volume $1$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two arrows that start at the same point in the plane span a slanted rectangle, a parallelogram.
The determinant of the two arrows gives its area.
For the arrows $(3, 0)$ and $(0, 2)$ the shape is an ordinary rectangle, and the determinant is $3 \cdot 2 - 0 \cdot 0 = 6$, its area.
If you list the arrows in the other order, you get $-6$.
The minus sign does not mean a negative area: it says that you turn clockwise to go from the first arrow to the second.

### L2 human

Here is a typical problem: find the volume of the tetrahedron with corners $p_0 = (1, 0, 0)$, $p_1 = (2, 1, 0)$, $p_2 = (1, 2, 1)$ and $p_3 = (1, 0, 3)$.
The three edges from $p_0$ are $u = (1, 1, 0)$, $v = (0, 2, 1)$ and $w = (0, 0, 3)$.
Put them in the columns and compute:
$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 6,$$
because the matrix is lower triangular.
The box on $u, v, w$ has volume $6$, and the tetrahedron has one sixth of it, so its volume is $1$.
Check with the triple product: $v \times w = (6, 0, 0)$, and $u \cdot (6, 0, 0) = 6$.
The method needs only the coordinates of the corners, which is the typical use of the signed volume.
The trap is to forget the factor $\tfrac{1}{6}$, or to report the signed value when it is negative.

### L3 human

The theorem on the [[volume scaling factor|RC5BTSG8T2]] says more: the map $x \mapsto Ax$ multiplies the volume of every region by $\lvert \det A \rvert$.
For a smooth map, the same holds near each point with the Jacobian determinant, and this is the content of the change of variables formula.
In the language of exterior algebra, $v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_n$ lies in the one-dimensional space $\Lambda^n \mathbb{R}^n$, and the determinant is its coordinate with respect to $e_1 \wedge \dots \wedge e_n$.
For $k < n$ vectors, $v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k$ records a $k$-dimensional signed volume, and its length is $\sqrt{\det(A^{\mathsf{T}} A)}$.
On a manifold an orientation is a consistent choice of sign for these volumes, and that is the starting point of integration of differential forms.
