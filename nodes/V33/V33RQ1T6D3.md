## Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Both are symmetric, and $B$ is [[positive definite|0QQCG3JWJ4]], since its leading minors are $2$ and $2 - 1 = 1$.
We solve the generalized eigenvalue problem $Ap = \lambda Bp$ of the [[pencil|KQ7V0J5CGG]] $A - \lambda B$ and check the [[simultaneous diagonalization|K559AQ9H67]] $P^{\mathsf{T}} B P = I$, $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P = D$.

1. The characteristic equation of the pencil factors:
$$\det(A - \lambda B) = (3 - 2\lambda)(1 - \lambda) - (1 - \lambda)^2 = (1 - \lambda)(2 - \lambda).$$
So $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$.
2. For $\lambda_1 = 1$, the matrix $A - B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has the null vector $p_1 = (0, 1)$.
3. For $\lambda_2 = 2$, the matrix $A - 2B = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the null vector $p_2 = (1, -1)$.
4. Scaling. We have $B p_1 = (1, 1)$ and $p_1^{\mathsf{T}} B p_1 = 1$, and $B p_2 = (1, 0)$ and $p_2^{\mathsf{T}} B p_2 = 1$, so no scaling is needed.
5. $B$-orthogonality. We have $p_1^{\mathsf{T}} B p_2 = (0, 1) \cdot (1, 0) = 0$, as the theorem predicts for distinct $\lambda_i$.
6. Let $P = (p_1 \ p_2) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$. Then $BP = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and
$$P^{\mathsf{T}} B P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
7. In the same way $AP = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and
$$P^{\mathsf{T}} A P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
8. A second check uses $B^{-1} A$. Since $\det B = 1$, we have $B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B^{-1} A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which is triangular with the eigenvalues $2$ and $1$.

## Remarks

The matrix $B^{-1} A$ is not symmetric, yet its eigenvalues are real, as the theorem says they must be.
The vectors $p_1 = (0, 1)$ and $p_2 = (1, -1)$ are not orthogonal for the dot product, since $p_1 \cdot p_2 = -1$; they are orthogonal for the inner product $x^{\mathsf{T}} B y$.
So $P$ is not an orthogonal matrix, and $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$ is a congruence, not a similarity.

Both $\lambda_i$ are positive, so $A$ is positive definite by part 4 of the theorem, which agrees with its leading minors $3$ and $3 - 1 = 2$.
In the coordinates $x = P y$, the two forms are $x^{\mathsf{T}} B x = y_1^2 + y_2^2$ and $x^{\mathsf{T}} A x = y_1^2 + 2 y_2^2$.
If we change the corner $a_{11} = 3$ to $1$, then $A - \lambda B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - 2\lambda & 1 - \lambda \\ 1 - \lambda & 1 - \lambda \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $(1 - \lambda)(-\lambda)$, so the generalized eigenvalues become $0$ and $1$, and the new $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is semidefinite but not definite.

## Explanations

### L1 human

We have two quadratic forms, $3x_1^2 + 2x_1 x_2 + x_2^2$ and $2x_1^2 + 2x_1 x_2 + x_2^2$.
With the new coordinates $x_1 = y_2$ and $x_2 = y_1 - y_2$, the first becomes $y_1^2 + 2y_2^2$ and the second becomes $y_1^2 + y_2^2$.
So one change of coordinates turns both forms into sums of squares at the same time.

### L2 human

The recipe is: solve $\det(A - \lambda B) = 0$, find null vectors, and scale them so that $p^{\mathsf{T}} B p = 1$.
Steps 6 and 7 check the two congruences by multiplication, and step 8 checks the eigenvalues in a second way.
Watch out: the ordinary eigenvalues of $A$ are the roots of $t^2 - 4t + 2$, that is $2 \pm \sqrt2$, and they are not the generalized eigenvalues $1$ and $2$.
