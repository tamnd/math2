## Summary

Information theory is the mathematical study of how information is measured, compressed, stored and sent over noisy channels.
Its basic quantities are the [[Shannon entropy|Y86E072V0V]] of a random variable and the [[mutual information|A83CYGDXFG]] between two random variables.
Its central results are coding theorems, such as [[Shannon's source coding theorem|PC8ASRWARE]] and the [[noisy-channel coding theorem|PR3NJB0SKG]].
They show that these quantities are the exact limits of what any code can achieve.
The subject was founded by Claude Shannon in 1948 and is used in communication engineering, statistics, computer science and physics.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Information theory asks how many bits are really needed to describe something, and how fast one can talk over a line that makes mistakes.
A fair coin toss carries one bit, while a coin that almost always lands heads carries much less, because its result is easy to guess.
Entropy turns this idea into a number.
A compressed file can not be shorter on average than its entropy.
A noisy channel has a capacity: below that rate one can send with as few errors as one likes, and above it one can not.
For example, a message of $1000$ fair coin tosses needs about $1000$ bits, but $1000$ tosses of a coin with heads probability $0.99$ can be compressed to about $81$ bits.
