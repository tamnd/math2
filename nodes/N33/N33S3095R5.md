## Example

Let $G$ be a finite connected [[graph|00C0BR7D7H]] without loops, with a distinguished vertex $s$, the *sink*.
Let $V$ be the set of the other vertices.
For $x \in V$, let $\deg(x)$ be its degree in $G$.
A *sandpile* is a map $\eta \colon V \to \Z_{\ge 0}$, the number of grains at each vertex.
A vertex $x$ is *unstable* if $\eta(x) \ge \deg(x)$.
*Toppling* an unstable vertex $x$ removes $\deg(x)$ grains from $x$ and adds one grain to each neighbour of $x$ for each edge joining them.
Grains sent to $s$ are lost.
A sandpile is *stable* if no vertex is unstable.
The *abelian sandpile model* is a Markov chain on stable sandpiles.
At each step, one grain is added at a uniformly random vertex of $V$, and unstable vertices are toppled until the sandpile is stable.

Check on the triangle with vertices $1, 2, s$, where every vertex has degree $2$.
Start from the stable sandpile $(\eta(1), \eta(2)) = (1, 1)$ and add a grain at $1$, which gives $(2, 1)$.
Vertex $1$ topples: $(0, 2)$, and one grain goes to $s$.
Vertex $2$ topples: $(1, 0)$, and one grain goes to $s$.
The result $(1, 0)$ is stable, and the process stops.

## Remarks

Since $G$ is connected and every toppling sends grains toward the sink, every sandpile becomes stable after finitely many topplings.
The *abelian property* was proved by Deepak Dhar in 1990.
It says that the final stable sandpile and the number of topplings at each vertex do not depend on the order of the topplings.
The *recurrent* sandpiles, those visited infinitely often by the chain, form an [[abelian group|082GHNPJ53]] under addition followed by stabilization, the *sandpile group*.
It is isomorphic to $\Z^V / \Delta \Z^V$, where $\Delta$ is the reduced [[Laplacian matrix|TN6M6DVZRP]] of $G$, obtained by deleting the row and column of $s$.
By the [[matrix tree theorem|S1PG1N2VEX]], its order is the number of [[spanning trees|0S4ZEZM7KE]] of $G$.
In the triangle above there are $3$ spanning trees, and the recurrent sandpiles are $(1, 1)$, $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$; the stable sandpile $(0, 0)$ is not recurrent.
The model was introduced by Per Bak, Chao Tang and Kurt Wiesenfeld in 1987 as the first example of [[self-organized criticality|FVJ624HTV0]].
On large boxes of $\Z^2$ the sizes of avalanches appear to follow a power law, without any parameter being tuned.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Drop grains of sand one by one on a grid of sites.
When a site holds too many grains, it topples and passes one grain to each neighbour, which may make the neighbours topple too, in an avalanche.
Grains that fall off the edge of the grid are lost.
Surprisingly, the final result does not depend on the order in which the sites topple.
After a long time the pile sits in a delicate state in which avalanches of all sizes occur.
