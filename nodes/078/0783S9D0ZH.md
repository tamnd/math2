## Statement

Inverting a matrix inverts its determinant, and so a change of basis never changes a determinant.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $A$ be an [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] $n \times n$ matrix over $K$, and let $\det$ be the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].

1. The element $\det A$ is a [[unit|019NFGFPR5]] of $K$, and $\det(A^{-1}) = (\det A)^{-1}$.
2. For every $n \times n$ matrix $M$ over $K$, $\det(A^{-1} M A) = \det M$. So similar matrices have the same determinant.

## Remarks

### A check with numbers

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\det A = 4 - 6 = -2$.
The [[inverse of a 2 by 2 matrix|8XWC7RKNN1]] is
$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{-2} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 3/2 & -1/2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Its determinant is
$$(-2)\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right) - 1 \cdot \tfrac{3}{2} = 1 - \tfrac{3}{2} = -\tfrac{1}{2},$$
which is $1 / \det A$, as part 1 says.
The [[proof|KS54E31XWV]] takes the determinant of both sides of $A A^{-1} = I_n$.

### Why invertibility is needed

The matrix $A$ must be invertible, so that $A^{-1}$ exists at all.
Over a field this means $\det A \ne 0$, by the [[invertibility criterion|FBSAPTP1PR]].
Over a ring the condition is stronger: over $\mathbb{Z}$ it means $\det A = \pm 1$, by the [[criterion over commutative rings|03Q7RE2HXR]].
For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $2 \ne 0$, but it has no inverse with integer entries, and indeed $1/2$ is not an integer.

### What it gives you

Over a field $F$, part 1 reads $\det(A^{-1}) = 1 / \det A$.
Part 2 says that the determinant is an invariant of similarity, like the trace and the [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]].
So the [[determinant of an endomorphism|0X0HQYSZ0K]] can be defined as the determinant of its matrix in any basis, since two bases give similar matrices.
Part 1 also gives a quick test: an integer matrix whose inverse also has integer entries must have $\det A \det(A^{-1}) = 1$ with both factors integers, so $\det A = \pm 1$.

### The converse

Equal determinants do not imply similarity.
The matrices $I_2$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ both have determinant $1$.
They are not similar, because $P^{-1} I_2 P = I_2$ for every invertible $P$, so $I_2$ is similar only to itself.

### Generalizations

For an automorphism $f$ of a finite-dimensional vector space, $\det(f^{-1}) = (\det f)^{-1}$.
Over a commutative ring a one-sided inverse is enough.
If $AB = I_n$, then $\det A \det B = 1$, so $\det A$ is a unit, and the [[adjugate identity|0528HKWZ1Z]] then shows that $A$ is invertible and $BA = I_n$.

## History

The rule is a direct consequence of the product rule for determinants, which Cauchy and Binet each proved in 1812.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The inverse matrix undoes what the matrix does.
If $A$ makes every area $3$ times bigger, then $A^{-1}$ must make every area $3$ times smaller.
So the determinant of the inverse is one divided by the determinant, here $1/3$.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: the determinant of a map does not depend on the coordinates you choose.
Let $M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so $P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
First compute $MP = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, and then
$$P^{-1} M P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The new matrix looks different, but its determinant is $6$, the same as $\det M = 2 \cdot 3 = 6$.
Watch out: $\det(A^{-1})$ is $1 / \det A$, not $-\det A$.
For $A = 2 I_2$ we have $\det A = 4$, and the correct value is $\det(\tfrac{1}{2} I_2) = \tfrac{1}{4}$, while $-\det A = -4$.

### L3 human

Part 1 says that $\det$ maps the group of units $\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$ of the ring $M_n(K)$ into the group of units $K^{\times}$, as every multiplicative map that sends $1$ to $1$ must.
Part 2 says that $\det$ is a class function for the conjugation action of $\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$ on $M_n(K)$.
The same is true of every coefficient of the characteristic polynomial, and the determinant is one of them, up to sign.
