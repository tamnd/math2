## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring and let $A$ be an [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] $n \times n$ matrix over $K$.
Let $\det$ be the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]].

1. $\det A$ is a [[unit|019NFGFPR5]] of $K$, and $\det(A^{-1}) = (\det A)^{-1}$.
2. For every $n \times n$ matrix $M$ over $K$, $\det(A^{-1} M A) = \det M$. So similar matrices have the same determinant.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

$A$ must be invertible, so that $A^{-1}$ exists.
Over a field the condition is $\det A \neq 0$; over a ring such as $\mathbb{Z}$ it is $\det A = \pm 1$, by the [[criterion over commutative rings|03Q7RE2HXR]].

### Consequences

- Over a field $F$, $\det(A^{-1}) = 1 / \det A$.
- The determinant is an invariant of similarity, like the trace and the [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]].
- So the [[determinant of an endomorphism|0X0HQYSZ0K]] does not depend on the basis.
- An integer matrix with an integer inverse has determinant $\pm 1$.

### Converse

Equal determinants do not imply similarity.
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ both have determinant $1$.
They are not similar, because $P^{-1} I_2 P = I_2$ for every invertible $P$.

### Generalizations

For a linear automorphism $f$ of a finite-dimensional space, $\det(f^{-1}) = (\det f)^{-1}$.
For a one-sided inverse over a commutative ring, $AB = I_n$ already implies $\det A \det B = 1$, and then $BA = I_n$ by the [[adjugate identity|0528HKWZ1Z]].

## History

The rule follows from the multiplication theorem, which Binet and Cauchy presented on the same day, 30 November 1812.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The inverse matrix undoes the matrix.
If $A$ scales areas or volumes by $3$, then $A^{-1}$ scales them by $1/3$.
So the determinant of the inverse is one divided by the determinant.

### L2 human

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, with $\det A = -2$.

1. By the [[inverse of a 2 × 2 matrix|8XWC7RKNN1]], $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{-2} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 3/2 & -1/2 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Directly, $\det(A^{-1}) = (-2)(-1/2) - 1 \cdot 3/2 = 1 - 3/2 = -1/2$.
3. This agrees with $1 / \det A = -1/2$.

The typical use is to see that a change of basis does not change $\det$.
A trap: $\det(A^{-1})$ is $1 / \det A$, not $-\det A$.

### L3 human

Part 1 says that $\det$ maps the group of units $\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$ of $M_n(K)$ into the group of units $K^{\times}$.
Part 2 says that $\det$ is a class function on $M_n(K)$ under the conjugation action of $\mathrm{GL}_n(K)$.
