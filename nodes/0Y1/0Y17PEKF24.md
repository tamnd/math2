## Definition

A diagonalizable matrix is a square matrix that becomes diagonal after a change of basis.
Let $F$ be a field and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$.
The matrix $A$ is *diagonalizable over $F$* when there is an [[invertible|0TWTS88TWN]] $n \times n$ matrix $P$ over $F$ such that $P^{-1} A P$ is a [[diagonal matrix|0Q9PGXD9E4]].
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$.
An [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]] $T \colon V \to V$ is *diagonalizable* when there is a basis of $V$ in which the [[matrix of $T$|0YYMY95FWS]] is diagonal.

## Notation

We write $P^{-1} A P = D$, or equivalently $A = P D P^{-1}$, with $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$.
The words "over $F$" name the field in which the entries of $P$ and $D$ must lie.

## Remarks

The definition says that $A$ is [[similar|027KDW2K72]] to a diagonal matrix.
The matrix $P$ must be invertible, because $P^{-1} A P$ has to make sense and $P$ has to be a change of basis.
The entries of $P$ must lie in $F$, and this is where the field enters.
For example, the rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ becomes diagonal with a complex $P$, but with no real $P$, so it is diagonalizable over $\C$ and not over $\R$.

The two halves of the definition agree.
The matrix of $T$ in one basis is a diagonalizable matrix exactly when its matrix in every basis is one, by the [[change of basis formula|0MQXVZEBMT]], and $T$ is diagonalizable exactly when these matrices are.
When $P^{-1} A P = D$, the columns of $P$ are eigenvectors of $A$ and the entries of $D$ are the eigenvalues, by the [[characterization by eigenvectors|056SW1E0AC]].
A case that fails is the shear $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$: its only eigenvalue is $1$, so $D$ would be $I$, and then $A = P I P^{-1} = I$, which is false.
The concept page on [[diagonalizable matrices|07SQCNV233]] has more examples and the tests.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A diagonalizable matrix becomes a diagonal matrix when we describe it in a good choice of axes.
In those axes, the map only stretches each axis by its own factor.
For example, a map may stretch one axis by $2$ and leave another axis as it is.
This makes powers and other functions of the matrix easy to compute.

### L2 human

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
The inverse is $P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, as $P P^{-1} = I$ shows.
First multiply $A$ by $P$:
$$A P = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Then multiply by $P^{-1}$ on the left:
$$P^{-1} A P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
So $A$ is diagonalizable, with the eigenvalues $2$ and $1$ and the eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(0, 1)$, the columns of $P$.
Watch out: a triangular matrix is not always diagonalizable, and the [[test by multiplicities|CDB8KCNNG9]] tells when it is.

### L3 human

An endomorphism is diagonalizable exactly when $V$ is a direct sum of one-dimensional invariant subspaces.
In the language of modules over $F[x]$, with $x$ acting as $T$, this says that $V$ is a direct sum of copies of the modules $F[x]/(x - \lambda)$.
