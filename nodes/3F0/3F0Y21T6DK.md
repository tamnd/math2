## Example

Let $\theta(n, p)$ be the $\Delta^0_0$ formula
$$p > n \wedge p > 1 \wedge \forall a \le p\, \forall b \le p\, (a \cdot b = p \rightarrow a = 1 \vee b = 1).$$
So $\forall n\, \exists p\, \theta(n, p)$ says that there are infinitely many primes.

1. [[WKL0|K5YPW1NTHZ]] proves $\forall n\, \exists p\, \theta(n, p)$. Euclid's proof works in $\mathsf{RCA}_0$: the least divisor $p > 1$ of $n! + 1$ is prime, and it is larger than $n$, since no number $2, \ldots, n$ divides $n! + 1$.
2. The sentence is $\Pi^0_2$ with a $\Delta^0_0$ matrix, so the [[conservation theorem|V609D9SZ05]] applies.
3. It gives a primitive recursive function $f$ with $\mathsf{PRA} \vdash \theta(n, f(n))$.
4. In this case one such $f$ is visible: $f(n)$ is the least $p$ with $1 < p \le n! + 1$ and $p \mid n! + 1$. It is primitive recursive, because the search is bounded by the primitive recursive function $n! + 1$.
5. [[Primitive recursive arithmetic|330YAP9Y4Q]] proves $\theta(n, f(n))$ by the argument of step 1, with $f(n)$ in place of the least divisor.

## Remarks

The point of the theorem is that step 3 holds for every $\Pi^0_2$ theorem of $\mathsf{WKL}_0$, also when its proof uses a path through an infinite binary tree, which no primitive recursive function can compute in general.
The ideal proof then still yields a primitive recursive witness function and a finitistic proof of each instance.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The statement "for every $n$ there is a prime above $n$" has a proof in $\mathsf{WKL}_0$.
The conservation theorem promises a simple program that finds such a prime, and here the program is: search the divisors of $n! + 1$.
