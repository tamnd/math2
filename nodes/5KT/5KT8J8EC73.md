## Statement

Let $S$ and $S'$ be inertial frames with coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$ and $(t', x', y', z')$.
Let $S'$ move with velocity $v$ along the $x$ axis of $S$, where $0 < \abs{v} < c$.
Let two events have coordinate differences $\Delta t = 0$ and $\Delta x \ne 0$ in $S$, so they are simultaneous in $S$.
Then in $S'$ their time difference is
$$\Delta t' = -\gamma \frac{v \, \Delta x}{c^2} \ne 0,$$
where $\gamma$ is the [[Lorentz factor|2E9AJVZS6E]].
So the two events are not simultaneous in $S'$.

## Remarks

Proof idea: the [[Lorentz transformation|WEVGN4C7PQ]] gives $t' = \gamma (t - v x / c^2)$; take differences with $\Delta t = 0$.
Example: with $c = 1$, $v = 0.6$ and $\Delta x = 1$, one finds $\Delta t' = -1.25 \times 0.6 = -0.75$.
Simultaneity is only relative for spacelike separated events: if two events are timelike or lightlike separated, every inertial frame puts them in the same time order.
If $\Delta x = 0$, then $\Delta t' = 0$: events that are simultaneous in $S$ and separated only perpendicular to the motion stay simultaneous in $S'$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Two lightning strikes that happen at the same moment for one observer can happen one after the other for an observer moving past.
There is no absolute "now" shared by all observers.
This never mixes up cause and effect, because it only affects events too far apart for light to connect them.

### L2 human

Relativity of simultaneity is the term $-v x / c^2$ in the time part of the Lorentz transformation, and it is the key to resolving most paradoxes of special relativity.
Length contraction depends on it, since measuring a length means locating both ends at the same time.
In a spacetime diagram, the lines of constant $t'$ are tilted lines, and the tilt grows with $v$.
