## Example

Let $v = (1, 2, 3) \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
The map $w \mapsto v \times w$ is linear, and we check that its matrix is
$$[v]_\times = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -3 & 2 \\ 3 & 0 & -1 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. The diagonal is $0$, and $a_{12} = -3 = -a_{21}$, $a_{13} = 2 = -a_{31}$ and $a_{23} = -1 = -a_{32}$, so $[v]_\times$ is [[skew-symmetric|03RNVFDEAN]].
2. For $w = (1, 0, 0)$ the cross product is
$$v \times w = (2 \cdot 0 - 3 \cdot 0, \; 3 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 0, \; 1 \cdot 0 - 2 \cdot 1) = (0, 3, -2).$$
3. The [[matrix-vector product|0CCQV3884B]] $[v]_\times w$ is the first column of $[v]_\times$, which is $(0, 3, -2)$, so the two agree.
4. For $w = (0, 1, 1)$,
$$v \times w = (2 - 3, \; 0 - 1, \; 1 - 0) = (-1, -1, 1), \qquad [v]_\times w = (-3 + 2, \; 0 - 1, \; 1 + 0) = (-1, -1, 1).$$
5. For $w = v$, $[v]_\times v = (0 - 6 + 6, \; 3 + 0 - 3, \; -2 + 2 + 0) = 0$, which matches $v \times v = 0$.

## Remarks

For a general $v = (v_1, v_2, v_3)$ the same computation gives
$$[v]_\times = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -v_3 & v_2 \\ v_3 & 0 & -v_1 \\ -v_2 & v_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Every real skew-symmetric $3 \times 3$ matrix has this form, so $v \mapsto [v]_\times$ is a linear bijection from $\mathbb{R}^3$ onto the real skew-symmetric $3 \times 3$ matrices, a space of dimension $3 = 3 \cdot 2/2$.

Step 5 shows that $[v]_\times$ is not invertible, and indeed every real skew-symmetric matrix of odd order has determinant $0$.
Skew-symmetry also gives $w \cdot ([v]_\times w) = 0$ for every $w$, which is the familiar fact that $v \times w$ is perpendicular to $w$; in step 4, $(0, 1, 1) \cdot (-1, -1, 1) = 0$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The cross product with a fixed vector can be written as multiplication by a matrix.
That matrix has zeros on the diagonal and opposite numbers in mirror positions.
Multiplying it by a vector gives the same answer as the cross product formula.

### L2 human

The typical use is to turn a cross product into a matrix product, so that the tools of matrices apply.
In mechanics, a body turning with angular velocity $\omega$ moves its point at position $r$ with velocity $\omega \times r = [\omega]_\times r$.
Watch out: the order matters, since $w \times v = -v \times w$ is given by $-[v]_\times = [v]_\times^{\mathsf{T}}$, not by $[v]_\times$.
