## Statement

The determinant of a square matrix times its central minor equals a $2 \times 2$ determinant built from four corner minors, one size smaller than the matrix.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 3$, and let $M$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$.
Let $M^{i}_{j}$ be the matrix obtained from $M$ by deleting row $i$ and column $j$.
Let $M^{1,n}_{1,n}$ be the $(n-2) \times (n-2)$ matrix obtained by deleting rows $1$ and $n$ and columns $1$ and $n$.
Then
$$\det M \cdot \det M^{1,n}_{1,n} = \det M^{1}_{1} \det M^{n}_{n} - \det M^{1}_{n} \det M^{n}_{1},$$
where $\det$ is the [[determinant|0C3CFHDN6R]], and the four determinants on the right are [[minors|0SD528P0EK]] of order $n - 1$.

## Remarks

### A first computation

Take
$$M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The central minor is the single entry $5$.
The four corner minors of order $2$ delete one corner row and one corner column:
$$\det M^1_1 = \det \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix} = 2, \quad \det M^3_3 = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = -3,$$
$$\det M^1_3 = \det \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 5 \\ 7 & 8 \end{pmatrix} = -3, \quad \det M^3_1 = \det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix} = -3.$$
The right side is $2 \cdot (-3) - (-3)(-3) = -15$.
So the identity gives $5 \det M = -15$, and $\det M = -3$.
Check by expansion along the first row: $1 \cdot 2 - 2 \cdot (40 - 42) + 3 \cdot (32 - 35) = 2 + 4 - 9 = -3$.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The ring must be commutative, as in every use of the determinant.
The size must be at least $3$, so that the central minor $M^{1,n}_{1,n}$ is a real submatrix.
For $n = 2$ the identity still holds with the convention that the empty determinant is $1$, and it reads $\det M = m_{22} m_{11} - m_{21} m_{12}$, which is the formula for a $2 \times 2$ determinant.

### What it gives you

The right side is the $2 \times 2$ determinant of the matrix of corner minors
$$\begin{pmatrix} \det M^1_1 & \det M^1_n \\ \det M^n_1 & \det M^n_n \end{pmatrix}.$$
When the central minor is a unit, we can solve for the determinant:
$$\det M = \frac{\det M^1_1 \det M^n_n - \det M^1_n \det M^n_1}{\det M^{1,n}_{1,n}}.$$
This is the single step of [[Dodgson condensation|RQ44FKS5QM]], which applies it to all connected submatrices, one size at a time.

### Watch out

The minor $M^1_n$ deletes row $1$ and column $n$, while $M^n_1$ deletes row $n$ and column $1$, and the two are different in general.
In the example above both are $-3$ by chance.
For the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ they are $\det M^1_3 = \det \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = 0$ and $\det M^3_1 = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1$.
The identity also does not determine $\det M$ when the central minor is $0$, because then the left side is $0$ for every value of $\det M$.

### Generalizations

The identity is the case $k = n - 2$ of [[Sylvester's determinant identity|7SSKBQYFW1]], after the interior rows and columns are moved to the front.
The [[proof by the adjugate|DBJZFHD9PR]] multiplies $M$ by a matrix built from two columns of the adjugate.
The same proof works for any two rows $p < q$ and any two columns $r < s$ in place of $1$ and $n$, and the identity keeps the same form.
For example, with rows $1, 2$ and columns $1, 2$ of the $3 \times 3$ matrix above, $\det M \cdot m_{33} = (-3) \cdot 10 = -30$, and $\det M^1_1 \det M^2_2 - \det M^1_2 \det M^2_1 = 2 \cdot (-11) - (-2)(-4) = -30$.

## History

The identity carries the names of Desnanot and of Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi.
It is also called the Lewis Carroll identity, because Charles Dodgson built his condensation method on it.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Take a square table of numbers.
Cross out the first row and first column, and find the determinant of what is left.
Do the same with the last row and last column, and with the two mixed choices.
These four numbers form a little $2 \times 2$ table, and its determinant equals the determinant of the whole table times the determinant of the middle part.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: compute a $4 \times 4$ determinant from smaller ones.
Take
$$M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

1. The corner minors of order $3$ are $\det M^1_1 = -3$, $\det M^4_4 = -7$, $\det M^1_4 = 1$ and $\det M^4_1 = 7$, each by the [[rule of Sarrus|7H2P40X0MV]].
   For example, $\det M^4_4 = \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1 + 0 + 0 - 0 - 2 - 6 = -7$.
2. The right side is $(-3)(-7) - 1 \cdot 7 = 14$.
3. The central minor is $\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = -1$.
4. So $\det M \cdot (-1) = 14$, and $\det M = -14$.
5. Check by the [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]] along row $1$, whose minors are $-3, 5, 7, 1$: $1 \cdot (-3) - 2 \cdot 5 + 0 \cdot 7 - 1 \cdot 1 = -14$.

The identity replaced one determinant of size $4$ by four of size $3$ and one of size $2$, and this trade is what condensation repeats.

### L3 human

The identity is a quadratic relation among the minors of a matrix.
Read as a recurrence on connected minors, it is the octahedron recurrence of algebraic combinatorics.
Every quantity that this recurrence produces is a Laurent polynomial in its starting values, a first case of the Laurent phenomenon of [[cluster algebras|TKK0TV2MYC]].
Alternating sign matrices arose in the work of Robbins and Rumsey on a deformation of this recurrence.
