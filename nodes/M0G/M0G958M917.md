## Statement

The theorem says that orthogonality reads the same in both directions only for symmetric forms, alternating forms and, over $\mathbb{C}$, scalar multiples of Hermitian forms.
Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $F$.
A [[bilinear form|0P799D6AMN]] $B$ on $V$ is *reflexive* when, for all $u, v \in V$, $B(u, v) = 0$ implies $B(v, u) = 0$.

1. A bilinear form $B$ on $V$ is reflexive if and only if $B$ is [[symmetric|0744CN4MDA]] or [[alternating|08ZQAKBKHF]].

Now let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{C}$, and let $h$ be a [[sesquilinear form|0W44K2VNBD]] on $V$, linear in the first slot and conjugate-linear in the second.
Call $h$ reflexive when $h(u, v) = 0$ implies $h(v, u) = 0$ for all $u, v \in V$.

2. A sesquilinear form $h$ on $V$ is reflexive if and only if $c\,h$ is [[Hermitian|8NJ7XY6F1S]] for some $c \in \mathbb{C}$ with $\lvert c \rvert = 1$.

Neither part assumes that $V$ has finite dimension or that the form is nondegenerate.
The [[proof|VMSAG76TPX]] compares the two linear forms $B(u, \cdot)$ and $B(\cdot, u)$, which have the same kernel.

## Remarks

### A form that is not reflexive

The [[form with matrix rows (1, 2) and (0, 3)|N9CJZW5596]] on $\mathbb{Q}^2$ is neither symmetric nor alternating.
It has $B(e_2, e_1) = A_{21} = 0$ but $B(e_1, e_2) = A_{12} = 2$, so $e_2$ is orthogonal to $e_1$ in one order only.
So the theorem is sharp: a form outside the two classes always has such a pair.

### What the two classes look like in each characteristic

When $1 + 1 \ne 0$, alternating and skew-symmetric are the same, by the [[relation of the two notions|SX9CD5NEPD]], so part 1 reads: $B$ is reflexive exactly when $B(v, u) = B(u, v)$ for all $u, v$, or $B(v, u) = -B(u, v)$ for all $u, v$.
When $1 + 1 = 0$, every alternating form is symmetric, so part 1 reads: $B$ is reflexive exactly when it is symmetric.
In both cases there is no mixture: a reflexive form cannot be symmetric on some pairs and skew on others.

### Why the scalar is needed in part 2

The factor $c$ cannot be dropped.
Let $h_0$ be the [[standard inner product on complex space|TFZ8DYVJ1J]] and $h = i h_0$.
Then $h(v, u) = i\, \overline{h_0(u, v)} = -\overline{h(u, v)}$, so $h$ is skew-Hermitian, and it is reflexive because its zeros are those of $h_0$.
It is not Hermitian, since $h(e_1, e_1) = i$ is not real, but $c = -i$ gives $c\,h = h_0$, which is Hermitian.

### What it gives you

For a reflexive form there is one [[orthogonal complement|01TXN7ETH2]] $W^\perp$ of each subspace and one radical, so the left and right versions coincide.
This is why the theory of orthogonality, isotropic vectors, hyperbolic planes and Witt's theorem is written for symmetric, alternating and Hermitian forms only.
For example, the symmetric form $x_1 y_1 - x_2 y_2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ has $(1, 1)^\perp = \operatorname{span}\{(1, 1)\}$, and we need not say on which side.

### The converse and a generalization

Both directions are part of the statement.
A symmetric form is reflexive, since $B(v, u) = B(u, v)$, and an alternating form is reflexive, since it is skew-symmetric and $B(v, u) = -B(u, v)$.
Part 2 also holds over any field $K$ with an automorphism $\sigma$ of order $2$ in place of complex conjugation, and the proof is the same.
The only new step is the choice of the scalar $c$, and Hilbert's Theorem 90 supplies it.

## Explanations

### L1 human

For the dot product, "$u$ is at a right angle to $v$" means the same as "$v$ is at a right angle to $u$".
For a general pairing this can fail: the pairing can give $0$ in one order and $2$ in the other.
The theorem says exactly when the order never matters for the value $0$.
This happens only for the symmetric pairings and for the alternating ones, which behave like signed areas.

### L2 human

Here is a typical test, on $\mathbb{Q}^2$ with $B(x, y) = x^{\mathsf{T}} A y$ and $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
The matrix is not symmetric, since $A_{12} = 1 \ne -1 = A_{21}$, and the form is not alternating, since $B(e_1, e_1) = 1$.
So part 1 says that $B$ is not reflexive, and we can find a pair that shows it.
Take $u = e_1$ and $v = e_1 - e_2$:
$$B(u, v) = A_{11} - A_{12} = 1 - 1 = 0, \qquad B(v, u) = A_{11} - A_{21} = 1 + 1 = 2.$$
So $v$ is orthogonal to $u$ in one order only.
The typical use of the theorem is to restrict the general theory to symmetric, alternating and Hermitian forms.
Watch out: one pair with $B(u, v) = B(v, u) = 0$ proves nothing, because reflexivity is a condition on all pairs.

### L3 human

The theorem explains why the classical groups come from three kinds of forms.
Symmetric forms give the [[orthogonal groups|0TKYAEJ8N7]], alternating forms give the [[symplectic groups|X12FS2JAP0]], and Hermitian forms give the [[unitary groups|0R9R0Y1HDK]].
In projective geometry, a nondegenerate reflexive form on a space of finite dimension gives the map $W \mapsto W^\perp$ on subspaces, which reverses inclusions and satisfies $W^{\perp\perp} = W$.
The heart of the proof is a small principle of duality: two linear forms with the same kernel are proportional.
