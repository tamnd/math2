## Summary

An abelian group is a [[group|G5S1M9]] $(G, \cdot)$ whose operation is [[commutative|099E72KTF8]].
Thus $G$ is a set with a [[binary operation|0M418FE7BB]] that is associative, that has an identity element $e$, in which each element has an inverse, and for which $ab = ba$ for all $a, b \in G$.
Another name is commutative group.
Abelian groups behave like the integers under addition, so we can compute in them almost as with numbers, and their structure is much better known than the structure of general groups.

## Motivation

The first groups that a student meets are numbers under addition: $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$ and $(\mathbb{R}, +)$.
In these groups the order of the two terms of a sum does not matter.
Many other groups do not have this property.
The symmetries of a triangle and the invertible matrices are examples where $ab \neq ba$ for some elements.
Many rules of school algebra need the commutative law, for example $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$.
So we give a name to the groups in which the commutative law holds, and we study them as a class of their own.

## Notation

An abelian group is often written in additive notation.
Then the operation is $a + b$, the identity element is $0$, the inverse of $a$ is $-a$, and the [[power|00DRK90Z6K]] $a^n$ becomes the multiple $na$.
We write $a - b$ for $a + (-b)$.
By convention, additive notation is used only for abelian groups, but an abelian group can also be written multiplicatively, as the group $(\mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$ is.
The word abelian is usually written with a small letter a.
In Mathlib, the classes are `CommGroup` for the multiplicative notation and `AddCommGroup` for the additive notation.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. The [[additive group of integers|0BSCJA5G53]] $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is abelian, because $m + n = n + m$ for all integers.
2. The groups $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$, $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ and $(\mathbb{C}, +)$ are abelian, because addition of numbers is commutative.
3. The nonzero rational numbers under multiplication form an abelian group: $1$ is the identity, $a/b$ has the inverse $b/a$, and $xy = yx$. The positive real numbers under multiplication are another such group, see [[the exponential map|7GEXM87S0H]].
4. The [[integers modulo n|03VFZ2WETT]] $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ under addition modulo $n$ form an abelian group, and [[they are cyclic|FB79N7AHZA]].
5. The [[Klein four-group|DX6X1D21FE]] is abelian, and it is not cyclic.
6. The [[circle group|04EAFVFTF7]] and the [[groups of $n$th roots of unity|0WX11EA3F3]] are abelian, because multiplication of complex numbers is commutative.

### Non-examples

1. The [[symmetric group $S_3$ is not abelian|QSMVQ4EJ3D]]: the commutative law fails, because $(1\,2)(1\,3) = (1\,3\,2)$ and $(1\,3)(1\,2) = (1\,2\,3)$.
2. The [[general linear group|0FCHE902D7]] $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ is not abelian: the commutative law fails, because $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ give $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. The [[quaternion group|0NRJGW864D]] $Q_8$ is not abelian: the commutative law fails, because $ij = k$ and $ji = -k$.
4. [[The natural numbers under addition are not a group|R68D2P175Z]], so they are not an abelian group, although addition is commutative: the inverse axiom fails, because $1$ has no inverse in $\mathbb{N}$.

### Basic properties

1. In an abelian group, $(ab)^n = a^n b^n$ for each integer $n$, by the [[laws of exponents|00DRK90Z6K]]. In additive notation this is $n(a + b) = na + nb$.
2. Conversely, a group with $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$ for all $a, b$ is abelian. Indeed $abab = aabb$, and the [[cancellation laws|0VSNX4P2Q0]] remove $a$ on the left and $b$ on the right, so $ba = ab$.
3. In an abelian group, $(ab)^{-1} = a^{-1} b^{-1}$, because $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1} a^{-1}$ in each group by [[uniqueness of inverses|0D9NDGE9KP]], and $b^{-1} a^{-1} = a^{-1} b^{-1}$.
4. [[A group of exponent 2 is abelian|GGAT7CNA82]]: if $g^2 = e$ for each $g$, then $ab = ba$ for all $a, b$.
5. [[Cyclic groups are abelian|04RG02S6BA]], but an abelian group need not be cyclic, as the Klein four-group shows.
6. [[Each group of order at most 5 is abelian|G98PPG0FCC]], so the smallest order of a group that is not abelian is $6$, the order of $S_3$.
7. Each [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] of an abelian group is abelian and [[normal|0CB0DX5BVV]], because $g h g^{-1} = h g g^{-1} = h$ for all $g, h$.

### History

The name honours the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel (1802 to 1829).
Abel found that a polynomial equation can be solved by radicals when its group of symmetries of the roots is commutative.
Camille Jordan later named the commutative groups after Abel.

### Common errors

1. The commutative law is used in a group that is not abelian. For example, the rules $(ab)^{-1} = a^{-1} b^{-1}$ and $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$ are false in $S_3$.
2. Abelian is confused with cyclic. Each cyclic group is abelian, but the Klein four-group is abelian and not cyclic.
3. Additive notation is read as the addition of numbers. In an abelian group written additively, the symbol $0$ means the identity element, which can be the number $1$ of a multiplicative group.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A group is a set of things that we can combine, with rules that let us always undo a step.
The group is abelian when the order of the two things that we combine does not change the result.
Adding numbers is abelian: $3 + 5$ and $5 + 3$ are both $8$.
Turning a square by quarter turns is also abelian: a quarter turn and then a half turn give the same result as a half turn and then a quarter turn.
Some groups are not abelian.
Putting on socks and then shoes is not the same as putting on shoes and then socks.

### L2 human

We show that the set $G = \mathbb{Q} \setminus \{-1\}$ with $a \ast b = a + b + ab$ is an abelian group.

1. Closure. We have $a \ast b + 1 = (a + 1)(b + 1)$. For $a, b \neq -1$, both factors are nonzero, so $a \ast b \neq -1$. The value is rational, so $a \ast b \in G$.
2. Associativity. Expand both sides: $(a \ast b) \ast c$ and $a \ast (b \ast c)$ are both equal to $a + b + c + ab + ac + bc + abc$.
3. Identity. The number $0$ is in $G$, because $0 \neq -1$. For each $a$, $a \ast 0 = a + 0 + 0 = a$ and $0 \ast a = a$. So $0$ is the identity element.
4. Inverse. For $a \neq -1$, put $b = -a/(1 + a)$. Then $a \ast b + 1 = (a + 1)(b + 1) = (a + 1) \cdot \frac{1}{1 + a} = 1$, so $a \ast b = 0$.
5. The inverse $b$ is in $G$: if $b = -1$, then $-a = -1 - a$, which gives $0 = -1$, a contradiction.
6. Commutativity. $a \ast b = a + b + ab = b + a + ba = b \ast a$, because addition and multiplication of rational numbers are commutative.

So $(G, \ast)$ is an abelian group.
The map $a \mapsto a + 1$ turns $\ast$ into ordinary multiplication, because $(a \ast b) + 1 = (a + 1)(b + 1)$.
The typical use of the commutative law is to rearrange a product freely, for example to write $abab$ as $a^2 b^2$.
A trap is to check commutativity on a few pairs only: the law must hold for all pairs, and one pair with $ab \neq ba$ is enough to show that a group is not abelian.

### L3 human

An abelian group is the same as a module over the ring $\mathbb{Z}$: the multiple $na$ is the action of the integer $n$ on $a$.
So the abelian groups form a category with good properties, the model example of an [[abelian category|Y9CZ3MTNF1]], and homological algebra starts there.
For each group $G$, the quotient by the [[commutator subgroup|0ASGSTXSBW]] is the largest abelian quotient of $G$, and it is studied in the sub-area on [[automorphisms, centres and commutators|6Q029S79W0]].
The finitely generated abelian groups have a complete classification as products of cyclic groups; this is the subject of the sub-area on [[finite and finitely generated abelian groups|Q52TX6JZ6X]].
The infinite abelian groups, such as $\mathbb{Q}$ and the Prüfer groups, have a rich theory of their own, see [[infinite abelian groups|Q0G2WW6R77]].
For locally compact abelian groups there is a duality theory, the [[Pontryagin dual|FMW57WNPDB]], which links them to Fourier analysis.
