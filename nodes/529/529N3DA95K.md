## Example

The formula $(p \to q) \to (q \to p)$ says that an implication implies its [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]].
We show that it is not a [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]].

1. By the table of [[material implication|0ZNX6DQ3EJ]], the formula is false only in a row where $p \to q$ is $\mathsf{T}$ and $q \to p$ is $\mathsf{F}$.
2. The implication $q \to p$ is $\mathsf{F}$ only when $q = \mathsf{T}$ and $p = \mathsf{F}$.
3. In the row $p = \mathsf{F}$, $q = \mathsf{T}$, the implication $p \to q$ is $\mathsf{F} \to \mathsf{T} = \mathsf{T}$.
4. So the formula is $\mathsf{T} \to \mathsf{F} = \mathsf{F}$ in this row, and it is not a tautology.

The full [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]] confirms this.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \to q$ | $q \to p$ | $(p \to q) \to (q \to p)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | T |
| F | T | T | F | F |
| F | F | T | T | T |

5. The last column is $\mathsf{T}, \mathsf{T}, \mathsf{F}, \mathsf{T}$: true in some rows and false in one, so the formula is a contingency.

## Remarks

The formula fails to be a tautology because of one row; the three true rows do not help.
It is not a contradiction either, so it lies in the third class, the contingencies.
A contradiction is a formula false in every row, such as $p \leftrightarrow \neg p$, whose column is $\mathsf{F}, \mathsf{F}$.

A mathematical instance of the failing row: let $p$ be "$x = -2$" and $q$ be "$x^2 = 4$", and take $x = 2$.
Then $p$ is false, $q$ is true, the implication "if $x = -2$, then $x^2 = 4$" holds for every $x$, and its converse fails at $x = 2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The formula says: if "$p$ gives $q$" is true, then "$q$ gives $p$" is true.
This is false in general.
"If it is a square, then it is a rectangle" is true.
"If it is a rectangle, then it is a square" is false.
So the formula is not a law of logic, although it is true in some cases.

### L2 human

We classify the related formula $(p \to q) \to (\neg q \to \neg p)$.

1. It is false only when $p \to q$ is $\mathsf{T}$ and $\neg q \to \neg p$ is $\mathsf{F}$.
2. The second condition needs $\neg q = \mathsf{T}$ and $\neg p = \mathsf{F}$, so $q = \mathsf{F}$ and $p = \mathsf{T}$.
3. Then $p \to q = \mathsf{T} \to \mathsf{F} = \mathsf{F}$, which contradicts step 1.
4. So this formula is a tautology: an implication implies its contrapositive, but not its converse.

The typical use of the non-example is as a warning in proofs: proving $p \to q$ does not prove $q \to p$.
A trap: a formula that is true in most rows is still not a tautology, since one false row is enough.
