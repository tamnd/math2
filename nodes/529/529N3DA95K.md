## Example

"If a shape is a square, then it is a rectangle" is true, but "if a shape is a rectangle, then it is a square" is false.
The formula $(p \to q) \to (q \to p)$ says that an implication implies its [[converse|09A3N7ZN5G]].
We show that it is not a [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]].

1. By the table of [[material implication|0ZNX6DQ3EJ]], the formula is false only in a row where $p \to q$ is T and $q \to p$ is F.
2. The implication $q \to p$ is F only when $q$ is T and $p$ is F.
3. In the row where $p$ is F and $q$ is T, the implication $p \to q$ is $\mathrm{F} \to \mathrm{T} = \mathrm{T}$.
4. So the formula is $\mathrm{T} \to \mathrm{F} = \mathrm{F}$ in this row, and it is not a tautology.

The full [[truth table|05RSK4Q657]] confirms this.

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \to q$ | $q \to p$ | $(p \to q) \to (q \to p)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | T |
| F | T | T | F | F |
| F | F | T | T | T |

5. The last column is T, T, F, T: true in some rows and false in one, so the formula is a contingency.

## Remarks

### One row is enough

The formula fails to be a tautology because of one row, and the three true rows do not help.
It is not a contradiction either, so it is a contingency.
A contradiction is false in every row, such as $p \leftrightarrow \neg p$, whose column is F, F.

### A mathematical instance

Let $p$ be "$x = -2$" and $q$ be "$x^2 = 4$".
The implication "if $x = -2$, then $x^2 = 4$" holds for every real $x$.
Its converse fails at $x = 2$, where $p$ is false and $q$ is true, which is the failing row of the table.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The formula says: if "$p$ gives $q$" is true, then "$q$ gives $p$" is true.
This is false in general.
"If it is a square, then it is a rectangle" is true.
"If it is a rectangle, then it is a square" is false.
So the formula is not a law of logic, although it is true in some cases.

### L2 human

We classify the related formula $(p \to q) \to (\neg q \to \neg p)$.

1. It is false only when $p \to q$ is T and $\neg q \to \neg p$ is F.
2. The second condition needs $\neg q$ T and $\neg p$ F, so $q$ is F and $p$ is T.
3. Then $p \to q$ is $\mathrm{T} \to \mathrm{F} = \mathrm{F}$, which contradicts step 1.
4. So this formula is a tautology: an implication implies its contrapositive, but not its converse.

In proofs, this means that a proof of $p \to q$ is not a proof of $q \to p$.
Watch out: a formula that is true in most rows is still not a tautology, since one false row is enough.
