## Statement

If every finite part of a set of formulas can be made true, then one truth assignment makes the whole set true at once.

Let $P$ be a nonempty set of propositional variables, of any cardinality.
A set $\Sigma$ of [[formulas|TT7WT5HPN9]] is [[satisfiable|QN7M1JCGT9]] when some [[truth assignment|H31CS3HK5P]] $v : P \to \{ 0, 1 \}$ makes every member of $\Sigma$ true.
The set $\Sigma$ is finitely satisfiable when every finite subset of $\Sigma$ is satisfiable.

1. If $\Sigma$ is finitely satisfiable, then $\Sigma$ is satisfiable.
2. Equivalently, for every set $\Gamma$ and every formula $\varphi$: if $\Gamma \models \varphi$, then there is a finite $\Gamma_0 \subseteq \Gamma$ with $\Gamma_0 \models \varphi$.

Here $\Gamma \models \varphi$ is [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]], a semantic relation.
It is different from derivability $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, and the theorem does not mention any proof system.
There are two proofs: a [[direct proof for countable P|71200Z9Z4N]] and a [[proof by Tychonoff's theorem|MAQEYCNFST]] for any $P$.

## Remarks

### Why the two parts are equivalent

Assume part 1, and suppose that no finite $\Gamma_0 \subseteq \Gamma$ has $\Gamma_0 \models \varphi$.
Then each set $\Gamma_0 \cup \{ \neg \varphi \}$ with $\Gamma_0$ finite is satisfiable, so $\Gamma \cup \{ \neg \varphi \}$ is finitely satisfiable.
Part 1 gives a model of $\Gamma \cup \{ \neg \varphi \}$, so $\Gamma \not\models \varphi$.

Now assume part 2, and let $\Sigma$ have no model.
The formula $\neg (p_0 \to p_0)$ is false under every assignment, so $\Sigma \models \neg (p_0 \to p_0)$ holds vacuously.
Part 2 gives a finite $\Sigma_0 \subseteq \Sigma$ with $\Sigma_0 \models \neg (p_0 \to p_0)$, and then $\Sigma_0$ has no model.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The word "finite" cannot be replaced by "of size at most $k$" for a fixed $k$.
The set $\{ p_1, \dots, p_k, \neg (p_1 \land \cdots \land p_k) \}$ has $k + 1$ members.
Every $k$ of them are satisfiable: if $p_i$ is left out, set $p_i$ false and the other variables true, and if the last formula is left out, set all variables true.
The whole set is not satisfiable.

Formulas must be finite.
With an infinite disjunction $\bigvee_n p_n$, the set $\{ \bigvee_n p_n, \neg p_0, \neg p_1, \dots \}$ is finitely satisfiable but not satisfiable.
Each formula of propositional logic has finitely many variables, and both proofs use this fact.

### What it gives you

The strong form of the [[completeness theorem|81ZNY1XKYJ]] follows from its finite form and compactness.
If $\Gamma \models \varphi$, a finite $\Gamma_0 \subseteq \Gamma$ has $\Gamma_0 \models \varphi$, so $\Gamma_0 \vdash \varphi$ and therefore $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$.

Compactness reduces infinite combinatorial problems to finite ones.
Examples are the de Bruijn-Erdős theorem on graph colorings, the extension of partial orders to linear orders, and Hall's theorem for infinite families of finite sets; see [[applications of propositional compactness|S7SW0CZZ9V]].

König's lemma follows too: an infinite, finitely branching rooted tree has an infinite branch.
Take a variable $p_x$ for each vertex $x$, read as "$x$ lies on the branch".
For each level, say that exactly one of its finitely many vertices lies on the branch, and for each vertex $x$ other than the root, add $p_x \to p_y$ with $y$ the parent of $x$.
A finite part of this set mentions only the levels up to some $N$, and the path from the root to a vertex at level $N$ satisfies it.

### The converse

The converse of part 1 holds, and its proof is one line: a model of $\Sigma$ is a model of each subset of $\Sigma$.
So $\Sigma$ is satisfiable if and only if it is finitely satisfiable.

### Choice principles

For a countable $P$, the [[direct proof|71200Z9Z4N]] uses no form of the axiom of choice.
For an arbitrary $P$, the theorem is equivalent over ZF to the [[ultrafilter lemma|3MKZST79MK]], and so to the [[Boolean prime ideal theorem|STPBXBKSZP]].
This principle cannot be proved in ZF, and it is strictly weaker than the [[axiom of choice|0HTGB13FE2]].

### Generalizations

The [[compactness theorem for first-order logic|1NR3HAWG9D]] is the same statement for sets of first-order sentences and their models.
In topological form, for a countably infinite $P$ the space $\{ 0, 1 \}^P$ is the [[Cantor space|W9VYJRBF9N]], and the sets of models of formulas are exactly its clopen sets.
Compactness of the logic is then compactness of this space.
Compactness fails for infinitary logics with countable disjunctions, as the example above shows, and it fails for second-order logic.

## History

Propositional compactness is a special case of the compactness theorem of first-order logic, with each variable a predicate symbol of arity zero.
Kurt Gödel proved the first-order theorem for countable languages in 1930, and Anatoly Maltsev proved the uncountable case in 1936.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Think of a list of rules, perhaps an infinite list, about which switches are on and which are off.
Suppose that any finite part of the list can be obeyed by some setting of the switches.
The compactness theorem says that one setting of the switches obeys the whole list at once.
For example, take the rules "switch 1 is on", "if switch 1 is on then switch 2 is on", "if switch 2 is on then switch 3 is on", and so on.
Each finite part can be obeyed, and the setting "all switches on" obeys all the rules.
The theorem says that such a setting exists even when nobody can guess it.

### L2 human

Let $G$ be a graph on a vertex set $V$ of any size, and suppose that every finite set of vertices spans a subgraph with a proper 2-coloring.
We show that $G$ itself has a proper 2-coloring.
Take a variable $p_x$ for each $x \in V$, read as "$x$ gets color 1".
For each edge $xy$ of $G$, the two formulas
$$p_x \lor p_y, \qquad \neg (p_x \land p_y)$$
say that exactly one end of the edge gets color 1.
Let $\Sigma$ be the set of all these formulas.

A finite $\Sigma_0 \subseteq \Sigma$ mentions only a finite set $F$ of vertices.
By hypothesis the subgraph on $F$ has a proper 2-coloring $c$.
Put $v(p_x) = 1$ when $x \in F$ and $c(x) = 1$, and $v(p_x) = 0$ otherwise.
Each edge $xy$ in $\Sigma_0$ has both ends in $F$, and $c(x) \ne c(y)$, so $v$ satisfies both formulas for that edge.
So $\Sigma$ is finitely satisfiable, and compactness gives a model $v$ of $\Sigma$.
Color $x$ with color 1 when $v(p_x) = 1$ and with color 2 otherwise; this is a proper 2-coloring of $G$.

The finite colorings need not fit together, since each one may swap the two colors on a part of the graph.
Compactness picks one model without any choice of how to glue them, and this is its typical use.

Watch out: compactness needs finite formulas.
"Some vertex gets color 1" for infinitely many vertices is an infinite disjunction, not a formula, and the theorem says nothing about it.

### L3 human

The set $\{ 0, 1 \}^P$ of truth assignments, with the product topology, is a compact Hausdorff space by Tychonoff's theorem.
The set of models of a formula is clopen, and compactness of the logic is compactness of this space.
This space is the Stone space of the free Boolean algebra on $P$.
More generally, the Stone space of the [[Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra|QC9M3Q8B8Q]] of a theory is the space of its complete extensions.

In first-order logic the same theorem gives nonstandard models of arithmetic and the upward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem.
Lindström's theorem characterizes first-order logic as the strongest logic that has compactness and the downward Löwenheim-Skolem property.
