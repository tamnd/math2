## Example

Part 1, a commuting pair.
Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product.
Both are [[symmetric|0N28QN4CYA]].

1. $A B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so $A B = B A$.
2. $A (1, 1) = (3, 3)$ and $A (1, -1) = (1, -1)$. Also $B (1, 1) = (1, 1)$ and $B (1, -1) = (-1, 1)$.
3. So $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ are [[eigenvectors|0B5H4208Z9]] of both matrices, and they are orthogonal.
4. With the [[orthogonal matrix|0QRBVFJ50X]] $Q = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, we get $Q^T A Q = \operatorname{diag}(3, 1)$ and $Q^T B Q = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$.

Part 2, a pair that does not commute.
Let $C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, and keep $B$.

5. $C B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B C = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. So $C B \neq B C$.
6. The eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$ of $C$ are distinct, so every eigenvector of $C$ is a nonzero multiple of $e_1$ or of $e_2$.
7. But $B e_1 = e_2$ and $B e_2 = e_1$. So neither $e_1$ nor $e_2$ is an eigenvector of $B$, and $B$ and $C$ have no common eigenvector.

## Remarks

- Part 1 illustrates [[simultaneous unitary diagonalization|BFQQDA489F]]. Here $A = 2I + B$ is a polynomial in $B$, so the commutation is no surprise.
- Part 2 shows that the commutation hypothesis is needed, even for two symmetric matrices.
- In part 1 the eigenvalues of $B$ are distinct. Then every matrix that commutes with $B$ is diagonal in the eigenbasis of $B$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix $B$ swaps the two coordinates, and $A$ is twice the identity plus this swap.
Both matrices keep the diagonal lines through $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, so one rotation of the axes makes both diagonal.
The matrix $C$ reflects in the horizontal axis.
Its special lines are the axes, and the swap moves them, so no common axes exist.

### L2 human

The test for a common orthonormal eigenbasis of symmetric matrices:

1. Compute $A B$ and $B A$. If they differ, there is no common eigenbasis.
2. If they agree, find the eigenvectors of the matrix with distinct eigenvalues. They diagonalize the other matrix too.

A trap: if neither matrix has distinct eigenvalues, an arbitrary orthonormal eigenbasis of one matrix need not diagonalize the other.
