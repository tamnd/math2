## Example

The first pair of [[symmetric|0N28QN4CYA]] matrices below commutes and shares an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, while the second pair does not commute and shares no eigenvector at all.
All matrices act on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the dot product.

**A commuting pair.**
Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. The two products agree:
$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 & 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 \\ 1 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = BA.$$
2. The vectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ are [[eigenvectors|0B5H4208Z9]] of both matrices: $A(1, 1) = (3, 3)$, $A(1, -1) = (1, -1)$, $B(1, 1) = (1, 1)$ and $B(1, -1) = (-1, 1)$.
3. The two vectors are orthogonal, since $1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (-1) = 0$.
4. With the [[orthogonal matrix|0QRBVFJ50X]] $Q = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, whose columns are the unit eigenvectors, we get
$$Q^{\mathsf{T}} A Q = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q^{\mathsf{T}} B Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

**A pair that does not commute.**
Let $C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, and keep $B$.

5. The two products differ:
$$CB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad BC = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
6. The eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$ of $C$ are distinct, so every eigenvector of $C$ is a nonzero multiple of $e_1$ or of $e_2$.
7. But $B e_1 = e_2$ and $B e_2 = e_1$, so neither $e_1$ nor $e_2$ is an eigenvector of $B$. Hence $B$ and $C$ have no common eigenvector.

## Remarks

The first pair illustrates [[simultaneous unitary diagonalization|BFQQDA489F]]: commuting self-adjoint matrices have one orthonormal eigenbasis.
Here the commutation is no surprise, since $A = 2I + B$ is a polynomial in $B$.
The second pair shows that the hypothesis is needed even when both matrices are symmetric.

The distinct eigenvalues of $B$ make the first pair easy.
When one matrix has distinct eigenvalues, each of its eigenvectors spans an eigenspace of dimension $1$, and a commuting matrix must map that line to itself.
So a commuting matrix is diagonal in the eigenbasis of $B$.
If we replace $B$ by $I$, which has the repeated eigenvalue $1$, then every orthonormal basis is an eigenbasis of $I$, but only some of them diagonalize $A$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix $B$ swaps the two coordinates, and $A$ is twice the identity plus this swap.
Both matrices keep the diagonal lines through $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, so one turn of the axes makes both diagonal.
The matrix $C$ reflects in the horizontal axis, and its special lines are the two axes.
The swap $B$ exchanges those axes, so no line is special for both $B$ and $C$.

### L2 human

To test whether two symmetric matrices have a common orthonormal eigenbasis, first compute both products.
If $AB \ne BA$, there is no common eigenbasis, and you can stop.
If $AB = BA$ and one of the matrices has distinct eigenvalues, its unit eigenvectors diagonalize the other matrix too.
For the first pair, $Q^{\mathsf{T}} A Q$ has the entries
$$\tfrac{1}{2}(1, 1) A (1, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} = \tfrac{6}{2} = 3, \qquad \tfrac{1}{2}(1, 1) A (1, -1)^{\mathsf{T}} = \tfrac{1}{2}(1, 1) \cdot (1, -1) = 0.$$
Watch out: when both matrices have a repeated eigenvalue, an arbitrary orthonormal eigenbasis of one matrix need not diagonalize the other.
