## Statement

Consider a continuous-time channel whose input signals are [[band-limited|P3T104G564]] to the frequency band $[-B, B]$, with $B > 0$ in hertz.
The input has average power at most $S > 0$.
The channel adds white Gaussian noise whose power within the band is $N > 0$, independent of the input.
Then the [[channel capacity|J7MZ2XQP9M]] of this channel, in bits per second, is
$$C = B \log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right).$$

## Remarks

The idea of the proof: by the [[sampling theorem|BMXDMHRXBG]], a signal band-limited to $[-B, B]$ is described by $2B$ samples per second.
Each sample passes through an [[additive white Gaussian noise channel|RYFPT7P01J]] with signal-to-noise ratio $S/N$, whose capacity is $\frac12 \log_2(1 + S/N)$ bits per sample.
Multiplying by $2B$ samples per second gives the formula.
A rigorous proof has to handle the fact that no nonzero signal is both band-limited and time-limited.
It uses signals that are nearly confined to a time interval and a band.

Example: a telephone line with $B = 3000$ hertz and $S/N = 1000$ (that is, $30$ decibels) has capacity about $3000 \log_2(1001) \approx 29\,900$ bits per second.

The capacity grows linearly in $B$ but only logarithmically in $S/N$.
If the noise power grows with the band, $N = N_0 B$, then as $B \to \infty$ the capacity tends to the finite limit $\frac{S}{N_0} \log_2 e$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The theorem gives the top speed at which data can cross a noisy analogue line without errors.
Two things set that speed: how wide the band of frequencies is, and how strong the signal is compared with the noise.
More bandwidth helps in direct proportion, while more power helps only slowly, through a logarithm.

### L2 human

The Shannon–Hartley formula is the continuous-time form of the capacity of the AWGN channel.
It combines the sampling theorem, which turns band-limited signals into $2B$ real numbers per second, with the capacity $\frac12 \log_2(1 + S/N)$ per real number.
The [[noisy-channel coding theorem|PR3NJB0SKG]] then says that rates below $C$ are achievable with error probability tending to zero, and rates above $C$ are not.
Engineers use the formula as a benchmark: the gap between a real modem and $C$ measures how much better coding could do.
When the noise is not flat across the band, the formula is replaced by an integral over frequency, and the power is allocated by water-filling.
