## Summary

A capillary wave is a wave on a liquid surface, or on an interface between two fluids, restored mainly by surface tension rather than gravity.
On water this is the case for wavelengths well below about $1.7$ cm; such waves are often called ripples.
In contrast to gravity waves, short capillary waves travel faster than long ones.
They matter for the first growth of wind waves, for radar sensing of the sea surface, and for thin films and droplets.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A liquid surface behaves a little like a stretched skin, because of surface tension.
When the surface is bent into very short ripples, this skin pulls it flat again, and the pull is stronger the sharper the bend.
So the shortest ripples bounce back fastest and run fastest.
The tiny ripples that a light breeze raises on a pond, or that run ahead of a fishing line pulled through water, are capillary waves.
