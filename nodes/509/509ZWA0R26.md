## Statement

The Paris-Harrington theorem gives a true statement of finite combinatorics, with no coding of logic in it, that Peano arithmetic cannot prove.
The statement is the strengthened finite Ramsey theorem, and its strength is exactly the $1$-consistency of Peano arithmetic.

Let $\mathrm{PH}$ be the [[strengthened finite Ramsey theorem|4P2BAT6YC3]], written as a $\Pi_2$ sentence of first-order arithmetic.
So $\mathrm{PH}$ says: for all $n, k, m \ge 1$ there is $N$ such that every $k$-coloring of the $n$-element subsets of $\{ 1, \dots, N \}$ has a homogeneous set $Y$ with $\lvert Y \rvert \ge m$ and $\lvert Y \rvert \ge \min Y$.
The *$1$-consistency* of a theory $T$, written $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(T)$, is the arithmetic sentence that says that every $\Sigma_1$ sentence provable in $T$ is true.

**Theorem** (Paris and Harrington).

1. $\mathrm{PH}$ is not provable in [[Peano arithmetic|MPSCREYC3M]] $\mathsf{PA}$.
2. $\mathsf{PA}$ proves that $\mathrm{PH}$ is equivalent to $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$.
3. $\mathrm{PH}$ is provable in $\mathsf{PA} + 1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, in [[second-order arithmetic|DC60FYCSPM]] and in $\mathsf{ZFC}$.

The [[proof|D0Q498H0R5]] proves part 1 by the method of [[indicators|JWTZ8NVVGK]], with the main combinatorial lemma cited.
Part 3 for second-order arithmetic and $\mathsf{ZFC}$ is the [[compactness proof|Q0RKBRZXBD]] of $\mathrm{PH}$.

## Remarks

### A small case by hand

Take pairs, $n = 2$, with $k = 2$ colors and $m = 3$.
A homogeneous set with $3$ elements is a one-colored triangle, and it is large when its least point is at most $3$.
We show that $N = 6$ works.

Color the $15$ pairs of $\{ 1, \dots, 6 \}$ red or blue.
At a point $v$, let $r_v$ and $b_v$ be the numbers of red and blue pairs that contain $v$, so $r_v + b_v = 5$ and $r_v b_v \le 2 \cdot 3 = 6$.
A triangle that is not one-colored has exactly two corners where its two sides differ in color, so:
$$\#\{\text{mixed triangles}\} = \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{v=1}^{6} r_v b_v \le \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 6 \cdot 6 = 18.$$
There are $\binom{6}{3} = 20$ triangles, so at least $2$ of them are one-colored.
Only one triple, $\{ 4, 5, 6 \}$, has least element above $3$, so some one-colored triangle has least point at most $3$, and it is large.

For $N = 5$, color the pairs $\{ i, i+1 \}$ and $\{ 1, 5 \}$ red, and the other five pairs blue.
The red pairs form a $5$-cycle, the blue pairs form the $5$-cycle $1, 3, 5, 2, 4$, and neither cycle contains a triangle.
So $N = 5$ fails, and the least $N$ is $6$, the Ramsey number $R(3, 3)$.
Here largeness cost nothing; as the exponent grows, it costs more than any function that $\mathsf{PA}$ proves total.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The theory is $\mathsf{PA}$.
The stronger theory $\mathsf{PA} + 1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ proves $\mathrm{PH}$, so the independence is always relative to a named theory.

The condition $\lvert Y \rvert \ge \min Y$ is needed.
Without it the sentence is the [[finite Ramsey theorem|YSZ82MRXT8]], and $\mathsf{PA}$ proves it with explicit bounds.

The exponent $n$ must vary.
For each fixed $n$, the sentence $\forall k \, \forall m \, \exists N \cdots$ is provable in $\mathsf{PA}$.
Only the statement for all exponents at once escapes $\mathsf{PA}$.

### What it gives you

$\mathrm{PH}$ is a natural instance of [[Gödel's incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]]: a true sentence about finite sets that $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove.
Let $\sigma(n, k, m)$ be the least $N$ in $\mathrm{PH}$.
Then $x \mapsto \sigma(x, x, x)$ eventually dominates every function that $\mathsf{PA}$ proves total, so it is not [[provably total|Z742YPHPNS]] in $\mathsf{PA}$.

By part 2, $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{PH}$ proves $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$.
That gives the plain [[consistency|VM8TQ70CVJ]] $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$.
The reason is that $0 = 1$ is a false $\Sigma_1$ sentence, so $1$-consistency says that $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove it.

### Stronger than consistency

$\mathrm{PH}$ is strictly stronger than $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, and the reason is short.
Suppose $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ proved $0 = 1$.
Then $\mathsf{PA}$ would prove $\neg \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, which is a $\Sigma_1$ sentence.
By $1$-consistency it would be true, which contradicts $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$.

So $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ proves $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA}))$, and this argument can be carried out inside $\mathsf{PA}$.
If $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ proved $\mathrm{PH}$, it would prove $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ by part 2, and so its own consistency.
The theory $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ is consistent, because it is true in the standard model.
So this is impossible by the [[second incompleteness theorem|4PZM5GJTR5]], and $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ does not prove $\mathrm{PH}$.

### The converse and generalizations

Could $\mathsf{PA}$ prove $\neg \mathrm{PH}$ instead?
It cannot, because $\mathrm{PH}$ is true in the standard model and every theorem of $\mathsf{PA}$ is true there.
So $\mathrm{PH}$ is independent of $\mathsf{PA}$ in the full sense: neither it nor its negation is provable.

The [[Kanamori-McAloon theorem|NM5WYBK6BG]] is a variant with regressive colorings, and it is also unprovable in $\mathsf{PA}$.
[[Friedman's finite form of Kruskal's theorem|12XRGBWH59]] and the finite forms of the [[graph minor theorem|WXNTPQGJ38]] are statements of the same kind that are independent of much stronger theories.

### Watch out

A common slip is to say that $\mathrm{PH}$ is "unprovable" without a theory.
It is unprovable in $\mathsf{PA}$, and it is provable in second-order arithmetic and in $\mathsf{ZFC}$.

A second slip is to think that $\mathrm{PH}$ is equivalent to $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$.
It is equivalent to the stronger $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, and the section above shows that $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ still does not prove it.

A third slip is to expect the independence to show up in small cases.
Each single instance, such as $\sigma(2, 2, 3) = 6$, is a finite check, and $\mathsf{PA}$ proves every true instance.

### Try it

First, check that the blue pairs $\{ 1, 3 \}$, $\{ 3, 5 \}$, $\{ 5, 2 \}$, $\{ 2, 4 \}$, $\{ 4, 1 \}$ of the $5$-point coloring contain no triangle.
A triangle needs three points that are pairwise joined, but each point has only two blue neighbors, and these two neighbors are not joined in blue.

Second, why does $\mathsf{PA}$ prove the instance $\exists N$ for $n = 2$, $k = 2$, $m = 3$?
It is a true $\Sigma_1$ sentence, with the witness $N = 6$, and $\mathsf{PA}$ proves every true $\Sigma_1$ sentence by checking the finitely many colorings.

## History

Jeff Paris and Leo Harrington published the theorem in 1977, in the Handbook of Mathematical Logic, edited by Jon Barwise.
Their proof used non-standard models of $\mathsf{PA}$ and initial segments of these models.
In 1981 Jussi Ketonen and Robert Solovay gave a combinatorial analysis of the growth of the witness function, with bounds through the fast-growing function $f_{\varepsilon_0}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some true statements about whole numbers cannot be proved from the usual axioms of arithmetic.
Gödel's examples talk about proofs and codes, so they look artificial.
The Paris-Harrington theorem gives an example from ordinary combinatorics, a statement about coloring finite sets of numbers.
It asks for a one-colored group of numbers that has at least as many members as its smallest member.
The statement is true, and set theory proves it, but the numbers that it needs grow too fast for arithmetic to prove that they exist.

### L2 human

We follow the plan of the unprovability proof.
For $a \le b$, let $Y(a, b)$ be the greatest $e$ such that every $e$-coloring of the $e$-subsets of $\{ a, \dots, b \}$ has a large homogeneous set with at least $e$ elements.
If $\mathsf{PA}$ proved $\mathrm{PH}$, then $\mathsf{PA}$ would prove that for every $a$ some $b$ has $Y(a, b) \ge a$.

Now take a [[non-standard model|1QWN5CV4CG]] $M$ of $\mathsf{PA}$, an infinite element $a$, and the least $b$ with $Y(a, b) \ge a$.
The indicator lemma gives an initial segment $I$ of $M$ with $a \in I$, $b \notin I$ and $I \models \mathsf{PA}$.
Inside $I$ the same reasoning gives some $b' < b$ with $Y(a, b') \ge a$, which contradicts the choice of $b$.

The typical use is the template: the same plan, with another combinatorial principle, proves other independence results.
The trap is that the indicator lemma is the hard step, and it uses the condition $\lvert Y \rvert \ge \min Y$ at every stage.

### L3 human

The proof is the first full use of [[indicators|JWTZ8NVVGK]]: a definable function $Y(a, b)$ that is non-standard exactly when some initial segment between $a$ and $b$ is a model of $\mathsf{PA}$.
The same result can be read through growth rates: the witness function of $\mathrm{PH}$ is measured by $f_{\varepsilon_0}$ of the [[fast-growing hierarchy|R6ZC4BJGXV]], and $\varepsilon_0$ is the [[proof-theoretic ordinal|C72RBH0CDR]] of $\mathsf{PA}$.
So the model-theoretic approach and the [[ordinal analysis|C5E5TG8PGP]] approach meet at the same boundary.
In [[reverse mathematics|TQTDKMBJ0F]], $\mathsf{ACA}_0$ proves the same arithmetic sentences as $\mathsf{PA}$, so it does not prove $\mathrm{PH}$, although it proves the infinite Ramsey theorem for each fixed exponent.
