## Statement

Let $\mathrm{PH}$ be the [[strengthened finite Ramsey theorem|4P2BAT6YC3]], written as a $\Pi_2$ sentence of first-order arithmetic.
So $\mathrm{PH}$ says: for all $n, k, m \ge 1$ there is $N$ such that every $k$-colouring of the $n$-element subsets of $\set{1, \dots, N}$ has a homogeneous set $Y$ with $\abs{Y} \ge m$ and $\abs{Y} \ge \min Y$.
The *$1$-consistency* of a theory $T$, written $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(T)$, says that every $\Sigma_1$ sentence provable in $T$ is true.

**Theorem** (Paris and Harrington).

1. $\mathrm{PH}$ is not provable in [[Peano arithmetic|MPSCREYC3M]] $\mathsf{PA}$.
2. In $\mathsf{PA}$, $\mathrm{PH}$ is equivalent to $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$.
3. $\mathrm{PH}$ is provable in $\mathsf{PA} + 1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, in [[second-order arithmetic|DC60FYCSPM]] and in $\mathsf{ZFC}$.

The [[proof|~a32fde15]] proves part 1 by the method of [[indicators|~1d28aa25]], with the main combinatorial lemma cited.
Part 3 for $\mathsf{ZFC}$ is the [[compactness proof|~35cec6ef]] of $\mathrm{PH}$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- The theory is $\mathsf{PA}$. The stronger theory $\mathsf{PA} + 1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ proves $\mathrm{PH}$, so independence is always relative to a stated theory.
- The condition $\abs{Y} \ge \min Y$ is needed. Without it the sentence is the [[finite Ramsey theorem|YSZ82MRXT8]], and $\mathsf{PA}$ proves it with explicit bounds.
- The exponent $n$ must vary. For each fixed $n$, the sentence $\forall k\, \forall m\, \exists N \cdots$ is provable in $\mathsf{PA}$.
- Part 2 uses the $1$-consistency, not the plain [[consistency|VM8TQ70CVJ]]. The theory $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ does not prove $\mathrm{PH}$, as the next section shows.

### Consequences

- $\mathrm{PH}$ is a true sentence about finite sets, with no coding of logic, that $\mathsf{PA}$ does not prove. This gives a natural instance of [[Gödel's incompleteness theorems|X9WYNYE29C]].
- Let $\sigma(n, k, m)$ be the least $N$ in $\mathrm{PH}$. Then $x \mapsto \sigma(x, x, x)$ eventually dominates every function that $\mathsf{PA}$ proves total, so it is not [[provably total|~25aedb3b]] in $\mathsf{PA}$.
- By part 2, $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{PH}$ proves $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, and so $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$, because $0 = 1$ is a $\Sigma_1$ sentence.
- $1\text{-}\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ also gives $\mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA}))$, because $\neg \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ is a false $\Sigma_1$ sentence. So $\mathsf{PA} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathsf{PA})$ does not prove $\mathrm{PH}$, by the second incompleteness theorem for this theory.

### Converse

The converse question is whether $\neg \mathrm{PH}$ is provable in $\mathsf{PA}$.
It is not, because $\mathrm{PH}$ is true in the standard model and $\mathsf{PA}$ proves only true sentences.
So $\mathrm{PH}$ is independent of $\mathsf{PA}$ in the full sense: neither it nor its negation is provable.

### Generalizations

- The [[Kanamori-McAloon theorem|~53c52c9d]] is a variant with regressive colourings, and it is also unprovable in $\mathsf{PA}$.
- [[Friedman's finite form of Kruskal's theorem|~0c69c0b2]] and the [[graph minor theorem|WXNTPQGJ38]] give miniaturized statements that are independent of much stronger theories.

## History

Jeff Paris and Leo Harrington published the theorem in 1977, in the Handbook of Mathematical Logic, edited by Jon Barwise.
Their first proof used non-standard models of $\mathsf{PA}$ and initial segments of these models.
In 1981 Ketonen and Solovay gave a combinatorial proof of the growth of $\sigma$, with bounds through the fast-growing function $f_{\varepsilon_0}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some true statements about whole numbers cannot be proved from the usual axioms of arithmetic.
Gödel's examples talk about proofs and codes, so they look artificial.
The Paris-Harrington theorem gives an example from ordinary combinatorics: a statement about colouring finite sets of numbers.
It asks for a one-coloured group of numbers that has at least as many members as its smallest member.
The statement is true, and set theory proves it, but the numbers that it needs grow too fast for arithmetic to prove that they exist.

### L2 human

The plan of the unprovability proof, in numbered steps.

1. Let $Y(a, b)$ be the greatest $e$ such that every $e$-colouring of the $e$-subsets of $[a, b]$ has a large homogeneous set with at least $e$ elements.
2. If $\mathsf{PA}$ proved $\mathrm{PH}$, then $\mathsf{PA}$ would prove that for every $a$ some $b$ has $Y(a, b) \ge a$.
3. Take a non-standard model $M$ of $\mathsf{PA}$, a non-standard $a$, and the least $b$ with $Y(a, b) \ge a$.
4. A cited lemma gives an initial segment $I$ of $M$ with $a \in I$, $b \notin I$ and $I \models \mathsf{PA}$.
5. Inside $I$, step 2 gives some $b' < b$ with $Y(a, b') \ge a$. This contradicts the choice of $b$.

The typical use: the same plan, with another combinatorial principle, proves other independence results.
The trap: step 4 is the hard lemma, and it uses the condition $\abs{Y} \ge \min Y$ in an essential way.

### L3 human

The proof is the first full use of [[indicators|~1d28aa25]]: a definable function $Y(a, b)$ that is non-standard exactly when some initial segment between $a$ and $b$ is a model of $\mathsf{PA}$.
The same results can be read through growth rates: the witness function of $\mathrm{PH}$ grows like $f_{\varepsilon_0}$, and $\varepsilon_0$ is the [[proof-theoretic ordinal|C72RBH0CDR]] of $\mathsf{PA}$.
So the [[non-standard models|1QWN5CV4CG]] approach and the [[ordinal analysis|C5E5TG8PGP]] approach give the same boundary.
In [[reverse mathematics|TQTDKMBJ0F]], $\mathsf{ACA}_0$ proves the same arithmetic sentences as $\mathsf{PA}$, so it does not prove $\mathrm{PH}$, although it proves the infinite Ramsey theorem for each fixed exponent.
