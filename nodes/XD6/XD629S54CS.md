## Statement

Everything that the system L derives is true: a derivation never leads from true hypotheses to a false conclusion.

Let $P$ be a set of propositional variables, and let $L$ be the [[Hilbert system|1MGMQX43B8]] with the rule [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]] (MP) and the axiom schemas
$$\begin{array}{ll} \text{(A1)} & \varphi \to (\psi \to \varphi), \\ \text{(A2)} & (\varphi \to (\psi \to \chi)) \to ((\varphi \to \psi) \to (\varphi \to \chi)), \\ \text{(A3)} & (\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi) \to ((\neg \psi \to \varphi) \to \psi). \end{array}$$
Write $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ when $\varphi$ has a derivation in $L$ from the set of formulas $\Gamma$.
Write $\Gamma \models \varphi$, [[logical consequence|1HK0VYWTV7]], when every [[truth assignment|H31CS3HK5P]] that makes all members of $\Gamma$ true also makes $\varphi$ true.

1. For every set of formulas $\Gamma$ and every formula $\varphi$, if $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, then $\Gamma \models \varphi$.
2. In particular, every theorem of $L$ is a [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]], and $L$ is [[consistent|VM8TQ70CVJ]]: there is no formula $\varphi$ with $\vdash \varphi$ and $\vdash \neg \varphi$.
3. Every [[satisfiable|QN7M1JCGT9]] set of formulas is consistent.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The proof uses two facts about $L$: each axiom is a tautology, and MP sends true formulas to a true formula.
Each fact is needed.

If we add the single axiom $p_0$ to $L$, then $\vdash p_0$, but the assignment with $v(p_0) = 0$ makes $p_0$ false, so $\not\models p_0$.
An unsound schema breaks the theorem in the same way: the schema $\varphi \to \neg \varphi$ has the instance $p_0 \to \neg p_0$, which is false when $v(p_0) = 1$.

An unsound rule breaks it too.
Add the rule "from $\varphi \to \psi$ infer $\psi \to \varphi$".
From the instance $p_0 \to (p_1 \to p_0)$ of A1 it gives $(p_1 \to p_0) \to p_0$, which is $1 \to 0 = 0$ when $v(p_0) = v(p_1) = 0$.

The theorem also depends on the truth table of each connective.
Read $\neg x$ as the constant $1$ and keep the usual table of $\to$.
Then A1, A2 and MP stay sound, but A3 becomes $(1 \to 1) \to ((1 \to \varphi) \to \psi)$, which has the value of $\varphi \to \psi$ and is not always true.
This trick is the method of [[independence of axioms|729RCBPAM3]], and it shows that A3 is not derivable from A1 and A2 with MP.

### What it gives you

To show $\Gamma \not\vdash \varphi$, it is enough to give one truth assignment that makes $\Gamma$ true and $\varphi$ false.
For example, $\not\vdash p_0 \to p_1$, because $v(p_0) = 1$ and $v(p_1) = 0$ make $p_0 \to p_1$ false.
Neither $\vdash p_0$ nor $\vdash \neg p_0$ holds, since neither formula is a tautology, so $L$ does not decide every formula.
A set $\Gamma$ with $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ and $\Gamma \vdash \neg \varphi$ has no model.

### The converse

The converse is true, and it is the [[completeness theorem for propositional logic|81ZNY1XKYJ]]: if $\Gamma \models \varphi$, then $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$.
Its proof is much longer, because it must build a derivation from a semantic fact.

### Generalizations

The same proof works for every Hilbert system whose axioms are tautologies and whose rules preserve truth.
It gives the [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]] of first-order logic, where the axioms are logically valid and the rules preserve truth in each structure.
It also works for other proof systems, such as [[natural deduction|6KT3BJZ7D1]], by induction on the derivation tree.
For [[intuitionistic logic|Q11N8X99FK]], the same induction gives soundness with respect to Kripke models and Heyting algebras.

## History

Soundness is the easy direction of the completeness theorem for propositional logic.
Paul Bernays proved that completeness theorem in his habilitation thesis of 1918.
Emil Post proved it independently in a paper of 1921, for the propositional part of Principia Mathematica.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A proof system is a set of rules for writing down formulas, step by step.
A truth table tells when a formula is true.
Soundness says that the rules never lead from true formulas to a false one.
For example, the rule modus ponens goes from "$p$" and "if $p$ then $q$" to "$q$".
When $p$ is true and "if $p$ then $q$" is true, then $q$ must be true.
So a proof in this system can never prove something false.

### L2 human

We use soundness to show that $\{ p \lor q \} \not\vdash p$ in $L$.
In $L$ the formula $p \lor q$ abbreviates $\neg p \to q$.
Take the truth assignment $v(p) = 0$ and $v(q) = 1$.
Then
$$v(\neg p) = 1, \qquad v(\neg p \to q) = 1 \to 1 = 1, \qquad v(p) = 0.$$
So $v$ makes the hypothesis true and the conclusion false, and $\{ p \lor q \} \not\models p$.
By soundness, $\{ p \lor q \} \not\vdash p$.

The typical use of soundness is this one: one truth assignment proves that no derivation exists.
Watch out: do not read soundness backwards.
Soundness does not say that every tautology has a derivation; that is the completeness theorem.

### L3 human

Soundness says that each truth assignment $v$ is a model of the deductive system: the set $\{ \varphi : v(\varphi) = 1 \}$ contains the axioms and is closed under MP.
Algebraically, a truth assignment gives a homomorphism from the [[Lindenbaum-Tarski algebra|QC9M3Q8B8Q]] to the two-element Boolean algebra, and soundness says that this map is well defined.
The same pattern, with a class of algebras or of structures in place of $\{ 0, 1 \}$, gives soundness for intuitionistic, modal and many-valued logics.
In first-order logic, soundness is the easy half of [[Gödel's completeness theorem|WQ1ZE255SF]].
