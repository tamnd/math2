## Example

The claim under test is "in every triangle $ABC$, the [[altitude|0C9MZY2RNA]] from $C$ meets the side $AB$ at its [[midpoint|0S706JBZBG]]".
We test it first on an equilateral triangle and then on a triangle with no special features.
The first test is not a generic example, and it shows why.

The special test uses $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (2, 0)$ and $C = (1, \sqrt{3})$.

1. The side lengths are $|AB| = 2$, $|AC| = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$ and $|BC| = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$, so the triangle is equilateral.
2. The side $AB$ lies on the $x$-axis, so the altitude from $C$ is the vertical line $x = 1$, and its foot is $(1, 0)$.
3. The midpoint of $AB$ is $\left(\frac{0 + 2}{2}, 0\right) = (1, 0)$.
4. The foot and the midpoint agree, so the special test supports the claim.

The generic test uses $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (4, 0)$ and $C = (1, 3)$.

5. The side lengths are $|AB| = 4$, $|AC| = \sqrt{1 + 9} = \sqrt{10}$ and $|BC| = \sqrt{9 + 9} = \sqrt{18}$, and no two are equal.
6. The side $AB$ lies on the $x$-axis, so the altitude from $C$ is the vertical line $x = 1$, and its foot is $(1, 0)$.
7. The midpoint of $AB$ is $\left(\frac{0 + 4}{2}, 0\right) = (2, 0)$.
8. The foot $(1, 0)$ is not the midpoint $(2, 0)$, so this triangle is a [[counterexample|0ED72CTBNP]], and the claim is false.

## Remarks

The equilateral triangle fails to be a generic example because it has extra symmetry, and the claim depends on exactly that symmetry.
In fact the foot of the altitude from $C$ is the midpoint of $AB$ exactly when $|CA| = |CB|$.
The reason is that the points at equal distance from $A$ and $B$ form the perpendicular bisector of $AB$, the line through the midpoint at a right angle to $AB$.
So the claim holds for every triangle that is isosceles with apex $C$, and it fails for every other triangle.

A generic test triangle has three different side lengths and no right angle.
The triangle of steps 5 to 8 has both properties.
Its squared sides are $16$, $10$ and $18$, and
$$10 + 16 = 26, \qquad 10 + 18 = 28, \qquad 16 + 18 = 34,$$
so no squared side is the sum of the other two, and by the converse of the Pythagorean theorem no angle is right.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Draw a triangle with three equal sides, and drop a line straight down from the top corner.
The line hits the base exactly in the middle.
It is tempting to say that this happens in every triangle.
Now draw a lopsided triangle, with the top corner far to the left.
The straight-down line hits the base to the left of the middle.
The first picture was too neat to be a fair test.

### L2 human

We find every triangle with $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (4, 0)$ and $C = (x, y)$, $y > 0$, for which the claim holds.
The side $AB$ lies on the $x$-axis, so the foot of the altitude from $C$ is $(x, 0)$, while the midpoint of $AB$ is $(2, 0)$.
The two points agree exactly when $x = 2$.
For $x = 2$ the distances from $C$ to the two ends are equal:
$$|CA|^2 = 2^2 + y^2 = 4 + y^2, \qquad |CB|^2 = (2 - 4)^2 + y^2 = 4 + y^2.$$
So the claim holds exactly for the triangles with $|CA| = |CB|$.
The lesson for geometry problems is to draw a figure with no equal sides and no right angle before you guess a property.
Watch out: a neat figure is easier to draw and to read, and that is the same reason it can mislead.
