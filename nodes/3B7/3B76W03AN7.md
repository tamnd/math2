## Example

Let $\Lambda$ be a finite graph with edge set $E$, and let $c_{j\sigma}, c_{j\sigma}^\dagger$ be fermion operators for site $j \in \Lambda$ and spin $\sigma \in \set{\uparrow, \downarrow}$, with $n_{j\sigma} = c_{j\sigma}^\dagger c_{j\sigma}$.
For a hopping amplitude $t > 0$ and an on-site repulsion $U \ge 0$, the *Hubbard model* is the [[lattice model|PAEME5EQRM]] with Hamiltonian
$$H = -t \sum_{\set{i, j} \in E} \sum_{\sigma} \big(c_{i\sigma}^\dagger c_{j\sigma} + c_{j\sigma}^\dagger c_{i\sigma}\big) + U \sum_{j \in \Lambda} n_{j\uparrow} n_{j\downarrow}.$$
The first term is the [[tight-binding model|57Z8F1Q74B]] kinetic energy, and the second penalizes two electrons on the same site.
Check on two sites with two electrons and total spin $0$.
In the basis of the covalent singlet and the symmetric ionic state, $H$ is the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2t \\ -2t & U \end{pmatrix}$.
So the ground-state energy is
$$E_0 = \frac{U}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{U^2}{4} + 4t^2}.$$
The three triplet states cannot hop, since hopping would put two equal spins on one site, so they have energy $0$.

## Remarks

For $U = 0$ the model is free electrons in a band, and for $t = 0$ it is a collection of isolated atoms; the physics comes from the competition.
For $U \gg t$, $E_0 \approx -4t^2/U$, so the singlet lies below the triplet by $J = 4t^2/U$: at half filling the low-energy physics is the antiferromagnetic [[quantum Heisenberg model|5QG15H8P5J]] with this coupling.
The model conserves the number of electrons of each spin and has $SU(2)$ spin symmetry.
Hubbard introduced it in 1963, and Gutzwiller and Kanamori studied it independently in the same year.
It is the simplest model of strongly correlated electrons, used for Mott insulators, itinerant magnetism and, in two dimensions, high-temperature superconductivity.
In one dimension it is exactly solvable.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Electrons in a solid hop from atom to atom, and two electrons on the same atom repel each other strongly.
The Hubbard model keeps only these two effects: hopping between neighbours and a cost $U$ for double occupancy.
When the repulsion wins, electrons stay put one per atom and the material is an insulator even though band theory says it should conduct.
Despite its simple form, most questions about it in two dimensions are still open.
