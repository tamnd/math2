## Statement

Let $n \ge 1$ and consider a [[quantum circuit|S82TXJB05T]] on $n$ qubits with the following properties.

1. It starts in the computational basis state $\lvert 0 \cdots 0 \rangle$.
2. Its gates are the Hadamard gate $H$, the phase gate $S = \operatorname{diag}(1, i)$ and the controlled-NOT gate, applied to any qubits.
3. Its measurements are measurements of single qubits in the computational basis, and later gates may be chosen depending on earlier outcomes.

Then a classical probabilistic algorithm samples the outcomes of the measurements with exactly the right joint distribution.
Its running time is polynomial in $n$ and in the number of gates and measurements.

## Remarks

Proof idea: the gates $H$, $S$ and CNOT generate the [[Clifford group|59QNQWRR77]], which maps the [[Pauli group|M03133GAB6]] to itself under conjugation.
The state at each time is the common $+1$ eigenvector of an abelian group of $n$ independent Pauli operators, its stabilizer.
The algorithm stores $n$ generators as an $n \times 2n$ binary matrix with signs, and updates them under each gate in $O(n)$ steps.
A measurement outcome is either determined, or uniformly random, and both cases are read off from the generators with polynomial work.

Example: $H$ on qubit $1$ and then CNOT from qubit $1$ to each of qubits $2, \dots, n$ gives the GHZ state $(\lvert 0 \cdots 0 \rangle + \lvert 1 \cdots 1 \rangle)/\sqrt2$, which is highly entangled.
Its stabilizer is generated by $X_1 X_2 \cdots X_n$ and $Z_i Z_{i+1}$ for $1 \le i < n$, and the simulation predicts that measuring all qubits gives all $0$s or all $1$s with probability $1/2$ each.

Hypothesis 2 is essential.
Adding the gate $T = \operatorname{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/4})$ gives a universal gate set, and no efficient classical simulation of such circuits is known.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some quantum circuits look very quantum: they create large entangled states.
The theorem says that circuits built only from a few special gates can still be simulated quickly on an ordinary computer.
So entanglement alone does not give a quantum computer its power.

### L2 human

The theorem rests on the stabilizer formalism that also underlies [[stabilizer codes|P2TBJXVGEC]].
Encoding, decoding and syndrome measurement for these codes use only Clifford operations, so they are classically simulable.
To reach universality, fault-tolerant schemes add a non-Clifford gate such as $T$, often through magic state injection.
The simulation cost grows with the number of non-Clifford gates, a measure used in resource theories of magic.
