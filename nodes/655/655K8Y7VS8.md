## Summary

A complete theory is a satisfiable [[theory|92SWXST1ZM]] $T$ that decides every [[sentence|DKFGQTRYZE]] $\sigma$ of its language: $T \models \sigma$ or $T \models \neg\sigma$.
A satisfiable theory is complete exactly when all its [[models|MVCH6N9SNW]] are [[elementarily equivalent|P3B1V182N4]], and exactly when its consequences form the [[theory of a structure|~305c87e7]].
The theory of a single structure is always complete, and proving that a natural list of axioms is complete is a central task of model theory.

## Motivation

A list of axioms is written to describe some structures.
If the list is complete, it settles every first-order question about them, and every model gives the same answers.
Completeness of an explicit, computable list of axioms also gives an algorithm that decides truth in the intended structure.
For example, the completeness of the axioms for real closed fields gives a decision procedure for elementary statements about $\R$.

## Notation

There is no special symbol.
Mathlib's `FirstOrder.Language.Theory.IsComplete` requires that the theory is satisfiable and that it models $\sigma$ or $\neg\sigma$ for each sentence $\sigma$.
Some books do not require satisfiability, so that the inconsistent theory counts as complete; others require that $T$ is deductively closed.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The theory of infinite sets in the empty signature; see [[this example|~61dc1297]].
- The theory of dense linear orders without endpoints, whose models include $(\Q, <)$ and $(\R, <)$; see [[this example|~3ac66b0a]].
- The theory of algebraically closed fields of a fixed characteristic, with models $\C$ and $\overline{\Q}$ in characteristic $0$; see [[this example|~81e191f1]].
- Every $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$, such as true arithmetic $\operatorname{Th}(\N, +, \cdot, 0, 1)$.
- A maximal consistent set of sentences, such as one built in the proof of the completeness theorem; see [[this example|B3CSQE56G8]].

### Non-examples

- The theory of groups; see [[this non-example|~e36be6b7]].
- The theory of fields: it does not decide $1 + 1 = 0$.
- Peano arithmetic, by Gödel's first incompleteness theorem.
- The theory of linear orders, which does not decide density.

### Basic properties

- A satisfiable theory is complete if and only if all its models are elementarily equivalent; see [[the characterization|~3e32bff6]].
- Every satisfiable theory $T$ has a complete extension, namely $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$ for any model $\mathcal{M}$ of $T$.
- A complete theory with a computably enumerable axiomatization is decidable.
- Ways to prove that $T$ is complete:
  1. the [[Łoś-Vaught test|ZR7Y5C235P]]: $T$ has no finite models and is $\kappa$-categorical for some $\kappa \ge \abs{L}$;
  2. [[quantifier elimination|7FDDXV3JK7]] together with a prime substructure that embeds into every model;
  3. a back-and-forth system or [[Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games|2RE7YAZK8R]] between any two models;
  4. an explicit structure $\mathcal{M}$ with $T \models \operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$.

### History

Gödel's first incompleteness theorem of 1931 shows that Peano arithmetic is not complete, although it was written down as an axiomatization of one structure, $(\N, +, \cdot, 0, 1)$.
In 1954 Łoś and Vaught independently gave the categoricity test for completeness.

### Common errors

- To call a theory complete because it is "the natural axioms". The field axioms are natural and not complete.
- To confuse a complete theory with the completeness theorem. The completeness theorem is about proof systems and holds for all theories.
- To think that a complete theory has only one model up to isomorphism. A complete theory with an infinite model has models of every infinite size at least $\abs{L}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A complete theory leaves no first-order question open.
The axioms of groups are not complete: they do not decide whether $x \cdot y = y \cdot x$ holds for all $x$ and $y$.
The axioms of dense linear orders without endpoints are complete: every first-order question about such an order has the same answer in $(\Q, <)$ and in $(\R, <)$.

### L2 human

We show that the theory $T$ of linear orders is not complete, using two models.

1. $(\Z, <) \models T$ and $(\Q, <) \models T$, since both are linear orders.
2. The density sentence $\delta$ holds in $(\Q, <)$ and fails in $(\Z, <)$ at $0, 1$.
3. So $T \not\models \delta$ and $T \not\models \neg\delta$, and $T$ is not complete.

The typical use of two models is to show that a theory is not complete.
A trap is to show completeness by checking finitely many sentences; completeness concerns all sentences.

### L3 human

The complete $L$-theories are the points of the Stone space of the Boolean algebra of sentences modulo equivalence, and each satisfiable theory corresponds to a closed set of points.
Classification theory studies complete theories, and properties such as stability, NIP and o-minimality are properties of a complete theory; see the sub-areas on [[stable theories|2T8HMVV8SB]] and [[NIP theories|RS09CRTDRA]].
The study of countable models of a complete theory, including Vaught's conjecture, is in the sub-area on [[countable models|6XEXRME4C1]].
