## Definition

A subspace is invariant under a linear map when the map sends each of its vectors back into it.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be an [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]].
A [[subspace|00YC1D652K]] $U$ of $V$ is *invariant* under $T$, or *$T$-invariant*, when $T(u) \in U$ for all $u \in U$.
Equivalently, $T(U) \subseteq U$.

## Notation

We write "$U$ is $T$-invariant" or "$U$ is $T$-stable".
For a family $\mathcal{F}$ of endomorphisms of $V$, the subspace $U$ is $\mathcal{F}$-invariant when it is $T$-invariant for each $T \in \mathcal{F}$.

## Remarks

The definition has two parts, and both matter.
First, $U$ must be a subspace, so that the restricted map $U \to U$ is a linear map between vector spaces.
Second, the image $T(u)$ must land in $U$ itself, not just somewhere in $V$.
The inclusion $T(U) \subseteq U$ may be strict: the kernel of $T$ is invariant, and $T$ sends it to $\{0\}$.

To test the condition, you only need a spanning set.
If $U = \operatorname{span}(u_1, \dots, u_k)$ and each $T(u_i)$ lies in $U$, then every $T(c_1 u_1 + \dots + c_k u_k) = c_1 T(u_1) + \dots + c_k T(u_k)$ lies in $U$ as well.
For example, $\{0\}$ and $V$ are invariant under every $T$, and a line $Fv$ with $v \ne 0$ is invariant exactly when $v$ is an [[eigenvector|0N6960W0BV]] of $T$.
Under the map $T(x, y) = (x, x + y)$ the line $\R e_1$ fails the condition, since $T(e_1) = (1, 1)$ leaves the line.

The [[concept page|0ACX5QT8TE]] has more examples, and the [[statement on polynomials in $T$|8YNE1HTZEP]] gives a supply of invariant subspaces: the kernel and the image of every polynomial in $T$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An invariant subspace is a part of the space that the map never leaves.
Vectors inside it stay inside it.
For example, the map that doubles the horizontal direction and triples the vertical one keeps the horizontal axis on itself.
We can then study the map on this smaller space alone.

### L2 human

Let $T(x, y, z) = (y, z, 0)$ on $F^3$, and let $U$ be the plane $z = 0$.
The plane $U$ is spanned by $e_1 = (1, 0, 0)$ and $e_2 = (0, 1, 0)$, so it is enough to test these two vectors.
We get
$$T(e_1) = (0, 0, 0) \in U, \qquad T(e_2) = (1, 0, 0) \in U,$$
so $U$ is $T$-invariant.
The plane $x = 0$ is spanned by $e_2$ and $e_3$.
Here $T(e_3) = (0, 1, 0)$ stays in the plane, but $T(e_2) = (1, 0, 0)$ does not, so this plane is not invariant.
Watch out: one spanning vector that stays inside proves nothing, and every spanning vector must be tested.

### L3 human

An endomorphism $T$ makes $V$ a module over $F[x]$, with $x$ acting as $T$, and the invariant subspaces are exactly the submodules.
The invariant subspaces of $T$ form a lattice under sum and intersection, and this lattice contains every kernel $\ker p(T)$ and every image $\operatorname{im} p(T)$ for $p \in F[x]$.
