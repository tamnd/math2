## Definition

Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be an [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]].
A [[subspace|00YC1D652K]] $U$ of $V$ is *invariant* under $T$, or *$T$-invariant*, when $T(u) \in U$ for all $u \in U$.
Equivalently, $T(U) \subseteq U$.

## Notation

We write "$U$ is $T$-invariant" or "$U$ is $T$-stable".
For a family $\mathcal{F}$ of endomorphisms, $U$ is $\mathcal{F}$-invariant when it is $T$-invariant for each $T \in \mathcal{F}$.

## Remarks

The subspaces $\{0\}$ and $V$ are $T$-invariant for every $T$.
The kernel and the image of each polynomial in $T$ are $T$-invariant, see the [[statement on polynomials in $T$|~62fe55d3]].
A line $Fv$ with $v \ne 0$ is $T$-invariant if and only if $v$ is an [[eigenvector|0N6960W0BV]] of $T$.
To check invariance, it is enough to check $T(u_i) \in U$ for the vectors $u_i$ of a spanning set of $U$, because $T$ is linear and $U$ is closed under linear combinations.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An invariant subspace is a part of the space that the map never leaves.
Vectors inside it stay inside it.
For example, a stretch of the plane along the axes keeps the horizontal axis on itself.
We can then study the map on this smaller space alone.

### L2 human

Let $T(x, y, z) = (y, z, 0)$ on $F^3$, and let $U$ be the plane $z = 0$.

1. The plane $U$ is spanned by $e_1 = (1, 0, 0)$ and $e_2 = (0, 1, 0)$.
2. We have $T(e_1) = (0, 0, 0) \in U$ and $T(e_2) = (1, 0, 0) \in U$.
3. By linearity, $T(U) \subseteq U$, so $U$ is $T$-invariant.
4. The plane $x = 0$ is not invariant, since $T(0, 0, 1) = (0, 1, 0)$ lies in it, but $T(0, 1, 0) = (1, 0, 0)$ does not.

The trap is to check only that $T(U)$ is a subspace, which is true for every subspace $U$.

### L3 human

An endomorphism makes $V$ a module over $F[x]$, and the invariant subspaces are the submodules.
The invariant subspaces of $T$ form a lattice under sum and intersection, which contains the lattice of kernels $\ker p(T)$.
