## Statement

Over a field, row and column replacements alone bring every square matrix to diagonal form.
Let $F$ be a [[field|0QA8CHH8MG]], let $n \ge 1$ and let $M$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$.
Then there are finitely many [[transvection matrices|0NMN00FNW1]] $L_1, \dots, L_r$ and $R_1, \dots, R_s$ and a [[diagonal matrix|0Q9PGXD9E4]] $D$ over $F$ such that
$$M = L_1 \cdots L_r \, D \, R_1 \cdots R_s.$$
Here $r$ or $s$ can be $0$, and an empty product is $I_n$.

## Remarks

### The theorem at work

Take $M = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which a row exchange would normally handle.
Right multiplication by $T_{21}(1)$ adds column 2 to column 1, and then right multiplication by $T_{12}(-1)$ subtracts column 1 from column 2:
$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = D.$$
So $M\,T_{21}(1)\,T_{12}(-1) = D$, and inverting the two factors gives $M = D\,T_{12}(1)\,T_{21}(-1)$.
Check:
$$T_{12}(1)\,T_{21}(-1) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = M.$$

### Why no exchanges or scalings are needed

A row exchange is not a transvection, but it can be imitated up to a sign by three of them:
$$T_{12}(1)\,T_{21}(-1)\,T_{12}(1) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Scalings are not needed at all, because the diagonal factor $D$ absorbs them.
The price is that $D$ is not unique: for nonzero $a$ and $b$, both $\operatorname{diag}(a, b)$ and $\operatorname{diag}(ab, 1)$ arise from the same matrix $\operatorname{diag}(a, b)$.
The reason is that $\operatorname{diag}(ab, 1) = \operatorname{diag}(a, b) \operatorname{diag}(b, b^{-1})$, and the factor $\operatorname{diag}(b, b^{-1})$ has determinant $1$, so it is a product of transvections.

### Why the hypotheses are needed

The proof divides by a nonzero entry, so it needs a field.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the statement still holds, because the Euclidean algorithm replaces the division.
Over a general commutative ring it fails.
Over the polynomial ring $F[x, y]$, Cohn showed that the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 + xy & x^2 \\ -y^2 & 1 - xy \end{pmatrix}$, whose determinant is $(1 - x^2y^2) + x^2y^2 = 1$, is not a product of transvections.
It has no form $LDR$ either: $D$ would have determinant $1$, hence unit diagonal entries, and such a diagonal matrix is a product of transvections.
The matrix must be square, because $D$ is a square diagonal matrix; for $m \times n$ matrices the analogue is the [[rank normal form|T2H2QV0M06]].

### What it gives you

Each transvection has [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] $1$, so $\det M = \det D$, by the [[multiplicativity of the determinant|04CPJ6AF00]].
The rank of $M$ is the number of nonzero diagonal entries of $D$, since the transvections are invertible.
The theorem is the main step in the proof that the [[special linear group is generated by transvections|YSS4XNY9RC]].
It also gives an induction principle: a property of square matrices that holds for diagonal matrices and transvections, and passes to products, holds for all square matrices.

### Generalizations

Over a Euclidean domain, the same argument with the Euclidean algorithm leads to the Smith normal form, which belongs to the theory of modules.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Using only the move "add a multiple of one row, or one column, to another", every square matrix over a field can be made diagonal.
No swaps and no scalings are needed.
These moves do not change the determinant, so the diagonal matrix keeps the determinant of the original matrix.

### L2 human

The working method is to pick a nonzero entry as a pivot, use row additions to clear its column and column additions to clear its row, and repeat on the smaller block that is left.
If the pivot position holds a $0$, one addition moves a nonzero entry there, as in the example above.
A trap: the factors on the right act on columns, and $T_{ij}(c)$ on the right adds $c$ times column $i$ to column $j$, with the indices in the opposite roles from rows.

### L3 human

The formal statement in Mathlib has exactly this form, with lists of transvections on both sides of a diagonal matrix.
Over a commutative ring $R$, the subgroup generated by transvections is the elementary group $E_n(R)$, and the question whether it equals $\mathrm{SL}_n(R)$ leads to algebraic K-theory; Cohn's matrix shows that the answer is no for $n = 2$ and $R = F[x, y]$.
