## Statement

Let $F$ be a [[field|0QA8CHH8MG]], let $n \ge 1$ and let $M$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$.
Then there are finitely many [[transvection matrices|0NMN00FNW1]] $L_1, \dots, L_r$ and $R_1, \dots, R_s$ and a [[diagonal matrix|0Q9PGXD9E4]] $D$ over $F$ such that
$$M = L_1 \cdots L_r \, D \, R_1 \cdots R_s.$$
Here $r$ or $s$ can be $0$, and an empty product is $I_n$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The proof divides by a nonzero entry, so it needs a field.
Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the statement still holds, by the Euclidean algorithm, but over general commutative rings it fails.
Over the polynomial ring $F[x, y]$, Cohn showed that $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + xy & x^2 \\ -y^2 & 1 - xy \end{pmatrix}$, of determinant $1$, is not a product of transvections.
Such an $M$ has no form $LDR$.
The diagonal $D$ would have determinant $1$ and unit entries, and such a $D$ is a product of transvections.
The matrix must be square, because the statement uses square diagonal matrices; a version for $m \times n$ matrices gives the [[rank normal form|T2H2QV0M06]] after scalings.

### Consequences

- Each transvection has [[determinant|00KDZRW4C0]] $1$, so $\det M = \det D$, by the [[multiplicativity of the determinant|04CPJ6AF00]].
- The rank of $M$ is the number of nonzero diagonal entries of $D$.
- The lemma is the main step in the proof that the [[special linear group is generated by transvections|YSS4XNY9RC]].

### Converse

Each product $L_1 \cdots L_r D R_1 \cdots R_s$ is an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$, so the converse holds with no extra work.
The diagonal matrix $D$ is not unique: $\operatorname{diag}(a, b)$ and $\operatorname{diag}(ab, 1)$ are both possible for an invertible $2 \times 2$ matrix with $\det = ab$.

### Generalizations

Over a Euclidean domain, the Euclidean algorithm replaces the division, and the result is the Smith normal form; this belongs to the theory of modules.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Using only the move "add a multiple of one row (or column) to another", every square matrix over a field can be made diagonal.
No swaps and no scalings are needed.
These moves do not change the determinant, so the diagonal matrix keeps the determinant of the original matrix.

### L2 human

Worked example: $M = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. Add column 2 to column 1, which is right multiplication by $T_{21}(1)$: we get $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. Subtract column 1 from column 2, which is right multiplication by $T_{12}(-1)$: we get $D = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. So $M T_{21}(1) T_{12}(-1) = D$, and $M = D\, T_{12}(1)\, T_{21}(-1)$.
4. Check: $T_{12}(1) T_{21}(-1) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $D$ times this is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = M$.

A trap: row exchanges are not transvections, but a row exchange can be imitated up to a sign by three transvections.

### L3 human

This is the form of the lemma in Mathlib, where it serves to prove that $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$ is generated by transvections.
It also serves to prove properties of the determinant by induction.
Over a ring, the subgroup generated by transvections is the elementary group $E_n(R)$, and the question whether it equals $\mathrm{SL}_n(R)$ leads to algebraic K-theory.
