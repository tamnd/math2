## Problem

Let $M$ and $W$ be two disjoint sets with $n$ elements each, called men and women.
Each man has a strict [[total order|0FRXBVVR03]] of preference on $W$, and each woman has a strict total order of preference on $M$.
A *matching* is a bijection $\mu \colon M \to W$, a perfect [[matching|0MJ04A50QW]] of the complete [[bipartite graph|0ZVQKTRKVQ]] between $M$ and $W$.
A pair $(m, w)$ with $\mu(m) \ne w$ *blocks* $\mu$ if $m$ prefers $w$ to $\mu(m)$ and $w$ prefers $m$ to $\mu^{-1}(w)$.
A matching is *stable* if no pair blocks it.
The problem is to decide whether a stable matching always exists, and to find one.

## Remarks

David Gale and Lloyd Shapley posed and solved the problem in their 1962 paper "College admissions and the stability of marriage".
A stable matching always exists, and the Gale–Shapley deferred acceptance algorithm finds one with at most $n^2$ proposals.
Example: with men $m_1, m_2$ and women $w_1, w_2$, where both men prefer $w_1$ and both women prefer $m_1$, the matching $\set{(m_1, w_1), (m_2, w_2)}$ is stable and the other matching is blocked by $(m_1, w_1)$.
An instance can have many stable matchings, and they form a distributive lattice.
The same question without the two sides, where any two people may be paired, is the stable roommates problem, and there a stable matching need not exist.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Pair up equal numbers of men and women, each of whom ranks the people of the other group.
A pairing is unstable if some man and woman would both rather be with each other than with their partners, because they would then leave.
The question is whether a pairing without such a pair always exists, and the answer is yes.
