## Statement

Every square matrix is a root of its own characteristic polynomial.
Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$ with [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]]
$$p_A(t) = \det(t I_n - A) = t^n + c_{n-1} t^{n-1} + \cdots + c_1 t + c_0 \in K[t].$$
Then
$$p_A(A) = A^n + c_{n-1} A^{n-1} + \cdots + c_1 A + c_0 I_n = 0,$$
where $p_A(A)$ is the [[value of the polynomial at A|0E3MV6E6MG]], with the constant term read as $c_0 I_n$.

## Remarks

### A first check on a 2 by 2 matrix

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.
Its trace is $5$ and its determinant is $4 - 6 = -2$, so
$$p_A(t) = (t - 1)(t - 4) - 6 = t^2 - 5t - 2.$$
Square the matrix:
$$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 6 & 2 + 8 \\ 3 + 12 & 6 + 16 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 10 \\ 15 & 22 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Then $5A + 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 10 \\ 15 & 22 \end{pmatrix}$ as well, so $A^2 - 5A - 2I = 0$, as the theorem says.
For every $2 \times 2$ matrix the theorem reads $A^2 - (\operatorname{tr} A) A + (\det A) I = 0$, and you can confirm it by multiplying out with letters $a, b, c, d$.

### Watch out: putting t = A is not a proof

The tempting argument is $p_A(A) = \det(A I - A) = \det 0 = 0$.
It is wrong, and the reasons teach something.
First, $p_A(A)$ is an $n \times n$ matrix, while $\det(A - A)$ is a scalar, so the two sides are not even the same kind of object.
Second, in $\det(tI - A)$ the letter $t$ sits inside the entries, and putting a matrix there gives a matrix whose entries are matrices.
Third, the same argument proves false statements.
Let $q(t) = \operatorname{tr}(tI - A) = nt - \operatorname{tr} A$, and "substitute" to get $q(A) = \operatorname{tr}(A - A) = 0$.
For $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, 0)$ we have $q(t) = 2t - 1$, but
$$q(A) = 2A - I = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1) \ne 0.$$
The [[proof with the adjugate|Y4MWFGD909]] avoids the trap: it compares coefficients of $t$ in an identity of matrices over $K[t]$, where every product makes sense.

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The ring $K$ must be commutative.
The determinant, and so $p_A$, is defined by the Leibniz sum of products of entries, and that sum depends on the order of the factors when the entries do not commute.

The matrix must be square, since otherwise $A^2$ is not defined and there is no determinant.

The sign convention matters only for the leading coefficient.
Some books use $\det(A - tI) = (-1)^n \det(tI - A)$, which also vanishes at $A$ but has leading coefficient $-1$ when $n$ is odd.
The statement above uses the monic form $\det(tI - A)$, which is also the form of Mathlib's `Matrix.charpoly`.

### Three proofs

The [[proof with the adjugate|Y4MWFGD909]] works over every commutative ring and is given in full.
It writes $\operatorname{adj}(tI - A)$ as a polynomial in $t$ with matrix coefficients and lets the identity $(tI - A) \operatorname{adj}(tI - A) = p_A(t) I$ telescope.

A second proof uses [[triangularization|0363JMQ42Y]] over $\mathbb{C}$.
Write $A = S U S^{-1}$ with $U$ upper triangular, and let $V_i$ be the span of $e_1, \dots, e_i$.
The factor $U - u_{ii} I$ maps $V_i$ into $V_{i-1}$, so the product $p_A(U) = \prod_i (U - u_{ii} I)$ maps $V_n$ step by step into $V_0 = 0$.
Each entry of $p_A(A)$ is a polynomial with integer coefficients in the entries of $A$, and a polynomial identity that holds on all of $M_n(\mathbb{C})$ holds over every commutative ring.

A third proof makes $K^n$ a module over $K[t]$, with $t$ acting as $A$.
Since $A e_j = \sum_i a_{ij} e_i$, the matrix $tI - A^{\mathsf{T}}$ sends the column $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ to zero, and multiplying by its adjugate shows that $\det(tI - A)$ kills every $e_i$.
This is the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem for modules|SH3R5VTMZH]].

### What it gives you

The [[minimal polynomial divides the characteristic polynomial|093X6MN6Y6]], so its degree is at most $n$.
Every power $A^k$ with $k \ge n$ is a combination of $I, A, \dots, A^{n-1}$, because $A^n$ is one and each higher power reduces to the lower ones.
When $c_0 = (-1)^n \det A$ is a unit, the theorem gives the inverse as a polynomial in $A$:
$$A^{-1} = -c_0^{-1} \left( A^{n-1} + c_{n-1} A^{n-2} + \cdots + c_1 I \right).$$
For the matrix above this is $A^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2}(A - 5I)$, and the page on [[inverse and powers by the Cayley-Hamilton theorem|R4CPYXFJEQ]] works such examples.
The basis-free form, $p_T(T) = 0$ for an endomorphism $T$, is the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem for linear maps|0QEM68Y0DE]].

### The converse

The converse is false: a matrix can be a root of a monic polynomial of degree $n$ that is not its characteristic polynomial.
The identity $I_2$ is a root of $(t - 1)(t - 2)$, because $(I - I)(I - 2I) = 0$, but $p_{I_2}(t) = (t - 1)^2$.
What is true is that every polynomial with root $A$ is a multiple of the minimal polynomial.

### Generalizations

For an endomorphism of a finitely generated module over a commutative ring, the [[module version|SH3R5VTMZH]] gives a monic polynomial that kills it, by the same determinant argument as in the third proof.
That argument is also the standard route to Nakayama's lemma in commutative algebra.
Over a field, the characteristic polynomial in turn divides the $n$-th power of the minimal polynomial, so the two polynomials have the same irreducible factors.

## History

Cayley stated the theorem for square matrices of every size in his 1858 memoir on the theory of matrices.
He gave a proof only for size $2$ and wrote that he had checked size $3$.
Hamilton had earlier proved a form of the result for linear maps of the quaternions.
Frobenius gave the first general proof in 1878.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A square matrix can be multiplied by itself, so you can put it into a polynomial.
The characteristic polynomial is the one whose roots are the eigenvalues, and the theorem says that the matrix itself is also a "root".
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ the polynomial is $t^2 - 5t - 2$, and indeed $A^2 = 5A + 2I$.
So you never need more than $I$ and $A$ to write any power of this matrix.

### L2 human

Here is a $3 \times 3$ check that also shows why the theorem is true.
Take the upper triangular matrix
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_A(t) = (t - 2)^2 (t - 3),$$
where $p_A$ is the product of the factors $t - a_{ii}$ because $tI - A$ is triangular.
First square $A - 2I$:
$$A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (A - 2I)^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The only nonzero row of $(A - 2I)^2$ is the third, and the third row of $A - 3I$ is zero, so
$$p_A(A) = (A - 2I)^2 (A - 3I) = 0.$$
Each factor pushes the vectors down the chain of spans of $e_1, e_2, e_3$, which is the idea of the triangular proof.
Watch out: the shorter product $(A - 2I)(A - 3I)$ is not zero, since its first row is $(0, -1, 0)$, so here the minimal polynomial is the whole of $p_A$.

### L3 human

The theorem is a set of $n^2$ polynomial identities with integer coefficients in the entries of a generic matrix.
So it is enough to prove it over one field of characteristic $0$ that contains these entries as independent variables, and then to specialize to any commutative ring.
In the language of modules, $K^n$ with $t$ acting as $A$ is the quotient $K[t]^n / (tI - A) K[t]^n$, and the theorem says that the determinant of a square presentation matrix annihilates the module.
The [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] and the invariant factors refine this, and the sub-area on canonical forms uses them to classify matrices up to similarity.
