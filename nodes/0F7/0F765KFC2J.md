## Statement

Let $K$ be a commutative ring, let $n \ge 1$, and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $K$ with [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]] $p_A(t) = \det(t I_n - A) = t^n + c_{n-1} t^{n-1} + \cdots + c_0$.
Then
$$p_A(A) = A^n + c_{n-1} A^{n-1} + \cdots + c_1 A + c_0 I_n = 0,$$
where $p_A(A)$ is the [[value of p_A at A|0E3MV6E6MG]].

## Remarks

The [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem for linear maps|0QEM68Y0DE]] states $p_T(T) = 0$ for an endomorphism $T$ of a finite-dimensional vector space.
The main proof is the [[proof with the adjugate|Y4MWFGD909]], valid over every commutative ring.

Two other proofs are standard.

- Triangularisation and density. Over $\C$, write $A = SUS^{-1}$ with $U$ upper triangular, by [[triangularisation|0363JMQ42Y]]. Then $p_A(U) = \prod_i (U - u_{ii} I)$, and the $i$-th factor maps $\operatorname{span}(e_1, \dots, e_i)$ into $\operatorname{span}(e_1, \dots, e_{i-1})$. So the product is $0$. Alternatively, matrices with $n$ distinct eigenvalues are diagonalisable and dense in $M_n(\C)$, and $A \mapsto p_A(A)$ is continuous. The identity then holds over every commutative ring, because its entries are polynomials with integer coefficients in the entries of $A$.
- Modules. Make $K^n$ a module over $K[t]$ with $t$ acting as $A$. The matrix $tI - A$ kills the column of basis vectors, and its adjugate shows that $\det(tI - A)$ kills the module. This is the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem for modules|SH3R5VTMZH]].

### Why each hypothesis is needed

- $K$ must be commutative, so that $\det$ and $p_A$ are defined in the usual way.
- The matrix must be square. For a nonsquare matrix, $A^2$ is not defined.
- Sign convention. $\det(A - tI) = (-1)^n \det(tI - A)$, so both conventions give an annihilating polynomial. But only $\det(tI - A)$ is monic for odd $n$. Mathlib's `Matrix.charpoly` uses $\det(tI - A)$, the convention of this statement.

### Why substituting $t = A$ is not a proof

The tempting argument is "$p_A(A) = \det(AI - A) = \det(0) = 0$".
It fails for three reasons.

1. $p_A(A)$ is an $n \times n$ matrix, but $\det(A - A)$ is a scalar. The two sides are objects of different kinds.
2. In $\det(tI - A)$, the variable $t$ appears inside the entries of a matrix. Replacing $t$ by a matrix gives a matrix whose entries are matrices, and its determinant is not defined by the usual formula.
3. The same argument would prove false statements. Let $q(t) = \tr(tI - A) = nt - \tr A$. The argument would give $q(A) = \tr(AI - A) = 0$. For $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, 0)$, $q(t) = 2t - 1$ and $q(A) = 2A - I = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1) \ne 0$.

The adjugate proof repairs this: it compares coefficients of $t$ in an identity of matrices over $K[t]$, where every product is well defined.

### Consequences

- The [[minimal polynomial divides the characteristic polynomial|093X6MN6Y6]], so $\deg m_A \le n$.
- Every power $A^k$ with $k \ge n$ is a combination of $I, A, \dots, A^{n-1}$.
- If $A$ is invertible, then $c_0 = (-1)^n \det A$ is a unit, and $A^{-1} = -c_0^{-1}(A^{n-1} + c_{n-1}A^{n-2} + \cdots + c_1 I)$. See [[inverse and powers by Cayley-Hamilton|R4CPYXFJEQ]].
- For $n = 2$: $A^2 - (\tr A)A + (\det A)I = 0$.

### Converse

The converse fails: a matrix can satisfy a monic polynomial of degree $n$ that is not its characteristic polynomial.
The identity $I_2$ satisfies the monic polynomial $(t - 1)(t - 2)$ of degree $2$, because $(I - I)(I - 2I) = 0$.
But $p_{I_2} = (t - 1)^2$.

### Generalizations

- The theorem holds for an endomorphism of a finitely generated module over a commutative ring, in the form of the [[Cayley-Hamilton theorem for modules|SH3R5VTMZH]]. This is the source of Nakayama's lemma.
- The characteristic polynomial divides a power of the minimal polynomial: $p_A \mid m_A^n$. See the theorem that the [[minimal polynomial divides the characteristic polynomial|093X6MN6Y6]].

## History

Cayley stated the theorem for matrices of every size in his 1858 memoir on the theory of matrices, but published a proof only for size $2$ and reported that he had checked size $3$.
Hamilton had earlier proved a version for linear functions of quaternions.
Frobenius gave the first general proof in 1878.
These facts follow the Wikipedia article on the theorem.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial.
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, the polynomial is $t^2 - 5t - 2$, so $A^2 = 5A + 2I$.
So every power of $A$ is a combination of $I$ and $A$.

### L2 human

We check the theorem for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. $p_A(t) = t^2 - (\tr A) t + \det A = t^2 - 5t + (4 - 6) = t^2 - 5t - 2$.
2. $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 6 & 2 + 8 \\ 3 + 12 & 6 + 16 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 10 \\ 15 & 22 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. $5A + 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 10 \\ 15 & 22 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. So $A^2 - 5A - 2I = 0$.

The typical use is to reduce powers and to write $A^{-1}$ as a polynomial in $A$.
A trap: with $\det(A - tI)$ for odd $n$ the polynomial has leading coefficient $-1$.
It still annihilates $A$, but it is not monic.

### L3 human

The theorem is a polynomial identity in the $n^2$ entries of a generic matrix over $\mathbb{Z}$.
So it is enough to prove it over $\C$, or over any field containing $\mathbb{Z}[a_{ij}]$, and then specialise.
In module language, it says that the Fitting ideal $\det(tI - A)$ annihilates $K[t]^n / (tI - A)K[t]^n \cong K^n$.
