## Summary

The Ball–James theory describes the fine microstructures of crystals that undergo a [[martensitic transformation|EZRAMFGRZT]] as limits of minimising sequences of a nonlinear elastic energy.
The energy density has several wells, one for each variant of martensite, and it is not [[quasiconvex|DVJ17KVWXP]], so the minimum is in general not attained.
Instead, minimising sequences develop finer and finer oscillations between wells, which are recorded by gradient [[Young measures|6TQSVJQDEH]].
The theory explains twins, austenite–martensite interfaces and laminates through rank-one compatibility conditions.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A crystal of martensite is often made of many thin layers in which the lattice is tilted in two alternating ways.
The theory says that these layers appear because the crystal tries to make its stored elastic energy as small as possible.
Each tilted shape alone is a state of zero energy, but a single shape may not fit the boundary conditions.
Mixing two shapes in very fine layers fits the boundary on average, and the finer the layers, the smaller the energy.
Two shapes can meet along a flat interface without tearing only if their deformation gradients differ by a matrix of rank one.
