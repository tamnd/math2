## Problem

Let $T > 0$ and $W > 0$.
Let $\mathcal B_W$ be the space of functions $f \in L^2(\R)$ whose [[Fourier transform|DPVM34ADZV]] $\hat f(\xi) = \int_\R f(t) e^{-2\pi i \xi t} \, dt$ vanishes outside $[-W, W]$, that is, the [[band-limited functions|P3T104G564]] with band $W$.
For nonzero $f \in \mathcal B_W$, let
$$\lambda(f) = \frac{\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} \abs{f(t)}^2 \, dt}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \abs{f(t)}^2 \, dt}$$
be the fraction of the energy of $f$ in the time interval $[-T/2, T/2]$.
Find the supremum of $\lambda(f)$ over nonzero $f \in \mathcal B_W$, and find the functions that attain it.

## Remarks

The problem is solved.
The answer is an [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] problem for the [[integral operator|H8P36XFV8K]]
$$(A g)(t) = \int_{-T/2}^{T/2} \frac{\sin\big(2\pi W (t - s)\big)}{\pi (t - s)} \, g(s) \, ds, \qquad t \in [-T/2, T/2],$$
on $L^2([-T/2, T/2])$.
This operator is compact, self-adjoint and positive, with simple eigenvalues $1 > \lambda_0 > \lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > \cdots > 0$ tending to $0$.
The supremum of $\lambda(f)$ is $\lambda_0$, and it is attained exactly by the multiples of the band-limited extension of the first eigenfunction, the first [[prolate spheroidal wave function|QNHYXDFJJY]].
More generally, $\lambda_n$ is the best concentration among band-limited functions orthogonal to the first $n$ eigenfunctions.
The eigenvalues depend on $T$ and $W$ only through the product $WT$.

A key fact behind the solution is that $A$ commutes with a second-order differential operator, so its eigenfunctions are the classical prolate spheroidal wave functions.
Roughly $2WT$ of the eigenvalues are close to $1$ and the rest are close to $0$.
Since $\lambda_0 < 1$, no nonzero function is both band-limited and supported in $[-T/2, T/2]$.

Slepian and Pollak published the solution in 1961; later papers by Landau, Pollak and Slepian developed the theory further.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A signal cannot be both strictly confined in time and strictly confined in frequency.
The question here is how close one can come.
Among signals with no frequencies above $W$, which one keeps the largest share of its energy inside a time window of length $T$?
The answer is a special function, the prolate spheroidal wave function, and the best share is a number just below $1$.
The count $2WT$ says roughly how many independent signals fit into a time window and a band.
This is the basis of the idea of "degrees of freedom" of a signal.
