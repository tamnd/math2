## Statement

Consider a fluid of hard cylindrical rods of length $L$ and diameter $D$ at number density $c$ and temperature $T$, with only excluded-volume interactions.
Describe the orientations by a probability density $f$ on the unit sphere $S^2$ with $f(u) = f(-u)$.
In the second virial approximation, the free energy per rod is as follows, up to an additive constant:
$$\frac{F}{k_B T} = \ln c + \int_{S^2} f(u) \ln\big(4\pi f(u)\big) \, du + \frac{c}{2} \int_{S^2} \int_{S^2} f(u) f(u') \, v(u, u') \, du \, du'.$$
Here $v(u, u') = 2 L^2 D \abs{\sin \gamma(u, u')}$ is the excluded volume of two rods at angle $\gamma$, to leading order in $D/L$.
Let $\phi = c \pi L D^2 / 4$ be the volume fraction.
Then, in the limit $D/L \to 0$:

1. There is a first-order transition from the isotropic phase, $f \equiv 1/(4\pi)$, to a nematic phase, in which $f$ is concentrated near a direction $\pm n$.
The phases are found by minimizing $F$ over $f$ and equating pressure and chemical potential.
2. The coexisting isotropic and nematic phases have volume fractions $\phi_I \approx 3.29 \, D/L$ and $\phi_N \approx 4.19 \, D/L$.

## Remarks

The idea is a competition between two entropies.
The orientational term $\int f \ln(4\pi f)$ is smallest for the isotropic state.
The excluded-volume term is smaller when the rods are parallel, because $\abs{\sin\gamma}$ is then small, and this leaves more free volume to move in.
The second term grows with $c$, so at high enough density alignment wins.
The numbers in clause 2 come from a numerical minimization over $f$; Onsager's original variational trial function gives slightly larger values.
For thin rods the higher virial terms become negligible compared to the second one, which is why the result becomes exact as $D/L \to 0$.
Example: for rods with $L/D = 100$, the transition occurs at a volume fraction of only about $3$ to $4$ percent.
The theory explains why suspensions of long thin particles, such as tobacco mosaic virus, form nematic phases at low concentration.
The nematic [[order parameter|XSA203109Z]] jumps at the transition, as in the [[Landau–de Gennes theory|971G1WN0QX]].

## Explanations

### L1 human

Long thin rods in a box, with nothing but the rule that they cannot overlap, line up on their own when they are crowded enough.
Lining up costs the freedom to point anywhere, but it gives each rod more room to move.
Onsager computed when the second gain beats the first loss.
The answer: for rods a hundred times longer than wide, a few percent of the volume is enough.

### L2 human

Onsager's theory is the first example of an ordering transition driven purely by entropy.
It is a density functional theory in which the unknown is the orientation distribution $f$.
The minimizer satisfies a nonlinear integral equation, $\ln(4\pi f(u)) = \lambda - c \int v(u, u') f(u') \, du'$, with a constant $\lambda$ fixed by normalization.
Near the transition, the free energy as a function of the order parameter has the shape assumed in Landau–de Gennes theory.
In particular, a cubic term makes the transition first order.
