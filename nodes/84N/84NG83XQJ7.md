## Example

On $\R^4$ with coordinates $(x, y, z, t)$, let
$$Q(x, y, z, t) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - t^2.$$
This is the form of [[Minkowski space|G49G4R1K04]], in units where the speed of light is $1$.

1. $Q$ is already diagonal, with coefficients $1, 1, 1, -1$.
2. So $p = 3$, $q = 1$ and $r = 0$, and the [[signature|0KQZYGEJTF]] is $(3, 1, 0)$.
3. In the other convention, the signature is the number $p - q = 2$.
4. Many physics texts use $-Q = t^2 - x^2 - y^2 - z^2$. It has $(p, q) = (1, 3)$ and $p - q = -2$.
5. The two forms describe the same geometry, because $Q$ and $-Q$ have the same zero set and the same orthogonal group.

## Remarks

In the convention $Q$, a vector $v$ is called spacelike when $Q(v) > 0$, timelike when $Q(v) < 0$ and lightlike when $Q(v) = 0$ and $v \ne 0$.
For example, $Q(1, 0, 0, 1) = 1 - 1 = 0$, so $(1, 0, 0, 1)$ is lightlike.
$Q(0, 0, 0, 1) = -1$, so the time axis is timelike.
The largest subspace on which $Q$ is positive definite has dimension $p = 3$: it is the space $t = 0$.
The group of linear maps that keep $Q$ is [[the indefinite orthogonal group|M2900DMEW6]] $O(3, 1)$, the Lorentz group.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Minkowski form adds three squares of space coordinates and subtracts the square of time.
So it has three plus signs and one minus sign.
Physicists write its signature as $(+, +, +, -)$, or as $(-, -, -, +)$ when they change all signs.

### L2 human

The typical use is in special relativity, where $Q(v)$ is the squared interval between two events.
A trap: a formula from a book with signature $(1, 3)$ changes sign in a book with signature $(3, 1)$.
For example, the squared length of the vector $(0, 0, 0, 1)$ is $-1$ in one convention and $1$ in the other.
