## Example

On $\mathbb{R}^4$ with coordinates $(x, y, z, t)$, let
$$Q(x, y, z, t) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - t^2.$$
This is the [[quadratic form|0HZNE5QZDJ]] of [[Minkowski space|G49G4R1K04]], in units where the speed of light is $1$.
We read off its signature and compare the two sign conventions of physics.

1. $Q$ is already diagonal, with coefficients $1, 1, 1, -1$.
2. So $p = 3$, $q = 1$ and $r = 0$, and the [[signature|0KQZYGEJTF]] is $(3, 1, 0)$.
3. In the convention where the signature is the number $p - q$, it is $3 - 1 = 2$.
4. Many physics texts use $-Q = t^2 - x^2 - y^2 - z^2$ in place of $Q$.
   It has $(p, q) = (1, 3)$ and $p - q = -2$.
5. The two forms describe the same geometry.
   They have the same zero set, and a linear map keeps $Q$ exactly when it keeps $-Q$.

## Remarks

With the form $Q$, a vector $v$ is called spacelike when $Q(v) > 0$, timelike when $Q(v) < 0$, and lightlike when $Q(v) = 0$ and $v \ne 0$.
For example, $Q(1, 0, 0, 1) = 1 - 1 = 0$, so $(1, 0, 0, 1)$ is lightlike.
Since $Q(0, 0, 0, 1) = -1$, the time axis is timelike.

The largest subspace on which $Q$ is positive definite has dimension $p = 3$, and the space $t = 0$ is one such subspace.
No subspace of dimension $2$ is negative definite, because $q = 1$.
Check: a plane meets the space $t = 0$ in at least a line, by a dimension count in $\mathbb{R}^4$, and $Q > 0$ on that line away from $0$.

The group of linear maps that keep $Q$ is [[the indefinite orthogonal group|M2900DMEW6]] $O(3, 1)$, the Lorentz group.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The Minkowski form adds the squares of the three space coordinates and subtracts the square of time.
So it has three plus signs and one minus sign.
Physicists write its signature as $(+, +, +, -)$, or as $(-, -, -, +)$ when they change all signs.

### L2 human

The typical use is in special relativity, where $Q(v)$ is the squared interval between two events.
Watch out: a formula from a book that uses $(1, 3)$ changes sign in a book that uses $(3, 1)$.
For example, the squared length of the vector $(0, 0, 0, 1)$ is $-1$ in one convention and $1$ in the other.
