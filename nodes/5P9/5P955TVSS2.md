## Summary

When a definition says "the", it promises that exactly one object fits, and that promise is a theorem that someone must prove.
"The limit of a sequence" makes sense only because a sequence of real numbers cannot converge to two different numbers.

A definition by definite description introduces an object as "the $x$ such that $C(x)$".
Such a [[definition|8ZCZ3HW91Z]] is legal only after a proof of
$$\exists! x \, C(x),$$
which has two parts: existence (at least one $x$ satisfies $C(x)$) and uniqueness (at most one $x$ does).
In logic the phrase "the $x$ such that $C(x)$" is a [[definite description|PD2R4XT79Q]], and $\exists!$ is the [[uniqueness quantifier|0QAJHFGZZJ]].

## Motivation

A name such as "the limit", "the identity element" or "the greatest common divisor" is used in later proofs as one fixed object.
If no object satisfies the condition, the name refers to nothing, and every statement about it is empty.
If two objects satisfy it, the name does not say which one is meant, and two readers may calculate with different objects.
For example, "the square root of $4$" is ambiguous until a convention picks $2$ and not $-2$.
So each "the" in a definition is backed by a theorem, or by an extra condition that removes the choice.

The same obligation returns all through mathematics.
A [[well-defined|HNGZEBKVY2]] operation needs a related proof, that a value does not depend on a choice.
The phrase [[up to|CBC72TX3NQ]] handles objects that are unique only up to an equivalence relation.

## Notation

The formula $\exists! x \, C(x)$ means "there is exactly one $x$ with $C(x)$", and it abbreviates
$$\exists x \, \big( C(x) \land \forall y \, (C(y) \Rightarrow y = x) \big).$$
Logic books write $\iota x \, C(x)$ for "the $x$ such that $C(x)$", but mathematics books rarely use the symbol $\iota$.
Instead, each description gets its own notation, such as $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n$, $e_G$ or $\gcd(a, b)$.
Books often put the uniqueness proof just before the definition, as a lemma, and then say that the lemma "justifies" the word "the".

## Remarks

### A first case: the limit

The [[limit of a sequence|05M5SWSZZD]] is defined like this: $L$ is a limit of $(a_n)$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $N$ with $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$ for all $n \ge N$.
Before we may write "the limit", we prove that a sequence has at most one limit.
Suppose that $L$ and $M$ are both limits and $L \ne M$, and take $\varepsilon = |L - M|/2$.
For all large $n$ both $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$ and $|a_n - M| < \varepsilon$ hold, so the triangle inequality gives
$$|L - M| \le |L - a_n| + |a_n - M| < 2\varepsilon = |L - M|,$$
which is impossible.
So $L = M$, which is the [[uniqueness of limits|0ZXJ2455NJ]].

Existence is a different matter: the sequence $(-1)^n$ has no limit at all.
So books say "the limit, if it exists", and each use of the name needs its own proof that the sequence converges.
In a general topological space a sequence can have two limits, and the [[Hausdorff condition|0D8T984XEE]] is the hypothesis that restores uniqueness.

### The two obligations

The limit shows the pattern that every definite description follows.
To define "the $x$ such that $C(x)$", we prove existence and uniqueness.

1. Existence: some $x$ satisfies $C(x)$.
   Often this is a construction, an axiom, or a theorem such as the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$.
2. Uniqueness: if $C(x)$ and $C(y)$, then $x = y$.
   The standard proof takes two arbitrary objects with the property and shows that they are equal.

Uniqueness is often the shorter proof, and books often prove it first.
When only uniqueness is known, the definition may still say "the $x$ with $C(x)$, if one exists".

### More uniqueness proofs

The [[identity element of a group|9BA5AWWYD9]] is unique, because two identities $e$ and $e'$ give
$$e = e e' = e'.$$
The inverse of an element $g$ of a group is unique: if $h$ and $h'$ are both inverses of $g$, then
$$h = h e = h (g h') = (h g) h' = e h' = h'.$$
The [[greatest common divisor|GZHZVV87T0]] is unique among the non-negative common divisors that every common divisor divides, because two such numbers divide each other.
The supremum of a bounded nonempty set of reals is unique, by the [[uniqueness of the supremum|04ETX30HT2]].
The empty set is unique, because two sets with no elements have the same elements and are equal by the axiom of extensionality.

### What it is not

Four phrases look like definite descriptions and fail.
[["The" square root of $-1$|DEMHF0QRQA]] fails uniqueness in $\mathbb{C}$: both $i$ and $-i$ satisfy $z^2 = -1$, and no algebraic property tells them apart.
"The basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$" fails uniqueness, because $\mathbb{R}^2$ has infinitely many bases, while "the standard basis" names one basis explicitly.
"The largest prime number" fails existence, because there are infinitely many primes.
"The limit of $(-1)^n$" fails existence, because the sequence does not converge.

### When uniqueness fails

When several objects satisfy the condition, the author has three honest options.
The first is to say "a" instead of "the", as in "an antiderivative of $f$" or "a basis of $V$".
The second is to add a condition that picks one object, as in "the non-negative square root" or "the non-negative gcd".
The third is to pass to classes and say "the" object [[up to|CBC72TX3NQ]] a named equivalence relation, as in "the cyclic group of order $n$, up to isomorphism".

The third option conflicts with the strict reading of "the".
Many authors write "the algebraic closure of $\mathbb{Q}$" although it is unique only up to isomorphism, and different constructions give different sets.
A careful reader checks which reading the author means.

### Watch out

The first error is to write "the solution" of an equation before proving that it has only one.
The equation $x^2 = 9$ has two real solutions, so "the solution" names nothing until we add a condition such as $x > 0$.
The second error is to prove only one of the two obligations.
"The integer $n$ with $n^2 = 2$" passes the uniqueness test among non-negative integers and fails existence.
The third error is to write "the $\delta$" in an epsilon-delta proof: if one $\delta$ works, every smaller positive number works too, so "a $\delta$" is correct.

### History

Bertrand Russell analyzed sentences of the form "the $F$ is $G$" in his 1905 paper "On Denoting".
He read such a sentence as "exactly one thing is $F$, and that thing is $G$", which is the formula in the Notation section.
Hilbert and Bernays later used a description operator in their formal systems, with the uniqueness proof as a condition for its use.

### Try it

First, is "the real number $x$ with $x^3 = 8$" a legal description?
Yes: $x = 2$ exists, and $x \mapsto x^3$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$, so no other real number has cube $8$.

Second, is "the real number $x$ with $x^2 + 1 = 0$" legal?
No: $x^2 + 1 \ge 1$ for every real $x$, so existence fails.

## Explanations

### L1 human

When we say "the" in mathematics, we promise that exactly one thing fits.
"The tallest student in the class" makes sense when exactly one student is tallest.
If two students have the same height, the phrase points at nobody in particular.
In mathematics, "the even prime number" means $2$.
It is even and prime.
Every larger even number has the divisor $2$, which is neither $1$ nor the number itself, so it is not prime.

### L2 human

Here is the shape of a typical proof that uses a definite description.
We show that $(gh)^{-1} = h^{-1} g^{-1}$ in a group, where "the inverse" is legal by the uniqueness proof above.
Uniqueness gives a method: to show that an element $k$ equals $(gh)^{-1}$, it is enough to show that $k$ is an inverse of $gh$.
So take $k = h^{-1} g^{-1}$ and multiply, using associativity at each step:
$$(gh)(h^{-1} g^{-1}) = g (h h^{-1}) g^{-1} = g e g^{-1} = g g^{-1} = e.$$
The product in the other order is $(h^{-1} g^{-1})(gh) = h^{-1} (g^{-1} g) h = h^{-1} h = e$ in the same way.
So $k$ is an inverse of $gh$, and by uniqueness $k = (gh)^{-1}$.
Watch out: the uniqueness proof must start from two arbitrary inverses, not from one inverse and one guessed element.

### L3 human

In first-order logic a description term can be eliminated.
A sentence $\psi(\iota x \, \varphi)$ becomes $\exists x \, \big(\varphi(x) \land \forall y \, (\varphi(y) \to y = x) \land \psi(x)\big)$, so a definition by description is a conservative extension once $\exists! x \, \varphi(x)$ is a theorem.
In Lean the uniqueness claim is the proposition `ExistsUnique`, written `∃!`, and an object is often extracted from an existence proof with `Classical.choose`.

For objects defined by a [[universal property|EN4DD2FAJH]], uniqueness holds up to a unique isomorphism that respects the defining maps.
Category theory therefore writes "the product" and "the quotient", and it relies on every construction being invariant under isomorphism.
The algebraic closure is weaker: it is unique up to isomorphism, but the isomorphism is not unique.
