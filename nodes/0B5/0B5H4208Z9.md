## Definition

An eigenvector is a nonzero vector that a linear map sends to a multiple of itself.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, let $T \colon V \to V$ be an endomorphism, and let $\lambda \in F$.
An *eigenvector* of $T$ for $\lambda$ is a vector $v \in V$ with
$$v \ne 0 \quad \text{and} \quad T(v) = \lambda v.$$
Then $\lambda$ is an [[eigenvalue|0BQAA2BK3Q]] of $T$.
An eigenvector of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over $F$ is an eigenvector of the map $x \mapsto Ax$ on $F^n$.

## Notation

Mathlib writes `Module.End.HasEigenvector T λ v` for the statement that $v$ is an eigenvector of $T$ for $\lambda$.

## Remarks

The condition $T(v) = \lambda v$ says that $T$ does not turn $v$ off its line.
The condition $v \ne 0$ is needed because $T(0) = \lambda \cdot 0$ holds for every $\lambda$, so the zero vector would be an "eigenvector" for every scalar and would say nothing about $T$.
The scalar $\lambda$ must lie in $F$, so a real matrix may have eigenvectors in $\mathbb{C}^n$ and none in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, the vector $(1, 1)$ is an eigenvector for $3$, because $A(1, 1) = (2 + 1, 0 + 3) = (3, 3)$.
The vector $(0, 1)$ is not an eigenvector, because $A(0, 1) = (1, 3)$ is not a multiple of $(0, 1)$.
Each nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is again an eigenvector for the same eigenvalue, so the definition really picks out lines.
The [[concept of an eigenvector|0N6960W0BV]] gives the geometric meaning and more examples.

## Explanations

### L1 human

An eigenvector keeps its line under the map: the map only stretches it, shrinks it or flips it.
The stretch factor is the eigenvalue.
For a reflection of the plane in a line, the vectors on the line are eigenvectors for $1$, and the vectors perpendicular to it are eigenvectors for $-1$.

### L2 human

We test whether $v = (1, 2)$ is an eigenvector of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

1. Compute $Av = (1 + 2, 2 + 0) = (3, 2)$.
2. A multiple $\lambda v = (\lambda, 2\lambda)$ with first entry $3$ needs $\lambda = 3$, and then its second entry is $6 \ne 2$.
3. So no $\lambda$ works, and $v$ is not an eigenvector of $A$.
4. The vector $w = (1, 1)$ is an eigenvector, because $Aw = (2, 2) = 2w$.

This is the typical use of the definition: compute $Av$ and compare it with the multiples of $v$.
Watch out: comparing one coordinate is not enough, because every coordinate must give the same $\lambda$.
