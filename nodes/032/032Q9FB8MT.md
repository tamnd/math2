## Summary

The product of an $m \times n$ [[matrix|0VD9ZSWWJG]] $A$ and an $n \times p$ matrix $B$ is the $m \times p$ matrix $AB$ with entries $(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b_{kj}$.
The [[formal definition|076JGP6R3T]] states this rule.
Column $j$ of $AB$ is the [[matrix-vector product|0PDVQEP0ZC]] $A b_j$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$.
The rule is chosen so that $AB$ is the matrix of the composite of the linear maps of $A$ and $B$: $(AB)x = A(Bx)$.
The product is [[associative|073E4XC016]] and [[distributive|0RXD6EZYBK]], but it is [[not commutative|0P506SPZP2]].

## Motivation

Suppose $y = Bx$ with $x \in F^p$, and $z = Ay$ with $y \in F^n$.
Then $z$ is a linear function of $x$, so $z = Cx$ for one $m \times p$ matrix $C$.
The coordinate $i$ of $z$ is $\sum_k a_{ik} y_k = \sum_k a_{ik} \sum_j b_{kj} x_j = \sum_j \bigl(\sum_k a_{ik} b_{kj}\bigr) x_j$.
So $C$ has the entries $\sum_k a_{ik} b_{kj}$, and we define $AB = C$.
Cayley introduced the product in this way, from the composition of linear substitutions.

## Notation

- The product is written $AB$ or $A \cdot B$. The symbol $A \times B$ is rare and can mean other products.
- The [[Hadamard product|SQT6QCEXZ8]] $A \circ B$ multiplies entry by entry, and the [[Kronecker product|0N7ENQRCXN]] $A \otimes B$ is a block matrix. Neither is the matrix product.
- In Mathlib, `A * B` is the product of `A : Matrix m n α` and `B : Matrix n p α`; it needs `Fintype n`.
- Powers are $A^0 = I$ and $A^{k+1} = A^k A$, for square $A$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The [[four views of a 2 × 2 product|D2XKRGZDBK]]: by entries, by columns, by rows and as a sum of outer products.
- The [[rotation by a right angle|52QTZVBM4Z]] $R$ has $R^2 = -I$ and $R^4 = I$.
- $AI = IA = A$ for the [[identity matrix|06DW9BAWB1]].
- A row times a column, $(1, 2, 3)(4, 5, 6)^{\mathsf{T}} = (32)$, is a $1 \times 1$ matrix, and a column times a row is an outer product.

### Non-examples

- The [[entrywise square|8B8Y3DYGYT]] of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 9 & 16 \end{pmatrix}$, but the matrix square is $\begin{pmatrix} 7 & 10 \\ 15 & 22 \end{pmatrix}$.
- A $2 \times 3$ matrix times a $2 \times 3$ matrix is not defined.

### Basic properties

- Associativity $(AB)C = A(BC)$, proved by sums and by composition in the [[associativity theorem|073E4XC016]].
- Distributivity $A(B + C) = AB + AC$ and $(A + B)C = AC + BC$, by the [[distributive law|0RXD6EZYBK]].
- $c(AB) = (cA)B = A(cB)$ over a commutative ring.
- In general $AB \neq BA$, even when both are defined and of the same size; see the [[counterexample|0P506SPZP2]].
- $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}} A^{\mathsf{T}}$, by the [[transpose of a product|09GK5PS824]].
- Nonzero matrices can have product zero, as in the [[zero divisor example|WP764BBPKQ]].
- The product of an $m \times n$ and an $n \times p$ matrix by the definition takes $mnp$ multiplications.

### History

- Cayley defined the product in his memoir of 1858, as the composition of linear transformations.
- Strassen showed in 1969 that $n \times n$ matrices can be multiplied with fewer than $n^3$ multiplications, about $n^{2.81}$.

### Common errors

- Multiplying entry by entry.
- Changing the order of the factors, for example writing $(AB)^2 = A^2 B^2$.
- Cancelling: $AB = AC$ does not imply $B = C$ unless $A$ is [[invertible|0EB9N0S33C]].
- Forgetting the size rule: the inner sizes must agree, and the outer sizes give the size of the product.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To find the entry in row $i$ and column $j$ of $AB$, take row $i$ of $A$ and column $j$ of $B$.
Multiply them entry by entry and add.
The rows of $A$ must have the same length as the columns of $B$.

### L2 human

The product is the matrix of a composite map: first apply $B$, then apply $A$.
This explains the order: $AB$ acts on $x$ as $A(Bx)$.
It also explains associativity, because composition of functions is associative, and non-commutativity, because doing two things in the other order usually gives a different result.

### L3 human

The matrices over a ring $R$ form a category whose objects are the integers $n \ge 0$, with composition given by the product.
The map $A \mapsto (x \mapsto Ax)$ is an isomorphism of this category with the category of free modules $R^n$ and linear maps, once the bases are fixed.
For square matrices this gives the [[matrix ring|AB2MD5CD13]] $M_n(R) \cong \operatorname{End}(R^n)$, with the opposite ring for some conventions over a noncommutative $R$.
