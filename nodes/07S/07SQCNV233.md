## Summary

A matrix is diagonalizable when a change of basis turns it into a diagonal matrix.
In the new basis the matrix does nothing but stretch each coordinate axis by its own factor, so every question about it becomes a question about $n$ numbers.
Exactly: let $F$ be a field and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$.
Then $A$ is *diagonalizable over $F$* when there are an [[invertible matrix|0EB9N0S33C]] $P$ and a [[diagonal matrix|044JNC7ZAA]] $D$, both with entries in $F$, such that
$$A = P D P^{-1}.$$
In other words, $A$ is [[similar|0E89HQATM8]] to a diagonal matrix, and the [[formal definition|0Y17PEKF24]] states this.
The columns of $P$ are then [[eigenvectors|0N6960W0BV]] of $A$, and the diagonal entries of $D$ are the matching [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]].
An [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]] of a finite-dimensional space is diagonalizable when its matrix in some basis is diagonal.

## Motivation

A diagonal matrix is the easiest matrix to work with.
It multiplies each coordinate by a number and never mixes two coordinates, so its products, powers and inverses are computed entry by entry.
Most matrices are not diagonal, but many of them are diagonal in a better basis.
If the basis vector $v$ gives a diagonal column, then $A v = \lambda v$, so the search for such a basis is the search for eigenvectors.

Once $A = P D P^{-1}$ is known, the hard computations become easy ones.
The [[powers of a diagonalizable matrix|0TNR9BQ871]] are $A^k = P D^k P^{-1}$, and this gives [[the Fibonacci numbers in closed form|AC3XJ3DC9X]] and [[the long-term state of a migration model|GP1NPA0Z0W]].
Systems of linear differential equations $x' = A x$ split into $n$ separate equations in the same way.
Later in the area, the matrices that are not diagonalizable lead to triangularization, generalized eigenspaces and the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].

## Notation

Books write $A = P D P^{-1}$ or $P^{-1} A P = D$, with $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$.
The factorization is also called the [[eigendecomposition|D0AP2R7272]] of $A$, and some older books say "diagonable".
The field matters, so we say "diagonalizable over $\R$" or "diagonalizable over $\C$" when there is a choice.
Watch the order of the factors: the eigenvectors are the columns of the matrix $P$ on the left of $D$ in $A = P D P^{-1}$.

## Remarks

### A first diagonalization

Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\R$.
Its characteristic polynomial is $t^2 - 7t + 10 = (t - 5)(t - 2)$, and the eigenvectors are $(1, 1)$ for $5$ and $(1, -2)$ for $2$.
Put them as the columns of $P$ and the eigenvalues in the same order on the diagonal of $D$:
$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad P^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Multiply back:
$$P D P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 5 & -4 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 12 & 3 \\ 6 & 9 \end{pmatrix} = A.$$
The [[full computation|306FBDECMB]] also finds every step of the eigenvectors and uses the result to compute $A^3$.

### A repeated eigenvalue can be harmless

The matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalue $4$ once and the eigenvalue $1$ twice.
It is still diagonalizable, because the eigenvalue $1$ has a whole plane of eigenvectors, the plane $x + y + z = 0$.
Two independent vectors from the plane and the vector $(1, 1, 1)$ give three independent eigenvectors, as [[the 3 × 3 example|K5NXSFJV50]] shows in full.
Every diagonal matrix is diagonalizable with $P = I$, and so is the scalar matrix $c I$, whose only eigenvalue is repeated $n$ times.

### What it is not

The [[shear|SBDF8APDF7]] $S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not diagonalizable over any field.
Its only eigenvalue is $1$, so a diagonal form would be $I$, and then $S = P I P^{-1} = I$, which is false.
The same argument shows that a nonzero [[nilpotent matrix|07BSPZPSMF]] is never diagonalizable, since its only eigenvalue is $0$.
Matrices of these two kinds are [[defective|5F2AV5855Y]]: they lack eigenvectors over every field.

A different failure happens for the [[rotation by a right angle|XS5H42KEP5]], $R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
Its characteristic polynomial $t^2 + 1$ has no real root, so $R$ has no real eigenvector and is not diagonalizable over $\R$.
Over $\C$ it has the two eigenvalues $i$ and $-i$, and it is diagonalizable there.

### How to tell

Three tests decide the question, and each has its own page.
The [[characterization by eigenvectors|056SW1E0AC]] says that $A$ is diagonalizable exactly when $F^n$ has a basis of eigenvectors of $A$, or equivalently when the eigenspaces add up to dimension $n$.
The [[test by multiplicities|CDB8KCNNG9]] asks that the characteristic polynomial splits over $F$ and that each geometric multiplicity equals the algebraic one.
The [[minimal polynomial criterion|0MPAJRB23T]] asks that the minimal polynomial is a product of distinct linear factors over $F$.
A quick sufficient condition is that $A$ has [[n distinct eigenvalues in F|0Y95Z7MT02]].
Every real symmetric matrix is diagonalizable, even with an orthogonal $P$, by the [[real spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]].
Two diagonalizable matrices that commute can be diagonalized by one common $P$, by [[simultaneous diagonalization|0FK17N4DKA]].

### Watch out

Counting eigenvalues with multiplicity proves nothing.
The shear has the eigenvalues $1, 1$, two of them for a $2 \times 2$ matrix, and it is not diagonalizable.
What counts is the number of independent eigenvectors.
In the other direction, a repeated eigenvalue does not rule out diagonalization, as the identity and the $3 \times 3$ example show.

Finally, keep the columns of $P$ and the entries of $D$ in the same order.
If $P$ has the columns $(1, -2)$ and $(1, 1)$ in the first example, then $D$ must be $\operatorname{diag}(2, 5)$, not $\operatorname{diag}(5, 2)$.

### History

The reduction of a quadratic form to a sum of squares along principal axes, studied by Lagrange and by Cauchy, is the diagonalization of a symmetric matrix.
In the nineteenth century, Weierstrass and Jordan treated general matrices and found that many are not diagonalizable.
Their work led to the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]], which describes every matrix over $\C$ up to similarity.

### Try it

First, is $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ diagonalizable over $\R$?
Yes: the eigenvalues $2$ and $3$ are distinct, with eigenvectors $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$, since $A(1, 1) = (3, 3)$.

Second, for which numbers $a$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & a \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ diagonalizable?
Only for $a = 0$, because the only eigenvalue is $1$ and the only diagonalizable matrix with that single eigenvalue is $I$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some matrices act on the plane by pure stretching along two special directions.
For example, a matrix may stretch the direction of $(1, 1)$ by $5$ and the direction of $(1, -2)$ by $2$.
If we use these two directions as our new axes, the matrix becomes the simple rule "multiply the first coordinate by $5$ and the second by $2$".
A matrix is diagonalizable when it has enough special directions to serve as axes, as many as the dimension.
A shear, which slides the plane sideways, keeps only one direction, so it is not diagonalizable.

### L2 human

Let us diagonalize $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\R$.
The characteristic polynomial is
$$\det(t I - A) = (t - 1)^2 - 4 = (t - 3)(t + 1),$$
so the eigenvalues are $3$ and $-1$, and they lie in $\R$.
For $3$, the matrix $A - 3I$ has both rows equal to multiples of $(-2, 2)$, so its kernel is spanned by $(1, 1)$.
For $-1$, the matrix $A + I$ has both rows equal to $(2, 2)$, so its kernel is spanned by $(1, -1)$.
With these columns we get
$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad P^{-1} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Check by multiplying back:
$$P D P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = A.$$
The same steps work for every matrix: find the eigenvalues, find a basis of each eigenspace, and count.
If the count reaches $n$, the bases are the columns of $P$.
If it falls short, as for the shear, the matrix is not diagonalizable, and no choice of $P$ can help.

### L3 human

Diagonalizability belongs to the endomorphism, not to the matrix, and it depends on the field.
It says that $V$ is the direct sum of one-dimensional invariant subspaces, or that $V$ is the direct sum of the eigenspaces.
Over $\C$, the matrices with $n$ distinct eigenvalues are dense, so diagonalizable matrices are dense in $M_n(\C)$, and many identities can be proved first for them.
The matrices that are not diagonalizable are described by the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]], and every complex matrix is the sum of a diagonalizable part and a nilpotent part that commute, by the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]].
Over a general field, the closest notion is a [[semisimple endomorphism|08VRX3Q4PB]], and over a perfect field such as $\Q$ or $\R$ these are the maps that become diagonalizable over the algebraic closure.
In infinite dimension, the spectral theorem for normal operators plays the same role, with the sum over eigenvalues replaced by an integral.
