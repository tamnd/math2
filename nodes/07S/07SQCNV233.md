## Summary

Let $F$ be a field and let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $F$.
The matrix $A$ is *diagonalizable* over $F$ when there is an [[invertible matrix|0EB9N0S33C]] $P$ over $F$ and a [[diagonal matrix|044JNC7ZAA]] $D$ over $F$ with $A = P D P^{-1}$, that is, when $A$ is [[similar|0E89HQATM8]] to a diagonal matrix.
Then the columns of $P$ are [[eigenvectors|0N6960W0BV]] of $A$, and the diagonal entries of $D$ are the matching [[eigenvalues|0BQAA2BK3Q]].
An [[endomorphism|0J7H0YBFDJ]] of a finite-dimensional space is diagonalizable when its matrix in some basis is diagonal.
A diagonalizable map is a set of independent stretches along the axes of a suitable basis, so its powers, its exponential and its long-term behaviour are easy to compute.

## Motivation

A diagonal matrix is the simplest kind of matrix: it multiplies each coordinate by a number, and it does not mix coordinates.
Products, powers and inverses of diagonal matrices are computed entry by entry.
Most matrices are not diagonal, but many become diagonal after a change of basis.
The question "is there a basis in which $A$ is diagonal" leads to eigenvectors, since a basis vector $v$ with a diagonal column satisfies $A v = \lambda v$.
So diagonalization turns the study of $A$ into the study of $n$ numbers and $n$ directions.

## Notation

We write $A = P D P^{-1}$ or $P^{-1} A P = D$, with $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$.
The factorization $A = P D P^{-1}$ is also called the [[eigendecomposition|~f8ac4249]] of $A$.
Some books write "diagonable" for diagonalizable, and British texts spell it "diagonalisable".
An endomorphism whose matrix becomes diagonal is also called *diagonalizable*, and the field must be named when it matters: "diagonalizable over $\R$".

## Remarks

### Examples

- A [[2 × 2 matrix with eigenvalues 5 and 2|~36eb7d45]], diagonalized with $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$.
- A [[3 × 3 matrix with a repeated eigenvalue|~e0a600d5]], diagonalizable because the eigenvalue $1$ has a plane of eigenvectors.
- Every diagonal matrix, with $P = I$, and every scalar matrix $c I$.
- Every matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues in $F$, by the [[criterion of distinct eigenvalues|0Y95Z7MT02]].
- Every real symmetric matrix, by the [[spectral theorem|06J4PXRG22]], even with an orthogonal $P$.

### Non-examples

- The [[shear matrix|~58e14c85]] $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not diagonalizable over any field: its only eigenvalue is $1$, and the eigenvectors form a line.
- Every nonzero [[nilpotent matrix|07BSPZPSMF]] is not diagonalizable: its only eigenvalue is $0$, so a diagonal form would be $0$.
- The [[rotation by a right angle|~43d2ecd4]] is not diagonalizable over $\R$, since it has no real eigenvalue, but it is diagonalizable over $\C$.

### Basic properties

- $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if $F^n$ has a basis of eigenvectors of $A$, if and only if $F^n$ is the direct sum of the eigenspaces, by the [[characterization by eigenvectors|056SW1E0AC]].
- $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if its characteristic polynomial splits and each geometric multiplicity equals the algebraic one, by the [[test by multiplicities|~1ac265ba]].
- $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if its [[minimal polynomial|03NDVCSHSB]] splits with simple roots, by the [[minimal polynomial criterion|0MPAJRB23T]].
- [[Powers|0TNR9BQ871]] are computed as $A^k = P D^k P^{-1}$.
- Commuting diagonalizable matrices are diagonalizable with one common $P$, by [[simultaneous diagonalization|0FK17N4DKA]].

### History

The reduction of quadratic forms and symmetric matrices to diagonal form goes back to the work of Lagrange, Cauchy and others on principal axes in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
Weierstrass and Jordan studied the general case in the 1860s and 1870s, and found that not every matrix is diagonalizable, which led to the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]].

### Common errors

- Thinking that $n$ eigenvalues counted with multiplicity give diagonalizability. The [[shear|~58e14c85]] has the eigenvalues $1, 1$ and is not diagonalizable.
- Thinking that a repeated eigenvalue rules out diagonalizability. The identity matrix and the [[matrix of all twos and ones|~e0a600d5]] are counterexamples.
- Putting the eigenvectors in the rows of $P$ instead of the columns, or listing the eigenvalues of $D$ in a different order from the columns of $P$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Some matrices act on the plane by stretching along two special directions.
For example, a matrix may stretch one direction by $5$ and another direction by $2$.
If we use these two directions as our new axes, the matrix becomes a list of two stretch factors.
A matrix is diagonalizable when such special directions exist, as many as the dimension.
A shear, which slides the plane sideways, has only one special direction, so it is not diagonalizable.

### L2 human

The diagonalization algorithm for an $n \times n$ matrix $A$:

1. Compute the [[characteristic polynomial|0VSSPAFM2C]] $p_A(t) = \det(t I - A)$ and its roots in $F$.
2. If $p_A$ does not split over $F$, then $A$ is not diagonalizable over $F$.
3. For each root $\lambda$, find a basis of $\ker(A - \lambda I)$ by row reduction.
4. If the bases of step 3 have $n$ vectors in total, put them as the columns of $P$, and put the matching eigenvalues on the diagonal of $D$.
5. Check by multiplying back: $A P = P D$.
6. If there are fewer than $n$ vectors, then $A$ is not diagonalizable.

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, step 1 gives $(t - 5)(t - 2)$, step 3 gives $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -2)$, and the [[worked example|~36eb7d45]] checks $A = P D P^{-1}$.
The typical use is to compute $A^k$ or $e^{tA}$.
The trap is step 6: a double root with only one independent eigenvector, as for the shear, means that $A$ is not diagonalizable.

### L3 human

Diagonalizability is a property of the endomorphism, not of the matrix, and it depends on the field.
A matrix over $F$ is diagonalizable over the algebraic closure exactly when its minimal polynomial has no repeated roots there.
Over $\C$, the diagonalizable matrices are dense, since a matrix with distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable and distinct eigenvalues are generic.
The non-diagonalizable matrices are classified by the [[Jordan normal form|085HQ5G5TE]], and every matrix is the sum of a commuting semisimple and nilpotent part, by the [[Jordan-Chevalley decomposition|011K11VPJN]].
In infinite dimension, the analogue of diagonalizability is the spectral theorem for normal operators, where sums over eigenvalues become integrals over a spectral measure.
