## Summary

A line is described by one point on it and one direction to walk in, and this description works the same way in the plane, in space and in every $\mathbb{R}^n$.
Let $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a point and let $d \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a nonzero [[vector|0QN3D80AWP]].
The [[line|0MP9BCF6DP]] through $a$ with direction vector $d$ is the set
$$L = \{\, a + t d : t \in \mathbb{R} \,\}.$$
The formula $r = a + t d$ is the vector equation of $L$, and the real number $t$ is the parameter.
Written out in $\mathbb{R}^3$, with $a = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$ and $d = (d_1, d_2, d_3)$, it becomes the three parametric equations
$$x = x_0 + t d_1, \qquad y = y_0 + t d_2, \qquad z = z_0 + t d_3.$$
In space a new possibility appears: two lines can be skew, neither parallel nor meeting.

## Motivation

In the plane a line has an equation $ax + by = c$, and it is natural to look for something similar in space.
But one equation $ax + by + cz = e$ in space describes a plane, not a line.
A line in space would need two such equations, and the line itself is hidden as the place where two planes meet.

The vector equation comes from motion instead.
A particle that is at the point $a$ at time $0$ and moves with constant velocity $d$ is at $a + t d$ at time $t$.
Its path is the line, and every value of $t$ hands you a point on it.
That makes points easy to produce, and it makes questions about intersections into equations in $t$.

## Notation

The vector form is $r = a + t d$, also written $r(t) = a + t d$ with $t \in \mathbb{R}$.
The parametric form lists one equation per coordinate, as in the Summary.
Solving each parametric equation for $t$ and setting the results equal gives the symmetric form
$$\frac{x - x_0}{d_1} = \frac{y - y_0}{d_2} = \frac{z - z_0}{d_3},$$
which makes sense only when $d_1$, $d_2$ and $d_3$ are all nonzero.
The line through two points $A \neq B$ has the two-point form $A + t(B - A)$.

## Remarks

### A first computation

The line through $A = (1, 2, 3)$ and $B = (4, 0, 5)$ has the direction $d = B - A = (3, -2, 2)$, so
$$r = (1, 2, 3) + t(3, -2, 2).$$
At $t = 0$ we are at $A$, at $t = 1$ we are at $B$, and at $t = \tfrac{1}{2}$ we are at the midpoint $(\tfrac{5}{2}, 1, 4)$.
The values $0 \leq t \leq 1$ trace the segment from $A$ to $B$.
The [[worked example|1HM16CK71V]] uses this line to test whether given points lie on it.

The same form also works in the plane.
The line $2x + 3y = 6$ contains $(3, 0)$, and $(-3, 2)$ is perpendicular to the normal $(2, 3)$, so the line is $(3, 0) + t(-3, 2)$.

### One line, many equations

A line has many vector equations, since any point on it can serve as the base and any nonzero multiple of $d$ as the direction.
The equations $a + t d$ and $a' + s d'$ describe the same line exactly when $d'$ is a nonzero multiple of $d$ and $a' - a$ is a multiple of $d$.
Changing the direction to $2d$ only changes the speed; changing it to $-d$ walks the same line backward.

### How two lines can sit

Two lines $a + t d$ and $b + s e$ are parallel when $e$ is a multiple of $d$, which in $\mathbb{R}^3$ means $d \times e = 0$.
In the plane, two lines that are not parallel always meet.
In space they may miss each other: the lines $(1, 0, 0) + t(1, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 2) + s(0, 1, 1)$ are not parallel and have no common point.
Such lines are called skew, and the [[classification example|NNEBK2BSBB]] sorts three pairs into parallel, meeting and skew.
For non-parallel lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the [[coplanarity test|7RMCRQ9ERC]] decides in one step: they meet exactly when $(b - a) \cdot (d \times e) = 0$.

### What a line is not

The set $\{a + t^2 d : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ looks like a line, but it is only a half-line, because $t^2$ is never negative; the [[non-example|FCHYT1DRBQ]] works this out.
The direction must be nonzero, since $a + t \cdot 0$ is the single point $a$.
And in $\mathbb{R}^3$ the equation $x + y + z = 1$ is a plane, not a line.

### Watch out

A point on the line is not its direction.
For the line through $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(4, 0, 5)$, the direction is the difference $(3, -2, 2)$, not $(4, 0, 5)$.

To intersect two lines, give them different parameters $t$ and $s$.
With one shared parameter you would be asking whether two particles are at the same place at the same time, which is a different question.
Equal $x$ and $y$ values are not enough either; a common point must also have equal $z$ values.

### History

Equations of lines in coordinates go back to the analytic geometry of Descartes and Fermat in the seventeenth century.
The vector form $a + t d$ belongs to the vector analysis of the nineteenth century.

### Try it

Find where the line $(1, 2, 3) + t(3, -2, 2)$ meets the plane $z = 0$.
The third coordinate is $3 + 2t$, which is $0$ at $t = -\tfrac{3}{2}$, and the point is $(-\tfrac{7}{2}, 5, 0)$.

Write the line $y = 2x + 1$ in the plane in vector form.
It contains $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 3)$, so it is $(0, 1) + t(1, 2)$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A line is the path of a particle that moves straight ahead at constant speed.
The point $a$ tells where it starts, and the arrow $d$ tells where it goes in one second.
At time $t$ it is at $a + t d$.
Negative times give the part of the line behind the start.

### L2 human

The typical uses are intersections, distances and the paths of particles.
For an intersection with a plane, substitute the parametric equations into the equation of the plane and solve for $t$.
For an intersection of two lines, set $a + t d = b + s e$; in $\mathbb{R}^3$ this is three equations in the two unknowns $t$ and $s$.
Solve two of them and test the third.

Watch out with the symmetric form when a component of $d$ is $0$.
For $d = (1, 0, 2)$ there is no fraction for $y$; write $\frac{x - x_0}{1} = \frac{z - z_0}{2}$ together with $y = y_0$.

### L3 human

A line is a one-dimensional affine subspace $a + \operatorname{span}\{d\}$ of the [[affine space|09XNAPTJQ7]] $\mathbb{R}^n$.
Its direction space $\operatorname{span}\{d\}$ is a subspace, treated in the sub-area on [[vector spaces|MVH97H8B2J]].
The sub-area on [[systems|DXSXFVDMXQ]] shows that the solution set of a consistent linear system is a particular solution plus the null space, which is a line when the null space has dimension $1$.
