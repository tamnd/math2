## Summary

Let $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a point and let $d \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a nonzero [[vector|0QN3D80AWP]].
The [[line|0MP9BCF6DP]] through $a$ with the direction vector $d$ is the set
$$L = \{\, a + t d : t \in \mathbb{R} \,\}.$$
The formula $r = a + t d$ is the vector equation of $L$, and $t$ is the parameter.
In $\mathbb{R}^3$, with $a = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$ and $d = (d_1, d_2, d_3)$, it gives the parametric equations $x = x_0 + t d_1$, $y = y_0 + t d_2$, $z = z_0 + t d_3$.
The same form works in the plane and in every $\mathbb{R}^n$, while an equation $ax + by = c$ describes a line only in the plane.
Two lines in space can be parallel, can meet in one point, or can be skew: not parallel and without a common point.

## Motivation

A particle moves with the constant velocity $d$ and is at the point $a$ at time $0$.
At time $t$ it is at $a + t d$, so its path is the line through $a$ in the direction $d$.
One equation $ax + by + cz = e$ in space gives a plane, not a line, so a line in space needs a different description.
The vector equation gives one formula that works in every dimension and makes points on the line easy to produce.

## Notation

- Vector form: $r = a + t d$, or $r(t) = a + t d$, with $t \in \mathbb{R}$.
- Parametric form: one equation per coordinate, as in the Summary.
- Symmetric form, if $d_1, d_2, d_3 \neq 0$: $\dfrac{x - x_0}{d_1} = \dfrac{y - y_0}{d_2} = \dfrac{z - z_0}{d_3}$.
- Two-point form: the line through $A \neq B$ is $A + t(B - A)$.

## Remarks

### Examples

- The $x$-axis in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is $(0, 0, 0) + t(1, 0, 0)$.
- The line through $A = (1, 2, 3)$ and $B = (4, 0, 5)$ is $(1, 2, 3) + t(3, -2, 2)$. The [[worked example|1HM16CK71V]] tests points on it.
- In the plane, the line $2x + 3y = 6$ is $(3, 0) + t(-3, 2)$, since $(3, 0)$ is on it and $(-3, 2)$ is perpendicular to $(2, 3)$.
- The lines $(1, 0, 0) + t(1, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 2) + s(0, 1, 1)$ are skew. The [[classification example|NNEBK2BSBB]] shows this and two other cases.

### Non-examples

- The set $\{a + t^2 d : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is a half-line, not a line. The [[non-example|FCHYT1DRBQ]] explains this.
- The set $\{a + t \cdot 0\}$ is the single point $a$: the direction vector must be nonzero.
- In $\mathbb{R}^3$ the equation $x + y + z = 1$ is a plane, not a line.

### Basic properties

- A line has many vector equations. The equations $a + t d$ and $a' + s d'$ give the same line if and only if $d' = c d$ for some $c \neq 0$ and $a' - a$ is a multiple of $d$.
- The values $0 \leq t \leq 1$ in $A + t(B - A)$ give the segment from $A$ to $B$, and $t = \tfrac{1}{2}$ gives the midpoint.
- The lines $a + t d$ and $b + s e$ are parallel if and only if $e$ is a multiple of $d$. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ this means $d \times e = 0$.
- In $\mathbb{R}^3$, non-parallel lines meet if and only if $(b - a) \cdot (d \times e) = 0$, by the [[coplanarity test|7RMCRQ9ERC]].
- In the plane, two non-parallel lines always meet. So skew lines exist only in dimension $3$ or higher.

### History

The equation of a line in coordinates goes back to the analytic geometry of Descartes and Fermat in the 17th century.
The vector form $a + t d$ belongs to the vector analysis of the 19th century.

### Common errors

- Students take a point of the line as its direction vector. For the line through $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(4, 0, 5)$, the direction is $(3, -2, 2)$, not $(4, 0, 5)$.
- Students conclude that two lines meet when they find equal $x$ and $y$ values; the $z$ values must agree as well.
- Students use the same parameter for two lines. To intersect $a + t d$ and $b + s e$, the parameters $t$ and $s$ must be different unknowns.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A line is the path of a particle that moves straight with constant speed.
The point $a$ tells where it starts, and the arrow $d$ tells where it goes in one second.
At time $t$ it is at $a + t d$.
Negative times give the part of the line behind the start.

### L2 human

Worked example: find where the line $(1, 2, 3) + t(3, -2, 2)$ meets the plane $z = 0$.

1. The third coordinate is $3 + 2t$.
2. It is $0$ for $t = -\tfrac{3}{2}$.
3. The point is $(1 - \tfrac{9}{2}, \; 2 + 3, \; 0) = (-\tfrac{7}{2}, 5, 0)$.

The typical uses are intersections, distances, and the paths of particles.
A trap: the symmetric form fails if a component of $d$ is $0$.
For $d = (1, 0, 2)$ write $\frac{x - x_0}{1} = \frac{z - z_0}{2}$ and $y = y_0$.

### L3 human

A line is a one-dimensional affine subspace $a + \operatorname{span}\{d\}$ of the [[affine space|09XNAPTJQ7]] $\mathbb{R}^n$.
Its direction space $\operatorname{span}\{d\}$ is a subspace, treated in the sub-area on [[vector spaces|MVH97H8B2J]].
The sub-area on [[systems|DXSXFVDMXQ]] shows that the solution set of a consistent linear system is $a + \text{(null space)}$, which is a line when the null space has dimension $1$.
