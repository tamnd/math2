## Example

Let
$$M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
This matrix is real and symmetric, but it is not the [[Gram matrix|0HX8HZ1WTQ]] of any two vectors in any real inner product space.
We suppose that it is, and we reach a contradiction in three ways.

1. Suppose that $M = G(v_1, v_2)$. Reading the entries, $\norm{v_1}^2 = \norm{v_2}^2 = 1$ and $\ip{v_1}{v_2} = 2$.
2. First test. The [[Cauchy-Schwarz inequality|0RFWW56Y22]] gives $\abs{\ip{v_1}{v_2}} \le \norm{v_1} \norm{v_2} = 1$, but $\ip{v_1}{v_2} = 2 > 1$.
3. Second test. Expand a squared [[norm|0MFJJV97RJ]]:
$$\norm{v_1 - v_2}^2 = \norm{v_1}^2 - 2 \ip{v_1}{v_2} + \norm{v_2}^2 = 1 - 4 + 1 = -2,$$
and a squared norm is never negative.
4. Third test. The Gram determinant of two vectors is $\norm{v_1}^2 \norm{v_2}^2 - \ip{v_1}{v_2}^2 \ge 0$, but $\det M = 1 - 4 = -3$.

Each test gives a contradiction, so $M$ is not a Gram matrix.

## Remarks

The second test is the general test in disguise.
With $c = (1, -1)$ it computes $c^{\mathsf{T}} M c = 1 - 2 - 2 + 1 = -2 < 0$, so $M$ is not positive semidefinite, and every Gram matrix is positive semidefinite.
The eigenvalues of $M$ are $3$ and $-1$, with the eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, and the negative eigenvalue is the obstruction.

Change the off-diagonal entry to $t$, and the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ t & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is a Gram matrix exactly when $-1 \le t \le 1$.
For $t = \frac12$ it is the Gram matrix of $(1, 0)$ and $(\frac12, \frac{\sqrt3}{2})$, two unit vectors at the angle $\frac{\pi}{3}$.
For $t = 1$ it is the Gram matrix of $(1, 0)$ taken twice, and its determinant $0$ shows the dependence.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The matrix claims that two vectors of length $1$ have dot product $2$.
That cannot happen, because the dot product of two unit vectors is the cosine of their angle, which lies between $-1$ and $1$.
So no pair of vectors has this table of dot products.

### L2 human

For a real matrix, symmetry is necessary to be a Gram matrix, but it is not sufficient: the matrix must also be positive semidefinite.
For a $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrix, this means that both diagonal entries and the determinant are $\ge 0$, and here the determinant $-3$ fails.
Watch out: a positive determinant alone is not enough either.
The matrix $-I_2$ has determinant $1$, but its diagonal entries are $-1$, and a squared length is never negative.
