## Summary

A student checks that $n^2 - n$ is even for $n = 1$ to $5$ and writes "so it is true for all $n$", and she is honestly convinced.
This part of [[reasoning and proof|SGSVYFDSYK]] asks why such arguments convince, what research has learned about how students come to need and write proofs, and what teachers and curricula do about it.
It starts with the place of logic, sets and language in a curriculum, and with the views of mathematics that teachers and students bring to class.
It then turns to the evidence: the Wason selection task and how people read conditionals, the proof schemes of Harel and Sowder, Tall and Vinner's concept images, and the studies of how students read, judge and write proofs.
Along the way it meets the tools that teachers use, from Toulmin's layout of arguments and the generic example to transition courses, the Moore method and inquiry-based learning, and the standards of NCTM and the Common Core.
It ends with proof assistants such as Lean, which check every step a student writes.
Research findings are reported with their scope and strength, and teaching opinions are marked as opinions.
General theories of learning are in [[mathematics education|9622VKAWMZ]], and the proof methods themselves are in the other parts of the area.

1. [[Teaching foundations of mathematics|H5BM68GXS9]]: where logic, sets and proof can sit in a curriculum.
2. [[Philosophy and mathematics in education|174ES70CS8]]: how a teacher's view of mathematics shapes what proof is for in class.
3. [[Teaching logic|K1Y2MAQQ9E]]: why most people fail the Wason selection task, and what it means for conditionals.
4. [[Mathematical language in education|S19WMS43TW]]: how symbols, definitions and everyday words trip learners.
5. [[Reasoning and proof in the classroom|E77B813MT4]]: how a class builds norms for what counts as a proof.
6. [[Teaching sets and relations|12ZNG0NRY7]]: what the New Math tried, and what a Venn diagram can prove.
7. [[Proof schemes|YVNRVXJR68]]: how to tell external, empirical and deductive standards of conviction apart.
8. [[Transition-to-proof courses|HQDCSJG379]]: what the university bridge course teaches, and what research says about it.
9. [[Toulmin model of argumentation|RVZCSFDB2Y]]: how to lay out an argument and find its unspoken rule.
10. [[Student difficulties with proof|5K2969GSWE]]: a map of the documented ways students fail to prove.
11. [[Proof comprehension and validation|52Y2MW27J0]]: how students and mathematicians read and judge proofs.
12. [[Moore method and inquiry-based learning|WKM6535VHG]]: courses in which students prove the theorems themselves.
13. [[Proof in school curricula|7ZSHDS63QY]]: how NCTM, the Common Core and other curricula place proof.
14. [[Generic and pre-formal proofs|SG9XA69J64]]: how one example, an action or a picture can carry a general argument.
15. [[Concept image and concept definition|C5S2X0WDJ8]]: why students who can recite a definition still reason from a picture.
16. [[Proof assistants in teaching|KGPHY3F0GC]]: what Lean and similar systems add to a proof course, from the Natural Number Game on.

1. [[Mathematical proof|0B94TKFQJX]] says what a proof is in the sense of mathematicians.
2. [[The language of mathematical statements|WMY13MXY3W]] covers connectives, conditionals and quantifiers.
3. [[Basic proof methods|958EF46EYJ]] gives the methods that students learn to use.
4. [[Writing and reading proofs|STSM6PR7TX]] treats the practice of writing and reading proofs, which this part studies from the side of the learner.
5. [[Mathematics education|9622VKAWMZ]] supplies the general field of research, with its theories and methods.
