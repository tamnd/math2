## Statement

Let an isotropic conductor have Seebeck coefficient $S(T)$, Peltier coefficient $\Pi(T)$ and Thomson coefficient $\mathcal{K}(T)$ at absolute temperature $T$.
Use the conventions of the [[thermoelectric effect|36BBVY7S4F]].
Assume the linear transport laws and the [[Onsager reciprocal relations|9YACB2RQ80]] hold.
Then
1. $\Pi = T S$;
2. $\mathcal{K} = T \dfrac{dS}{dT}$.

## Remarks

Write the linear laws with the forces $\nabla(1/T)$ and $E/T$; the Onsager symmetry of the cross coefficients is exactly relation 1.
Relation 2 follows from energy conservation, which gives $\mathcal{K} = d\Pi/dT - S$ in steady state, together with relation 1.
Example: if $S$ is independent of temperature, the Thomson effect vanishes and $\Pi$ grows linearly with $T$.
For a material with $S = 200\,\mu\mathrm{V/K}$ at $T = 300$ K, relation 1 gives $\Pi = 60$ mV, so a current of $1$ A carries $60$ mW of heat.
William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, found these relations before Onsager's theory, by an argument that treated the reversible effects separately from heat conduction.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The three thermoelectric effects look different, but they are three faces of one effect.
Measure how much voltage a temperature difference makes, and these relations tell how much heat a current carries and how much heat appears along a warm wire.

### L2 human

These relations were among the first tests of the Onsager reciprocal relations, and they hold well in experiments.
In practice, $S(T)$ is measured, and $\Pi$ and $\mathcal{K}$ are computed from it.
The figure of merit of a thermoelectric material, $ZT = \sigma S^2 T / \kappa$, involves only $S$ for this reason.
