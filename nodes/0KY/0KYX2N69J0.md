## Summary

Let $G$ be a [[group|G5S1M9]] and let $N$ be a [[normal subgroup|0CB0DX5BVV]] of $G$.
The quotient group $G/N$ is the set of [[left cosets|03DEH43F4H]] $gN$ with $g \in G$, with the product
$$(aN)(bN) = abN.$$
This product is well defined because $N$ is normal.
The identity element is $N = eN$, and the inverse of $aN$ is $a^{-1}N$.
The map $\pi \colon G \to G/N$, $\pi(g) = gN$, is a surjective [[homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]] with [[kernel|0W88CEGQP1]] $N$.
Thus $G/N$ is the group that we get from $G$ when we treat all elements of $N$ as the identity.

## Motivation

On a clock with $12$ hours, $5$ hours after $9$ o'clock is $2$ o'clock.
The clock forgets multiples of $12$ and keeps only the remainder.
In group terms, the clock is the group $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ in which the subgroup $12\mathbb{Z}$ is treated as $0$.
A quotient group does the same for any group $G$ and any normal subgroup $N$.
Two elements $a$ and $b$ become equal when $a^{-1}b \in N$, that is, when $aN = bN$.
The quotient group lets us study a group in two parts: the subgroup $N$ and the smaller group $G/N$.

## Notation

We write $G/N$ and read it as "$G$ modulo $N$".
The element $gN$ is also written $\bar{g}$ or $[g]$.
When $G$ is written additively, the cosets are $a + N$, and the sum is $(a + N) + (b + N) = (a + b) + N$.
We write $\pi$ for the projection $G \to G/N$; some books call it the quotient map or the natural map.
Some books use the name factor group.

## Remarks

### Examples

- For each positive integer $n$, the quotient $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is the group of integers modulo $n$ ([[integers modulo n as a quotient group|0YJ3WRR9RY]]). For $n = 2$ the two cosets are the even and the odd integers ([[the integers modulo the even integers|4SCS55K5MA]]).
- The quotient $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to the [[circle group|04EAFVFTF7]] ([[the real numbers modulo the integers|~301791c6]]).
- For the [[quaternion group|0NRJGW864D]] $Q_8$ with [[centre|0DZ93493VN]] $\{\pm 1\}$, the quotient $Q_8/\{\pm 1\}$ is isomorphic to the [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]] ([[the quaternion group modulo its centre|~c48195ed]]).
- For $n \ge 2$, the quotient $S_n/A_n$ of the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] by the [[alternating group|0Z5905Q73D]] has two elements, the even and the odd permutations. The [[sign|0JEJJCB0EX]] gives $S_n/A_n \cong \{1, -1\}$.
- For a field $F$, the [[determinant|0YZ3T5Q7XN]] gives $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)/\mathrm{SL}_n(F) \cong F^\times$.
- For each group $G$, the quotient $G/G$ is the [[trivial group|0BNPC9850Y]], and $G/\{e\}$ is isomorphic to $G$ by $g\{e\} \mapsto g$.
- The quotient $G/[G, G]$ by the [[commutator subgroup|0ASGSTXSBW]] is the [[abelianisation|0AQZV0905N]] of $G$.

### Non-examples

- The cosets of a subgroup that is not normal do not form a group under $(aH)(bH) = abH$. In $S_3$, the subgroup $H = \{e, (1\,2)\}$ gives two different answers for the same product of cosets ([[cosets of a subgroup that is not normal|N5NTPAR2KT]]).
- $G/N$ is not a subgroup of $G$ in general. The group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has an element of order $2$, but $\mathbb{Z}$ has no element of order $2$. So $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is not isomorphic to any subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$.

### Basic properties

- The product is well defined. Suppose that $a'N = aN$ and $b'N = bN$. Then $a' = an_1$ and $b' = bn_2$ with $n_1, n_2 \in N$.
- Then $a'b' = an_1bn_2 = ab(b^{-1}n_1b)n_2$. Since $N$ is normal, $b^{-1}n_1b \in N$, so $a'b' \in abN$ and $a'b'N = abN$.
- The converse also holds. If the product of left cosets of a subgroup $N$ is well defined, then $N$ is normal. For $n \in N$ and $g \in G$, the equation $nN = eN$ gives $ng^{-1}N = g^{-1}N$, so $gng^{-1} \in N$.
- Normality is the same as $gN = Ng$ for each $g$ ([[normality via cosets|01E7SWYJX3]]). So for a normal subgroup the left and the right cosets are the same, and $G/N$ does not depend on this choice.
- If $G$ is finite, then $|G/N| = [G : N] = |G|/|N|$ by [[Lagrange's theorem|3C53TD]].
- The projection $\pi$ is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $N$. With [[kernels are normal|0C9ZKYSPYR]], this shows that the normal subgroups of $G$ are exactly the kernels of homomorphisms from $G$.
- The order of $gN$ in $G/N$ divides the order of $g$, because $(gN)^k = g^kN$.
- If $G$ is abelian, then $G/N$ is abelian. If $G$ is cyclic with generator $g$, then $G/N$ is cyclic with generator $gN$.
- $G/N$ is abelian if and only if $N$ contains the commutator subgroup ([[abelian quotients|0MS8QGA6S5]]).
- If $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then $G$ is abelian ([[the G modulo its centre theorem|0AH9Q6YNMT]]).
- Homomorphisms out of $G/N$ are the homomorphisms out of $G$ that send $N$ to $e$ ([[universal property of the quotient|0GJ83E78A5]]). The [[first isomorphism theorem|0CXXJNG5B6]] identifies $G/\ker\varphi$ with the image of $\varphi$.
- The subgroups of $G/N$ correspond to the subgroups of $G$ that contain $N$ ([[correspondence theorem|0S9ZVZ34MD]]).

### History

Évariste Galois used normal subgroups in his letter of 1832 to Auguste Chevalier, in his study of the solution of equations by radicals.
Otto Hölder defined quotient groups in 1889.
He used them to show that the factors of a composition series do not depend on the series, up to isomorphism and order.
Emmy Noether stated the isomorphism theorems in general form for modules in 1927, in a paper in Mathematische Annalen.

### Common errors

- The representative of a coset is not unique. We have $aN = bN$ if and only if $a^{-1}b \in N$. Each formula on $G/N$ must give the same result for all representatives.
- A quotient is not a subgroup. The elements of $G/N$ are subsets of $G$, and the identity of $G/N$ is the whole set $N$.
- The order of $G/N$ is $|G|/|N|$, not $|G| - |N|$.
- $G/N \cong H$ does not give $G \cong N \times H$. For $G = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and $N = \{0, 2\}$, both $N$ and $G/N$ have order $2$, but $G$ is cyclic of order $4$ and is not isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A quotient group is a group in which we agree to forget some differences.
Clock arithmetic is the standard example.
On a clock, $13$ o'clock and $1$ o'clock are the same, because they differ by $12$.
We put all the numbers with the same remainder into one bag.
The bags are the elements of the new group.
To add two bags, take one number from each bag, add the numbers, and find the bag of the sum.
The answer does not depend on the numbers we take.
This works for any group, provided that the subgroup we forget is normal.

### L2 human

We compute the quotient $S_3/A_3$.
Compose permutations from right to left.

1. Let $A_3 = \{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$. It has index $2$ in $S_3$, so it is normal by [[the index two theorem|0V547YQRMV]].
2. The cosets are $A_3$ and $(1\,2)A_3$. We compute $(1\,2)(1\,2\,3) = (2\,3)$ and $(1\,2)(1\,3\,2) = (1\,3)$.
3. So $(1\,2)A_3 = \{(1\,2), (2\,3), (1\,3)\}$, the set of transpositions.
4. The product of two transpositions is in $A_3$. For example, $(1\,3)(2\,3) = (1\,3\,2)$. So $\big((1\,2)A_3\big)\big((1\,2)A_3\big) = A_3$.
5. The table has two elements: $A_3$ is the identity, and $(1\,2)A_3$ has order $2$. So $S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
6. Step 4 does not depend on the representatives. Each product of two transpositions is an even permutation, so it lies in $A_3$.

The typical use of a quotient group is to reduce a question about $G$ to questions about $N$ and $G/N$, which are smaller.
Many proofs by induction on $|G|$ use this step.
A trap is to define a map on $G/N$ by a formula in a representative and to forget the check that the formula does not depend on the choice.

### L3 human

The construction works for each algebraic structure with a compatible equivalence relation.
For rings, the quotient $R/I$ by an ideal $I$ is a ring; for vector spaces, the quotient $V/W$ by a subspace is a vector space.
For groups, the compatible equivalence relations on $G$ correspond exactly to the normal subgroups, by $a \sim b$ if and only if $a^{-1}b \in N$.
In the [[category of groups|2CX7RPPXA9]], the projection $G \to G/N$ is the cokernel of the inclusion $N \to G$, and every [[epimorphism|RVXDSY04KG]] is of this form up to isomorphism.
The sequence $1 \to N \to G \to G/N \to 1$ is a short exact sequence, and $G$ is an extension of $G/N$ by $N$.
The extension problem asks which groups $G$ arise from given $N$ and $G/N$; the example of $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ shows that the answer is not unique.
For topological groups, such as $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, the quotient also carries the quotient topology.
