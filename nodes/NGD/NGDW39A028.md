## Example

Let $p$ be "the letter is posted" and $q$ be "the letter is burned".
We show that [[standard deontic logic|7B74J6J5JV]] proves $Op \to O(p \vee q)$.
In words: if you ought to post the letter, then you ought to post it or burn it.

1. $p \to (p \vee q)$ is a tautology.
2. The [[necessitation rule|G989VP2NGP]] gives $O(p \to (p \vee q))$.
3. The axiom $\mathrm{K}$ gives $O(p \to (p \vee q)) \to (Op \to O(p \vee q))$.
4. Modus ponens with steps 2 and 3 gives $Op \to O(p \vee q)$.

We also check the formula in [[Kripke semantics|6BQ71KQ6QT]].

5. Let $w \models Op$ in a serial model. Then $p$ is true at every world $v$ with $w R v$.
6. At each such $v$, $p \vee q$ is true, since $p$ is true there.
7. So $w \models O(p \vee q)$.

## Remarks

The formula is valid, so the "paradox" is not an inconsistency.
It shows a gap between $O$ and the English "ought".
A speaker who says "you ought to post it or burn it" suggests that burning is an acceptable way to comply, and $\mathsf{SDL}$ does not support this suggestion.
The argument uses only the derived rule "from $\vdash \varphi \to \psi$ infer $\vdash O\varphi \to O\psi$".
So every logic in which $O$ is monotone has the same theorem.

The permission form is $Pp \to P(p \vee q)$, proved in the same way.
Many speakers read "you may post it or burn it" as "you may post it and you may burn it".
This reading is the free choice principle $P(p \vee q) \to (Pp \wedge Pq)$.
Adding it to $\mathsf{SDL}$ gives $Pp \to Pq$ for all $p, q$: from $Pp$ we get $P(p \vee q)$, and free choice gives $Pq$.
So with free choice, if one thing is permitted then everything is permitted.

Ross stated the paradox in 1941, for the logic of imperatives.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Your boss says: "You must post this letter."
Standard deontic logic then also says: "You must post the letter or burn it."
The second sentence follows because posting the letter makes "post or burn" true.
But it sounds as if burning were now fine.
The logic is correct about what follows; the trouble is that everyday "or" in a rule often hints at free choice.

### L2 human

We test the free choice principle on a model.

1. Take $W = \set{w, v}$, $R = \set{(w, v), (v, v)}$, $V(p) = \set{v}$, $V(q) = \emptyset$.
2. At $w$, $Op$ holds, since $p$ is true at the only ideal world $v$.
3. So $P(p \vee q)$ holds at $w$.
4. $Pq$ fails at $w$, since $q$ is false at $v$.
5. So $P(p \vee q) \to (Pp \wedge Pq)$ fails at $w$, and free choice is not valid in $\mathsf{SDL}$.

The typical use of the example is to test a new deontic logic: does it keep monotonicity, and does it allow free choice?
A trap: blocking the paradox by dropping necessitation does not help, since monotonicity alone gives it.
