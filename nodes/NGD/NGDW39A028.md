## Example

Let $p$ be "the letter is posted" and $q$ be "the letter is burned".
We show that [[standard deontic logic|7B74J6J5JV]] proves the following formula.
$$Op \to O(p \vee q)$$
In words: if you ought to post the letter, then you ought to post it or burn it.
We give a derivation first, and then a check in the models.

1. The formula $p \to (p \vee q)$ is a tautology, so it is an axiom.
2. The [[necessitation rule|G989VP2NGP]] applied to step 1 gives $O(p \to (p \vee q))$.
3. The instance $O(p \to (p \vee q)) \to (Op \to O(p \vee q))$ of the axiom $\mathrm{K}$ is an axiom.
4. Modus ponens on steps 2 and 3 gives $Op \to O(p \vee q)$.

Now the check in [[Kripke semantics|6BQ71KQ6QT]].

5. Suppose that $w \models Op$ in a serial model.
   Then $p$ is true at every world $v$ with $w R v$.
6. At each such $v$ the disjunction $p \vee q$ is true, because its first disjunct is.
7. So $p \vee q$ is true at every ideal world of $w$, and $w \models O(p \vee q)$.

## Remarks

The formula is valid, so the paradox is not an inconsistency.
It shows a gap between the box $O$ and the English word "ought".
A speaker who says "you ought to post it or burn it" suggests that burning is an acceptable way to comply, and $\mathsf{SDL}$ supports no such suggestion.
The derivation uses only the derived rule that infers $\vdash O\varphi \to O\psi$ from $\vdash \varphi \to \psi$.
So every logic in which $O$ is monotone has the same theorem, and dropping necessitation alone does not remove it.

The permission form $Pp \to P(p \vee q)$ is proved in the same way.
Many speakers hear "you may post it or burn it" as "you may post it and you may burn it".
That reading is the free choice principle, shown below.
$$P(p \vee q) \to (Pp \wedge Pq)$$
Adding it to $\mathsf{SDL}$ is a disaster.
From $Pp$ we get $P(p \vee q)$, and free choice then gives $Pq$, for an arbitrary $q$.
So with free choice, if one thing is permitted then everything is permitted.

Ross raised the paradox in 1941, for the logic of imperatives.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Your boss says: "You must post this letter."
Standard deontic logic then also says: "You must post the letter or burn it."
The second sentence follows, because posting the letter makes "post or burn" true.
But it sounds as if burning were now fine.
The logic is right about what follows.
The trouble is that an "or" inside a rule often hints at a free choice, and the logic does not mean that.

### L2 human

We test free choice on a two-world model.
Take $W = \{w, v\}$, $R = \{(w, v), (v, v)\}$, $V(p) = \{v\}$ and $V(q) = \emptyset$.
The only ideal world of $w$ is $v$, and $p$ is true there, so $Op$ and $P(p \vee q)$ hold at $w$.
The atom $q$ is false at $v$, so $Pq$ fails at $w$.
So $P(p \vee q) \to (Pp \wedge Pq)$ fails at $w$, and free choice is not valid in $\mathsf{SDL}$.
This is the typical use of Ross's example: it tests whether a new deontic logic keeps monotonicity, and whether it can allow free choice without collapse.
