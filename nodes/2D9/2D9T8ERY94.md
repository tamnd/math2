## Example

Let $\mathfrak{M}$ have one world $a$ with $a R a$, and let $\mathfrak{M}'$ have two worlds $b_0$, $b_1$ with $b_0 R' b_1$ and $b_1 R' b_0$ only.
Let the variable $p$ be true at every world of both models, and let all other variables be false everywhere.
We show that $Z = \set{(a, b_0), (a, b_1)}$ is a [[bisimulation|7T740SFHHV]].

1. $Z$ is nonempty.
2. Atom clause: at $a$, $b_0$ and $b_1$, the variable $p$ is true and every other variable is false. So paired worlds agree on all variables.
3. Zig clause at $(a, b_i)$: the only move from $a$ is $a R a$. The answer is $b_i R' b_{1-i}$, and $(a, b_{1-i}) \in Z$.
4. Zag clause at $(a, b_i)$: the only move from $b_i$ is $b_i R' b_{1-i}$. The answer is $a R a$, and $(a, b_{1-i}) \in Z$.
5. These are all pairs of $Z$, so $Z$ is a bisimulation, and $a \mathrel{\underline{\leftrightarrow}} b_0$ and $a \mathrel{\underline{\leftrightarrow}} b_1$.

## Remarks

The example shows that a bisimulation need not be a bijection: one world is paired with two.
It also shows that modal formulas cannot express reflexivity of a world, since $a R a$ holds and $b_0 R' b_0$ fails.
The map $b_i \mapsto a$ is a bounded morphism, and $Z$ is the converse of its graph.
If $p$ is made false at $b_1$ and kept true at $a$ and $b_0$, then $p \wedge \lozenge \neg p$ is true at $b_0$ and false at $a$.
So $a$ and $b_0$ are then not bisimilar.

## Explanations

### L1 human

One room has a door that leads back into the same room.
Two other rooms have doors that lead into each other.
Somebody who walks through doors and only looks at the walls cannot tell the two plans apart.
In both, every walk goes on forever and always shows the same wall.

### L2 human

1. Take the formula $\square \lozenge p$. At $a$, the only successor is $a$, and $\lozenge p$ is true at $a$, so $\square \lozenge p$ is true at $a$.
2. At $b_0$, the only successor is $b_1$, and $b_1$ sees $b_0$ where $p$ is true, so $\square \lozenge p$ is true at $b_0$.
3. The two worlds agree, as the invariance theorem predicts.

The typical use is to show that a frame property is not modally definable: reflexivity holds at $a$ and fails at $b_0$, but bisimilar worlds agree on every formula.
A trap: the frame condition "$R$ is reflexive" is still definable on frames by $p \to \lozenge p$, because frame validity quantifies over all valuations; the argument here concerns one model.
