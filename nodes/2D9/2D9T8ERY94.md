## Example

A single world with a loop and two worlds that point at each other are different graphs with the same behavior.
Let $\mathfrak{M}$ have one world $a$ with $a R a$, and let $\mathfrak{M}'$ have two worlds $b_0$, $b_1$ with $b_0 R' b_1$ and $b_1 R' b_0$ only.
Let $p$ be true at every world of both models, and let every other variable be false everywhere.
We check that $Z = \{(a, b_0), (a, b_1)\}$ is a [[bisimulation|7T740SFHHV]].

1. $Z$ is nonempty.
2. Atom clause: $p$ is true and every other variable is false at $a$, $b_0$ and $b_1$, so paired worlds agree.
3. Zig at $(a, b_i)$: the only move from $a$ is $a R a$.
   The answer is $b_i R' b_{1-i}$, and $(a, b_{1-i}) \in Z$.
4. Zag at $(a, b_i)$: the only move from $b_i$ is $b_i R' b_{1-i}$.
   The answer is $a R a$, and again $(a, b_{1-i}) \in Z$.
5. These are all the pairs of $Z$, so $Z$ is a bisimulation, and $a$ is bisimilar to both $b_0$ and $b_1$.

## Remarks

The example shows that a bisimulation need not be a bijection: one world is paired with two.
It also shows that, in a model, no formula expresses "this world sees itself", since $a R a$ holds and $b_0 R' b_0$ fails.
The map $b_i \mapsto a$ is a bounded morphism, and $Z$ is the converse of its graph.

Change the valuation and the match breaks.
Make $p$ false at $b_1$, and keep it true at $a$ and $b_0$.
Then $p \wedge \lozenge \neg p$ is true at $b_0$, since $b_0$ sees $b_1$, and false at $a$, since $a$ sees only itself.
So $a$ and $b_0$ are then not bisimilar, and the zig clause fails at once: the move $b_0 R' b_1$ needs a successor of $a$ where $p$ is false.

## Explanations

### L1 human

One room has a door that leads back into the same room.
Two other rooms have doors that lead into each other.
Someone who walks through doors and only looks at the color of the walls cannot tell the two plans apart.
In both, every walk goes on forever and always shows the same walls.

### L2 human

Let us test the formula $\square \lozenge p$ on both sides.
At $a$, the only successor is $a$, and $\lozenge p$ is true at $a$ because $a$ sees itself and $p$ holds there.
So $\square \lozenge p$ is true at $a$.
At $b_0$, the only successor is $b_1$, and $b_1$ sees $b_0$, where $p$ is true.
So $\square \lozenge p$ is true at $b_0$, as the invariance theorem predicts.

Watch out: reflexivity is still definable on frames, by $p \to \lozenge p$.
Frame validity uses every valuation, and the valuation that makes $p$ true at $b_0$ only refutes $p \to \lozenge p$ at $b_0$.
The bisimulation above concerns one fixed valuation, so it says nothing against frame definability.
