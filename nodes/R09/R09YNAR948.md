## Example

We look for integers $x$ and $y$ that make $y$ as large as possible under the conditions
$$-x + y \le 1, \qquad 3x + 2y \le 12, \qquad 2x + 3y \le 12, \qquad x \ge 0, \qquad y \ge 0.$$
This is an [[integer programming|9GX3WH5EQF]] problem.
A tempting [[auxiliary problem|DYSJB93V11]] drops the condition that $x$ and $y$ are integers, which gives the [[linear programming relaxation|PYMHHM5Z6F]].
We follow the plan "solve the relaxation, then round" and see where it fails.

1. The conditions $y \le 1 + x$ and $y \le \frac{12 - 2x}{3}$ bound $y$ from above.
   The first bound grows with $x$ and the second falls, so the largest $y$ is where they meet.
2. Set the bounds equal and solve:
   $$1 + x = \frac{12 - 2x}{3} \implies 3 + 3x = 12 - 2x \implies x = \frac{9}{5} = 1.8, \quad y = 2.8.$$
3. The remaining condition holds there, since $3 \cdot 1.8 + 2 \cdot 2.8 = 5.4 + 5.6 = 11 \le 12$.
   So the relaxation has its optimum at $(1.8, 2.8)$, with $y = 2.8$.
4. Rounding to the nearest integers gives the point $(2, 3)$.
5. That point breaks a condition: $-2 + 3 = 1$ and $3 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 3 = 12$ are allowed, but $2 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 3 = 13 > 12$.
6. In fact no allowed point has $y = 3$, because $-x + 3 \le 1$ needs $x \ge 2$ and $2x + 9 \le 12$ needs $x \le 1.5$.
7. The true answer is $y = 2$, reached at $(1, 2)$ and at $(2, 2)$.
   For $(1, 2)$ the three sums are $1$, $7$ and $8$, and for $(2, 2)$ they are $0$, $10$ and $10$, all within the bounds.

## Remarks

The relaxation is not equivalent to the integer problem.
Every integer solution is a real solution, but not the other way round, so in Pólya's terms the passage is a unilateral reduction to a less ambitious problem.
Such a problem gives a bound, not an answer.
Here the bound is useful when we read it correctly: every allowed integer point has $y \le 2.8$, hence $y \le 2$, and the point $(1, 2)$ shows that $2$ is reached.
The error was the rounding, which treated the auxiliary problem as if it were equivalent.

The gap between the two problems depends on the data.
If the third condition were $2x + 3y \le 13$, the relaxation would have its optimum at $(2, 3)$, which is already an integer point, and rounding would do no harm.
So a rounding that works once proves nothing about the next problem.

## Explanations

### L1 human

We want whole numbers that obey three rules and make $y$ as large as we can.
A simpler problem lets $x$ and $y$ be any numbers, fractions included.
That problem has its best point at $x = 1.8$ and $y = 2.8$.
Rounding gives $x = 2$ and $y = 3$, but this point breaks one of the rules.
The simpler problem was a different problem, so its answer could not just be rounded.

### L2 human

We use the relaxation in the correct way, as a bound.
Every integer solution is also a real solution, so its $y$ is at most $2.8$, and an integer $y$ with $y \le 2.8$ has $y \le 2$.
On the row $y = 2$ the conditions become
$$x \ge 1, \qquad 3x \le 8, \qquad 2x \le 6,$$
and the integers with $1 \le x \le \frac{8}{3}$ are $x = 1$ and $x = 2$.
So the maximum of $y$ is $2$, reached at $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 2)$.

This is how relaxations serve in practice: the auxiliary problem bounds the answer, and an allowed point that meets the bound finishes the proof.
A bound that no allowed point meets proves nothing about the optimum, so the search on the row $y = 2$ is a necessary step.
