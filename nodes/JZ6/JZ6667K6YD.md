## Example

We prove: for all integers $a$, $b$ and $c$, if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, then $a \mid c$.
Here $a \mid b$, read "$a$ [[divides|096PW7Q014]] $b$", means that $b = ak$ for some integer $k$.
The proof is a [[direct proof|0NTF6DMVAP]] with two hypotheses.

1. Let $a$, $b$ and $c$ be integers, and assume $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$.
2. By the definition of $a \mid b$, there is an integer $k$ with $b = ak$.
3. By the definition of $b \mid c$, there is an integer $l$ with $c = bl$.
   We use a new letter $l$, because the two quotients can differ.
4. Substitute the equation of step 2 into the equation of step 3, and regroup by the associative law:
   $$c = bl = (ak)l = a(kl).$$
5. The number $kl$ is an integer, because a product of integers is an integer.
6. So $c = a \cdot (kl)$ with $kl$ an integer, and $a \mid c$ by the definition of divisibility.

Check: with $a = 3$, $b = 12$ and $c = 60$ we have $k = 4$ and $l = 5$, and the proof gives $60 = 3 \cdot 20$ with $kl = 20$.

## Remarks

Each hypothesis is unfolded with its own new letter, and then the two equations are combined.
That is the pattern of every direct proof with several hypotheses.

The same pattern proves other closure facts about divisibility.
If $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$, then $b = ak$ and $c = al$, so
$$b + c = ak + al = a(k + l),$$
and $a \mid b + c$.
Together with reflexivity, $a = a \cdot 1$, transitivity says that divisibility is a preorder on the integers.
It is not a partial order there, because $2 \mid -2$ and $-2 \mid 2$ while $2 \ne -2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

"$3$ divides $12$" means that $12$ is $3$ times a whole number, here $3 \cdot 4$.
"$12$ divides $60$" means that $60$ is $12$ times a whole number, here $12 \cdot 5$.
Put the two together: $60 = 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 = 3 \cdot 20$.
So $3$ divides $60$.
The proof does the same thing with letters, so it works for all numbers at once.

### L2 human

Here is the result at work: if $6 \mid n$, then $3 \mid n$ and $2 \mid n$.
First, $3 \mid 6$ because $6 = 3 \cdot 2$, so $3 \mid 6$ and $6 \mid n$ give $3 \mid n$ by transitivity.
In the same way, $2 \mid 6$ because $6 = 2 \cdot 3$, and transitivity gives $2 \mid n$.
So transitivity passes divisibility along a chain of numbers, and that is how it is used in practice.
Watch out: if you write $b = ak$ and $c = bk$ with the same $k$, the proof covers only pairs with equal quotients.
That includes $3 \mid 12$ and $12 \mid 48$, where both quotients are $4$, but not $3 \mid 12$ and $12 \mid 60$.
