## Definition

Fix a first-order language $\mathcal{L}$ whose primitive logical symbols are $\neg$, $\to$, $\forall$ and $=$.
The [[substitution|NCDZ969V7X]] $\varphi[t/x]$ and the condition "$t$ is substitutable for $x$ in $\varphi$" are explained in the Notation section.

A *prime formula* is an atomic formula or a formula of the form $\forall x\, \psi$.
Every formula is built from prime formulas with $\neg$ and $\to$ alone.
A formula is a *tautology* when it is true under every assignment of truth values to its prime formulas, by the truth tables of $\neg$ and $\to$; see [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]].
A *generalization* of $\varphi$ is a formula $\forall x_1 \cdots \forall x_n\, \varphi$, with $n \geq 0$ and any variables $x_i$.

The *logical axioms* of the system $\mathsf{H}$ are all generalizations of the formulas in the following six groups.

1. Every tautology.
2. $\forall x\, \varphi \to \varphi[t/x]$, where $t$ is substitutable for $x$ in $\varphi$ (quantifier instantiation).
3. $\forall x\, (\varphi \to \psi) \to (\forall x\, \varphi \to \forall x\, \psi)$ (distribution of $\forall$ over $\to$).
4. $\varphi \to \forall x\, \varphi$, where $x$ does not occur free in $\varphi$.
5. $x = x$.
6. $x = y \to (\alpha \to \alpha')$, where $\alpha$ is an atomic formula and $\alpha'$ is obtained from $\alpha$ by replacing $x$ by $y$ at zero or more places.

The only rule of inference of $\mathsf{H}$ is [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]]: from $\varphi$ and $\varphi \to \psi$, infer $\psi$.
A *deduction* of $\varphi$ from a set $\Gamma$ of formulas is a finite sequence $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n$ of formulas with $\varphi_n = \varphi$.
In it, each $\varphi_i$ is a logical axiom, a member of $\Gamma$, or follows by modus ponens from $\varphi_j$ and $\varphi_k = (\varphi_j \to \varphi_i)$ with $j, k < i$.
We write $\Gamma \vdash_{\mathsf{H}} \varphi$, or $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, when such a deduction exists, and $\vdash \varphi$ when $\Gamma$ is empty.

## Notation

The [[formulas|HYJ10AF6RT]] and [[terms|6D3WA7N2W4]] of $\mathcal{L}$ are the usual ones.
The other connectives and the quantifier $\exists$ are abbreviations; in particular $\exists x\, \varphi$ abbreviates $\neg \forall x\, \neg \varphi$.
For a formula $\varphi$, a variable $x$ and a term $t$, $\varphi[t/x]$ is the result of replacing each free occurrence of $x$ in $\varphi$ by $t$.
The term $t$ is *substitutable* for $x$ in $\varphi$ when no variable of $t$ becomes bound at a replaced place.
The system above is the one of Enderton's textbook.
Its name $\mathsf{H}$ is local to this sub-area.
For a language without equality, drop groups 5 and 6.
The members of $\Gamma$ are called *hypotheses* or *non-logical axioms*.
Some books write $\varphi(t)$ for $\varphi[t/x]$ when $x$ is clear from the context, and others write $\varphi^x_t$ or $\varphi(x := t)$.

## Remarks

### The variant with a rule of generalization

Mendelson's textbook uses a different system, which we call $\mathsf{H}_G$.
Its axioms are not closed under generalization, and it has a second rule.

1. Three propositional schemas: $\varphi \to (\psi \to \varphi)$; $(\varphi \to (\psi \to \chi)) \to ((\varphi \to \psi) \to (\varphi \to \chi))$; $(\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi) \to ((\neg \psi \to \varphi) \to \psi)$.
2. $\forall x\, \varphi \to \varphi[t/x]$, where $t$ is substitutable for $x$ in $\varphi$.
3. $\forall x\, (\varphi \to \psi) \to (\varphi \to \forall x\, \psi)$, where $x$ does not occur free in $\varphi$.
4. The rules are modus ponens and *generalization*: from $\varphi$, infer $\forall x\, \varphi$.

In a theory with equality, $\mathsf{H}_G$ adds the axioms $\forall x\, (x = x)$ and $x = y \to (\varphi(x, x) \to \varphi(x, y))$, where $\varphi(x, y)$ comes from $\varphi(x, x)$ by replacing some free occurrences of $x$ by $y$, and $y$ is substitutable at those places.

### How the two systems differ

| Feature | $\mathsf{H}$ (Enderton) | $\mathsf{H}_G$ (Mendelson) |
|---|---|---|
| Propositional axioms | all tautologies | three schemas |
| Rules | modus ponens | modus ponens and generalization |
| Generalization | a metatheorem, the [[generalization theorem|VKSK35Y52Z]] | a rule |
| $\{P(x)\} \vdash \forall x\, P(x)$ | false | true |
| [[Deduction theorem|8M9ZFDR4DH]] | holds with no restriction | needs a restriction on generalization |
| Meaning of $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ | every structure and assignment that satisfy $\Gamma$ satisfy $\varphi$ | every model of $\Gamma$ satisfies $\varphi$ under every assignment |

When $\Gamma$ is a set of sentences, the two systems derive the same formulas from $\Gamma$.
This follows from the [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]] and [[completeness|WQ1ZE255SF]] of both systems, because for a set of sentences the two meanings in the last row agree.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A Hilbert-style calculus is a list of rules for writing proofs line by line.
Most of the work is done by axioms, and there is only one way to move from old lines to new lines: modus ponens.
For example, from "every number is even" the axiom of group 2 gives "the number 7 is even", and this step is correct as a step of logic even though the premise is false.
The calculus checks the form of each line, not its truth in some situation.

### L2 human

We check that a short sequence is a deduction of $\forall x\, Q(x)$ from $\Gamma = \{\forall x\, (P(x) \to Q(x)),\ \forall x\, P(x)\}$.

1. $\forall x\, (P(x) \to Q(x))$: a member of $\Gamma$.
2. $\forall x\, (P(x) \to Q(x)) \to (\forall x\, P(x) \to \forall x\, Q(x))$: group 3, with $\varphi = P(x)$ and $\psi = Q(x)$.
3. $\forall x\, P(x) \to \forall x\, Q(x)$: modus ponens from lines 1 and 2.
4. $\forall x\, P(x)$: a member of $\Gamma$.
5. $\forall x\, Q(x)$: modus ponens from lines 4 and 3.

The typical use of the calculus is in metatheorems, where short lists of axioms and rules keep the induction on deductions short.
A trap is group 2 with a term that is not substitutable: $\forall x\, \neg \forall y\, (x = y) \to \neg \forall y\, (y = y)$ is not an axiom, and it is false in every structure with two elements.

### L3 human

The definition is an inductive definition of the set of theorems: the least set that contains the axioms and is closed under modus ponens.
So each property of theorems is proved by induction on deductions, and this is how the [[generalization theorem|VKSK35Y52Z]], the [[deduction theorem|8M9ZFDR4DH]] and the soundness theorem are proved.
Closing the axioms under generalization moves the rule of generalization into the axioms, which keeps modus ponens as the only rule and makes the deduction theorem unconditional.
The [[equivalence of proof systems|A9AAHN4BMH]] shows that the calculus derives the same sentences as natural deduction, the sequent calculus and tableaux.
