## Definition

The Hilbert-style predicate calculus $\mathsf{H}$ is a proof system for first-order logic whose proofs are lists of formulas, built from logical axioms with modus ponens as the only rule.

Fix a first-order language $\mathcal{L}$ whose primitive logical symbols are $\neg$, $\to$, $\forall$ and $=$.
A *prime formula* is an atomic formula or a formula of the form $\forall x\, \psi$.
Every formula is built from prime formulas with $\neg$ and $\to$ alone.
A formula is a *tautology* when it is true under every assignment of truth values to its prime formulas, by the truth tables of $\neg$ and $\to$; see [[tautology|05006AJ7DZ]].
A *generalization* of $\varphi$ is a formula $\forall x_1 \cdots \forall x_n\, \varphi$, with $n \geq 0$ and any variables $x_i$.

The *logical axioms* of $\mathsf{H}$ are all generalizations of the formulas in the following six groups.

1. Every tautology.
2. $\forall x\, \varphi \to \varphi[t/x]$, where the term $t$ is substitutable for $x$ in $\varphi$.
3. $\forall x\,(\varphi \to \psi) \to (\forall x\, \varphi \to \forall x\, \psi)$.
4. $\varphi \to \forall x\, \varphi$, where $x$ does not occur free in $\varphi$.
5. $x = x$.
6. $x = y \to (\alpha \to \alpha')$, where $\alpha$ is an atomic formula and $\alpha'$ is obtained from $\alpha$ by replacing $x$ by $y$ at zero or more places.

The only rule of inference of $\mathsf{H}$ is [[modus ponens|0Y0MQYST0M]]: from $\varphi$ and $\varphi \to \psi$, infer $\psi$.
A *deduction* of $\varphi$ from a set $\Gamma$ of formulas is a finite sequence $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_n$ of formulas with $\varphi_n = \varphi$.
In it, each $\varphi_i$ is a logical axiom, a member of $\Gamma$, or follows by modus ponens from $\varphi_j$ and $\varphi_k = (\varphi_j \to \varphi_i)$ with $j, k < i$.
We write $\Gamma \vdash_{\mathsf{H}} \varphi$, or $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$, when such a deduction exists, and $\vdash \varphi$ when $\Gamma$ is empty.

## Notation

The [[formulas|HYJ10AF6RT]] and [[terms|6D3WA7N2W4]] of $\mathcal{L}$ are the usual ones.
The other connectives and $\exists$ are abbreviations, and $\exists x\, \varphi$ abbreviates $\neg \forall x\, \neg \varphi$.
The [[substitution|NCDZ969V7X]] $\varphi[t/x]$ is the result of replacing each free occurrence of $x$ in $\varphi$ by $t$.
The term $t$ is *substitutable* for $x$ in $\varphi$ when no variable of $t$ becomes bound at a replaced place.
Enderton writes $\varphi^x_t$ for $\varphi[t/x]$, and other books write $\varphi(t)$ or $\varphi(x := t)$.
The members of $\Gamma$ are called *hypotheses* or *non-logical axioms*.
For a language without equality, drop groups 5 and 6.

## Remarks

### Why each condition is there

The tautologies of group 1 let the calculus skip all propositional reasoning, since truth tables decide which formulas are tautologies.
Prime formulas count as letters here, so $\forall x\, P(x) \to \forall x\, P(x)$ is a tautology and $\forall x\, P(x) \to P(c)$ is not.

The condition in group 2 keeps instantiation sound.
Without it, $\forall x\, \neg \forall y\,(x = y) \to \neg \forall y\,(y = y)$ would be an axiom.
Its antecedent is true in every structure with at least two elements, and its consequent is false in every structure, as [[the captured variable example|YJPNPDDJQT]] shows.

The condition in group 4 is there for a similar reason.
The formula $P(x) \to \forall x\, P(x)$ fails in a structure with two elements where $P$ holds of one, under the assignment that sends $x$ to that element.

The closure under generalization replaces a rule of generalization.
It lets the [[generalization theorem|VKSK35Y52Z]] prove that $\forall x\, \varphi$ is deducible whenever $\varphi$ is deducible and $x$ is not free in the hypotheses.

### A small deduction

We check that five lines deduce $\forall x\, Q(x)$ from $\Gamma = \{ \forall x\,(P(x) \to Q(x)),\ \forall x\, P(x) \}$.

1. $\forall x\,(P(x) \to Q(x))$, a member of $\Gamma$.
2. $\forall x\,(P(x) \to Q(x)) \to (\forall x\, P(x) \to \forall x\, Q(x))$, group 3 with $\varphi = P(x)$ and $\psi = Q(x)$.
3. $\forall x\, P(x) \to \forall x\, Q(x)$, modus ponens from lines 1 and 2.
4. $\forall x\, P(x)$, a member of $\Gamma$.
5. $\forall x\, Q(x)$, modus ponens from lines 4 and 3.

The [[concept page|N95HV0VWSM]] gives more examples and the main properties.

### The variant with a rule of generalization

Mendelson's textbook uses a different system, which we call $\mathsf{H}_G$.
Its axioms are not closed under generalization, and it has a second rule.
The propositional axioms are three schemas,
$$\varphi \to (\psi \to \varphi), \qquad (\varphi \to (\psi \to \chi)) \to ((\varphi \to \psi) \to (\varphi \to \chi)), \qquad (\neg \psi \to \neg \varphi) \to ((\neg \psi \to \varphi) \to \psi).$$
The quantifier axioms are $\forall x\, \varphi \to \varphi[t/x]$, with $t$ substitutable for $x$ in $\varphi$, and $\forall x\,(\varphi \to \psi) \to (\varphi \to \forall x\, \psi)$, with $x$ not free in $\varphi$.
The rules are modus ponens and *generalization*: from $\varphi$, infer $\forall x\, \varphi$.
With equality, $\mathsf{H}_G$ adds $\forall x\,(x = x)$ and the schema $x = y \to (\varphi(x, x) \to \varphi(x, y))$.
Here $\varphi(x, y)$ comes from $\varphi(x, x)$ by replacing some free occurrences of $x$ by $y$, at places where $y$ is substitutable.

### How the two systems differ

| Feature | $\mathsf{H}$ (Enderton) | $\mathsf{H}_G$ (Mendelson) |
|---|---|---|
| Propositional axioms | all tautologies | three schemas |
| Rules | modus ponens | modus ponens and generalization |
| Generalization | a metatheorem, the [[generalization theorem|VKSK35Y52Z]] | a rule |
| $\{ P(x) \} \vdash \forall x\, P(x)$ | false | true |
| [[Deduction theorem|8M9ZFDR4DH]] | holds with no restriction | needs a restriction on generalization |
| Meaning of $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ | every structure and assignment that satisfy $\Gamma$ satisfy $\varphi$ | every model of $\Gamma$ satisfies $\varphi$ under every assignment |

When $\Gamma$ is a set of sentences, the two systems deduce the same formulas from $\Gamma$.
This follows from the [[soundness|4A84VHDTHV]] and [[completeness|WQ1ZE255SF]] of both systems, because for sentences the two meanings in the last row agree.

## Explanations

### L1 human

A Hilbert-style calculus is a list of rules for writing proofs line by line.
Most of the work is done by axioms, and there is only one way to move from old lines to new lines: modus ponens.
For example, from "every number is even" an axiom gives "if every number is even, then the number 7 is even".
Modus ponens then gives "7 is even", and this step is correct logic even though the premise is false.
The calculus checks the form of each line, not its truth in some situation.

### L2 human

Here is a check of whether a line is an axiom of group 2.
Take the line $\forall x\, \exists y\, R(x, y) \to \exists y\, R(f(y), y)$, with $\exists y$ standing for $\neg \forall y\, \neg$.
Here $\varphi = \exists y\, R(x, y)$ and $t = f(y)$.
The only free $x$ in $\varphi$ lies inside $\exists y$, and $t$ contains $y$, so $y$ would be captured.
So the line is not an axiom, and it should not be one: in $\mathbb{N}$ with $R$ read as $<$ and $f(n) = n + 1$, the antecedent is true and the consequent says that some $n$ has $n + 1 < n$.
Renaming first gives the axiom $\forall x\, \exists z\, R(x, z) \to \exists z\, R(f(y), z)$, which is true in $\mathbb{N}$.
The typical use of the definition is in proofs about all deductions, where the short list of axioms keeps the induction short.

### L3 human

The definition is an inductive definition of the set of theorems: the least set that contains the axioms and is closed under modus ponens.
So each property of theorems is proved by induction on deductions, and this is how the [[generalization theorem|VKSK35Y52Z]], the [[deduction theorem|8M9ZFDR4DH]] and the soundness theorem are proved.
Closing the axioms under generalization moves the rule of generalization into the axioms.
That keeps modus ponens as the only rule and makes the deduction theorem hold with no condition.
The [[equivalence of proof systems|A9AAHN4BMH]] shows that the calculus deduces the same sentences as natural deduction, the sequent calculus and tableaux.
