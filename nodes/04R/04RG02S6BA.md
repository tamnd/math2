## Statement

Let $G$ be a [[cyclic group|0KVGH0J5B3]].
Then $G$ is [[abelian|082GHNPJ53]]: $xy = yx$ for all $x, y \in G$.

## Remarks

### Why each hypothesis is needed

The hypothesis is that one element generates $G$.
Two generators are not enough.
The [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$ is generated by $(1\,2)$ and $(1\,2\,3)$, but it is not abelian.
With products read from right to left, $(1\,2)(1\,2\,3) = (2\,3)$ and $(1\,2\,3)(1\,2) = (1\,3)$.

### Consequences

1. For each element $g$ of any group, the [[cyclic subgroup|08ABT85339]] $\langle g \rangle$ is abelian, also when the whole group is not.
2. A group that is not abelian is not cyclic. For example, $S_3$ and the [[quaternion group|0NRJGW864D]] $Q_8$ are not cyclic.
3. Each subgroup of a cyclic group is [[normal|0CB0DX5BVV]], because each subgroup of an abelian group is normal.
4. Each group of prime order is abelian, because [[it is cyclic|05MM4EK4SA]].

### Converse

The converse is false: an abelian group need not be cyclic.

1. The [[Klein four-group|DX6X1D21FE]] is abelian, but each of its elements has order at most $2$, so no element generates its $4$ elements.
2. The group [[$(\mathbb{Q}, +)$|~b8d64d52]] is abelian, but it is not cyclic.
3. The group [[$\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$|~fac25186]] is abelian and generated by two elements, but it is not cyclic.

### Generalizations

1. If a group is generated by a set of pairwise [[commuting elements|YJRBCWQV6B]], then the group is abelian. The theorem is the case of a set with one element.
2. A [[locally cyclic group|RXWA8TF2RM]], in which each finitely generated subgroup is cyclic, is abelian: two elements $x, y$ lie in the cyclic subgroup $\langle x, y \rangle$, so they commute.

## Explanations

### L1 human

In a cyclic group, every element is a power of one element $g$.
To combine two powers of $g$, we add the exponents.
Addition of integers does not depend on the order, so the result does not depend on the order either.
For example, $g^2 g^3 = g^5 = g^3 g^2$.

### L2 human

We check the theorem in $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ under addition, with generator $1$.

1. Write $2$ as two copies of $1$, and $5$ as five copies of $1$.
2. Then $2 + 5$ and $5 + 2$ are both seven copies of $1$.
3. Seven copies of $1$ give $7 \bmod 6 = 1$. So $2 + 5 = 5 + 2 = 1$.

The typical use is a quick test for groups that are not cyclic.
If two elements of a group do not commute, the group is not cyclic.
For example, $S_3$ is not abelian, so it is not cyclic.

A trap: the test works in one direction only.
The Klein four-group is abelian and not cyclic, so "abelian" does not prove "cyclic".

### L3 human

The theorem is a special case of a general fact: a homomorphic image of an abelian group is abelian.
The cyclic group $\langle g \rangle$ is the image of $\mathbb{Z}$ under $k \mapsto g^k$, and $\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian.
A related fact uses the [[centre|0DZ93493VN]] $Z(G)$ of a group: if the quotient $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then $G$ is abelian.
The same argument shows that a ring generated by one element over $\mathbb{Z}$, such as $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ or $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, is commutative.
