## Statement

The evaluation maps into the double dual are compatible with every linear map, and this is the exact sense in which they are canonical.
Let $F$ be a field, let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over $F$, and let $f \colon V \to W$ be a [[linear map|0RJF668P1T]].
Let $\operatorname{ev}_V \colon V \to V^{**}$ and $\operatorname{ev}_W \colon W \to W^{**}$ be the [[evaluation maps|0ZJM9D7NPS]].
Let $f^{**} = (f^*)^* \colon V^{**} \to W^{**}$ be the [[dual map|0C2TQMW5A1]] of the dual map $f^* \colon W^* \to V^*$.
Then
$$f^{**} \circ \operatorname{ev}_V = \operatorname{ev}_W \circ f,$$
that is, the following square commutes:
$$\begin{array}{ccc} V & \xrightarrow{\ \ f\ \ } & W \\ {\scriptstyle \operatorname{ev}_V} \big\downarrow & & \big\downarrow {\scriptstyle \operatorname{ev}_W} \\ V^{**} & \xrightarrow{\ f^{**}\ } & W^{**} \end{array}$$

## Remarks

### A check with numbers

Let $f \colon \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ be $f(x, y) = x + 2y$, let $v = (1, 3)$, and let $\varphi \in \mathbb{R}^*$ be $\varphi(t) = 5t$.
Going right and then down, we get $f(v) = 7$ and then
$$\operatorname{ev}_W(7)(\varphi) = \varphi(7) = 35.$$
Going down and then right, we use $f^*(\varphi) = \varphi \circ f$, so
$$f^{**}(\operatorname{ev}_V(v))(\varphi) = \operatorname{ev}_V(v)(\varphi \circ f) = \varphi(f(1, 3)) = 5 \cdot 7 = 35.$$
Both paths give $35$, as the lemma says they must for every $v$ and every $\varphi$.

### What the hypotheses do

The map $f$ must be linear, because otherwise $f^*$ does not send linear forms to linear forms and $f^{**}$ is not defined.
No finite dimension is needed: the square commutes for all spaces, as the [[proof|D9WGVX1X9F]] shows by unwinding definitions.
Finite dimension only adds that the two vertical maps are isomorphisms, by the theorem on the [[double dual|0ZJM9D7NPS]].

### What it gives you

In finite dimension the vertical maps can be inverted, and the lemma becomes
$$f^{**} = \operatorname{ev}_W \circ f \circ \operatorname{ev}_V^{-1}.$$
So after we identify $V$ with $V^{**}$ and $W$ with $W^{**}$, the map $f^{**}$ is $f$ itself.
In matrices this is the rule $(A^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}} = A$, by applying the theorem on the [[matrix of the dual map|0ZPB3180AY]] twice.

### Why there is no such square for the dual

Could the same work for a family of isomorphisms $V \to V^*$?
For $f \colon V \to W$, we would need to compare $V \to V^*$ with $W \to W^*$, but the dual map goes $W^* \to V^*$, against $f$.
So no square of this shape can be formed.
The isomorphisms $V \cong V^*$ that bases give also change with the basis, as the [[example in the plane|A3CP855PNV]] shows.

### Generalizations

In the language of category theory, the lemma says that $\operatorname{ev}$ is a [[natural transformation|KYCPCC57FT]] from the identity functor to the double dual functor on vector spaces over $F$.
On finite-dimensional spaces it is a natural isomorphism.
The same square commutes for modules over any commutative ring, with the same proof.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Start with a vector $v$ in $V$ and try to reach the double dual of $W$.
You can apply $f$ first and then turn $f(v)$ into "evaluate at $f(v)$".
Or you can turn $v$ into "evaluate at $v$" first and then push it along with $f^{**}$.
The lemma says that both routes end at the same place.
That is what "natural" means for the evaluation map.

### L2 human

Here is a typical use: the matrix of $f^{**}$ without computing two duals.
Let $f \colon \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ have matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ in the standard basis.
Identify each space with its double dual by $\operatorname{ev}$, which sends the standard basis to the dual basis of the dual basis.
The lemma says that $f^{**}$ then acts as $f$, so its matrix is again $A$.
Check: the matrix of $f^*$ is $A^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, and transposing once more gives back $A$.
Watch out: the analogous claim for a family of maps $V \to V^*$ cannot even be written down, because no square of this shape exists.
