## Example

Let $L = \set{\cdot, ^{-1}, e}$ and let $T_{\mathrm{grp}}$ be the [[group axioms|RX9MB386C5]]: associativity, $\forall x \, (x \cdot e = x \land e \cdot x = x)$ and $\forall x \, (x \cdot x^{-1} = e \land x^{-1} \cdot x = e)$.
We check that $T_{\mathrm{grp}}$ is not [[complete|655K8Y7VS8]], so that it is not the [[theory of any structure|X9HV9C6X4F]].

1. Let $\gamma = \forall x \, \forall y \, (x \cdot y = y \cdot x)$.
2. $(\Z, +, -, 0)$ is a [[group|G5S1M9]] and satisfies $\gamma$, since $m + n = n + m$. So $T_{\mathrm{grp}} \not\models \neg\gamma$.
3. The symmetric group $S_3$ is a group and satisfies $\neg\gamma$: for the transpositions $a = (1\,2)$ and $b = (1\,3)$, we have $ab = (1\,3\,2)$ and $ba = (1\,2\,3)$. So $T_{\mathrm{grp}} \not\models \gamma$.
4. So $T_{\mathrm{grp}}$ decides neither $\gamma$ nor $\neg\gamma$, and it is not complete.
5. For every structure $\mathcal{M}$, the set $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$ contains $\gamma$ or $\neg\gamma$. So no set of sentences with the same consequences as $T_{\mathrm{grp}}$ is of the form $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal{M})$.

## Remarks

A second sentence that $T_{\mathrm{grp}}$ does not decide is $\exists x \, \neg(x = e)$: it fails in the trivial group and holds in $\Z$.
The sentences "there are at least $n$ elements", "every element has order dividing $n$" and "there is an element of order $n$" give many more.
The theory of a single group, such as $\operatorname{Th}(\Z, +, -, 0)$, is complete; it is one of the complete extensions of $T_{\mathrm{grp}}$.
The theory of [[abelian groups|FWP8NY5VE7]] is also not complete, by the second sentence above.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The group axioms describe many different groups.
Some of them are commutative, like the integers with addition, and some are not, like the symmetries of a triangle.
So the axioms cannot decide whether "every two elements commute" is true.
A complete theory must decide every such statement.

### L2 human

We check the computation in step 3 with $S_3$ acting on $\set{1, 2, 3}$, composing from right to left.

1. $ab$ sends $1 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 3$, $2 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 1$, $3 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 2$. So $ab = (1\,3\,2)$.
2. $ba$ sends $1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 2$, $2 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 3$, $3 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 1$. So $ba = (1\,2\,3)$.
3. $(1\,3\,2) \ne (1\,2\,3)$, since the first sends $1$ to $3$ and the second sends $1$ to $2$.

The typical use of such a pair of models is to show that a sentence is independent of a theory.
A trap is to think that a theory with infinitely many models is never complete; $\mathrm{DLO}$ is complete and has many models.
