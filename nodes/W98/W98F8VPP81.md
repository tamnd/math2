## Example

In $\R^3$, let $U = \{(x, y, 0)\}$ be the $xy$-plane and let $W = \{(0, y, z)\}$ be the $yz$-plane.
We check the [[second isomorphism theorem|0APN65D6Y6]] for this pair, in which the two planes overlap in a line.

1. The [[intersection|0V33SPH7RR]] $U \cap W$ consists of the vectors with $z = 0$ and $x = 0$, so it is the $y$-axis $\{(0, y, 0)\}$.
2. The [[sum|0RMKABS2S5]] $U + W$ is all of $\R^3$, since
   $$(x, y, z) = (x, y, 0) + (0, 0, z).$$
3. In $U / (U \cap W)$, the coset of $(a, b, 0)$ is the line $\{(a, t, 0) : t \in \R\}$ in the $xy$-plane.
   Two vectors of $U$ have the same coset exactly when their difference lies on the $y$-axis, that is, when they have the same first entry.
4. In $(U + W) / W = \R^3 / W$, the coset of $(a, b, c)$ is the plane $\{(a, s, t) : s, t \in \R\}$, that is, the plane $x = a$.
5. The map of the theorem sends $(a, b, 0) + (U \cap W)$ to $(a, b, 0) + W$, which is the plane $x = a$.
6. The map is well defined, since its value depends only on the first entry $a$, which is the same for all vectors of the coset.
7. It is injective, since different values of $a$ give different planes.
   It is surjective, since every plane $x = a$ is the image of the line through $(a, 0, 0)$.

So $U / (U \cap W) \cong (U + W) / W$, and both quotients are copies of $\R$, with the coordinate $a$ as the label.

## Remarks

The dimensions are $\dim U = \dim W = 2$, $\dim (U \cap W) = 1$ and $\dim (U + W) = 3$.
Each side of the theorem has dimension $1$, since $2 - 1 = 1$ and $3 - 2 = 1$.
Setting these equal is [[Grassmann's formula|06HTJNCMPA]] for this pair, $3 + 1 = 2 + 2$.

The sum $U + W$ is not direct, since $U \cap W$ is a line, and the theorem still applies.
If we replace $W$ by the $z$-axis, the intersection becomes $\{0\}$, the sum is still $\R^3$, and the theorem gives $U \cong \R^3 / (z\text{-axis})$, a plane on each side.
If instead we take $W = U$, both sides are the zero space, since $U / U$ and $(U + U) / U$ each have one element.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The $xy$-plane adds one new direction to the $yz$-plane, the $x$-direction, because the $y$-direction is in both.
The left side of the theorem sees this inside the $xy$-plane: once you ignore the $y$-direction, only the $x$-coordinate is left.
The right side sees it inside all of space: once you ignore the whole $yz$-plane, again only the $x$-coordinate is left.
Both sides are the same line of values of $x$.

### L2 human

Each side of the theorem is computed in a different space.
The left side lives inside $U$, where we glue together the points of each line parallel to the $y$-axis.
The right side lives inside $U + W = \R^3$, where we glue together the points of each plane parallel to the $yz$-plane.
Each line of the first kind lies in exactly one plane of the second kind, namely the plane with the same value of $x$, and this is the isomorphism.
The trap is to expect the theorem to need $U \cap W = \{0\}$; here the overlap is a whole line and everything still works.
