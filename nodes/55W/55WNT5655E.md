## Statement

Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a countable set, and let $p$ and $q$ be [[probability mass functions|0D6J4B671D]] on $\mathcal{X}$.
Assume that $-\sum_{x} p(x) \log p(x)$ is finite, with the convention $0 \log 0 = 0$.
Then
$$-\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} p(x) \log p(x) \le -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} p(x) \log q(x),$$
where the right side may be $+\infty$ and terms with $p(x) = 0$ are $0$.
Equality holds if and only if $p(x) = q(x)$ for every $x \in \mathcal{X}$.

## Remarks

Proof idea: use $\ln t \le t - 1$ for $t > 0$, with equality only at $t = 1$, where $\ln$ is the [[natural logarithm|04DXAFN2ES]].
Summing over $x$ with $p(x) > 0$ gives $\sum p(x) \ln \frac{q(x)}{p(x)} \le \sum (q(x) - p(x)) \le 0$.
One can also apply [[Jensen's inequality|0RWMRTPC87]] to the concave function $\log$.
The inequality says that the [[Kullback–Leibler divergence|PQBW43X5RE]] $D(p \,\|\, q) = \sum p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ is non-negative and vanishes only for $q = p$.

Example: with $p = (\frac12, \frac12)$ and $q = (\frac14, \frac34)$ on two points, the left side is $1$ bit and the right side is $\frac12 \cdot 2 + \frac12 \log_2 \frac43 \approx 1.21$ bits.

Taking $q$ uniform on a set of $n$ points gives $H(p) \le \log n$ for every $p$ supported on these points.
The hypothesis that $q$ sums to $1$ is needed.
On two points, with $q(x) = 1$ for both $x$ and $p$ uniform, the right side is $0$ and the left side is $\log 2$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Suppose outcomes really follow the law $p$, but you plan your code as if they followed $q$.
Gibbs' inequality says you can not do better on average than by planning with the true law.
The average surprise under a wrong model is always at least the true entropy.

### L2 human

In the language of [[information theory|38JECZ46C2]], the right side is the [[cross-entropy|K6MCP56ETX]] of $q$ relative to $p$, and the difference of the two sides is the Kullback–Leibler divergence.
The inequality is the source of almost every basic inequality of Shannon theory.
It gives $H(X) \le \log \abs{\mathcal{X}}$, $I(X; Y) \ge 0$, and $H(X \mid Y) \le H(X)$, and with densities in place of mass functions it gives the maximum entropy property of the normal and exponential laws.
