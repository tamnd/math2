## Summary

Superfluidity is the ability of a quantum fluid to flow without viscosity.
It occurs in liquid helium-4 below about $2.17$ K, in helium-3 at much lower temperatures, and in dilute Bose–Einstein condensates.
The superfluid flow is described by a macroscopic wave function, so its velocity is the gradient of a phase.
It is irrotational except at [[quantum vortices|V4KQ2RVR7E]], and its circulation is quantised.
Landau's criterion explains why such a flow cannot lose energy below a critical speed.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Below a certain temperature, liquid helium does strange things.
It flows through tiny pores that block ordinary liquids, it creeps up the walls of its container, and a ring of it can keep flowing for ever.
The reason is quantum mechanics: a large fraction of the atoms share one quantum state and move together as a single wave.
To slow such a flow, the fluid would have to create an excitation, and at low speed this costs more energy than the flow can provide.
A bucket of superfluid does not rotate as a whole; instead it fills with tiny whirlpools, each carrying one quantum of circulation.
