## Summary

Let $G$ be a [[group|G5S1M9]] with identity $e$, and let $g \in G$.
The integer powers of $g$ are $g^0 = e$, $g^{k+1} = g^k g$ for $k \geq 0$, and $g^{-k} = (g^{-1})^k$ for $k > 0$.
The cyclic subgroup generated by $g$ is the set $\langle g \rangle = \{g^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ of all integer powers of $g$.
It is a [[subgroup|0X1JXT47GA]] of $G$, and it is the smallest subgroup of $G$ that contains $g$.
Each element of a group lies in its own cyclic subgroup, so cyclic subgroups are the first subgroups that we can find in any group.

## Motivation

Take one element $g$ of a group and ask for the smallest subgroup that contains it.
A subgroup that contains $g$ must contain $g^2 = gg$, then $g^3$, and so on.
It must contain $e$ and the inverse $g^{-1}$, and then all the negative powers.
So it must contain every power $g^k$ with $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.
The [[laws of exponents|00DRK90Z6K]] show that these powers already form a subgroup.
Thus the powers of $g$ answer the question exactly.
The answer has two forms: either the powers repeat after $n$ steps, or they never repeat.

## Notation

We write $\langle g \rangle$ for the cyclic subgroup generated by $g$.
In additive notation the powers are the multiples $kg$, and $\langle g \rangle = \{kg : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, also written $\mathbb{Z}g$.
For example, in $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ we have $\langle 3 \rangle = 3\mathbb{Z}$.
The same angle brackets denote the [[subgroup generated by a subset|0RP7VWQ0YN]], and $\langle g \rangle = \langle \{g\} \rangle$.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. In $(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the multiples of $3$ are $0, 3, 6, 9$, and then $12 \equiv 0$. So $\langle 3 \rangle = \{0, 3, 6, 9\}$, with $4$ elements.
2. In the [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$, with products of permutations read from right to left, $(1\,2\,3)^2 = (1\,3\,2)$ and $(1\,2\,3)^3 = e$. So $\langle (1\,2\,3) \rangle = \{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$.
3. In $\mathbb{C}^\times$, [[the powers of $i$|~2bdc2622]] are $1, i, -1, -i$, and then they repeat. So $\langle i \rangle$ has $4$ elements.
4. In $\mathbb{Q}^\times$, [[the powers of $2$|~5a3994de]] are all distinct, so $\langle 2 \rangle = \{2^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is infinite.
5. In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, $\langle 1 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}$, and $\langle 0 \rangle = \{0\}$. In any group, $\langle e \rangle = \{e\}$.

### Non-examples

1. [[The nonnegative powers of $2$|~e9eb00ea]], $\{1, 2, 4, 8, \ldots\}$, are not $\langle 2 \rangle$ in $\mathbb{Q}^\times$. The negative powers are missing, and the set is not a subgroup, because $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is not in it.
2. In $S_3$, the set $\{e, (1\,2\,3)\}$ is not $\langle (1\,2\,3) \rangle$. It misses the power $(1\,2\,3)^2 = (1\,3\,2)$, so it is not closed under products.
3. The [[Klein four-group|0PMRQJVTME]] $V$ is a subgroup of itself, but it is [[not a cyclic subgroup|DX6X1D21FE]]: each $\langle g \rangle$ with $g \in V$ has at most $2$ elements.

### Basic properties

1. The set $\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup. It contains $g^0 = e$, and $g^a g^b = g^{a+b}$ and $(g^a)^{-1} = g^{-a}$ by the [[laws of exponents|00DRK90Z6K]].
2. If $H$ is a subgroup and $g \in H$, then $\langle g \rangle \subseteq H$. By induction on $k$, closure gives $g^k \in H$ for $k \geq 0$, and closure under inverses gives $g^{-k} = (g^k)^{-1} \in H$.
3. If $g$ has finite [[order|0WE2NHZJ7B]] $n$, then $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \ldots, g^{n-1}\}$, and these $n$ elements are distinct. If $g$ has infinite order, then $k \mapsto g^k$ is injective. Both facts are [[the theorem on the order of $\langle g \rangle$|031M8HX7DA]].
4. The map $k \mapsto g^k$ is a [[group homomorphism|0JYTG5K9Y4]] from $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ onto $\langle g \rangle$, by the law $g^{a+b} = g^a g^b$.
5. The group $\langle g \rangle$ is [[cyclic|0KVGH0J5B3]], so it is [[abelian|082GHNPJ53]] by [[this theorem|04RG02S6BA]].
6. We have $\langle g \rangle = \langle g^{-1} \rangle$, because $g^k = (g^{-1})^{-k}$ for each $k$.
7. If $G$ is finite, then the order of $g$ divides $|G|$, by [[this corollary of Lagrange's theorem|08V7JHS3QZ]].

### History

Gauss studied the powers of a single residue modulo a prime in the Disquisitiones Arithmeticae of 1801.
He proved there that, for each prime $p$, some residue has powers that give all the nonzero residues modulo $p$.
In the language of this page, the group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is the cyclic subgroup generated by such a residue.

### Common errors

1. A student takes only the positive powers $g, g^2, g^3, \ldots$. In a finite group this gives the right set, because $g^{-1} = g^{n-1}$ when $g$ has order $n$. For an element of infinite order it is wrong, as non-example 1 shows.
2. A student assumes that $\langle g^k \rangle = \langle g \rangle$ for each $k \neq 0$. In $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, $\langle 2 \rangle = \{0, 2\}$ but $\langle 1 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.
3. In additive notation, a student reads $g^k$ as a product of $g$ with itself. In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the "power" $3^2$ in the group sense is $3 + 3 = 6$, not $9$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Pick one element of a group and combine it with itself again and again.
Also combine its inverse with itself again and again.
All the results together form a small group inside the big group.
For example, in clock arithmetic with $12$ hours, start at $3$ and keep adding $3$.
We get $3, 6, 9$ and then $12$, which is $0$ on the clock, and then the values repeat.
So the cyclic subgroup generated by $3$ is $\{0, 3, 6, 9\}$.

### L2 human

We compute $\langle 8 \rangle$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}, +)$.

1. The multiples of $8$ modulo $12$ are $0 \cdot 8 = 0$, $1 \cdot 8 = 8$, $2 \cdot 8 = 16 \equiv 4$ and $3 \cdot 8 = 24 \equiv 0$.
2. So the multiples repeat with period $3$, and $8$ has order $3$.
3. The negative multiples give nothing new: $-8 \equiv 4$ and $-16 \equiv 8$.
4. So $\langle 8 \rangle = \{0, 4, 8\} = \langle 4 \rangle$.

The typical use is to find subgroups quickly: each element gives one, and its size is the order of the element.
In a finite group this gives the bound "order of $g$ divides $|G|$", through Lagrange's theorem.

A trap: two different elements can generate the same cyclic subgroup, as $4$ and $8$ do here.
So the number of cyclic subgroups is in general smaller than the number of elements.

### L3 human

The cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ is the image of the unique homomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to G$ that sends $1$ to $g$.
The kernel of this homomorphism is $n\mathbb{Z}$ if $g$ has finite order $n$, and $\{0\}$ if $g$ has infinite order.
So the [[first isomorphism theorem|0CXXJNG5B6]] gives $\langle g \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ or $\langle g \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}$.
This is why [[the classification of cyclic groups|0736AXH2ME]] has only the two cases $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.

The same construction appears across algebra: the cyclic submodule $Rm$ of a module, the principal ideal $(a)$ of a commutative ring, and the one-parameter subgroups $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ of a Lie group.
In a topological group, the closure of $\langle g \rangle$ is a monothetic group, and in the [[circle group|04EAFVFTF7]] the closure of $\langle e^{2\pi i \theta} \rangle$ with $\theta$ irrational is the whole circle.
In a finite group, the exponent is the least common multiple of the orders of the elements, so the cyclic subgroups determine it.
