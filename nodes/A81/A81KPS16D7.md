## Proof

Steps 1 to 11 prove the direction "only if" of part 1, and part 3, by induction on $n = \dim V$.
Steps 12 to 13 prove the direction "if" of part 1, and steps 14 to 17 prove part 2.

1. Let $\mathbb{K}$ be $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. For $n \geq 0$, let $P(n)$ be the following claim. Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional inner product space over $\mathbb{K}$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]]. Then $V$ has an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of [[eigenvectors|0B5H4208Z9]] of $T$.
2. Base case $n = 0$. Then $V = \set{0}$, and the empty family is an orthonormal basis. It consists of eigenvectors, since it has no members. So $P(0)$ holds.
3. Let $n \geq 1$, and let $V$ and $T$ be as in $P(n)$, with [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}$. By part 2 of [[the theorem on real eigenvalues|0C4NT4GT3X]], there are $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ and $e \in V$ with $\norm{e} = 1$ and $Te = \lambda e$.
4. Let $W$ be the [[orthogonal complement|079D68S3AW]] of the line $L = \set{a e : a \in \mathbb{K}}$. A vector $w$ lies in $W$ if and only if $\ip{w}{e} = 0$. This follows from linearity in the first slot and conjugate symmetry.
5. By [[the dimension of the orthogonal complement|0E2NYPVSP9]], $\dim W = n - \dim L = n - 1$.
6. Let $w \in W$. Self-adjointness gives $\ip{Tw}{e} = \ip{w}{Te} = \ip{w}{\lambda e} = \overline{\lambda} \ip{w}{e} = 0$. So $Tw \in W$.
7. The restriction of $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}$ to $W$ is an inner product on $W$. Let $S \colon W \to W$ be $S(w) = T(w)$. It maps into $W$ by step 6, and it is linear because $T$ is linear.
8. For $w, w' \in W$, we get $\ip{Sw}{w'} = \ip{Tw}{w'} = \ip{w}{Tw'} = \ip{w}{Sw'}$. So $S$ is self-adjoint on $W$.
9. By $P(n - 1)$, $W$ has an orthonormal basis $f_2, \dots, f_n$ of eigenvectors of $S$. Each $f_k$ is nonzero, and $T f_k = S f_k = \mu_k f_k$ for some $\mu_k$. So $f_k$ is an eigenvector of $T$.
10. Put $f_1 = e$. Each $f_k$ with $k \geq 2$ lies in $W$, so $\ip{f_k}{f_1} = 0$ and $\ip{f_1}{f_k} = 0$. With $\norm{f_1} = 1$ and step 9, the family $f_1, \dots, f_n$ is orthonormal.
11. An orthonormal family is [[linearly independent|0JWM6GJNQW]]. So these $n$ vectors in the $n$-dimensional space $V$ form [[a basis|096V7H13H9]]. This proves $P(n)$. By induction, $P(n)$ holds for all $n$. The eigenvalues are real by part 1 of the theorem on real eigenvalues.
12. Direction "if" of part 1. Let $V$ be real, and let $f_1, \dots, f_n$ be an orthonormal basis with $T f_k = \lambda_k f_k$. Then the matrix $D$ of $T$ in this basis is diagonal with real entries.
13. By [[the matrix of the adjoint|0DBTE6HVBD]], the matrix of $T^*$ in this basis is $D^T = D$. Two operators with the same matrix are equal, so $T^* = T$.
14. Part 2, "only if". Let $A$ be real [[symmetric|0N28QN4CYA]]. On $\mathbb{R}^n$ take $\ip{x}{y} = y^T x$. Then $\ip{Ax}{y} = y^T A x = (A^T y)^T x = \ip{x}{Ay}$, so $x \mapsto Ax$ is self-adjoint.
15. By part 1, $\mathbb{R}^n$ has an orthonormal basis $q_1, \dots, q_n$ with $A q_k = \lambda_k q_k$. Let $Q$ have the columns $q_k$, and let $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$.
16. The $(i, j)$ entry of $Q^T Q$ is $q_i^T q_j = \ip{q_j}{q_i}$, so $Q^T Q = I$. So $Q$ is [[orthogonal|0QRBVFJ50X]], and $Q Q^T = I$ by [[the one-sided inverse theorem|0753Y2X5K6]]. By [[matrix multiplication|076JGP6R3T]] column by column, $AQ = QD$. Hence $A = Q D Q^T$.
17. Part 2, "if". If $A = Q D Q^T$ with $D$ diagonal, then $A^T = (Q^T)^T D^T Q^T = Q D Q^T = A$. So $A$ is symmetric.

## Idea

Find one unit eigenvector $e$ as a maximizer of the quadratic form, then look at the hyperplane orthogonal to $e$.
Self-adjointness makes this hyperplane invariant, and $T$ stays self-adjoint there, so induction gives the rest of the basis.
The converse holds because a diagonal real matrix equals its transpose.

## Variants

- Step 3 can use the fundamental theorem of algebra instead. Over $\mathbb{C}$, $T$ has [[an eigenvalue|08CJB2NQ92]]. Over $\mathbb{R}$, a complex eigenvector of the symmetric matrix of $T$ has an eigenvalue that is real. Its real or its imaginary part is then a real eigenvector.
- [[The variational proof|~8046c4e7]] avoids induction: it takes the maxima on smaller and smaller spheres, one after another.
- Over $\mathbb{C}$, part 3 also follows from [[the complex spectral theorem|04CW1P44VD]], because a self-adjoint operator is normal.
