## Proof

We argue by induction on $n = \dim V$. Steps 2 to 11 show that $T$ has an eigenvalue in the scalar field.

1. Let $\mathbb{K}$ be $\R$ or $\C$. For $n \ge 0$, let $P(n)$ be the following claim. Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional inner product space over $\mathbb{K}$, and let $T \colon V \to V$ be [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]]. Then $V$ has an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of [[eigenvectors|0B5H4208Z9]] of $T$.
2. Base case $n = 0$. Then $V = \set{0}$, and the empty family is an orthonormal basis. It vacuously consists of eigenvectors. So $P(0)$ holds.
3. Let $n \ge 1$, and let $V$ and $T$ be as in $P(n)$, with [[inner product|0VGSYBWHV2]] $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}$. If $\mathbb{K} = \C$, then $\C$ is [[algebraically closed|02SJG1HC32]] by the [[fundamental theorem of algebra|0JP0W07Y9A]]. So $T$ has an [[eigenvalue|08CJB2NQ92]].
4. Now let $\mathbb{K} = \R$. Take an [[orthonormal basis|0Y85GYEGSF]] $e_1, \dots, e_n$ of $V$, and set $a_{ij} = \ip{T(e_j)}{e_i} \in \R$. By the [[formula for coordinates|09DZ8CD1AK]], $T(e_j) = \sum_{i} a_{ij} e_i$.
5. Over $\R$, conjugate symmetry is symmetry. So self-adjointness gives $a_{ji} = \ip{T(e_i)}{e_j} = \ip{e_i}{T(e_j)} = \ip{T(e_j)}{e_i} = a_{ij}$. Let $A = (a_{ij})$, a real symmetric matrix.
6. View $A$ as a complex matrix. Since $\C^n \ne \set{0}$, the [[existence of an eigenvalue|08CJB2NQ92]] over $\C$, applied to $z \mapsto Az$, gives $\lambda \in \C$ and $z \in \C^n$ with $z \ne 0$ and $Az = \lambda z$.
7. Set $s = \sum_{i,j} \overline{z_i}\, a_{ij} z_j$. Since $\sum_j a_{ij} z_j = \lambda z_i$, we get $s = \lambda \sum_i \abs{z_i}^2$.
8. Since the $a_{ij}$ are real, $\overline{s} = \sum_{i,j} z_i a_{ij} \overline{z_j}$. Exchanging the names $i$ and $j$ and using $a_{ji} = a_{ij}$ gives $\overline{s} = s$. So $s$ is real.
9. The number $\sum_i \abs{z_i}^2$ is real and positive, as $z \ne 0$. By step 7, $\lambda$ is real.
10. Write $z = x + i y$ with $x, y \in \R^n$. Since $A$ and $\lambda$ are real, comparing real and imaginary parts of $Az = \lambda z$ gives $Ax = \lambda x$ and $Ay = \lambda y$.
11. As $z \ne 0$, one of $x, y$ is nonzero; call it $c$. Set $v = \sum_j c_j e_j$, which is nonzero. By step 4, $T(v) = \sum_i \big(\sum_j a_{ij} c_j\big) e_i = \sum_i \lambda c_i e_i = \lambda v$. So $\lambda \in \R$ is an eigenvalue of $T$.
12. In both cases, let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $T$ and $v$ an eigenvector for it. By the [[theorem on eigenvalues|0C4NT4GT3X]], $\lambda$ is real.
13. Set $e = v / \norm{v}$. Then $\ip{e}{e} = 1$ and $T(e) = \lambda e$.
14. Let $W$ be the [[orthogonal complement|079D68S3AW]] of the line $L = \set{a e : a \in \mathbb{K}}$. A vector $w$ lies in $W$ if and only if $\ip{w}{e} = 0$, by linearity in the first argument and conjugate symmetry.
15. By the [[dimension formula|0E2NYPVSP9]], $\dim W = n - \dim L = n - 1$.
16. Let $w \in W$. Then $\ip{T(w)}{e} = \ip{w}{T(e)} = \ip{w}{\lambda e} = \overline{\lambda \ip{e}{w}} = \lambda \overline{\ip{e}{w}} = \lambda \ip{w}{e} = 0$, as $\lambda$ is real. So $T(w) \in W$.
17. The restriction of $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}$ to $W$ is an inner product. Let $S \colon W \to W$ be $S(w) = T(w)$. It maps into $W$ by step 16, and it is linear since $T$ is.
18. For $w, w' \in W$, $\ip{S(w)}{w'} = \ip{T(w)}{w'} = \ip{w}{T(w')} = \ip{w}{S(w')}$. So $S$ is self-adjoint on $W$.
19. By $P(n - 1)$, $W$ has an orthonormal basis $f_2, \dots, f_n$ of eigenvectors of $S$. Each $f_k$ is nonzero and satisfies $T(f_k) = S(f_k) = \mu_k f_k$ for some $\mu_k$, so it is an eigenvector of $T$.
20. Put $f_1 = e$. Each $f_k$ with $k \ge 2$ lies in $W$, so $\ip{f_k}{f_1} = 0$, and $\ip{f_1}{f_k} = \overline{0} = 0$. With steps 13 and 19, $f_1, \dots, f_n$ is orthonormal.
21. An orthonormal family is [[linearly independent|0JWM6GJNQW]]. These $n$ vectors in the $n$-dimensional space $V$ therefore form [[a basis|096V7H13H9]].
22. So $f_1, \dots, f_n$ is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $T$. This proves $P(n)$, and by induction $P(n)$ holds for all $n$.
23. The eigenvalues of these eigenvectors are real, by the [[theorem on eigenvalues|0C4NT4GT3X]].

## Idea

Find one unit eigenvector $e$, then look at the hyperplane orthogonal to it. Self-adjointness makes this hyperplane invariant, and $T$ stays self-adjoint there, so induction gives the rest of the basis. Over $\R$, the eigenvalue comes from viewing the symmetric matrix over $\C$: its complex eigenvalues are real, so a real eigenvector exists.
