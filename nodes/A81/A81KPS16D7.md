## Proof

We find one unit eigenvector, show that its orthogonal complement is invariant, and apply induction to the smaller space.
Steps 1 to 8 prove the direction "only if" of part 1 together with part 3.
Steps 9 and 10 prove the direction "if" of part 1, and steps 11 to 13 prove part 2.

1. Let $\mathbb{K}$ be $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. For $n \geq 0$, let $P(n)$ be the claim: every [[self-adjoint|0MB8HQW495]] operator $T$ on an $n$-dimensional inner product space $V$ over $\mathbb{K}$ has an [[orthonormal basis|0FWJ0VW561]] of [[eigenvectors|0B5H4208Z9]]. We prove $P(n)$ by induction on $n$.
2. Base case. For $n = 0$ we have $V = \{0\}$, and the empty family is an orthonormal basis whose members are all eigenvectors, since it has no members.
3. Induction step: one eigenvector. Let $n \geq 1$ and assume $P(n - 1)$. By the [[theorem on real eigenvalues|0C4NT4GT3X]], $T$ has a real eigenvalue $\lambda$ and an eigenvector $e$ with $Te = \lambda e$. We divide $e$ by its norm, so that $\norm{e} = 1$.
4. The complement. Let $W$ be the [[orthogonal complement|079D68S3AW]] of the line $L$ spanned by $e$. A vector $w$ lies in $W$ exactly when $\ip{w}{e} = 0$, because $\ip{w}{ae} = \overline{a} \ip{w}{e}$ for every scalar $a$. By the [[dimension formula for the complement|0E2NYPVSP9]], $\dim W = n - 1$.
5. The complement is invariant. Let $w \in W$. Self-adjointness and $Te = \lambda e$ give
$$\ip{Tw}{e} = \ip{w}{Te} = \ip{w}{\lambda e} = \overline{\lambda} \ip{w}{e} = 0,$$
so $Tw \in W$.
6. The restriction is self-adjoint. The inner product of $V$ restricts to an inner product on $W$. Let $S \colon W \to W$ be $Sw = Tw$; it maps into $W$ by step 5, and it is linear because $T$ is. For $w, w' \in W$ we get $\ip{Sw}{w'} = \ip{Tw}{w'} = \ip{w}{Tw'} = \ip{w}{Sw'}$.
7. Induction. By $P(n - 1)$, the space $W$ has an orthonormal basis $f_2, \dots, f_n$ of eigenvectors of $S$. Each $f_k$ is nonzero and $T f_k = S f_k = \mu_k f_k$, so each $f_k$ is an eigenvector of $T$.
8. The basis of $V$. Put $f_1 = e$. Each $f_k$ with $k \geq 2$ lies in $W$, so $\ip{f_k}{f_1} = 0$, and $\norm{f_1} = 1$. So $f_1, \dots, f_n$ is an orthonormal family of $n$ vectors. Orthonormal families are [[linearly independent|0JWM6GJNQW]], so it is a [[basis|096V7H13H9]] of the $n$-dimensional space $V$. This proves $P(n)$, and the eigenvalues are real by the theorem on real eigenvalues.
9. Direction "if" of part 1. Let $V$ be real, and let $f_1, \dots, f_n$ be an orthonormal basis with $T f_k = \lambda_k f_k$. The matrix $D$ of $T$ in this basis is diagonal with real entries.
10. By the [[matrix of the adjoint|0DBTE6HVBD]] in an orthonormal basis, the matrix of $T^*$ is $D^{\mathsf{T}} = D$. Two operators with the same matrix in one basis are equal, so $T^* = T$.
11. Part 2, direction "only if". Let $A$ be a real [[symmetric|0N28QN4CYA]] matrix, and give $\mathbb{R}^n$ the dot product $\ip{x}{y} = y^{\mathsf{T}} x$. Then
$$\ip{Ax}{y} = y^{\mathsf{T}} A x = (A^{\mathsf{T}} y)^{\mathsf{T}} x = (Ay)^{\mathsf{T}} x = \ip{x}{Ay},$$
so $x \mapsto Ax$ is self-adjoint, and part 1 gives an orthonormal basis $q_1, \dots, q_n$ with $A q_k = \lambda_k q_k$.
12. Let $Q$ have the columns $q_1, \dots, q_n$ and let $D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$. The entry $(i, j)$ of $Q^{\mathsf{T}} Q$ is $q_i^{\mathsf{T}} q_j$, which is $1$ for $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise, so $Q^{\mathsf{T}} Q = I$ and $Q$ is [[orthogonal|0QRBVFJ50X]]. By the [[one-sided inverse theorem|0753Y2X5K6]], also $Q Q^{\mathsf{T}} = I$.
13. Column $k$ of $AQ$ is $A q_k = \lambda_k q_k$, which is column $k$ of $QD$ by the rule of [[matrix multiplication|076JGP6R3T]]. So $AQ = QD$, and multiplying on the right by $Q^{\mathsf{T}}$ gives $A = Q D Q^{\mathsf{T}}$. Conversely, if $A = Q D Q^{\mathsf{T}}$ with $D$ diagonal, then $A^{\mathsf{T}} = Q D^{\mathsf{T}} Q^{\mathsf{T}} = A$, so $A$ is symmetric.

## Idea

The proof needs only one eigenvector at each stage.
Once we have a unit eigenvector $e$, self-adjointness does the rest: it moves $T$ across the inner product, so a vector orthogonal to $e$ stays orthogonal to $e$ after we apply $T$.
So the hyperplane orthogonal to $e$ is a smaller space on which $T$ is still self-adjoint, and induction supplies the remaining eigenvectors.
The converse holds because a real diagonal matrix equals its own transpose.

## Variants

Step 3 can use the fundamental theorem of algebra instead of the maximum of the quadratic form.
Over $\mathbb{C}$, every operator has [[an eigenvalue|08CJB2NQ92]], and it is real because $T$ is self-adjoint.
Over $\mathbb{R}$, view the symmetric matrix of $T$ as a complex matrix: it has a complex eigenvector, its eigenvalue is real, and then the real part or the imaginary part of that eigenvector is a real eigenvector.

The [[variational proof|GHGBEV51GZ]] replaces the induction by a sequence of maxima on smaller and smaller unit spheres, and it also orders the eigenvalues.
Over $\mathbb{C}$, part 3 follows from the [[complex spectral theorem|04CW1P44VD]], because a self-adjoint operator is normal.
