## Proof

Call a matrix a *T-product* if it is a finite product of [[transvection matrices|0NMN00FNW1]]; for $n = 1$ the only T-product is the empty product $I_1$.
By the [[theorem on elementary matrices|F7F6AA3XQ7]], $T_{ij}(c) X$ adds $c$ times row $j$ of $X$ to row $i$.
Also, $X T_{ij}(c)$ adds $c$ times column $i$ to column $j$.
By the same theorem, $T_{ij}(c)^{-1} = T_{ij}(-c)$, so the inverse of a T-product is a T-product, with the factors in reverse order.

**Claim.** For each $A \in \mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ there are T-products $L$ and $R$ with $L A R = D_n(\det A)$.

We prove the claim by induction on $n$.

**Base case $n = 1$.**

1. Let $A = (a)$ with $a \ne 0$. Then $\det A = a$.
2. So $A = (a) = D_1(\det A)$, and the claim holds with $L = R = I_1$.

**Induction step.** Let $n \ge 2$, let $A \in \mathrm{GL}_n(F)$, and assume the claim for size $n - 1$.

3. Column 1 of $A$ is nonzero. Otherwise $A e_1 = 0$, so $e_1 = A^{-1} A e_1 = 0$, which is false.
4. Make $a_{21} \ne 0$. If $a_{21} \ne 0$, do nothing. If $a_{21} = 0$ and $a_{11} \ne 0$, multiply on the left by $T_{21}(1)$; the new entry $(2, 1)$ is $a_{11}$. If $a_{11} = a_{21} = 0$, then $a_{k1} \ne 0$ for some $k \ge 3$ by step 3; multiply on the left by $T_{2k}(1)$, and the new entry $(2, 1)$ is $a_{k1}$.
5. Make $a_{11} = 1$. Multiply on the left by $T_{12}(c)$ with $c = (1 - a_{11})/a_{21}$. The new entry $(1, 1)$ is $a_{11} + c\, a_{21} = 1$.
6. Clear column 1. For each $i \ge 2$, multiply on the left by $T_{i1}(-a_{i1})$. This subtracts $a_{i1}$ times row 1 from row $i$, so entry $(i, 1)$ becomes $0$. Column 1 is now $e_1$.
7. Clear row 1. For each $j \ge 2$, multiply on the right by $T_{1j}(-a_{1j})$. This subtracts $a_{1j}$ times column 1 from column $j$. Column 1 is $e_1$, so only entry $(1, j)$ changes, and it becomes $0$.
8. By steps 4 to 7 there are T-products $P$ and $Q$ with $P A Q = \operatorname{diag}(1, A')$, a [[block matrix|0ASDFEQS6P]] with $A'$ of size $n - 1$.
9. The matrix $B = \operatorname{diag}(1, A')$ is invertible, as a product of the invertible matrices $P$, $A$ and $Q$. Write $B^{-1}$ in blocks as $\begin{pmatrix} y & r \\ s & Y' \end{pmatrix}$. Block multiplication gives $B B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} y & r \\ A's & A'Y' \end{pmatrix}$ and $B^{-1} B = \begin{pmatrix} y & rA' \\ s & Y'A' \end{pmatrix}$. Both equal $I_n$, so $A'Y' = Y'A' = I_{n-1}$, and $A' \in \mathrm{GL}_{n-1}(F)$ by the [[definition of the inverse|0TWTS88TWN]].
10. Each transvection is triangular with diagonal entries $1$, so its determinant is $1$, by the [[determinant of a triangular matrix|0K1KKMFHYH]].
11. By the [[multiplicativity of the determinant|04CPJ6AF00]] and step 10, $\det(P A Q) = \det A$. By the [[determinant of a block triangular matrix|0J9TVTNB4K]], $\det \operatorname{diag}(1, A') = \det A'$. So $\det A' = \det A$.
12. By the induction hypothesis, $L' A' R' = D_{n-1}(\det A')$ for T-products $L'$ and $R'$ of size $n - 1$.
13. For a transvection of size $n - 1$, $\operatorname{diag}(1, T_{ij}(c)) = T_{i+1, j+1}(c)$ of size $n$. By block multiplication, $\operatorname{diag}(1, XY) = \operatorname{diag}(1, X) \operatorname{diag}(1, Y)$. So $\operatorname{diag}(1, L')$ and $\operatorname{diag}(1, R')$ are T-products.
14. By block multiplication and steps 8, 11 and 12, $\operatorname{diag}(1, L')\, P A Q\, \operatorname{diag}(1, R') = \operatorname{diag}(1, D_{n-1}(\det A)) = D_n(\det A)$.
15. Set $L = \operatorname{diag}(1, L') P$ and $R = Q \operatorname{diag}(1, R')$. These are T-products, and $L A R = D_n(\det A)$. This proves the claim.

**Part 1.**

16. Let $D = D_n(\delta)$ with $\delta = \det A$. By the claim, $A = L^{-1} D R^{-1}$.
17. For $n \ge 2$ and a transvection $T_{ij}(c)$, the product $D T_{ij}(c) D^{-1} = I_n + c\, D E_{ij} D^{-1}$ equals $T_{ij}(c\, d_i / d_j)$, where $d_k$ is entry $(k, k)$ of $D$. So $D X D^{-1}$ is a T-product for each T-product $X$.
18. So $A = L^{-1} (D R^{-1} D^{-1}) D = T D$, with the T-product $T = L^{-1} D R^{-1} D^{-1}$. For $n = 1$, $A = D_1(\det A)$ by step 2.

**Part 2.**

19. Each T-product has determinant $1$ by steps 10 and 11, so the T-products lie in $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$.
20. If $A \in \mathrm{SL}_n(F)$, then $D_n(\det A) = D_n(1) = I_n$, and part 1 gives $A = T$, a T-product.
21. Products and inverses of T-products are T-products. So the T-products form the subgroup generated by the transvections, and this subgroup is $\mathrm{SL}_n(F)$.

**Part 3.**

22. By part 1, each $A \in \mathrm{GL}_n(F)$ is a product of transvections and one matrix $D_n(\delta)$ with $\delta \ne 0$.
23. These generators are invertible, so the subgroup they generate lies in $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$, and by step 22 it is all of $\mathrm{GL}_n(F)$.

## Idea

Row reduction with row additions only can still bring a $1$ into the corner.
The reason is that a suitable multiple of a nonzero entry below can be added.
Then the first row and column are cleared, and induction treats the rest.
The determinant is kept at each step, so the last diagonal entry is $\det A$.

## Variants

The [[lemma on reduction to diagonal form|CFB41AZX87]] gives $A = L D R$ with a diagonal $D$ of determinant $\det A$.
One can then reduce $D$ to $D_n(\det A)$ with the $2 \times 2$ identity $\operatorname{diag}(a, a^{-1}) = T_{12}(a)\, T_{21}(-a^{-1})\, T_{12}(a)\, T_{12}(-1)\, T_{21}(1)\, T_{12}(-1)$.
