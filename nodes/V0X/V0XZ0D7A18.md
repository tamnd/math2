## Proof

We write $A_i$ as a product $A X_i$, where the determinant of $X_i$ is the unknown $x_i$, and then we apply multiplicativity.

1. Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over the field $F$ with $\det A \ne 0$, and let $b \in F^n$.
   By the [[invertibility criterion|FBSAPTP1PR]], $A$ is invertible.
2. Existence: $x = A^{-1} b$ satisfies $Ax = (A A^{-1}) b = b$, by [[associativity|073E4XC016]].
3. Uniqueness: if $Ay = b$, then $y = A^{-1}(Ay) = A^{-1} b = x$.
4. Now let $x$ be the solution of $Ax = b$, and fix $i$.
   Let $X_i$ be the [[identity matrix|06DW9BAWB1]] $I_n$ with column $i$ replaced by $x$.
5. By the definition of the [[matrix product|076JGP6R3T]], column $j$ of $A X_i$ is $A$ times column $j$ of $X_i$.
   For $j \ne i$, this is $A e_j$, which is column $j$ of $A$.
   For $j = i$, it is $Ax = b$.
   So $A X_i = A_i$.
6. Row $i$ of $X_i$ has the single nonzero entry $x_i$, in position $(i, i)$, because the other columns are unit vectors $e_j$ with $j \ne i$.
   Deleting row $i$ and column $i$ of $X_i$ leaves $I_{n-1}$, whose determinant is $1$.
7. The [[Laplace expansion|0C08BQ3M05]] along row $i$ therefore has one term:
$$\det X_i = (-1)^{i+i}\, x_i \det I_{n-1} = x_i.$$
8. By [[multiplicativity|04CPJ6AF00]] and steps 5 and 7,
$$\det A_i = \det(A X_i) = \det A \cdot \det X_i = x_i \det A.$$
9. Since $\det A \ne 0$, we can divide, and $x_i = \det A_i / \det A$.

## Idea

Put the solution $x$ into column $i$ of the identity matrix.
That matrix is triangular apart from one column, and its determinant is just $x_i$.
Multiplying it on the left by $A$ keeps every column of $A$ except column $i$, which becomes $Ax = b$.
So $A_i = A X_i$, and the product rule turns this into $\det A_i = x_i \det A$.

For example, take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $b = (5, 10)$, with solution $x = (1, 3)$.
Then
$$A X_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 10 \end{pmatrix} = A_2,$$
and $\det A_2 = 15 = 5 \cdot 3 = \det A \cdot x_2$.

## Variants

Steps 4 to 8 use no division and no inverse, so they hold over every commutative ring: each solution of $Ax = b$ satisfies $x_i \det A = \det A_i$.
Another version uses multilinearity in the columns alone.
Write $b = \sum_j x_j a_j$, where $a_j$ is column $j$ of $A$, and expand $\det A_i$ in its column $i$.
Every term with $j \ne i$ has the column $a_j$ twice, so it vanishes, and the term $j = i$ is $x_i \det A$.
