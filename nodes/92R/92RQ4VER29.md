## Definition

Model a component of the climate system by a [[dynamical system|BBZP1R872N]] $\dot x = f(x, \mu)$, possibly with noise.
Here $x$ is the state and $\mu$ is a forcing parameter.
A *tipping point* is a value or a rate of change of $\mu$ with the following property.
A small further change of the forcing makes the state leave the neighbourhood of a stable [[equilibrium|0N8NGKDZDZ]] or attractor, and move to a distant one.
Three mechanisms are distinguished:

1. *bifurcation-induced tipping*: as $\mu$ crosses a critical value $\mu_c$, the stable state the system follows loses stability or disappears, typically in a [[saddle-node bifurcation|MAP5J6HK0T]];
2. *noise-induced tipping*: random fluctuations push the state over the boundary of its basin of attraction while $\mu$ stays fixed;
3. *rate-induced tipping*: $\mu$ changes faster than a critical rate, and the state cannot track its moving stable state, which exists for each fixed $\mu$.

## Remarks

Example: the scalar system $\dot x = \mu + x - x^3$ has two stable equilibria for $\abs{\mu} < 2 / (3 \sqrt 3)$.
If $\mu$ decreases slowly through $-2/(3\sqrt3)$, the upper equilibrium merges with the middle one and disappears, and $x$ jumps to the lower branch; this is clause 1.
To return, $\mu$ must rise above $+2/(3\sqrt3)$, so the change is not reversed by undoing the forcing.
The [[Stommel box model|JF9HFV4E4J]] and the [[Budyko–Sellers model|GEQJK8XJ4C]] have tipping points of this kind.

Non-example: the linear system $\dot x = \mu - x$ has the single stable equilibrium $x = \mu$, which moves continuously with $\mu$ and attracts every state; it has no tipping point.

Near a saddle-node bifurcation the linear rate of return to the stable state tends to $0$ (critical slowing down).
In a noisy system this raises the variance and the lag-one autocorrelation of the state, which are used as early warning signals.
See [[bifurcation theory|D9TW46P19Y]] for the general theory.
