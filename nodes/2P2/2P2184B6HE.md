## Summary

*Linear logic* is the logic of a one-sided [[sequent calculus|W72CA3R9MP]] with an involutive negation $A^{\perp}$.
Its connectives are the multiplicatives $\otimes$, $\mathbin{\wp}$, $1$, $\bot$, the additives $\mathbin{\&}$, $\oplus$, $\top$, $0$, and the exponentials $!$ and $?$.
[[Weakening|VHRXX0T5XT]] and [[contraction|V0MZVC2XA9]] are allowed only for formulas of the form $?A$; see [[the definition|H408H4WHBA]].
A derivable sequent $\vdash A^{\perp}, B$ reads "one use of $A$ gives one $B$", so each hypothesis is a resource that is used exactly once unless it carries an exponential.
Linear logic splits each classical connective into two, keeps the symmetry of classical logic, and contains intuitionistic and classical logic through translations.

## Motivation

In classical and intuitionistic logic, a hypothesis can be used any number of times, including zero.
This is right for facts, but wrong for resources such as money, memory cells or messages.
If we drop weakening and contraction, the rules for conjunction split: one conjunction splits the context ($\otimes$, "both at once"), and one shares it ($\mathbin{\&}$, "a choice of one").
The exponential $!A$ then puts back the old behavior where it is needed: $!A$ means "as many copies of $A$ as we like".

## Notation

We use Girard's notation: $\otimes$ (tensor), $\mathbin{\wp}$ (par), $\mathbin{\&}$ (with), $\oplus$ (plus), $!$ (of course), $?$ (why not), $A^{\perp}$ (linear negation) and $A \multimap B = A^{\perp} \mathbin{\wp} B$.
Other authors use different symbols for the same connectives.
For example, Troelstra writes $\star$ for $\otimes$, $+$ for $\mathbin{\wp}$, $\sqcap$ for $\mathbin{\&}$ and $\sqcup$ for $\oplus$, and Restall writes $\circ$, $+$, $\wedge$ and $\vee$.
Some authors also exchange the names of the constants $\bot$ and $0$, so the rules of each source must be checked.

## Remarks

### Examples

1. $A \otimes B \multimap B \otimes A$ is derivable; see [[the derivation|8KN0QTZ1ED]].
2. The Girard translation sends $p \to (q \to p)$ to $!p \multimap (!q \multimap p)$, which is derivable; see [[the translation|GH4V713P9E]].
3. $!A \multimap A \otimes A$ is derivable with contraction on $?A^{\perp}$; see [[controlled contraction|9ZMEDB2YP4]].
4. $A \multimap A \mathbin{\&} A$ is derivable, since the rule for $\mathbin{\&}$ shares the context.
5. $!(A \mathbin{\&} B) \multimap {!A} \otimes {!B}$ is derivable; the exponential turns an additive conjunction into a multiplicative one.

### Non-examples

1. $A \multimap A \otimes A$ is not derivable, although $p \to p \wedge p$ is an intuitionistic theorem; see [[the non-example|V444A285N3]].
2. $A \multimap (B \multimap A)$ is not derivable, since weakening is not available for $B$; see [[the non-example|V1ZRAX3GBM]].
3. $A \mathbin{\&} B \multimap A \otimes B$ is not derivable for atoms.
   In a cut-free search, every choice of rules leads to a premise $\vdash A$ or $\vdash B$ with one atom, which is not derivable.

### Basic properties

1. Linear logic has cut elimination; for the multiplicative part we prove it in [[cut elimination for MLL|SYMW99C0EV]].
2. Negation is involutive: $A^{\perp\perp} = A$, so the calculus can be one-sided.
3. Provability is undecidable for full propositional linear logic and decidable for MLL and MALL; see [[decidability of linear logic|BNRWJB2R8C]].
4. Phase spaces give a complete semantics of provability, see [[phase semantics|64MHHQQ6G6]], and [[coherence spaces|WSGZ5HGK1X]] give a semantics of proofs.
5. Proofs of the multiplicative part can be written as [[proof nets|HN6WN1VE39]].

### History

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry reports that Girard introduced linear logic in 1987, from a semantic analysis of the models of System F.
Girard's paper appeared in Theoretical Computer Science 50 in 1987.
The same entry notes that earlier substructural logics include the Lambek calculus of 1958, relevant logic and affine logic.

### Common errors

1. Reading $\mathbin{\wp}$ as an ordinary "or".
   It is the dual of $\otimes$, and $A \multimap B$ is $A^{\perp} \mathbin{\wp} B$.
2. Confusing the two conjunctions.
   $A \otimes B$ gives both $A$ and $B$, while $A \mathbin{\&} B$ gives one of them, chosen by the user.
3. Mixing notations.
   In some books $0$ and $\bot$ are exchanged, and $+$ may mean $\mathbin{\wp}$ or $\oplus$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Linear logic counts its hypotheses.
With one euro we can buy a coffee, and with one euro we can buy a tea.
With one euro we cannot buy both: we need two euros.
Linear logic has one "and" for "both at once", and another "and" for "a choice of one".
A special mark, "!", says that a hypothesis is in unlimited supply, and then it may be used many times.

### L2 human

We derive $A \multimap A \mathbin{\&} A$ and check why the tensor version fails.

1. The formula is $A^{\perp} \mathbin{\wp} (A \mathbin{\&} A)$.
2. Two copies of the axiom $\vdash A^{\perp}, A$ and the rule for $\mathbin{\&}$, which shares the context $A^{\perp}$, give $\vdash A^{\perp}, A \mathbin{\&} A$.
3. The rule $\mathbin{\wp}$ gives $\vdash A^{\perp} \mathbin{\wp} (A \mathbin{\&} A)$.
4. For $A \otimes A$, the rule $\otimes$ splits the context: one premise gets $A^{\perp}$ and the other gets nothing, and $\vdash A$ is not derivable.

The typical use of linear logic is to describe processes that consume and produce resources, as in [[linear type systems|DJGYZTS0YC]].
A trap: $A \mathbin{\&} A$ does not give two copies of $A$; it gives one copy, of our choice.

### L3 human

Categorically, the models of the multiplicative part are $*$-autonomous categories, and $!$ is a comonad whose coalgebras carry the structure needed for weakening and contraction.
The Wikipedia article on linear logic reports a translation of intuitionistic logic into linear logic that preserves and reflects provability, in which $A \to B$ becomes ${!A} \multimap B$.
The proofs of linear logic are studied with [[proof nets|HN6WN1VE39]] and [[coherence spaces|WSGZ5HGK1X]], and its complexity with [[light linear logics|E7JVJSSWBT]].
The [[full Lambek calculus|V5XVVN16GS]] gives the algebraic side, and [[affine logic|K67C2N2QAH]] adds weakening back.
