## Example

We prove by [[first-order resolution|DEB3CF4Z2D]] that
$$\forall x\,(P(x) \to Q(x)),\quad \forall x\,(Q(x) \to R(x)),\quad P(a) \ \models\ R(a).$$
Read $P$ as "is a square", $Q$ as "is a rectangle", $R$ as "is a parallelogram" and $a$ as a fixed figure.

1. Negate the goal. We refute the set of the three premises together with $\neg R(a)$.
2. Clause form. The sentence $\forall x\,(P(x) \to Q(x))$ is equivalent to $\forall x\,(\neg P(x) \lor Q(x))$, so it gives the [[clause|T3SXVH3ZZC]] $C_1 = \{\neg P(x), Q(x)\}$.
3. The second premise gives $\{\neg Q(x), R(x)\}$; we rename its variable apart and write $C_2 = \{\neg Q(y), R(y)\}$.
4. The other two sentences give the ground clauses $C_3 = \{P(a)\}$ and $C_4 = \{\neg R(a)\}$. No sentence has an existential quantifier, so no Skolem symbol is needed.
5. Resolve $C_3$ with $C_1$ on $P(a)$ and $\neg P(x)$. The [[unification|RJJ9D0WF53]] of $P(a)$ and $P(x)$ gives the equation $a = x$ and the mgu $\sigma_1 = \{x \mapsto a\}$.
6. Check: $P(x)\sigma_1 = P(a)$. The resolvent is $\{Q(x)\}\sigma_1 = \{Q(a)\}$; call it $C_5$.
7. Resolve $C_5$ with $C_2$ on $Q(a)$ and $\neg Q(y)$. The equation $a = y$ gives the mgu $\sigma_2 = \{y \mapsto a\}$, and $Q(y)\sigma_2 = Q(a)$.
8. The resolvent is $\{R(y)\}\sigma_2 = \{R(a)\}$; call it $C_6$.
9. Resolve $C_6$ with $C_4$ on $R(a)$ and $\neg R(a)$. The two atoms are already equal, so the mgu is the empty substitution $\varepsilon$.
10. The resolvent is $(\varnothing \cup \varnothing)\varepsilon = \square$.

The set $\{C_1, C_2, C_3, C_4\}$ has a refutation, so it is unsatisfiable by soundness, and $R(a)$ follows from the premises.

## Remarks

The refutation can also start with the two general clauses.
Resolve $C_1 = \{\neg P(x), Q(x)\}$ with $C_2 = \{\neg Q(y), R(y)\}$ on $Q(x)$ and $\neg Q(y)$.
The equation $x = y$ gives the mgu $\{x \mapsto y\}$, and the resolvent is $\{\neg P(y), R(y)\}$.
This clause is the derived rule $\forall y\,(P(y) \to R(y))$, so resolution can produce general lemmas, not only ground facts.
Then $\{\neg P(y), R(y)\}$ and $\{P(a)\}$ give $\{R(a)\}$ with the mgu $\{y \mapsto a\}$, and $\{R(a)\}$ and $\{\neg R(a)\}$ give $\square$.

When the fact $P(a)$ is replaced by $P(b)$, there is no refutation.
The new set is satisfiable: take the domain $\{a, b\}$ with $P$, $Q$ and $R$ true at $b$ only.
By soundness, a satisfiable set has no refutation.
The prover still derives clauses such as $\{Q(b)\}$, $\{R(b)\}$, $\{\neg Q(a)\}$ and $\{\neg P(a)\}$.
But $\{R(b)\}$ never clashes with $\{\neg R(a)\}$, since the constants $a$ and $b$ do not unify.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Every square is a rectangle, and every rectangle is a parallelogram.
A given figure is a square.
To show that it is a parallelogram, we assume the opposite and look for a clash.
"Not a square, or a rectangle" together with "a square" gives "a rectangle".
"Not a rectangle, or a parallelogram" then gives "a parallelogram".
This clashes with the assumption "not a parallelogram", so the assumption is false.

### L2 human

The work of a resolution prover on this input is the following.

1. It stores the four clauses $C_1$ to $C_4$ with variables renamed apart.
2. It picks pairs of complementary literals whose atoms unify, for example $\neg P(x)$ in $C_1$ and $P(a)$ in $C_3$.
3. It computes the mgu, here $\{x \mapsto a\}$, and applies it to the rest of both clauses.
4. It adds the resolvent and repeats until $\square$ appears.

The typical use is the check of a chain of rules in a knowledge base, as in logic programming with Horn clauses.
A trap: in a step that uses $C_1$ and $C_2$, their variables must be different.
Renaming apart is part of the rule.
