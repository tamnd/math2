## Example

Let $F$ be a CNF over $n$ variables, and let $w \ge 1$.
The *width* of a clause is its number of literals, and $w(F \vdash \square)$ is the least width of the widest clause in a [[resolution|6JSH907W5R]] refutation of $F$.

**Algorithm $\mathrm{Search}_w$.**

1. Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the set of clauses of $F$ that have width at most $w$.
2. For every pair of clauses in $\mathcal{C}$ that clash on exactly one variable, form their resolvent. Add it to $\mathcal{C}$ if its width is at most $w$ and it is not yet in $\mathcal{C}$. Record the two premises.
3. Repeat step 2 until $\square \in \mathcal{C}$ or until a round adds no clause.
4. If $\square \in \mathcal{C}$, output the recorded derivation of $\square$. Otherwise output "no refutation of width $w$".

**Analysis.**

- *Size.* A clause of width at most $w$ picks at most $w$ of the $2n$ literals, so there are at most $(2n + 1)^w$ such clauses. So $\mathcal{C}$ never has more than $(2n + 1)^w$ clauses.
- *Time.* Each round adds a clause or stops, and each round checks at most $\abs{\mathcal{C}}^2$ pairs. So the time is polynomial in $\abs{F}$ and $n^{w}$, that is, $\abs{F}^{O(1)} n^{O(w)}$.
- *Correctness.* Every clause in $\mathcal{C}$ is derived by resolution from $F$, so an output derivation is a refutation of width at most $w$.
- *Completeness.* Suppose $F$ has a refutation in which every clause has width at most $w$. By induction along the refutation, each of its clauses enters $\mathcal{C}$. So $\square$ enters $\mathcal{C}$.

**A run.**
Take $F = \{x \lor y,\ x \lor \neg y,\ \neg x \lor y,\ \neg x \lor \neg y\}$ and $w = 2$.

1. Round 1 resolves $x \lor y$ with $x \lor \neg y$ on $y$, which gives $x$. In the same way it gives $\neg x$, $y$ and $\neg y$. Pairs such as $x \lor y$ and $\neg x \lor \neg y$ clash on two variables, so they give no resolvent.
2. Round 2 resolves $x$ with $\neg x$, which gives $\square$.
3. The output has seven clauses: the four axioms, $x$, $\neg x$ and $\square$.

## Remarks

- With $w = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ in turn, the search finds a refutation in time $n^{O(w(F \vdash \square))}$, up to a factor polynomial in $\abs{F}$.
- By the first part of the [[size-width relation|GWDGRPAQSM]], $w(F \vdash \square) \le w(F) + \log_2 S_T(F \vdash \square)$, where $S_T$ is the least tree-like size. So the search runs in time $n^{O(w(F) + \log S_T(F \vdash \square))}$, which is quasi-polynomial in $n + S_T(F \vdash \square)$ when $w(F)$ is at most polylogarithmic in $n$.
- The output may be a dag-like refutation, since a clause in $\mathcal{C}$ can be used many times. So the algorithm automates tree-like resolution only in the weak sense, with general resolution as the output system.
- For general resolution the second part of the size-width relation gives only $n^{O(w(F) + \sqrt{n \log S(F \vdash \square)})}$. This is not quasi-polynomial in $S$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

The program tries all short clauses that it can make from the formula.
If a proof that uses only short clauses exists, the program finds it.
Since there are not many short clauses, the program is fast when the clauses may be short.

### L2 human

The search is breadth-first over the set of clauses of width at most $w$.
The number $(2n + 1)^w$ counts the choices of at most $w$ literals with a blank, and it is what makes the time $n^{O(w)}$.
A trap is to think that width search is fast for every formula with a short refutation.
The [[pigeonhole formulas|RJ571F7FF0]] have initial clauses of width $n$, so the search must start from width $n$.
For formulas whose refutations all need width close to $n$, the bound $n^{O(w)}$ is exponential.

### L3 human

Width search is the basic positive result about proof search in resolution.
Together with the size-width relation it gives the quasi-polynomial bound for tree-like resolution.
The [[Atserias-Müller theorem|W7FP9TZMVP]] shows that no polynomial-time version exists for general resolution, unless P = NP.
