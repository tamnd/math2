## Proof

We use the rules of the [[full Lambek calculus|Q47701D0E1]] as stated there.
Let $\mathsf{X}$ be $\mathsf{FL}$, $\mathsf{FL}_e$, $\mathsf{FL}_w$ or $\mathsf{FL}_{ew}$.
The *degree* $|A|$ of a formula is the number of occurrences of atoms, constants and connectives in $A$, so a proper subformula has a smaller degree.
The height $h(D)$ of a derivation $D$ is the largest number of rule applications on a branch, so an axiom has height $0$.

**Main lemma.**
Let $D_1$ be a cut-free derivation of $\Gamma \Rightarrow A$ and $D_2$ a cut-free derivation of $\Delta, A, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$, where $A$ is the marked occurrence.
Then $\Delta, \Gamma, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$ has a cut-free derivation.
We prove this by induction on the pair $(|A|, h(D_1) + h(D_2))$ in the [[lexicographic order|0G77XFBV00]], which is a well-order on pairs of natural numbers.
The formula $A$ is *principal* in $D_1$ if $D_1$ is the axiom $\Rightarrow 1$ or ends with a right logical rule (including $0\mathrm{R}$) that makes $A$.
It is principal in $D_2$ if $D_2$ is the axiom $0 \Rightarrow$ or ends with a left logical rule (including $1\mathrm{L}$) that makes the marked $A$.

Case 1: $D_2$ is the axiom $A \Rightarrow A$.

1. Then $\Delta$ and $\Sigma$ are empty and $\Pi = A$, so the goal is $\Gamma \Rightarrow A$.
2. $D_1$ derives it without cut.

Case 2: $D_1$ is the axiom $A \Rightarrow A$.

1. Then $\Gamma = A$, so the goal is $\Delta, A, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$.
2. $D_2$ derives it without cut.

Case 3: $A$ is not principal in $D_1$, and $D_1$ is not an axiom.

1. Then $D_1$ ends with a left rule, with left exchange or left weakening on $\Gamma$, or with right weakening.
2. If $D_1$ ends with right weakening, its premise is $\Gamma \Rightarrow$ (empty right side).
   Left weakening adds $\Delta$ and $\Sigma$, and right weakening adds $\Pi$ if $\Pi$ is not empty.
   This uses no cut.
3. If $D_1$ ends with ${\cdot}\mathrm{L}$, then $\Gamma = \Gamma_1, B \cdot C, \Gamma_2$, with premise $\Gamma_1, B, C, \Gamma_2 \Rightarrow A$ of smaller height.
4. The induction hypothesis, with the same $A$ and a smaller sum of heights, gives a cut-free derivation of $\Delta, \Gamma_1, B, C, \Gamma_2, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$.
5. ${\cdot}\mathrm{L}$ then gives $\Delta, \Gamma_1, B \cdot C, \Gamma_2, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$, which is the goal.
6. If $D_1$ ends with ${\backslash}\mathrm{L}$, then $\Gamma = \Gamma_1, \Gamma', B \backslash C, \Gamma_2$, with premises $\Gamma' \Rightarrow B$ and $\Gamma_1, C, \Gamma_2 \Rightarrow A$.
7. The induction hypothesis on the second premise and $D_2$ gives $\Delta, \Gamma_1, C, \Gamma_2, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$, and ${\backslash}\mathrm{L}$ with $\Gamma' \Rightarrow B$ gives the goal.
8. The rules ${/}\mathrm{L}$, ${\wedge}\mathrm{L}$, $1\mathrm{L}$, exchange and left weakening are treated as in steps 3 to 5.
   For ${\vee}\mathrm{L}$ we apply the induction hypothesis to both premises.

Case 4: $A$ is principal in $D_1$ but not in $D_2$, and $D_2$ is not the axiom $A \Rightarrow A$.

1. Then $D_2$ ends with a right rule, a left rule on a formula other than the marked $A$, a structural rule, or left weakening that makes the marked $A$.
2. If left weakening makes the marked $A$, its premise is $\Delta, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$.
   Left weakening on each formula of $\Gamma$ gives the goal without cut.
3. Otherwise the marked $A$ occurs in the context of each premise that contains it.
   We apply the induction hypothesis to $D_1$ and each such premise, which has smaller height.
4. Then we apply the same last rule.
   For a rule with two premises that splits the context, such as ${\cdot}\mathrm{R}$, only one premise contains $A$.
   For ${\wedge}\mathrm{R}$ and ${\vee}\mathrm{L}$, both premises contain $A$, and we cut in both.
5. If $D_2$ ends with exchange of the marked $A$ and a neighbor $C$, the cut on the premise gives $C$ on the other side of $\Gamma$.
   As many exchanges as $\Gamma$ has formulas move $C$ back past $\Gamma$.

Case 5: $A$ is principal in both $D_1$ and $D_2$.

1. $A = 1$: $D_1$ is $\Rightarrow 1$, so $\Gamma$ is empty, and $D_2$ ends with $1\mathrm{L}$ with premise $\Delta, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$, which is the goal.
2. $A = 0$: $D_1$ ends with $0\mathrm{R}$ with premise $\Gamma \Rightarrow$, and $D_2$ is $0 \Rightarrow$, so the goal is $\Gamma \Rightarrow$, which is that premise.
3. $A = B \cdot C$: $D_1$ has premises $\Gamma_1 \Rightarrow B$ and $\Gamma_2 \Rightarrow C$ with $\Gamma = \Gamma_1, \Gamma_2$, and $D_2$ has premise $\Delta, B, C, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$.
4. The induction hypothesis on $C$, of smaller degree, gives a cut-free derivation of $\Delta, B, \Gamma_2, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$.
   The induction hypothesis on $B$ then gives $\Delta, \Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$.
5. $A = B \backslash C$: $D_1$ has premise $B, \Gamma \Rightarrow C$, and $D_2$ has premises $\Gamma' \Rightarrow B$ and $\Delta', C, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$ with $\Delta = \Delta', \Gamma'$.
6. The induction hypothesis on $B$ gives $\Gamma', \Gamma \Rightarrow C$.
   The induction hypothesis on $C$ gives $\Delta', \Gamma', \Gamma, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$, which is the goal.
7. $A = C / B$ is the mirror image of step 5 and step 6.
8. $A = B \wedge C$ with ${\wedge}\mathrm{L}$ on $B$: $D_1$ has premise $\Gamma \Rightarrow B$, and $D_2$ has premise $\Delta, B, \Sigma \Rightarrow \Pi$.
   The induction hypothesis on $B$ gives the goal.
   The case of $C$ and the case of $\vee$ are the same.

The five cases cover all pairs $D_1$, $D_2$.
If $A$ is not principal in $D_1$, Case 2 or Case 3 applies.
If $A$ is principal in $D_1$, Case 1, Case 4 or Case 5 applies.
This proves the main lemma.

**Part 1.**

1. Let $D$ be a derivation in $\mathsf{X}$ with $n$ cuts.
   We use induction on $n$.
2. If $n > 0$, choose a cut with no cut above it.
   Its two premises have cut-free derivations.
3. The main lemma replaces this cut and the two derivations above it by a cut-free derivation of the same sequent.
4. The new derivation has $n - 1$ cuts, and the induction hypothesis gives a cut-free derivation.

**Part 2.**

1. In each rule other than cut, every formula of a premise is a subformula of a formula of the conclusion.
   We check this rule by rule: each premise contains the context and immediate subformulas of the principal formula.
2. By induction from the end sequent upward, every formula of a cut-free derivation is a subformula of a formula of the end sequent.

**Part 3.**

1. In each rule other than cut, each premise has a size at most the size of the conclusion.
   Only exchange keeps the size equal, and the other rules lower it.
2. Let $S$ be a sequent of size $n$.
   By Part 2 and step 1, every sequent of a cut-free derivation of $S$ has size at most $n$ and contains only subformulas of $S$.
3. Each formula has size at least $1$, so such a sequent has at most $n$ formulas.
   The set $T$ of such sequents is finite, since $S$ has finitely many subformulas.
4. Let $T_0$ be the set of axioms in $T$, and let $T_{k+1}$ add to $T_k$ the conclusions in $T$ of rules other than cut with premises in $T_k$.
5. The sets $T_k$ grow inside the finite set $T$, so they stop growing after at most $|T|$ steps.
6. By Part 1, $S$ is derivable if and only if it lies in the last set.
   The algorithm computes these sets and checks whether $S$ is in the last one.

## Idea

A cut on $A$ is moved up through the derivation until $A$ is made by the last rule on both sides.
There it is replaced by cuts on the immediate subformulas of $A$.
The pair (degree of $A$, sum of heights) decreases at each step, and without contraction no step copies a cut.

## Variants

Gentzen's proof for LK uses the mix rule, which removes all copies of the cut formula at once, to handle contraction.
A semantic proof of cut admissibility uses a completion of a residuated frame built from the cut-free calculus, and it gives the same result for many extensions at once.
For linear logic, a proof by [[phase semantics|64MHHQQ6G6]] in the style of Okada shows cut admissibility without a syntactic reduction.
