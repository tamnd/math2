## Example

The first proposition of Book I of [[Euclid's Elements|83WHDAV68J]] constructs an equilateral triangle on a given segment $AB$.
We follow the proof step by step and find the one step that the axioms do not support.

1. Draw the [[circle|060W5CBZP6]] with center $A$ through $B$, and the circle with center $B$ through $A$.
   Euclid's third postulate allows a circle with any center and any radius, so this step is justified.
2. Take a point $C$ where the two circles meet.
   No postulate and no common notion of Euclid says that the two circles meet, so this step has no justification in the system.
3. Then $AC = AB$, because $B$ and $C$ lie on the circle with center $A$.
   Also $BC = BA$, because $A$ and $C$ lie on the circle with center $B$.
4. So $AB$, $AC$ and $BC$ are equal, by the common notion that things equal to the same thing are equal to each other, and the triangle $ABC$ is equilateral.

A model shows that the gap in step 2 is real.
We take as "plane" the set $\mathbb{Q}^2$ of points with rational coordinates, with $A = (0, 0)$ and $B = (1, 0)$.

5. The two circles have the equations
   $$x^2 + y^2 = 1, \qquad (x - 1)^2 + y^2 = 1.$$
6. A common point would satisfy the difference of the two equations.
   $$x^2 - (x - 1)^2 = 2x - 1 = 0, \quad \text{so } x = \tfrac{1}{2}.$$
7. Then $y^2 = 1 - \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{3}{4}$, so $y = \pm \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.
8. The number $\sqrt{3}$ is [[irrational|080T2TXVQV]].
   If $y$ were rational, then $\sqrt{3} = \pm 2y$ would be rational, so $y$ is not rational.
9. Hence the two circles have no common point in $\mathbb{Q}^2$.
   Each circle still has infinitely many rational points, such as $(1, 0)$ and $(\tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{4}{5})$ on the first one.

## Remarks

The example is a non-example of the [[axiomatic method|GD2JBXJV5Z]]: a fact that the figure shows is used as if it were an axiom.
It does not show that Euclid's result is false.
In the real plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ the circles meet at $(\tfrac{1}{2}, \pm\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2})$, and the triangle exists.
What it shows is that the existence of the meeting point needs an assumption of its own.

Modern systems state that assumption.
In [[Hilbert's axioms|KM1FB8XZ2R]] the axioms of continuity give it, and other systems add an explicit axiom that two circles meet under suitable conditions.
Other gaps of the same kind appear in the *Elements*, for example facts about the order of points on a line, which Hilbert covered with his axioms of betweenness.
The model $\mathbb{Q}^2$ is also a lesson in method: to show that a statement does not follow from some assumptions, find a structure where the assumptions hold and the statement fails.

## Explanations

### L1 human

To build a triangle with three equal sides, Euclid draws two circles around the ends of a segment and takes the point where they cross.
The picture shows the crossing point at once.
But none of Euclid's starting rules says that two circles must cross.
If we allow only points whose coordinates are fractions, the two circles of radius $1$ around $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 0)$ do not cross.
The crossing points would need the number $\sqrt{3}$, which is not a fraction.

### L2 human

We check the claim in step 9 that the rational point $(\tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{4}{5})$ is on the first circle but not on the second.
On the first circle:
$$\left(\tfrac{3}{5}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{4}{5}\right)^2 = \tfrac{9}{25} + \tfrac{16}{25} = 1.$$
On the second circle we would need $1$, and we get less:
$$\left(\tfrac{3}{5} - 1\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{4}{5}\right)^2 = \tfrac{4}{25} + \tfrac{16}{25} = \tfrac{20}{25}.$$
So the circles in $\mathbb{Q}^2$ are full of points, and the gap is only at the place where they should meet.
Watch out: the claim is not that $\mathbb{Q}^2$ satisfies all of Euclid's geometry.
The claim is smaller and exact: no postulate of Euclid says that the circles meet, and in $\mathbb{Q}^2$ they do not.
