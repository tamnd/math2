## Example

The [[symmetric group|0515A6D2Y1]] $S_3$ is the group of all permutations of $\{1, 2, 3\}$, with composition from right to left.
It has $3! = 6$ elements: the identity $\mathrm{id}$, the transpositions $(1\,2)$, $(1\,3)$, $(2\,3)$, and the $3$-cycles $(1\,2\,3)$, $(1\,3\,2)$.
We compute the [[order|0WE2NHZJ7B]] of each element.

1. The identity: $\mathrm{id}^1 = \mathrm{id}$, so $\ord(\mathrm{id}) = 1$.
2. The transposition $(1\,2)$: it is not the identity, because it moves $1$. Its square sends $1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 1$ and $2 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 2$, and it fixes $3$. So $(1\,2)^2 = \mathrm{id}$ and $\ord((1\,2)) = 2$.
3. The transpositions $(1\,3)$ and $(2\,3)$: the same computation with other labels gives order $2$.
4. The $3$-cycle $\sigma = (1\,2\,3)$: it sends $1 \mapsto 2$, $2 \mapsto 3$, $3 \mapsto 1$. So $\sigma^2$ sends $1 \mapsto 3$, and $\sigma^2 = (1\,3\,2) \neq \mathrm{id}$.
5. Then $\sigma^3$ sends $1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 1$, and in the same way $2 \mapsto 2$ and $3 \mapsto 3$. So $\sigma^3 = \mathrm{id}$ and $\ord(\sigma) = 3$.
6. The $3$-cycle $(1\,3\,2) = \sigma^{-1}$ has order $3$, because [[an element and its inverse have the same order|0PMT9YJZVW]].

The orders are $1$, $2$, $2$, $2$, $3$, $3$.
Each of them divides $|S_3| = 6$, as the [[result on element orders|08V7JHS3QZ]] states.

## Remarks

No element of $S_3$ has order $6$.
So $S_3$ is not [[cyclic|0KVGH0J5B3]], because a cyclic group of order $6$ has a generator of order $6$.
This shows that a group of order $n$ need not have an element of order $n$.
The group $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ also has order $6$, but [[its element orders|77N4TVRM15]] are $1, 6, 3, 2, 3, 6$, so the two groups are not [[isomorphic|08MG7H8JW5]].
For $S_n$ in general, the order of a permutation is the least common multiple of the lengths of its disjoint cycles.
For example, $(1\,2)(3\,4\,5)$ in $S_5$ has order $\operatorname{lcm}(2, 3) = 6$.

## Explanations

### L1 human

Put three cards in a row.
Swapping two cards and then swapping them again puts the cards back, so a swap has order $2$.
Moving each card one place to the right, with the last card going to the front, puts the cards back after $3$ moves, so this move has order $3$.
No move of three cards needs $6$ repeats to come back.

### L2 human

We use the orders to answer a question about products.

1. The elements $(1\,2)$ and $(1\,2\,3)$ have orders $2$ and $3$.
2. Their product $(1\,2)(1\,2\,3)$, from right to left, sends $1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 1$, $2 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 3$ and $3 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 2$.
3. So the product is $(2\,3)$, and it has order $2$, not $6$.

The typical use of the table of orders is to tell groups apart, as with $S_3$ and $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$.
A trap is to think that the order of a product is the product of the orders; this fails here because $(1\,2)$ and $(1\,2\,3)$ do not commute.
